To live in the Return Sump is to be sensitive to very small changes: a finger’s width of water, a little warmth retained by iron, a pressure wave traveling through stone before any sound. Fog and dimness make distance nearly useless. Touch, taste, and vibration are more reliable. The important clock is not the sun but the relay; the important seasons are the delayed strengthening and weakening of the seeps.

The living rust is not one continuous growth, but several communities occupying slightly different water. Close to the seep mouths, where the upwelling is milky, pale filaments form a slippery nap. Farther out, iron-using colonies give the film its familiar red-brown color. On the open shelves, grazers reduce it to cells sheltered in pits and crystal boundaries, from which it can spread again after each immersion. In protected slots it becomes thicker, supporting minute worms and larvae that feed among the trapped silt. Dead film remains as stain, then as sediment, then sometimes as a thin addition to the stone itself. The habitat is therefore partly built from past generations of its own food supply. Occasionally a pallid thread appears in the clear return water, suggesting that similar growths line conduits below the visible pools.

The buttonbacks are the film’s most numerous harvesters. An individual may spend much of its adult life on a single dome, working through the same shallow grooves and returning to the same stone refuge when the water changes. Their crowding is practical rather than sociable: packed shells divide the current, and each animal gains some shelter from the legs and bodies around it. A newly molted buttonback is almost invisible until it feeds; then rust-colored material shows faintly inside it.

Their discarded skins have a long usefulness. At first they stand among the living, apparently complete. Later they collapse into the channels, where insect larvae cut them apart for building material and microbial film colonizes their inner surfaces. Even the finest fragments become part of the shell gravel. After the seeps strengthen following distant rain, young buttonbacks appear in great numbers. Many remain close to where they hatched, but the returning water carries some into neighboring pans, so that even pools without a surface connection are not entirely isolated populations.

For most of their lives, the flying insects are not flying insects at all. They are slow, case-bearing larvae, hauling small mineral architectures through the shallows. A case may have a roof of buttonback shell, a ballast of black sand, one white panel of porcelain, and—very rarely—a copper splinter that has not yet dulled. The materials are not merely decorative. Curved shells make light walls; sand gives weight in moving water; broad flakes can be fitted over a weak seam. As the larva grows, it adds to the front of the case or abandons it and builds another. Old cases accumulate in the sediment like tiny, broken sections of pipe.

The larvae scrape film from stone, but they also take dead buttonbacks, fragments of molt, and organic silt. They are among the Sump’s principal scavengers. When the water ebbs, a larva withdraws and grips the bottom; when it returns, the case becomes a small sheltered chamber through which food-bearing water can pass. Before pupation, many fasten themselves on damp undersurfaces just beyond the ordinary reach of the water: beneath a ceramic pipe lip, inside a spillway fracture, or in the lower cavities of the rake heap.

The adults live briefly and do not appear to feed. The lamps serve them much as the engines serve the amphibians—not chiefly as light, but as small pockets of warmth in the cold air. Moisture gathers on their heavy wings, and their drifting flight has the deliberation of animals already spending the last of their reserves. After mating, the females return to wet stone and lay their eggs in seams that the return water will moisten without immediately sweeping clean. The adults that die beside the lamps fall onto the shelves or into the pans and reenter the same economy that built their cases.

The thread eels occupy the water beneath all this activity. A pale knot under a stone may contain animals of very different sizes and ages, resting together where the current is slack. The thinnest remain among the grazing shelves. The largest keep to narrow clefts, and what appears to be a short pale loop at the entrance may be the only visible part of an animal extending much farther down.

They hunt largely by touch. When the manufactured tide ebbs, the knots loosen and the eels enter the narrowing currents, taking young buttonbacks, insect larvae, and anything else dislodged from shelter. A recently fed eel may show a faint dark line inside its otherwise colorless body. Their turning toward the upper penstock before the return is more than an odd reflex. Meeting the incoming sheet head-on keeps them from being rolled sideways across the shelves, and some use that first push of water to carry themselves back beneath stones. In dry periods, the deep slots become their refuges; the largest individuals may then go a long time without fully emerging.

Warmth, rather than open water, organizes the broad-headed amphibians. The engine beds are the only extensive surfaces reliably warmer than the fog, and the animals piled upon them are making use of scarce heat rather than seeking company. Their stacks constantly rearrange themselves by small degrees: one slips a foot from beneath another, one raises its head at a drip, one moves to the iron edge and is replaced. There is no breeding chorus and almost no audible communication.

They feed without pursuit. A buttonback washed against a seep cup, a larva loosened from its case, or an insect fallen with wet wings is taken by a short movement of the broad mouth. Much of their water comes directly through the skin from condensation and damp iron.

When the seeps strengthen after rain, some of them leave the engines to lay eggs in protected overflow cups and flooded ceramic fittings. The larvae develop out of sight, with delicate external gills, in water that rarely experiences the full disturbance of the open pans. They grow slowly. An adult warming itself on the engine bed may have spent most of its early life within a few yards of that spot, hidden in a slot or pipe and gradually accumulating enough substance to tolerate the colder world above it.

The largest hunter is also the least often occupied with hunting. Most of its existence consists of waiting, digestion, and long intervals in which it scarcely seems to breathe. Its small eyes are of limited use in the mist; its wide head and body, held close to the bottom, are better suited to registering displacement in shallow water. It favors the discharge because animals are repeatedly concentrated there. Eels moving with the current and amphibians displaced from wet ledges pass within reach of a place where it can remain motionless for hours.

Its den lies beneath the rake heap. The heap offers what almost nowhere else in the Sump does: a dry cavity, insulated by shell grit and raised above the returning water. The animal’s broad, webbed tracks are sometimes visible in rust-colored silt, though they are usually softened by the next tide. There cannot be many such hunters. The chemical richness of the seeps supports dense growth on surfaces, but not a lavish supply of large prey. One meal may be followed by days without another attempt. Even the rake does not necessarily drive it from its den; it may simply shift deeper into the heap while the chain moves and return when the machinery settles.

The rake heap has other inhabitants. Insects pupate in its damp lower cavities and sometimes emerge through its shell crust. Buttonbacks colonize fresh wet deposits before they dry. Eels wait around its base when the rake is lifted, taking small animals exposed by the combing. Thus the only true island in the Sump is made partly from the remains and discarded coverings of its own animals, raised a little at a time by a machine intended to keep a screen clear.

All these lives are fitted into the eleven-minute circulation. The ebb is a hunting interval for eels and a test of attachment for everything on the shelves. The return redistributes microbial threads, carries young crustaceans into new crevices, wets egg-laying seams, and supplies the hunter’s ambush. The stillness before the relay is the only time when nearly all the inhabitants do the same thing. Buttonbacks brace against the stone; larvae withdraw; eels orient themselves; insects settle. It is not quite alarm. It is preparation, repeated so often that it has become part of the structure of behavior.

The slower changes are just as important. Several days after rain on the plain, the stronger seeps bring a pulse of dissolved iron and sulfur. The film spreads beyond its usual borders, buttonbacks multiply, and the eels and amphibians enter a brief period of greater activity. More insect adults appear around the lamps. In drought, the process contracts without wholly stopping. Grazers crowd toward the remaining wet seams; eels and amphibian larvae retreat into clefts; the broadheads become sluggish on their warm iron; the smooth hunter waits longer between meals. The pumps preserve a minimum circuit through which life can persist.

For all its crowded surfaces, then, the Return Sump is not abundant in the manner of a sunlit marsh. Its richness lies in small bodies, slow growth, and repeated use. Almost nothing is spent on display. A shell may serve first as protection, then as an empty refuge, then as part of an insect’s case, then as gravel in an island. Iron passes from seep to film, from film to grazer, from grazer to eel or amphibian, and back into sediment. Even the works have acquired biological uses: the engines are warm ground, the pipes are nurseries, the lamps are pockets of air in which insects can fly, and the discharge is a hunting place.

The animals do not merely endure the circulation. They have come to live within its interval. When the stillness passes through the hollow, it is the stillness of creatures whose whole world is about to change by the width of a finger.
