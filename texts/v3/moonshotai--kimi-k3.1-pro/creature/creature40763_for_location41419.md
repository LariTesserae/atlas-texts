A census of organisms in the High Catchment would be blank. A census of recurrences would be crowded.

The mires are the oldest residents, though none keeps its substance for long. A particular mire is not really the water in its basin, nor the silica and clay beneath that water. It is the continuing relation among basin, cloud tide, inflow, and crust. One might be recognizable by its milky centre and rust-coloured margin, another by the three notches through which it overflows, another by the soft percussion of flakes being plucked from its floor as it contracts. When a mire migrates, it does not crawl. Deposition extends one margin while drying and erosion release another. After sufficient time, the whole outline has shifted, and none of its original particles remain. If it possesses memory, that memory consists only of abandoned shorelines, buried crusts, and channels worn where its overflow used to go. It does not feed, reproduce, or resist its own dissolution. Its persistence is not survival. That distinction matters.

The runnels are more numerous and much briefer. Each is an itinerary rather than a body. Water condenses, gathers, and performs an old sequence: down a fluted wall, across a gravel seam, up the next slab, around a pool’s impossible rim, into a cleft where it becomes audible before it becomes visible. The individual stream may last only part of a morning. Yet the same gesture can be repeated by new water for centuries, guided by a channel that is itself being slowly altered by the performance. Some runnels have several possible endings. Which one occurs depends on the local down, the state of a fold, or whether a lens farther along the route still holds. A dry channel is therefore not necessarily an abandoned inhabitant. Sometimes it is closer to an unoccupied gesture.

At the boundaries between downs, the lenses gather. They are neither drops nor pools, but disagreements made visible. Fine sediment sometimes draws curved bands within them, showing that their clear stillness is not internal rest. They tremble when a loaded fin begins to hum. Rain dimples one side and runs off another in a direction rain should not choose. Some lenses survive several cloud tides; others exist only long enough to join two temporary streams. When they fail, their contents do not simply spill. They depart according to the several downs that had been holding the boundary in place. For a moment there may be water falling around a dry stone in four directions, and then nothing but a darkening on the rock.

The recurrent passages are stranger residents still. A passage here need not be continuously open to have identity. It may be a favoured adjacency: this shelf answering that cleft, this amphitheatre briefly adjoining the foot of a fin whose summit is otherwise hours away. Such a route has no fixed area and often no visible threshold. Its territory consists of what can be reached through it. Water uses it, then sound, then light, each in a different way. When it closes, nothing has been destroyed. When it reopens, nothing has been built. Nevertheless, something recognizable has returned.

The most far-ranging inhabitants are acoustic. An echo in the Catchment does not merely repeat a sound; it reports a journey. The sharp tick of a pebble may come back softened by a wet chamber, flattened by a long passage, or accompanied by the boom of some lower cliff that the pebble could never have struck directly. The original sound is soon over. What can persist, for as long as the arrangement lasts, is a characteristic way of answering. One basin gives a single hollow reply. Another answers twice, with the second response arriving from somewhere beneath the listener. A sounding cairn may produce a sequence so reliable that it resembles a call, though there is no caller and no ear for which the call is intended.

Even the silences belong to this acoustic population. They are not stores of withheld sound. Nothing is crouching in them. They occur when the routes that usually carry, divide, and return the Catchment’s noises cease, for a while, to connect. Water is still moving and stone is still cooling somewhere, but the sounds no longer arrive. Then a fold shifts, a fin contracts, and the region becomes crowded with voices that are all, ultimately, impacts of water and rock.

The second shadows are the briefest inhabitants. They belong less to the objects that cast them than to the longer roads taken by light. When they remain after the ordinary shadows have vanished, it is not because the stone remembers being illuminated. Light is still arriving. For several breaths an empty fin-edge or the outline of a pool can stand faintly on a wall, delayed and slightly misplaced. Nothing occupies such a shadow. Nothing emerges from it. Its home is the route, and when the last light completes that route, the inhabitant is gone.

The constructed mechanisms are different, because they are the only residents known to have been shaped by intention. They do not repair themselves, coordinate, or form a hidden civilization of machines. Each has an extremely narrow office: to catch, to fill, to tip, to ring. Yet those offices remain meaningful in a place where a map quickly ceases to be one. A balanced stone receives water until its weight changes. It knocks another stone. Sound goes out. A reply may return from across the cleft, from below it, or not at all. Only a person would call that a question. Physically, it is a sequence of loads and impacts. But it is a sequence arranged so that its outcome can vary with the Catchment’s arrangement, and in that sense the builders’ questions still function.

Their patience is often misunderstood as waiting. The mechanisms are not waiting for their makers to return. They would operate in exactly the same way if no mind remained anywhere capable of interpreting them. Their patience is simply that a dry basin can remain ready to fill for a very long time.

The builders themselves cannot responsibly be counted among the present inhabitants. The old shelter beside the mire establishes repeated bodily presence, not permanent residence and not a nameable people. The seven worn depressions do not yield seven individuals. The polished bench does not reveal whether its users rested, observed, kept vigil, or merely leaned forward while listening. Whoever chose the place understood at least one dry relationship within the wet amphitheatre and built without pretending that the three approaching gutters would always agree about downhill. Beyond that, the shelter resists biography. There is no evidence of catastrophe, and no necessity for a tragic story. What remains of the builders is not a spirit or an enduring settlement, but a compatibility between their methods and the place: gather what arrives, make small adjustments, listen again.

It is tempting, finally, to call the whole Catchment a single inhabitant. That, too, requires care. It has no purpose, no boundary it defends, no descendants, and no interest in continuing. It is better understood as an old event whose consequences have become architecture. Water wears a channel; the channel gathers later water. Water loads a fin; the fin answers with sound. Sound finds openings that sight cannot; recurrent folds favour seams already polished by earlier passages. Crust both records and alters the movement of a mire. Nothing consumes anything else in the manner of an ecosystem, yet each process occupies the consequences of another. There are niches without competitors, succession without ancestry, and endurance without self-preservation.

Sometimes these residents briefly enter one sequence. A thread of water leaves a wall. A lens dimples. Somewhere below, a mire accepts new overflow and its crust begins to contract. The changing load sets a fin humming. A constructed catchment fills, tips its stone, and adds one hard note. The note travels through a passage no eye can find. Much later, an answer arrives from an apparently dry basin.

Nothing hears it in the sense of needing to know. Nevertheless, the exchange is completed.

That is the sort of inhabitation the High Catchment permits: outlines that outlast their particles, journeys that outlast their travellers, and responses that outlast the intention to ask for them. Nothing lives there in the strict sense. But a great deal has a place, a duration, and a characteristic way of returning. Nothing survives; much continues.
