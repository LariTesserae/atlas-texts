The Weeping Ward is inhabited, but it is not populated. Nothing living there keeps a household, makes a nest, or travels between feeding and sleeping grounds. Its residents are best understood in cross-section, like the ward itself: root mats and films of decay below the pavement, mosses on the cold walls, ferns beside the warm mains, reeds and waterweed in the channels, willows in the courtyards, and narrow meadows above them all in the gutters.

To live there, a thing must be able to remain wet without being carried away. It must tolerate a climate whose warmth depends on pipework and whose rainfall may come from a roof truss. It must also be able to continue after being broken. Most of the ward’s plants spread by spores, rhizomes, water-carried seed, or fragments of their own bodies. Nothing depends on a flower being visited, a fruit being eaten, or a seed being carried in fur or feathers. Even the willows can dispense with seed for long periods. A twig lodged in suitable silt is enough to begin again.

On the cold seepage walls, the mosses and liverworts engage in a slow accumulation that is part growth and part mineralization. Their lower layers remain saturated even when water is scarcely visible on the brick. Limestone precipitates among the older stems, fixing them into pale, porous crusts while the living surface advances beyond them. In places, a wall’s green upholstery has a dead stone backing made from earlier generations of itself. The colony is therefore difficult to date. Its outer edge may be months old; its attachment to the wall may be much older; its substance includes both.

The black-stemmed ferns have a different sort of order. Their colonies are arranged around flanges, valves, elbows, and lengths of insulated pipe rather than in anything resembling a bed. Close to the hottest metal there may be bare mineral scale and a margin of scorched stems. Just beyond it grow small, tough fronds; farther out, where the warmth is weaker and condensation more frequent, the fronds broaden and overlap. A repair can shift this entire arrangement. A newly sleeved pipe cools one colony and makes another patch of wall habitable. Ferns brown beside the old leak while spores settle around the new one. They do not migrate in any visible sense, but their populations move along the mains.

Where cold seepage and warm leakage meet, the boundaries are especially sharp. The little clouds at ankle height pass over growth that may be scalded on one side and lime-encrusted on the other. Between those extremes lie some of the ward’s richest seams, though richness never means profusion everywhere. Oil, vibration, scouring water, and deep shadow leave long stretches nearly bare. The gardens are lush because the barren places make no attempt to compete with them.

In the drainage cuts, horsetails lean according to current rather than wind. Their rhizomes follow the joints of old channels, emerging wherever silt has accumulated deeply enough to hold them. The sedges of the loading bays build thicker and less permanent foundations. Their roots catch each new skin of sediment until the surface becomes a springy raft of living and dead fiber. Such an island may be lifted at one edge by a willow root, narrowed by a repair, or partly torn away during an intake clearing. What remains continues from the margins. Its age is not written in a trunk but in alternating layers of root, silt, and blackened stem.

The waterweed is cropped, but never grazed. Beneath the sluices it streams in long green ribbons until the current changes or the machinery takes it. A mat torn free at one grille may lodge below another; a length deposited on the rake bank may rot there, or may be carried back into the circuit before it has lost the capacity to root. The stone bank is consequently both midden and nursery. Most of what arrives on it ends as compost and silt. A small portion begins elsewhere.

The courtyard willows are the nearest thing the ward has to old inhabitants, though even their age is ambiguous. What appears to be several trees may be one continuing body connected below the pavement. Roots braid through brickwork, enter abandoned drains, and emerge on the far side of a cistern. Trunks thicken against the clearances allowed them; where a maintenance gantry passes, the bark remains polished and the wood grows asymmetrically around it. A falling limb is not necessarily an ending. If it falls into water, it may become the beginning of another courtyard’s growth. The willows loosen some masonry and bind other masonry firmly enough that removing them would be more destructive than leaving them in place.

Above, the gutter meadows are quieter and less conspicuous. Their plants are small, with shallow roots and little need for showy flowers. Sedges, fine grasses, and low self-seeding growth predominate. Wind, runoff, and vibration do the work of distribution. Seed and spores wash into downpipes, lodge behind flashing, or descend all the way to the canals. Most of these journeys end in heat, deep water, or compost. Enough end in another seam of damp substrate to maintain the circuit.

Decay is the ward’s largest invisible population. Fungal threads and bacterial films work through the leaf mats, the saturated timber, and the organic silt where air and warmth permit. Because no animals assist them, death remains unusually legible. A willow leaf can lie through several rises and falls of water with its outline intact. Fern fronds brown without becoming riddled. Horsetail stems remain jointed after their bases have softened. There are no burrows in the compost, no pellets, no gnawed ends, no small tunnels opening and closing beneath the leaves. A leaf may retain its shape after much of its substance has gone. The almost-soily smell of the deepest passages comes from this patient internal dismantling, not from earth brought in and established there.

The engines supply nearly everything else that an ecosystem elsewhere would obtain from weather, disturbance, and animals. They prune without feeding, disperse without gathering, and flood without drinking. The pumps make tides; the condensers make rain; the rakes make harvest and migration; a relief valve makes a brief, violent storm. Their interventions are frequent enough to prevent any single growth from closing the ward entirely, but never so comprehensive that the whole green population must begin again. An intake clearing strips one reach. A flood returns fragments to another. A new mineral crust buries old moss while fresh moss extends beyond it. Equilibrium here is not stillness. It is a succession of small local endings, none quite large enough to become general.

The frequent repairs leave open the question of people. If keepers still enter the ward, their presence is episodic rather than domestic. A fresh pipe sleeve proves work, not residence. There are no animals gathered around stored food, no plants arranged for harvest, no secondary life following human habitation. For the gardens, moreover, there is little ecological difference between a person fitting new flashing and an automatic rake clearing a screen. Both alter the conditions for attachment. Both are survived, if possible, by fragment and regrowth.

Few of the ward’s residents have individual biographies in the ordinary sense. A sedge island is continually becoming another thickness of itself. A fern colony advances and retreats around repairs. A willow may outlive every visible trunk that once seemed to define it. Their continuity matters more than their separate lifespans.

What lives in the Weeping Ward, then, is a green, colonial, almost mouthless ecology, sorted into temperatures and thicknesses, moved by water, and pruned by iron. If the whole place sometimes resembles a single organism, it is because the machinery provides circulation while the plants provide soft, accumulating tissue. No consciousness is required to complete the resemblance. In the warm seams, ferns uncurl; on the cold walls, moss is slowly fixed into stone; beneath the sluices, waterweed streams, is torn away, and streams again. The ward settles around them, and they continue by holding on—or by breaking in a place from which they can begin elsewhere.
