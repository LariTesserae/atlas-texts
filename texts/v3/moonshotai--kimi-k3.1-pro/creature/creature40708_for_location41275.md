Life in the High Hollow is not spread thinly. It occurs in knots.

A hundred kilometres of black leaf may support little more than dormant mites, a few beetles sealed against the cold, and the plant’s own fine rootlets working through what they leave behind. Then a bladder district sags, fog begins to gather, and within days the same kind of tissue is crowded with appetite.

The first inhabitants of a new pool do not arrive. They wake.

Dry flecks in the wax uncurl into transparent seam-fleas. Midge eggs darken. Cup efts, which have spent the dry interval folded into leathery discs no thicker than a fingernail, soften and crawl out of the basin walls. The largest are the length of a hand, grey and blunt-headed, with frills that beat slowly in the cold water. They hunt the seam-fleas and are hunted, in turn, by almost anything that can catch them. If the pool lasts long enough, their young develop dark bands along the spine. If it does not, the whole generation dries again or goes over the edge when the district tears.

Scab beetles arrive at the strained tissue, where sap has begun to bead. Their larvae develop inside the clear crust, warmed a little by the living stem beneath. Wax mites come up out of the felt. Seed midges gather over the water in brief, dense columns, and needlewings find them. By the time people discover the pond, it already has hunters, scavengers, parasites, and animals whose only business is to wait for it to disappear.

A pool is therefore less a habitat than an event. Its inhabitants must be able to leave, dry down, or complete their lives quickly. Nothing there expects permanence.

The growth itself is the largest participant in this economy. Root hairs enter frozen droppings, discarded skin, and dead wings. A carcass left on living tissue may become furred with fine new growth before scavengers have finished with it. Nothing lies on the surface indefinitely unless cold or dryness preserves it. Animals do not merely live upon the plant; they are continually, slowly, being incorporated into it.

Felt-oxen carry life between the knots.

Their shag collects midge eggs, mites, fragments of husk, and sometimes a rim of frozen pond water around the lower legs. Their droppings make brief feeding grounds for beetles. A herd that has crossed several saddles may bring the first active mites to a seam that has been wet for months. In this sense, the oxen are not only grazers but a means by which distant pockets learn of one another.

They are more deliberate animals than their bulk suggests. The rim of each broad hoof is sensitive, and an ox approaching doubtful membrane will often extend one foot and stand motionless before committing its weight. Herds tend to walk out of step. A dozen animals moving together in rhythm can set a leaf saddle drumming dangerously; a herd that breaks its own cadence crosses with little more than a succession of soft pressures.

Calves learn this before they learn to graze. They also learn the herd’s stops: where to wait for a membrane to settle, where to turn away from new ice, where a structural cord offers a narrow but reliable crossing. A lead animal may remember a slow-growing node for years, though not necessarily find water there. The herd’s memory is often older and more reliable than the geography it remembers.

Their low calls pass from body to body through hooves and membrane. People travelling with a herd sometimes wake with their teeth aching and know, before anyone has spoken, that the oxen are bunched and calling. Most herds are wild. Some camps accompany the same animals for years, taking shed hair, milk when it is available, and occasionally an old or injured beast, without ever supposing that the herd belongs to them.

Needlewings make a different kind of gathering.

On a flat surface, one looks badly proportioned: a narrow body burdened with too much folded wing. In flight, the length of those wings becomes intelligible. They hunt in clicking, irregular lines through spaces that appear empty, taking midges too small to see from a camp platform. Several hundred feeding together can make a bay sound as though fine grit were being poured through it.

Their roosts are warmer than the surrounding tissue, not because the stem is warm in any generous sense, but because so many bodies have gathered in its throat. They hang in layered masses and shift continually. Animals on the cold outer surface work inward; those within gradually move out. A large roost therefore seems to breathe even when there is no wind.

Travellers listen for them. A living roost marks a stem that has remained open through the darkness and usually indicates an updraft worth investigating for condensation. Its sudden silence is more ominous than its noise. Ice may have sealed the throat, or pressure may be changing below it. Sometimes the needlewings leave in time. Sometimes the stem opens again in summer with thousands of dry bodies packed along its inner wall.

Beneath the leaves live the husk-runners, light seed-eaters that nest in abandoned bladders and around camps careless enough to leave food uncovered. They move in quick, uneven bursts and signal danger by drumming their hind feet. The stillhands hunt them.

A stillhand has a small, furred body slung among six long limbs. Its feet can close around a cord or spread against a membrane, and the sensitive inner surfaces of its limbs remain in contact with the structure while it waits. It can distinguish, at least well enough for hunting, between the random ticking of ice and the repeated pressure of an approaching animal. It does not need to see its prey. It prefers storms, when every other vibration is smothered by weather.

Stillhands are rare. A wet seam may flourish precisely because none has found it. When one does, it may remain until the runners are gone and then vanish so completely that only the altered behaviour of the other animals records its passage. People generally respond by abandoning a shelter or changing a route rather than by pursuing it. A handline that returns a slow, additional tension when plucked is left alone. Not every taut line has been tightened by wind.

Between such inhabited pockets, absence remains real. There are old hoofprints filled with vegetable dust, roosts reduced to grey husks, and basins whose wax lining has not held water within living memory. A traveller can spend days finding nothing that requires a name more specific than *fibre*, *ice*, or *damage*. The richness of the seams does not make the crossings less empty.

People are gathered in the same way.

There is no single nation of the High Hollow and no settlement large enough to be called a town. Along different rib systems, languages and customs differ more than tools do. Similar constraints have produced similar camps: small groups at slow-growing nodes, shelters that can be abandoned, possessions limited to what can be carried without making the carrier a danger to the membrane.

Some people follow felt-oxen. Some keep nodes, pruning new growth before it closes a shelter, binding young stems so that a useful opening remains open, and maintaining catchments where fog can be persuaded to become drinking water. Some travel between camps with cord, blades, lamp fat, and reports. These are not fixed kinds of people. A family may keep a node for several years, lose it to a summer of growth, and spend the next part of its life with a herd.

Node-keeping is not agriculture on any grand scale. The plant cannot be commanded. Most of the craft lies in doing something small early rather than something violent late: cutting one bud instead of a whole blade, easing a line before it pulls tight, opening a vent while the pressure difference is still slight. A good keeper leaves many marks, none of them large. A bad one leaves a wound that contracts beneath the camp.

Food is dry, fatty, fermented, or briefly fresh. Oily seeds are crushed into meal; certain kinds of young pith can be soaked and soured in sealed cases until their bitterness becomes tolerable. Herds provide milk and meat where people live alongside them. Scab-beetle larvae are valued where they can be gathered without stripping a seam bare. Hot food exists, but it is a small luxury produced beside a shielded fat lamp, not the centre of every meal. Lamps rest on pads of dead husk and are never set directly against living tissue.

Clothing is layered ox felt, papery husk, and waxed leaf-hide, made to be repaired with cord and an awl. Wealth is practical but not merely crude: a well-balanced glass-rib blade, a sleeping sack that remains dry at the seams, cord whose behaviour in cold is known, a store of lamp fat, and information recent enough to trust.

A road is rarely a visible path. It is a sequence of expected answers.

One bound cluster of stems should sound a low, open chord. Beyond a rib, another should answer with two close tones. Between them there may be a long dry crossing, a place where a handline must be thrown rather than walked, and a membrane on which travellers proceed one at a time. If the chord is wrong, the route-listener stops. Ice, growth, damage, and deliberate retuning all alter sound differently, though not always clearly enough to be certain.

Children learn with small flutes and with their palms pressed to structural ribs. They learn to distinguish an echo from an answer, a herd from weather, and the ordinary ticking of sap from the repeated, patient sound of something chewing inside a wall. Route songs are rehearsed in shelters, where their sequences can be remembered, but on the road they are reduced to a chord, a touch on the wrist, or two taps passed down a line. In deep wind, even people sleeping side by side may communicate entirely through pressure.

News is dated as carefully as a map. *There is water beyond the split cone* is not useful information by itself. One wants to know who saw it, how long ago, whether the basin was still sagging, and what the oxen were doing. A report of abundance may already be a report of a place where something used to live.

Customs of approach vary, but silent arrival is widely distrusted. On many routes, a visitor sounds the shelter’s own chord softly and waits for an answering tap before touching the platform. Water is often shown before questions are asked. This is not always generosity in any abstract sense; it is also how travellers remain worth receiving. The camp offering a drink today may be the camp whose catchment has frozen next season.

The brief twilight brings gatherings as well as growth. At certain especially slow nodes, people meet to exchange tools, arrange marriages, settle disputes, compare routes, and listen to musicians. Music is not separate from surveying. A flute phrase can reveal the size of a dark bay; a soft drum pattern can show where a membrane is slack; a song sung close to the platform lets others feel its rhythm when voices would otherwise be lost. The difference between a performance and an investigation is often only the reason for beginning.

Such gatherings cannot last. Too many people empty catchments, attract scavengers, and put more movement into a structure than it will comfortably carry. They disperse while there is still something left to eat.

Summer is not simply the kind season. It brings food, but it also tilts shelters, seals doors, tightens lines, and opens new throats to the wind. Herds leave familiar grazings. Needlewings darken the air around midge hatches, and stillhands become active along the routes taken by other animals. A camp may be safest in the deepest cold and uninhabitable when abundance arrives.

The long night draws everything inward again. Oxen stand close, their breath freezing into their shag. Roosts become dense and quiet. People repair cord, retell dated journeys, and wake whenever the structural note changes. No one supposes that the growth is benevolent. Some camps describe it as one body; others as many plants fused by competition and accident. Both kinds of camp prune it, avoid heating it, and know that it will seal a wound around a shelter as readily as around a broken stem.

At night, almost none of this can be seen. A camp is one shaded lamp behind old leaf-hide. A herd is a darker roughness on a saddle. A roost is warmth hidden inside a stem. A route-listener wakes, lays a hand against a rib, and waits: weather entering somewhere far off; oxen shifting their weight; the small, irregular tapping of husk-runners near a seed bag.

Nothing visible has changed. She does not call the darkness empty. She does not, strictly, call it silent either. It is a lull—and in a lull, one can hear who else is alive.
