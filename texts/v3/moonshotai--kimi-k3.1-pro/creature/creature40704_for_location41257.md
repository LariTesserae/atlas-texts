## Life in the Weeping Crown

The Crown’s life is easiest to understand by beginning with containers rather than species. A hanging marsh is one; so is the basin of a cauldron tree, a flooded cuff of moss, the space beneath an orchid’s roots, or the wet hollow left when a branch tears away. Many animals spend their entire existence inside one such container. They are not, in any meaningful sense, natives of the whole mountain. They are natives of a particular tree, a particular seep, sometimes a particular depth of water.

A mature cauldron tree may contain several successive wetlands. Its newest basin is often little more than a green-brown puddle held between branches. Below it may lie an older reservoir, deep and leaf-dark, its inner surface lined with fine living roots. Lower still, a cracked and abandoned cup holds only wet debris. The tree grows around all three, slowly closing old leaks and opening new gutters.

The translucent cistern shrimp spend the day beneath floating leaves or among the root hairs, their last meal visible as a green thread inside the body. They graze pollen, bacterial film, and the softer parts of drowned leaves. Black midge larvae filter the same water. Among them hunt the nymphs of glass darters, slender predatory insects whose adults later patrol the leaves above without ever needing to descend to the forest floor.

The flat red amphibians are commonly called hand-newts. Adults can lie on a wet leaf with scarcely more thickness than the leaf itself. At dusk they emerge and climb, spreading broad, soft hands against bark and frond. They do not hop; in the canopy, a hop is too often a fall. They take darters, small worms, and one another’s unguarded eggs. Some return always to the basin in which they matured. Others wander through connected crowns during heavy rain and may be found years later in a tree several hundred paces away.

Overflow provides the nearest thing these tree wetlands have to rivers. A hard rain can carry shrimplets, insect eggs, and young amphibians from one basin into another on rafts of leaf fragments. Fishing birds and sailwings accidentally transport a little more life on muddy feet. The exchanges are rare enough that neighboring trees may hold visibly different populations: spotted hand-newts in one, plain red ones in the next; shrimp with black eyes here, colorless eyes there. The canopy is crowded, but it is not well mixed.

Neither is a cauldron tree necessarily a single tree for long. Small tenant trees establish in its crown and send roots down the outside of the host. At first these roots resemble loose ropes. Later they branch, thicken, and enter cracks in the basalt far below. When the cauldron tree finally dies, the tenants may remain as a living cage around its hollow trunk, holding soil and water in place. Ferns occupy the interior; another cauldron tree eventually establishes there. In the oldest thickets it can be difficult to tell which trunk is supporting which, or whether the roots gripping a cliff belong to any visible crown.

Fallen wood enters the same slow succession. Orchids and tree ferns root along the upper surface while the heartwood is still hard enough to ring under a stone. Beetles open the ends; pale fungi thread the interior; hand-newts shelter beneath bark that has not yet loosened. A trunk may carry flowers for decades before it becomes soft enough to crush underfoot. On the cliffs, ladder moss grows downward in successive green rungs, its oldest portions decaying into pockets where narrow-leaved cliff plants take root. Whole hanging meadows descend in this manner, almost imperceptibly, while their upper edges are continually renewed.

The open sinter terraces only appear uninhabited. Their rust-colored skins are largely microbial, and their ivory surfaces are crossed by the clean, wandering trails of minute mineral-shelled snails. Some midge larvae live in runoff too warm for hand-newts or marsh frogs. Predatory beetles wait where that water cools, taking whatever the flow delivers. Living felts in the hotter channels trap fresh sinter and become incorporated into it. Here, instead of splitting stone, life helps deposit it.

Deeper in the drowned clefts, beyond the reach of roots, the water contains colorless flatworms, blind shrimp, and slow bacterial growths fed by warmth and dissolved minerals rather than by forest debris. Too little is known of them to say whether they belong to the surface province at all. Some may follow hot seams into rock whose surface is frozen. They could outlive the forest without noticing its disappearance.

The hanging marshes are more sociable places, though much of their society consists of waiting. Their small fish are often peculiar to one bowl or one connected group of bowls. They spawn among submerged sedge roots, where the current is steady but not strong enough to carry the eggs into an outlet. White fishers stand over them for hours. The birds’ stillness is not sleep; the head turns by minute degrees, following movement under the floating leaves. A successful strike may be the only quick action a fisher performs all morning.

Their nests are white because they are built from split reed pith, plant down, and old feathers. They sit low in the sedge, pale and conspicuous to human eyes, but resemble the exposed tops of surrounding tussocks when seen from above. The birds will defend them with stabbed bills and sudden wing-claps, then return to stillness as soon as the intruder has gone.

The wooden bell-notes in the marshes come from small brown bell frogs. Their stiff little vocal sacs produce a knock rather than a croak. Males call from floating leaves, and much of what a rival perceives may arrive through the stems and water rather than through the noisy air. When several are calling, the notes seem scattered without pattern. Patient listening reveals exchanges: one frog answers another, misses twice, then resumes at a different interval.

The largest ordinary animals of the tablelands are the splayfoots. They are slope-backed, dark-coated browsers with four broad toes on each foot. On basalt the toes draw close together; on peat they spread, allowing the animal to cross ground that would take a human leg to the knee. They crop tree-fern croziers, sedge shoots, and the lower leaves of tenant thickets. Their droppings carry seeds between forest and open plateau, and their repeated passage keeps certain paths free of encroaching growth long after heavier vegetation has closed everything around them.

Those paths are older than any surviving marker. Splayfoots use them for generations, polishing projecting stone with their feet and wearing shallow hollows at habitual resting places. They are usually seen singly or in loose, widely separated groups. The low grinding call by which they keep track of one another travels poorly through the canopy but well through wet ground and rock. A person may notice it first as pressure in the soles of the feet, or as a faint tremor in the water of a tree basin. Another animal may answer several minutes later from an apparently impossible distance.

People often follow splayfoot paths until the paths diverge from the old markers. This is where the animals cease to be reliable guides. A splayfoot knows forage, shelter, and the habits of its own ancestors; it does not know that a route skirting a marsh may approach the margin of breathable air.

The sailwings range more widely than anything else native to the Crown. At rest their long membranes fold into narrow, dark bundles along the body. They roost beneath basalt ledges and in the larger cliff meadows, launching by dropping into open air and turning only when the gorge has given them room. Their landings are unexpectedly deliberate: a few adjustments of the wing spars, a short skid of the hind feet, and then stillness.

They eat canopy fruit, large insects, carrion, and whatever small animals they can surprise near a roost. They also carry seeds, spores, and mud between cliffs. A tenant tree whose nearest relative stands across a four-kilometre drop may owe its existence to a sailwing’s interrupted meal. Yet even sailwings are bounded by the Crown’s influence. They can cross a cold saddle or glide above hoarfrost for a short time, but they do not roost beyond the warm air. Their world consists of many separate places joined by narrow permissions.

Cicadas occupy the spaces between those permissions more densely than the anchors’ sparse visible surfaces would suggest. Their nymphs feed on roots for years. Adults favor trees near the gray seams and exposed disks, perhaps because seepage there is unusually dependable, perhaps for reasons no one has identified. Near an anchor, the dry sawing of a chorus returns a fraction late. Other cicadas sometimes begin during that delay, producing a ragged second chorus that sounds like an answer from much farther away. In the dry columns where rain curves aside, discarded cicada husks may remain crisp and pale for weeks while everything a hand’s breadth away is soaked.

Nothing grows differently on an exposed anchor merely because it is an anchor. Moss fringes it according to light and moisture. Roots approach or avoid it according to the stone beneath. Birds sometimes stand in the rainless air to preen. People dry notebooks there. Any sacredness these places possess has been added later and inconsistently.

The margins are more important to the inhabitants than the anchors themselves. Cauldron trees become smaller and more widely spaced; tree-cistern animals disappear while the ground flora is still lush; certain frogs persist along a warm seep long after the surrounding forest has thinned. White fishers can abandon a marsh within a day. Splayfoots turn back when a path begins to hurt their breathing. Trees respond by generations, and peat by centuries.

When an anchor changes its deep tone, these different clocks become visible. A new marsh may receive fish, insects, and nesting birds before it has developed a proper soil. Elsewhere, old cauldron basins remain in place after their water has gone, filling slowly with dry leaves. Splayfoots continue for a while to use paths leading to forage that no longer exists. The forest does not move as a body. It sorts itself out piece by piece.

People are among the few inhabitants who insist on treating the whole province as one place. Even that is a comparatively recent habit. Their population consists of scattered path-keepers, plant and animal observers, anchor listeners, and those who accompany or supply them. Some remain for years; some families return over generations. The shelters and field buildings never accumulate into a village.

Their work is modest. They clear one blocked step, count one marsh’s nests, measure the width of a seep, note the tone and date, and leave a ceramic shelter in a condition in which the next wet traveler can sleep. They cannot maintain the Crown. At most, they maintain the possibility of other people crossing it.

Most days are not numinous. They involve mud inside bedding, food spoiled by humidity, arguments over whether two hand-newts belong to the same species, and a bird count lost to low cloud. Residents speak in short, carrying phrases and repeat important words without embarrassment. They do not wait for quiet. Around the anchors, conversations sometimes break down simply because a delayed cicada chorus makes an answer sound like an echo.

Their bodies give them no special protection. A person born within the warm province is as vulnerable outside it as anyone else. Hence the markers, and hence the distrust of apparently easy routes. Water may lead toward colder ground as readily as toward a cliff; green vegetation may persist beyond air in which a traveler can safely exert themselves. The oldest rule of the paths is not that the markers are infallible, but that intuition is worse.

The anchor listeners keep the longest human records. A single book may contain a dozen handwritings, several systems of measurement, and no conclusion. Its later entries often correct the assumptions of its earlier ones. Some listeners refer to the anchors as neighbors, not because they believe them conscious, but because a neighbor is something whose decisions must eventually be accommodated whether or not they can be explained.

The anchors have never been shown to be alive. Nevertheless, they belong to the natural history of everything above them. Their rare adjustments enter the lives of shrimp, birds, trees, and people on different timescales, while roots hold the slopes, animals open paths and carry seeds, microbes lay down stone, and water continually rearranges the smaller habitats.

Most of the Crown’s inhabitants have no conception of the whole mountain. A hand-newt has its basin; a white fisher its marsh; a splayfoot the paths it can remember. Only the sailwings, and occasionally people, travel widely enough to mistake that for knowing it.
