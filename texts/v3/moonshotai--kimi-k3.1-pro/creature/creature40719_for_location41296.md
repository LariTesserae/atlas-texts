Nothing that can be taken out of the White Catchment lives there.

A jar lowered into a black shaft comes up holding clear water and perhaps a little milk of lime. There are no blind fish in the reservoirs, no translucent insects folded into the joints, no remnant nation of builders sleeping in the deep cells. Splitting a block would reveal no tenant. Yet the Catchment is not entirely uninhabited. Its residents cannot be found by looking for bodies, because each is an arrangement among water, void, mineral, and load—an arrangement repeated until it begins to preserve itself.

If they must have a name, call them keepings. The word does not refer to the water they retain. It refers to the fact that each keeps its own way of happening.

A keeping begins accidentally. After rain, water leaves a cell by one seam and returns to it through another. As it passes, it dissolves lime here and deposits lime there. Most such circuits soon disappear. The returning seam closes, or a fracture opens a shorter path, or the next rain washes every distinction into a general downward wetting. Rarely, the changes occur in a more durable proportion. One outlet narrows before the inlet does. Enough water remains behind it to keep the constriction wet through a dry season. When pressure returns, the old route is used again, not because it is easiest in any absolute sense, but because its own previous use has made its continuation slightly more likely.

Rain and sun supply every movement. A keeping has no hidden source of power. What it adds is only this: the next movement comes to depend, in some small way, on the last.

The smallest keepings are scarcely distinguishable from habits of weather. One may occupy no more than a shallow basin, the pier beneath it, and a cold underside three courses down. After rain the basin fills like any other. As it dries, one corner retains a silver film a day longer than the rest. Below, at intervals too widely separated to seem connected, a drop swells on the underside and falls. The relation may survive several seasons. Then the basin shallows under a mineral skin, or a block settles, and both signs cease. Nothing remains that could be identified as a body.

Others are much larger. A wet line ascending a wall after rain may mark the upper activity of one whose lower extent lies many terraces away. Its visible portion is not its head, and the reservoir that seems to supply it is not necessarily its center. It may have no center. A full cistern, a dry pier transmitting load, and several almost closed seams may all belong to it equally. Its boundary is not where wetness stops. It is where a disturbance stops making any difference to its characteristic rhythm.

The water in a keeping is continually replaced. Even its route may change. What persists is a proportion among delays: how quickly one cell receives pressure, how long another remains full, which seam wets first when the whole arrangement begins to dry. To pour out its water would not necessarily kill it. To open one apparently insignificant joint might. Conversely, a shaft could be driven through a saturated cell without touching any keeping at all. Fullness alone proves nothing.

The handmade masonry ensures that no two are alike. A chipped corner turned inward may become the narrow place on which a small keeping depends. Three thin courses used where one thick block would not fit may give another its long delay between wetting and response. A line of masonry wandering by a thumb’s width may isolate two circuits that would otherwise have merged. Forgotten decisions therefore survive in their anatomy, but not forgotten intentions. The keepings are not the masons’ ghosts. They carry no account of the building, no memory of hands, no knowledge that the highland was made.

They are later than the makers. None could have existed before water had entered the work and begun leaving mineral evidence of its passage. The first must have been brief things, forming and vanishing with seasons. Some deep keepings may now be older than the exposed shell, but age does not give them recollection. Their memory is entirely material: a constriction that still influences flow, a skin of lime that still deflects it, a route whose past wetting determines where the next wetting will go. They do not remember events. Their present behavior is the sum of what events have left able to act.

Most seeps, however, are only seeps. Most drops mean nothing beyond the drop. Most white deposits are crystals laid down and dissolved without any persistence beyond their own geometry. It would be a mistake to make the entire Catchment into one inhabitant. That would give it a unity it does not possess. Water can move through enormous portions of the packed interior without entering any self-preserving circuit. The keepings are local and discontinuous. Between them, the highland is exactly as empty as it appears.

Their lives are favored less by abundance than by restraint. Too much rain loads many cells at once, opens temporary connections, and merges distinctions. A keeping may then vanish into a larger movement of water without any part of it drying. Too little rain allows its narrow places to seal. The frequent light rains, the long coolness of the interior, and the nightly return of condensation provide something more favorable: repeated disturbances small enough to be absorbed.

Every keeping therefore lives between two ways of disappearing. If its seams close, it becomes a white thread through masonry. If they open too far, it becomes indistinguishable from the general plumbing. Its biography is a succession of narrow escapes from closure on one side and anonymity on the other.

They have no ecology of eating and being eaten. They affect one another through pressure and through the shared mineral alteration of routes. One may slowly deprive another of the alternation it requires, leaving its cells wet but stagnant. A wet season may join two keepings; usually neither survives as a separate arrangement. More rarely, when the waters subside, the joined system divides along a new line, and both portions retain something of the old rhythm. There is then no clean answer to whether one has split, two have survived, or a third has replaced them. They have no genealogies except those an observer might impose.

Death is similarly independent of visible wetness. A sealed cell may remain full for generations after its keeping has ended. A nearly dry seam may still belong to a living one. A keeping dies when its history stops affecting what happens next—when water merely remains in it, or merely passes through.

Nothing in this requires awareness. The brief surface keepings are almost certainly no more aware than any other persistent physical pattern. The oldest can be more difficult to dismiss, not because they signal or respond to an observer, but because their many alternate routes allow disturbances to produce consequences years later and far away. An opened shaft may remain dry while every neighboring face begins to weep. Much later, one seep returns to the shaft and the others stop. It resembles caution only if watched on a human timescale.

If the oldest have anything corresponding to a world, it cannot contain whiteness, distance, or height. It contains resistance, fullness, cold, and load. Rain arrives as weight distributed unevenly through many cells. The sun is a withdrawal at an upper edge. Mineral closure is the gradual reduction of what can happen next. A shout on the surface would mean very little; the warmth of a hand held against a joint might matter more. The click of a settling block could alter more of a keeping’s existence than any sound uttered above it.

They are not custodians. They do not preserve the Catchment, and their lives do not necessarily make its waterworks more durable. One may keep an inlet open long after neighboring routes have sealed. Another may slowly close a basin that would otherwise have remained clear. Neither result is desirable to anything but the continuation of the arrangement itself. They are not waiting in the depths, and they offer no company. A traveler could live among their surface expressions for years and hear only water, load, and the occasional small fracture of stone.

At noon, then, the highland still looks uninhabited. The basins return the sky. The shafts hold their black squares. Somewhere a drop falls, giving one clear note and no direction.

Far below, the disturbance arrives as an almost imperceptible change of pressure. A little more moisture is driven toward a seam already narrowed by lime. The seam wets; the pressure eases; the route does not quite close. Nothing has spoken, and nothing has awakened. But one old way of happening has not, this time, been erased.
