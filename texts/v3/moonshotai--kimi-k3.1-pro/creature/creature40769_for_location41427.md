## The Tenantry of the Roaring Close

A census taken in the central court would be almost worthless. It would record a moth, several gnats, perhaps a frog at a cistern rim, and conclude that the grove was nearly uninhabited. Meanwhile, a single handspan of bark could contain eggs, nymphs, mites, beetle larvae, hunting spiders, and the packed nurseries of ants. The Close’s inhabitants are not arranged around its open space. They are arranged around reliabilities: a pool that holds through the dry interval, a seam that opens after rain, a root rail that remains continuous when the lane beside it does not.

The vegetation is the slowest and most acquisitive resident. It is not merely the substance of the houses. It takes dust from the moss, nitrogen from droppings, moisture from the bodies of drowned insects, and small meals from the dead that settle into wall cavities. Roots follow pressure and damage. A favored drinking place becomes a basin; a neglected passage is gently narrowed by growth; a nest used for many seasons acquires a sill shaped by the feet that landed on it. This is not benevolence. The growth also splits pools, invades brood chambers, and drinks cisterns dry. Its interests coincide with those of its tenants only often enough to keep them both alive.

The pavement ants are the most numerous animals whose entire bodies are ordinarily visible. Their nests occupy the warm, low districts, and their trails extend upward through galleries that no ant could ever survey. They prune shoots from traveled routes, carry fibers into dry recesses, clear drowned wings from gutters, and tend seams of translucent nymphs for the sweet moisture those nymphs release. They also eat any nymph too weak to withdraw when the bark opens. There is no contradiction in this to the ants.

Their maps are laid upon the world rather than carried in their heads. When a chorus adds a turning to a lane, the column simply takes the turning. Workers may spend the whole of their short adult lives traveling a distance that did not exist the day before. At contraction, some are crushed into seams or left isolated on a surface whose continuation has abruptly moved elsewhere. The survivors reestablish the trail. Within a few hours, the new route looks as inevitable as the old one.

Tile beetles live beneath the waxy leaves, feeding on rot, algal film, and the softer tissues of dying roots. Their small movements keep some passages open and block others with equal industry. When a whole population shifts before a wetting, the roof seems to tighten its scales. Under the pavement lives the many-foot: a long, flattened animal, or possibly several related animals, known by the traveling corruption of the root laminate above it. It is never seen entire. A pale segment appears where a root has worn thin; legs move in a gutter; something blunt and blind noses through a mass of shed skins and is gone. It consumes carrion and ant brood, hunts beetle larvae, and leaves behind channels that later carry water. Whether it is one old inhabitant patrolling the buried streets or a nation of shorter ones is less certain than the fact that the streets require something like it.

The snails are the Close’s most untroubled travelers. They keep to rails, cistern lips, and the undersides of causeways, grazing the thin living films that accumulate there. A snail may begin crossing a gallery before the chorus and finish several nights afterward, having passed without incident through a distance that birds would not attempt. Its shell records the journey only as another faint layer of growth. Some shells carry moss in their grooves and the eggs of minute animals beneath their lips. Snails are therefore not merely inhabitants but slow vehicles by which small districts exchange life.

In the deep root cups, the world is narrower still.

The cup fish are minute, quick, and locally various. Fish in neighboring basins may differ in the number of dark marks along their sides or in the depth at which they rest, their populations having mixed only when an overflow briefly joined one pool to another. Their ancestors may have arrived as eggs on the feet of birds, in wet feathers, or in water flung from some animal’s coat. A fish can live its whole life beneath one reflected opening of leaves. To it, a cistern overflow is not plumbing but catastrophe and opportunity: food arrives, predators are tumbled from their stations, and for a few seconds other countries exist.

White larvae hang or coil in the same pools, eating what the water brings. The fish eat them; the frogs eat both. Those larvae that survive emerge as gnats and other small fliers, so that a basin’s population does not end at its rim. During the dry intervals, frogs flatten themselves into damp recesses and become almost indistinguishable from bark. When wetted, they return to their old pools with the abruptness of objects put back where they belong.

A basin frog’s territory is partly water and partly frequency. Another frog may be tolerated at the same cistern if its pulse can be distinguished; one calling too close in pitch is driven off, even if there is room enough for both on the root. Their spawn is fixed to submerged surfaces rather than left to drift. During folding, a frog keeps one foot against the cistern wall. During contraction, it abandons everything else. A pool may change its relation to the court, but a frog that has maintained contact with its own pool still knows where it lives.

The great singers spend most of their lives waiting. As nymphs, they are packed behind the bark seams, translucent enough that the darker shapes of their internal organs can be seen when the wall opens. They feed from fine rootlets and grow by nearly imperceptible stages. At maturity, the flute-cicadas fasten themselves within resonating chambers and put their bodies against the wood. A singer alone is small and findable. A thousand singers sharing a wall become a condition of the world.

Not all of them call. There are always a few that remain silent, feeding, mating, or shifting chambers beneath the cover provided by their neighbors. Others begin late, stop early, or occupy damaged flutes whose notes carry poorly through the general roar. These are the animals most often taken when a district falters. The chorus is not a perfect common endeavor. It is a vast accumulation of private risks, each reduced for a while by the risks of others.

The moths understand that cover differently. The large, pale ones seen resting on rails are only the most visible members of a much softer multitude. At the loudest passages they leave the recesses, together with tiny wasps, winged ants, midges, and fliers so delicate that a drop of condensation can destroy them. They cross the exposed court when wingbeat can no longer be separated from background. Pollen adheres to their bodies; spores collect in the fringes of their wings. Flowers that have remained shut through rain and sunlight open under the appropriate vibration, and for a few minutes the empty air becomes a thoroughfare.

Some moths lay their eggs in the added distance. This is not as reckless as it appears. A chamber reached only during full chorus may have no established hunter, no ant trail, and no route by which a snake can approach quietly. If the fold persists, the larvae hatch in a room that is already old in everything but address. If it closes too soon, the eggs simply become part of the small, unrecorded mortality on which the Close feeds.

Most animals treat folded space as dangerous weather. Spiders flatten themselves and keep their feet on bark. Ants hold their chemical paths. Beetles withdraw beneath leaf-tiles. The snails continue. But newly opened rooms are not invariably empty. A snail may already be on the rail. A spider may be guarding an egg case in the recess. A nest may contain fibers from a plant now growing only in another district. Once, such a chamber might hold a mature frog beside water no visible gutter supplies. Whether these tenants survived within a fold, entered by some route that could not be kept in sight, or belonged to an older configuration newly returned, the Close does not make clear. Its vacant rooms occasionally have a past before they have an entrance.

The hunters live by the failures of all this.

The listening spiders spend the roaring hours withdrawn into pockets, their senses saturated. The snakes remain in the dry flutes, slender bodies arranged along seams, motionless enough to be mistaken for splits in the wood. They do not hunt the chorus. They hunt the missed call, the blocked chamber, the individual that moves when everything else has stopped. Their territories are less like areas than like recurring opportunities: a particular hush in a northern wall, a gutter where contraction always forces small animals toward the same root.

The pale birds occupy the upper chambers, nesting in the shallow recesses and lining them with fibers carried from lane and gallery. They are nearly voiceless. Even their nestlings beg more by touch than by sound, striking against the nest rim when a parent’s feet land nearby. A young bird learns perches before it learns distances. It learns where a lane must narrow, where an open body of air will return a moth to reachability, and which landing can be held while the court tightens beneath it. Then it waits, sometimes through several choruses, with an patience indistinguishable from emptiness.

At the outer arcades live the inhabitants too large to belong entirely to the interior. Old tortoises keep to the lower basins and the worn approaches, carrying seeds and mud between districts in the irregularities of their shells. Heavier low-country browsers enter only during the quiet intervals. They crop obstructing growth, rub the entries smooth, and leave dung in outer root pockets where beetles and moss can reach it. The first intricate ticking of water through the upper leaves usually sends them away. Their bodies are too broad for the contractions, and panic would make the arcades lethal.

Some of the oldest, lowest chambers fit no animal that now comes. Their roofs are polished at a height above a tortoise and below the present browsers. Something once laired there, often enough for roots to learn the shape. Ants still use the floors; birds store fibers in the recesses; new growth keeps the passages from closing entirely. In this way the Close contains inhabitants that no longer have bodies.

After each chorus, the dead are quickly redistributed. Wings go beneath the leaf-tiles. Bodies are drawn into ant nests or consumed in the gutters. A bird’s abandoned nest becomes a beetle chamber, then a pocket of moss, then a place where a root can divide. Nothing is wasted, but nothing is peacefully shared. Frogs consume the fish that clean their pools. Ants devour the singers they tended. Beetles clear one drain while patiently blocking another. The city persists not because its tenants cooperate, but because none of them can exploit the whole of it.

To live in the Close is to possess one narrow certainty. The cup fish has its pool. The ant has its trail. The moth has the brief safety of unbearable sound. The bird has a place where distance must be returned. The snail has the surface beneath it. Even the vegetation knows only pressure, sweetness, damage, and repetition.

The court remains empty because it is the one place where almost none of these certainties can be kept. Life crowds around it, beneath it, inside its walls, and sometimes in the distances that open across it. At full roar, a visitor may feel terribly alone. In fact, they are surrounded by animals holding still, eggs waiting in bark, frogs touching the sides of their cisterns, hunters listening for an error, and slow living growth closing patiently around every route they take.
