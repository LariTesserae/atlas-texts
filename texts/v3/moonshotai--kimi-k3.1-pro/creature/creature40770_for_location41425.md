A census of the Upper Basin would be misleading. It would count the few thousand people living in the wall housing and above the workshops, and perhaps the crews on long rotation, but it would miss most of the place’s other inhabitants. These occupy narrower territories: a warm outfall, a seam of soil behind a coping stone, the dry angle beneath a lamp, a pond corner where the current never quite reaches.

The human population is not simply a workforce, although nearly everyone’s life is arranged around work. There are sluice tenders, turbine fitters, screen crews, masons, relay technicians, pump drivers, and people who spend their days taking samples from water that looks unchanged to anyone else. But there are also dredgers, freight dispatchers, glasshouse gardeners, cooks, nurses, teachers, laundry workers, and specialists in keeping inhabited rooms dry against walls designed for an entirely different purpose. Some families have been there for generations. Others arrive on two-year contracts and discover, twenty years later, that they have become the people newcomers ask for directions.

Residents rarely know the basin as a whole. The famous view from the pass belongs mostly to visitors. Inside, a person’s basin may consist of three levels, two lifts, a stretch of canal, and the reliable route between home and a shift station. People speak of walls and landings rather than districts. They know where a walkway becomes slippery before rain is visible, which door swells in cold weather, and which apparent shortcut leaves you on the wrong side of a pressure main. Even long-established residents occasionally discover a stair, culvert, or roof passage they had never needed before.

Most households also have some small obligation beyond their own rooms. It may be no more than watching a row of drainage holes, sweeping leaves from one grating, or reporting a new stain on a particular length of wall. These duties are often informal and inherited along with a tenancy. Children learn them early. They can identify the nearest dry refuge and the difference between an ordinary pump alarm and one that requires an adult before they know much about the valley below. They grow up stepping over channels without looking down and pausing, almost unconsciously, when a familiar machine changes note.

Experience therefore carries a particular kind of authority. The most respected person in a room is not always the senior official, but the one whose judgment can safely be acted upon. If such a person says that a bearing sounds wrong, machinery may be stopped before the instruments show anything. This trust takes years to acquire and can be damaged by a single careless certainty.

Official drawings are treated as evidence, not as complete truth. A plan may show a straight drain where successive repairs have made a crooked one, or record a wall’s original thickness without mentioning the brickwork added behind it. Every experienced worker carries a private supplement of discrepancies. The best write theirs down, annotate the shared logs, and make apprentices repeat the knowledge back to them. Others hoard it and become difficult to replace. When an old worker dies, there is sometimes grief not only for the person but for the repairs whose reasons have died with them. Younger technicians, quite reasonably, resist this kind of authority; arguments between instrument readings and experienced hands can be long and bitter. The basin needs both, though neither side always admits it.

Domestic life is shaped by a simple imbalance: heat and water are usually plentiful, while space, dryness, and daylight are not. Rooms are dry by effort. Bedding is aired in rotation, cupboards stand clear of suspect walls, and shoes spend the night on racks above heating pipes. Window reveals are often painted pale colors to borrow a little more light from workshops and bulkhead lamps. Behind the blackened brick and wired glass, interiors can be unexpectedly bright with scrubbed wood, enamel, patterned cloth, and plants in anything that will hold soil. A reliably dry storage room may be spoken of with more envy than a view.

At almost any hour, someone is eating breakfast and someone else is returning from a repair. Canteens, laundries, bath rooms, and the warmer glasshouse passages serve as meeting places because private rooms are small and shift patterns rarely align. Social life happens in intervals: a meal before a gate inspection, a card game delayed by a pump start, a conversation continued over several days on the same bridge. There are garden competitions, disputes over noise, courtships conducted by taking unnecessarily long routes between levels, and children who know exactly which workshop doors are likely to produce a spare offcut for a toy. People complain about the damp for decades without leaving. Others depart after one cloud season, unable to endure the diminished sky.

The community is not naturally harmonious. Old residents can be dismissive of contract specialists; permanent crews resent instructions prepared far below the basin; housing and maintenance interests quarrel over money and access. A deferred repair may be remembered personally long after its administrative cause has been forgotten. Yet certain obligations cut across dislike. A person may be refused a seat at a canteen table and still be trusted completely with a gate. An unexplained pressure change, a blocked intake, or a child missing on the upper walkways produces cooperation before anyone has time to decide whether it is deserved.

The basin’s other inhabitants follow an entirely different geography. They do not recognize roads, property boundaries, or levels. Their map is made of heat leakage, mineral seepage, shade, shelter, and the interval between maintenance visits. A wall only a few paces long can pass through several climates. One side is cold and repeatedly scoured by rain; another receives warm condensation throughout the year. Lime-rich water supports pale films and dense growth, while runoff from metal roofing may leave an adjoining strip nearly bare. In midwinter, algae can remain green beside a heat exchanger while ferns a short distance away are touched by frost.

Much of the vegetation is consequently neither wild nor altogether cultivated. The willows, alders, reeds, and roof plantings have intended work, but they do not remain neatly within it. Willow roots find old drainage lines. Reed fragments establish themselves below outfalls. Seeds arrive in freight packing, on boots, in fill, and in soil brought up for gardens. Ferns appear where mortar has softened. Moss advances across surfaces no one thought worth planting. A warm pipe can carry a narrow progression of growth along its length, each patch ending where the temperature or cleaning routine changes.

The gardeners spend as much time editing this growth as encouraging it. They leave moss where it does no more than darken wet stone, but lift it where it would conceal a crack or keep iron damp. They value a reed bed’s roots and distrust the same roots near a culvert. A fern may be admired for years and then removed in an afternoon when an expansion joint must be opened. This care is not especially sentimental. A roof bed can be dismantled if the structure beneath it needs repair; a willow can be pollarded almost to nothing. Often a cutting is taken first.

The roof gardens preserve a different kind of basin history. They contain herbs, leafy vegetables, beans, currants, and stubborn old plants whose original names have been lost. Glasshouses supplement this with warmth-loving crops, but they do not make the settlement self-sufficient; grain and much else still come up from the valley. Cuttings and seed move between households more readily than furniture. Some plants have outlived several gardeners and the beds in which they were first grown. When a family leaves, a strip of its garden may remain distributed among neighbors who did not particularly like one another.

Animal life remains small and dispersed. Snails graze the algal films and retire to protected, comparatively dry surfaces when rain becomes heavy. Slugs work through the roof beds at night. Springtails and other minute creatures inhabit the deep garden soils, while spiders occupy sheltered corners where warm air draws in prey. The pale moths around lamps are not all residents; many are wanderers that have found a tolerable refuge and may be gone after the next cold night. Water beetles persist only where booms, vegetation, or masonry interrupt the flow enough to give them still water. The open canals are too purposeful for them.

Mice are trapped around food stores and never quite eliminated. A few depot cats, tolerated as mousers, are the only domestic animals that seem entirely at home. They belong more to buildings than to households, know which rooms remain warm after shutdown, and appear at canteen doors with an accurate understanding of shift times. Birds pass through, sometimes investigate a ledge or roof garden, and seldom establish much. There is space for nests, but little undisturbed space.

A storm briefly makes this scattered occupancy visible. The skilled crews go to their stations; most other residents have the equally important task of keeping clear, securing what can move, watching local drains, and making sure that doors, messages, meals, and children reach the right places. Gardeners close vents and accept in advance that some beds will be lost. Water carries leaves, soil, snails, and fragments of reed into new locations. Afterwards, repairs and recolonization begin almost together. A crew inspects a wall for movement while, lower down, someone rescues plants from a ruined bed and a beetle resumes circling a pond corner that has temporarily become quieter.

No inhabitant knows the whole basin. Its continuity lies in overlap: one person remembers a buried culvert, another keeps the sensors honest, a gardener notices that a familiar fern has begun to lean, and a family preserves a cutting from a garden demolished before they were born. The arrangement is imperfect and often contentious, but knowledge and attachment are too widely distributed to disappear all at once.

Life there is neither an ornament added to the works nor a force opposed to them. It occupies their tolerances. It takes the warmth that escapes, the soil that accumulates, the dry room that can be defended, the channel that can be shared. People, gardens, mosses, and small animals persist by being adjusted before adjustment becomes erasure. Nothing is expected to remain unchanged. The hope, usually unstated, is that it will remain worth mending.
