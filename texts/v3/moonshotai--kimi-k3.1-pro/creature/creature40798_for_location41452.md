Two kinds of life unquestionably inhabit the Unworn Sea. A third, if it deserves the name, inhabits nothing longer than an interval.

The first lives by attachment. The second lives by being carried. Neither makes the sea less empty.

On long-lived stone, the olive-black film can look like no more than a difference in wetness. Each scale lies so close to the mineral that water seems to pass over bare rock rather than living tissue. Only at the thinnest edges, where ordinary daylight shines through, does the green become apparent.

It has no roots. There is nothing for roots to enter. It takes dissolved salts through its surface and fastens itself directly to the crystal beneath, growing by additions so slight that a season may change only the translucency of its margin. Cold slows it but does not usually kill it. Rime may whiten an exposed patch; spray may strip away its outer cells; when the air clears, what remains continues. The mortal fact in its existence is not temperature but movement in the rock.

A patch does not spread across an island in a surge of green. After a few stable seasons, there may be several scales in the lee of a crystalline seam. Later, if the same stone remains, there may be a stain the size of a hand. Its outline records small accidents: a fracture that sheltered one edge, a seam that offered too little nourishment, a place where storm water repeatedly found purchase. It does not grow toward completeness. It grows where it has not yet been removed.

In protected fissures, the same life lengthens into filaments. Their branching is a compromise between exposure and shelter: enough water moving around them to bring salts, never enough to tear them free. The dark bases remain close to the mineral. Farther out, where light reaches, the soft branches turn bronze. They move, but not in streaming banners. A filament trembles, bends slightly, and returns. Most of its existence is below the threshold of visible motion.

When a rise founders, the film often goes down still attached. For a while it may continue living on the descending stone. The water is so clear that sunlight follows it long after the island has vanished from the surface. There is no struggle, no release of buoyant fragments, no cloud of spores. The patch simply becomes smaller in the available light. Growth slows, then stops. Farther down, its substance comes apart too gradually and too sparsely to discolor the water. Nothing grazes its path into darkness.

The drifting life never possesses even that temporary place.

A cellular thread in open water is less substantial than the films on stone. It may appear only as a brief discontinuity in a shaft of sun: a green hairline, a curved stroke, something that seems to be a flaw in the light rather than an object suspended within it. Then the angle changes, and it is gone.

It cannot swim. It has no holdfast, no preferred depth, no means of keeping itself beneath the sun. When light and dissolved minerals coincide, it adds a little living substance to itself. The rest of the time, it persists. An upwelling may bring it out of a long, cold dimness; an internal front may carry it sideways across a province of ocean; a descending sheet may return it to darkness before it has divided. Its life is not a journey toward anything. It is a sequence of conditions over which it has no control.

A vortex may keep one turning near its hollow center while the entire submerged storm travels. The thread crosses an immense distance without exerting a motion of its own. Another may remain near the surface through several storms, surviving because each violent passage happens to leave it in water that is still sunlit and still supplied with salts.

Division does not produce abundance. A thread separates into two living lengths, and the current takes them apart. There are no green tides, no floating mats, no fertile discoloration following an upwelling. From any distance greater than a few body lengths, the sea looks exactly as it did before.

The anchored and drifting kinds do not depend upon each other. They are not stages in one cycle, nor partners in an exchange. No food web gathers around them. No larger organism waits in the clear depths. Their rarity is not an illusion produced by transparency; it is the essential fact of their existence. They remain because, after numberless losses, a few suitable stones and a few illuminated threads have always remained.

Then there are the intervals of coherent water.

Life does not increase during these episodes, but it can become visible. An exact boundary between densities may gather several drifting threads that had previously been separated by days of empty ocean. Held at the same depth, they form a faint green plane within the water. From one angle there is nothing. From another, sunlight reveals a color extending through the blue like a flaw in glass.

It resembles a bloom until one notices that the green is not spreading. Not one cell needs to have divided. The sea has merely placed its scarcity side by side. For a few minutes, lives that would never otherwise occupy the same horizon become visible together. When the boundary fails, the plane vanishes without thinning into a cloud. The threads are elsewhere again.

The coherent episode itself can seem more alive than anything it contains. It has an inside and an outside. It preserves differences in heat, pressure, and dissolved substance. It performs work that maintains, for a time, the very condition under which that work remains possible. Waves cross it without spending themselves in breakage. Crystal extends through it without accumulating rubble. For seconds or an hour, it is a body of water in the most literal sense.

But the flawless crystal is not its skeleton. The smooth ridge is not a limb. The separated layers are not tissues except by analogy. Nothing assembles them for a purpose, and nothing inherits the arrangement when it fails. The episode does not cultivate the green threads or protect the films on stone. It may scatter one and submerge the other with the same indifference as the chaos that follows.

Nothing thinks in the silence. Nothing intends the precision. The sea has not awakened; it has only ceased, briefly, to dissipate every distinction as soon as it arises.

If this is life, it is life without ancestry, memory, or practice. No episode learns from an earlier one. None is a rehearsal for a longer coherence. Each maintains a vast and temporary difference, then ceases, leaving water, noise, and sometimes a changed arrangement of rock. There is no corpse because there was never an enduring body. There is no descendant because no pattern survives to be copied.

The films and threads require no such ambiguity. They are alive in the narrow, ordinary way. They use energy, maintain their small boundaries, and occasionally give rise to another patch or another thread. They do not direct the great episodes. Neither do they promise a future abundance. There is no coming meadow concealed in their patience, no inevitable progression from green cell to crowded sea.

On a rise that has kept one sunlit fracture for a handful of years, bronze filaments branch in the shelter, and above them a scale lies open beneath the white sun. Far away, where no rock interrupts the light, a single thread turns slowly in clear water. Nothing crosses the distance between them. Nothing calls. Nothing is waiting to be joined.

For as long as the stone remains, the film takes the light. For as long as the thread remains illuminated, it adds a little green substance to itself. Between them lies almost the whole sea.
