Most of Newground’s visible inhabitants are human, but humanity has no privileged claim on the place. A household, a roof garden, and a cistern colony of newts may all depend on the same faulty coupling, and any one of them may give the first useful notice that it has begun to fail.

### People of the rounds

Outsiders call the people Newgrounders. Among themselves, they are more likely to identify one another by a round: the north cistern watch, a boiler-room shift, a delivery circuit, the roofs tended by a particular gardening crew. A round is both work and society. To belong to one is to be expected somewhere. If a person fails to appear, someone usually notices before the absence becomes an official matter.

Newgrounders often distinguish between being *settled* and being *established*. Settled suggests that the ground has agreed to something. Established means that the water usually comes, the neighbors know one’s step, there is a refuge within reach, and somebody will lend a coupling when the pipe moves. A household may be dragged half a mile on skids and remain established. Another may stay exactly where it was and become isolated when a ridge cuts its accustomed routes.

Neighborhoods therefore gather around reliable relationships rather than fixed streets: a shared warm outflow, a cistern whose keeper is trusted, a stair maintained by several warehouses, a stretch of roof where children can be sent with bread and messages. Ask where someone lives and the answer may sound like directions for performing a small task. This is not evasiveness. An address that cannot be walked is already becoming historical.

Their rooms reflect the same understanding. Stoves stand on skids. Cupboards latch twice. Shelves have lips, beds fold, and tables are made to come apart without splitting. Even wealthy rooms often look as though they could be lifted or tilted at short notice, because many have been. Beauty is by no means neglected, but it favors enamel, woven cloth, carved folding furniture, painted panels, and other things that can survive removal. A family’s oldest possession may be a lock whose key has opened a succession of otherwise unrelated doors.

Readiness is not the same as prosperity. Wealth in Newground often means slack: spare hose, an uncrowded room, a second route, reserved crane time, enough panels to reject the first bad fit. Poverty means that every pipe is already at full extension and every warm corner is sublet. A well-supplied district experiences a *making* as an expensive interruption. A poorer one may lose its cistern, its customers, and the daily meetings that made it a neighborhood, even when most of its people survive.

Social life collects at warm thresholds. Boiler doorways become informal meeting rooms; glasshouse steps are places to mend gloves and exchange news; cistern keepers often know more about a district’s domestic arrangements than its landlords do. Tea matters out of proportion to its quantity. A kettle set out beside work is a small declaration that someone expects to remain long enough to drink, and that the present route is safe enough for others to stop.

The cold and the quiet leave their marks on behavior. Gloves and chalk are ordinary pocket contents, even among clerks. Soles are replaceable, because young fractures wear them quickly. People speak closely and without wasted volume, but visitors who mistake this for serenity are corrected by experience. Children learn early that silence is not evidence of stillness. They watch vanes, birds, hanging dust, and the behavior of animals before they trust a silent street. They also learn their addresses as sequences, and can often recite a route more reliably than they can draw it.

Trades overlap because the city’s problems refuse to stay within them. A structural crew may need a gardener to explain why a root mat has slipped, or a pipe-fitter to admit that the supposed ground movement began as a hidden leak. Surveyors read masonry, traffic, hares, and old maps with equal attention. A cistern keeper reporting that the newts have left can stop work as effectively as an inspector with a cracked gauge, though neither is obeyed blindly. Newground expertise consists less in possessing a single sign than in knowing how many different things could have produced it.

Keeping a route open is regarded as ordinary civic maintenance. The night watch does part of it; so do deliveries, school journeys, visits to the sick, and the daily passage of herds. A neighborhood wishing to preserve an underused arcade may hold a market there, route a wedding procession through it, or simply send more people the long way around. A child sent for bread may therefore be doing useful public work. People argue vigorously over whether such repetition instructs the basin, alters its physical behavior, or merely keeps obstructions from accumulating. The argument changes the vocabulary. It rarely changes the walking.

During a *making*, all this preparation becomes visible. It does not look fearless. It looks like frightened people performing familiar tasks quickly: unfastening, lifting, guiding those who cannot run, leaving what must be left. Afterward there are injuries to count, water rights to contest, missing households to find, and sometimes nothing salvageable but hardware and ledgers. The first use of new ground is consequently deliberate. Once surveyors and crews permit it, a new passage is walked, carted, stood in, and made busy. Useful *suggestions* are altered and adopted; obstructive ones are cut back. An ankle-high doorway may become part of a nesting recess, a thickened wall may support stalls, and a flight of steps to nowhere may acquire a rail and a date before anyone decides what else to do with it.

No one has to believe the ground is listening in order to become careful about what is repeatedly done to it.

### The other households

Plant life follows plumbing more faithfully than property lines. Every green band has a history involving warmth, leakage, transport, or neglect. Even apparently natural thickets may have begun in soil spilled from a cart, spores carried on a deer’s coat, or a handful of mat pinned over dust and then forgotten.

Gardeners do not move individual gardens so much as whole small populations. A rolled roof mat carries moss, fern spores, sedge seed, springtails, pale thread-worms, snail eggs, and the damp darkness required by all of them. Some mats are older than the roofs beneath them. Their keepers know which portions tolerate rolling, which must be shaded, and which will conceal a spreading crack rather than restrain it. After a *making*, establishing these communities is part of making the surface habitable, not decoration reserved for later.

The red-gilled newts are treated with particular care. Cistern colonies are moved in wet cloth or perforated zinc tins when their water must be abandoned. Their usefulness does not make them infallible: sometimes they leave because of contamination, sometimes because another pipe has become warmer, and sometimes for reasons no keeper can explain. An experienced observer notices gill condition, clustering, eggs, and the insects on which they feed before offering an opinion. A cistern without newts may be perfectly serviceable, but experienced residents tend to watch it more closely.

Slate hares occupy the awkward boundary between pest, grazing animal, and unofficial survey instrument. Roof crews put out trimmings where the hares can be observed, partly to keep them from stripping valuable mats and partly because predictable animals are easier to read. Their preference for certain older passages is taken seriously, though no competent surveyor treats it as proof. The hares are not warning anyone. They are seeking shelter, safety, and food; people have simply learned to notice what they seek.

The small deer are more difficult to ignore. A herd can close a stair as effectively as a barrier, spread moss spores across three districts, strip a new planting, and preserve a heavily used bridge route all in the same week. Their presence on high walkways during a *making* is reassuring only in the narrow sense that they are excellent at avoiding being underneath things. At other times they are traffic, nuisance, and a moving source of green dust.

Wire martens inspire less patience. Crews sometimes leave harmless insulation offcuts away from cable trays in the hope that a marten will steal those instead. The arrangement works just often enough to encourage further attempts. Gray birds produce a similar contradiction: the city pays nest removers to evict them from semaphore boxes, then stops work when several districts of them rise at once. The same animal can be an obstruction at noon and part of an alarm system by the afternoon shift.

None of these creatures are employees, omens, or little citizens with human motives. They steal, breed, obstruct, abandon apparently good shelter, and sometimes die in well-maintained machinery. Their usefulness comes from long, unsentimental attention. Newgrounders may grow fond of them without forgetting that.

### The uncertain inhabitant

Whether anything native lives in the deeper seams remains unresolved. There are borehole stories, workshop stories, and the sort of testimony that improves whenever the original witness leaves the district. No generally accepted specimen has emerged. The basin itself, however, is treated as a participant in daily life: not worshipped, not reliably personified, but allowed for.

It has habits, if not a mind. It repeats some forms and neglects others. It preserves use more readily than intention. It can make a carefully planned city look like a set of hints it has misunderstood.

Thus Newground’s inhabitants are all, in different ways, recent and provisional. Their oldest continuities are often actions rather than objects: a watch still walked, a cistern name carried to new water, a lock transferred to another door, a herd returning by a route whose banks have changed.

Late at night, an arcade may contain no houses and no visible settlement—only a watchman passing, hares stirring behind a grille, and condensation ticking through the roots overhead. Yet the route is expected, the mat is tended, and someone will notice if either disappears. In Newground, that is often what inhabitation means: not that the ground holds something permanently, but that lives keep returning to a pattern, and other lives have learned to notice when it changes.
