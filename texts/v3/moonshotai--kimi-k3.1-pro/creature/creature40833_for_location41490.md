### The Lives Beneath the Bead

The film is alive before it is visible. Close to the seep, it is only a faint drag on the water, a place where the surface seems less eager to shine. Farther out, where sulfur-rich seepage meets cooler condensate, it thickens into a watery grayness. It is not one organism but a shifting population of appetites. Some of its cells prosper on sulfur, some on iron, some in the narrow transition between chemistries. Where one way of feeding fails, another spreads. The stain appears to creep, though much of its movement is growth on one side and collapse on the other.

Veil-fleas make the film visible by removing it. A hungry flea is almost nothing: a clear disc, a rim of delicate legs, a slight distortion of whatever lies beneath. A well-fed one carries a smoky loop of gut and can be seen more easily than the stone on which it stands. Its legs need not work in any fixed order, so it can reverse without turning or change direction while remaining facing, insofar as it has a facing, the same way. In a crowded sheet, an individual may walk partly on glass and partly on the backs of its neighbors. When the sheet shifts, what looked like a shelf of silver tissue slides uphill, divides around a pool, and becomes two shelves.

Their grazing trails are brief dark calligraphy. The film closes in from the edges; water brings fresh cells; other fleas cross the trail and extend it in another direction. No trail is a path for long, and no crowd owns the pasture beneath it. Even the young, which gather along the coolest rims where condensation is frequent, are continually being carried elsewhere by the rearrangement of the adults.

Glass limpets provide the nearest thing to shelter, though neither shelter nor limpet lasts. Their shells are clear and slightly yielding, more like thickened skin than stone. When a limpet clamps down, it seals a little water beneath itself. Within that closure are often other lives: quick, jointed creatures wedged into the shell seams, slow rasping things that feed at its margin, and smaller riders living on what those animals shed or overlook. Some steal food. Some remove scale. Some do both, according to the heat and the availability of water. A limpet is therefore not merely an animal or a item of prey. It is a cistern, a pasture, a refuge, and occasionally a nursery. When one is forced open, a whole little population experiences the same disaster at different speeds.

Thread-mouths make use of that crowding without needing to pursue it. At rest, a thread-mouth closes around a drop or lies along a capillary vein, its body so nearly colorless that it resembles a tension in the water. Its mouth is folded into the loop. Only when it feeds does that seam darken, part, and reveal the fine inward points by which it holds struggling prey.

It can feel a veil-flea’s footfall through a pool, but in clear sunlight it often hunts by sight and position rather than touch. It settles where prey is not, but where prey will presently be adjacent. Its body may remain below a ledge while its mouth waits in the reflected pool of another terrace. A passing cloud can therefore save an animal without the animal ever knowing it was threatened: the bright patch dims, the route closes, and the thread-mouth strikes into an emptied neighborhood. At dusk it hunts less beautifully, by contact, or simply waits.

The palm-backs are set apart less by their size than by their hesitation. Other animals test heat, chemistry, and footing. A palm-back also tests objects.

Its remaining limbs brace against the glass while the two working forelimbs turn a fragment over, shift it between the opposed digits, and try its balance. Sharpness alone does not make a useful needle. A lever must be narrow enough to enter a shell seam, but not so fine that it bends under pressure. It must have enough length to multiply a small animal’s strength, but not so much that the far end becomes unmanageable. A grain used for cracking a shell must sit securely in the grip. A palm-back may discard several promising pieces before choosing one that looks, to anything else, exactly like the debris around it.

They are not infallible. Young palm-backs often choose splendid needles far too long for them. They overshoot shells, lose levers, and sometimes succeed only in turning a limpet onto a neighbor, which clamps shut and takes the opportunity with it. Adults differ in method. Some are persistent leverers; some prefer three or four economical blows with a grain; some specialize in robbing a feeding animal at the moment its attention narrows. Once a tool is released, it becomes geology again. There is no handle to recognize, no cache to defend, no worn place declaring that choice occurred here.

Yet methods can persist without objects. Young palm-backs learn by crowding the edges of meals, attempting to steal scraps, and being driven off. No adult deliberately demonstrates a technique, but an adult’s impatience provides repeated opportunities to observe one. A youngster sees where the working digits close, which end of the needle is allowed to project, how the body braces before pressure is applied. In one shell colony, most palm-backs may consequently become leverers; in another, separated for much of the day by failed adjacencies rather than distance, cracking may be more common. A practice can outlive the ledge on which it arose.

Their eggs are entrusted to living limpets. A laying palm-back waits for the slight lifting that comes with heat and feeding, then places the clear eggs deep in a shell seam. When the limpet closes, the eggs are held in dampness through the next pulse. The hatchlings emerge with all their limbs present and cling initially to the shell margin, too small to manage anything but their own grip. Their first shelter has been another animal’s decision to shut.

Palm-backs also learn the reflections, though never as permanent doors. Each useful opening belongs to a particular clarity and angle of light. An individual may approach a drop, extend one foot, and withdraw it when the image within begins to soften. Another may wait beside a darkened passage while the physical route around the seep remains available; whether this is patience or merely an economy of effort is difficult to know. At noon, a palm-back can visit several shell colonies without crossing the spaces between them. At dusk, the same colonies are separate countries. Their knowledge is therefore not a map of the cavity alone. It is a map of the cavity together with the sun.

Death leaves little more evidence than tools do. A veil-flea killed by heat becomes first opaque, then ragged at the rim. Seam-dwellers pick it apart; colorless growth closes over the remainder. An abandoned limpet shell may shelter smaller animals through one hot interval before being gnawed, dissolved, or broken. Even a dead palm-back is briefly terrain: an arch under which something minute can hide, a slope on which film gathers, then a series of diminishing fragments. Nothing accumulates deeply enough to become soil.

A heat pulse sorts all these lives by their means of enduring it. The lowest film dies back. Veil-fleas pour upward, their sheets thinning into processions and then into scattered discs. Limpets clamp shut with their water, riders, and eggs. Thread-mouths tighten around the last drops or retreat into seams. Palm-backs abandon whatever they are holding. Some migrants reach a cool terrace by a reflection; others arrive as the light weakens and find that the remainder of their route has ceased to exist. They wait on warm ribs, crowded and almost silent.

Condensation restores the cavity in pieces. A drop gathers beneath an overhang. Shells lift. Water touches a grazing sheet stranded above the seep. The film spreads again from protected seams, at first too sparse to see. Thread-mouths take up positions where new images have made new neighbors. Palm-backs return to the shell colonies and begin, once more, by picking things up and putting them down.

A fresh fracture can be populated faster than any animal could crawl its length. If its first wet faces appear in clear sunlight, a reflection may place them beside an established pasture. Water carries the film through; a veil-flea follows; microscopic passengers arrive on both. Young limpets occupy the sharp ribs. Palm-backs come later, when there are shells worth opening and fragments worth testing. The ledge can possess a food web before it possesses a single rounded grain.

No inhabitant knows the whole arrangement. The film knows gradients. A limpet knows pressure, chemistry, and the return of water. A thread-mouth knows tremors and profitable brightness. A palm-back knows a changing sequence of grips, shell seams, heat, and light. The rising mountain matters to them only as the gift or removal of surfaces.

At the outer lip, the bead still holds its white point of sun. Beneath it, a young palm-back lifts a glass needle, finds it too long, and lets it fall. Nearby, an older animal opens a limpet with three small taps. The youngster approaches, is shouldered away, and returns. After the next heat pulse, it chooses a shorter needle.

Nothing has been built. Nothing has been kept. But a method has begun to pass from one brief life to another, and the next grain’s tick against a shell is too faint to disturb the bead.
