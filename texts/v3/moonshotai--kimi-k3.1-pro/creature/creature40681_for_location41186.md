## The inhabitants of the Sinter

Life in the Sinter is not a remnant ecology surviving in the cracks of a city. It is an ecology of cracks, flows, maintenance, leakage, and repeated disturbance. It occupies every scale at which the basin operates. The maintenance systems divide organisms into obstructions, useful cleaners, and unavoidable populations; the organisms themselves observe no such categories.

The first inhabitants of any surface are the films. Clear films gather where condensate carries organic leakage from the synthesis houses. Rust-red films prefer mineral seepage and the warm, oxygen-poor margins of drains. Others form nearly invisible layers within the pores of ceramic, surviving on traces too dilute to discolor the water. To a fingertip, one film is greasy, another faintly granular, another almost indistinguishable from the wall beneath it. Under strong flow they trail microscopic streamers; in still water they thicken, trap grit, and become food.

A newly deposited wall is therefore not sterile for long. The water that cools it already carries living cells. Constructors, tools, boots, and animal bodies carry more. Before the ceramic has lost its construction haze, its wettest corners may already be slick. This is the closest thing the Sinter has to soil: not a depth of earth, but a damp surface with enough time between disturbances.

The brown “fur” on old walls consists of jointed animals, many no larger than grains of dark aggregate. Individually they are difficult to notice. In colonies they breathe and shift with the seepage, thousands of small bodies changing direction together. They graze the films, consume soft fragments from carcasses, and leave behind still smaller particles for the current. When constructors begin loosening a wall, the animals often withdraw into its pores before the outer material comes away. Some are crushed or enclosed; enough survive in drains and neighboring masonry to return. A patch that looks lifeless after demolition may lift into brown motion again at the next change in flow.

The larger shell-backed grazers are called lidbacks by many wall crews, though names vary across the basin. Their shells take color from local grit, so a colony can look like a patch of wall that has decided to move. Each animal presses close to the surface and scrapes a narrow path through the film. In a dense colony, feeding passes from individual to individual in staggered waves. The whole wall appears to shiver.

Their usefulness depends on address and density. A moderate colony keeps a broad heat-exchange surface clear. The same animals packed around a door seal can worry its edge until it weeps. Their empty shells accumulate in flutes and joints, narrowing channels; their living bodies can be washed away in a storm and delivered intact into machinery. Crews may scour them from one conduit while leaving another colony undisturbed. The dead shells become ledges for the next generation, and eventually part of the aggregate.

Pale eels occupy the spaces between these surfaces. Most are no longer than a hand or forearm, though larger ones inhabit the deep drainage reaches. They have little use for sight. Their world is a geography of pressure: the deep stroke of a pump station, the flutter of a leaking seal, the brief suction behind a closing gate. A pump signature that helps a person locate an address can also serve an eel as a landmark. When construction opens a passage that did not exist the day before, the eel does not need to recognize it. It follows the altered gradients.

They sift small animals from sediment, take carrion, and enter astonishingly narrow conduits. An eel found in a pipe that should be isolated is not merely a nuisance; it is evidence of a connection. Some pump listeners read an unexpected animal presence as readily as an instrument trace. During storms, eels travel through reversed drains and newly opened wall cavities, sometimes appearing inside dwellings whose occupants had believed themselves sealed. Most find their way back with the water. A few remain in warm cisterns and become, for a while, somebody else’s maintenance problem.

At the outfalls stand the sieve waders. Their long legs hold their bodies above the worst of the surge, while their broad mouths work through aerated flow. They take suspended film, tiny jointed animals, and soft organic floc. Between feeds they fold themselves into narrow, angular shapes and wait with the patience of objects. Their molted coverings often remain standing after the animal has gone, so an outfall can appear crowded with twice its real population.

Waders are among the first animals to exploit a new discharge and among the last to abandon an old one. When drainage reverses, they follow the eels and the food stirred loose by them. Their legs and mouths are hazards at fine grates, but on open margins they perform a service no cleaning schedule quite replaces. Maintenance records tend to remember the blockages more clearly than the service.

The small flying scavengers—flue-wings in some districts—live by warmth, shelter, and sudden opportunity. They pack exhaust ducts and upper arches, their folded flight surfaces protected from the worst of the wet. Their calls are piercing because soft signals would be useless in the Sinter. At close roosting range, the bellows and shrieks that irritate human workers are precise enough to keep a colony together.

A demolition front is a feeding event to them. Exposed grazers, crushed wall-fur, ruptured films, and drowned animals become available all at once. The fliers emerge in sheets, darken the constructor haze, and vanish again when the food is stripped or the rain becomes dangerous. Their movements across the basin can follow work schedules more closely than weather. A district may see them only when its outer rooms are being removed; another, where old ducts provide permanent roosts, lives beneath their noise continually.

The reservoir animals belong to a slower order of life. Many are called veils, whether they are truly sheet-bodied or merely appear so in the fragmented views supplied by maintenance instruments. Some are single animals; others may be colonies grown so continuous that the distinction matters more to biologists than to pump operators. Supported by deep water, they draw suspended matter through broad filtering surfaces. Microbial floc, fine carcass material, and particles shed by shallower communities all pass through them.

Their movements are too slow to watch comfortably. A muscular contraction may take minutes; a change of position, days. Yet the mass displaced is large enough to register at street grates as a patient rise and fall of water. Old individuals acquire equipment numbers and, unofficially, names. Crews recognize them by hydraulic signature before they recognize them by shape. A healthy veil can steady a reservoir’s filtering cycle. A dead one is an emergency of tissue, blockage, and suddenly uncontrolled microbial growth.

People are not, for the most part, fed by this web. Their food comes from the closed culture houses. But they are not outside the web. Their organic leakage nourishes films; their heat creates habitat; their demolition feeds scavengers; their pumps supply the pressure landmarks by which other animals travel. People are the basin’s largest source of disturbance and one of its largest sources of usable waste.

Their own adaptation is social and technical rather than bodily. Human skin tolerates the perpetual damp badly, human hearing is poorly matched to load-field cracks and freight impacts, and human dwellings require seals that no wall can guarantee for long. Nevertheless, basin residents become exquisitely sensitive to the conditions that matter.

A child may learn the vibration of the local pump station through boot soles before learning to read a district map. Adults sleep through impacts that would terrify a visitor, then wake because one familiar stroke has arrived late. Quiet is welcome; the wrong quiet is alarming. Directions are given by load nodes, pressure phases, and the feel of particular floors. Visual landmarks are useful, but everyone understands their provisional status.

There is no single way to be housed. Some households occupy old foundation reaches, where rooms change rarely enough to accumulate heavy furniture and private modifications. Others live in blocks designed to be shed during floodworks, with possessions nested in sealed cases and partitions made to release rather than resist. Some people spend their working lives near the deep culture vats and see open sky only when construction happens to uncover a shaft. Roof dwellers know glare, rain cells, and flier colonies; pier dwellers know the private acoustics of occupied mass.

A household may persist at one address through several entirely different sets of rooms. Its continuity lies in tenancy, relationships, routes, and portable practices: helmets fitted to particular heads, cooking cultures kept closed, a table designed to come apart, an old person’s memory of where the drains used to run. After a construction front, such memory can be more useful than any current map. Someone who remembers that two apparently separate addresses once shared a drainage reach may be the first to understand why both are flooding.

This continuity is not always benign. An address can outlive its building without preserving its inhabitants’ right to remain there. A route can survive while the shops and gathering places that gave it purpose are moved elsewhere. People with secure foundation holdings may watch reconstruction as spectacle; those in sacrificial outer blocks experience the same front as eviction. Disputes over phase notices, flood classifications, and load decisions are therefore disputes over the shape of daily life. To control a wall is a small power. To control when a district changes phase is a much greater one.

Outdoor language is compressed. Helmets carry induction speech when conditions permit, but touch remains more reliable in crowds and storms. Districts develop their own hand codes, along with a shared technical vocabulary for sealing, waiting, load movement, and dangerous flow. Indoor speech, by contrast, can be unhurried and elaborate. Music often accepts the pumps rather than trying to defeat them: rhythms are placed in their intervals, and some performances are intended as much for the floor and bones as for the ear.

Food has its own forms of continuity. Without harvests, freshness means the condition of a culture batch, the interval since decanting, the care with which a synthesis line was cleaned. Cooks distinguish textures and acids with great precision, and storm interruptions produce local shortages and improvisations. A household may lose every fixed surface it owns and still carry a sealed cooking culture into the next set of rooms. Recipes survive reconstruction better than kitchens do.

Children collect lidback shells for counters, learn to distinguish constructor hiss from ordinary seepage, and discover early which apparent walls are actually warm, wet colonies of animals. Pump listeners, film crews, reservoir wardens, culture workers, and storm teams all know different versions of the same district. None knows it entire. Even the people whose work is ecological tend to be pragmatic: a colony is protected here, flushed there, cursed in one conduit and welcomed in the next.

The constructors themselves are not alive, but no account of the inhabitants can leave them out. Their swarms open and close habitat as weather does elsewhere. They crush colonies, expose food, carry films between surfaces, and enclose shells and bodies in new ceramic. A wall deposited in an afternoon may therefore be full of older lives without being old itself. Its faint speckling is not simply decoration or contamination; it is evidence that construction and decomposition have never been fully separated.

A major storm brings these different lives into coincidence. People move sealed goods inward, check whose rooms are expected to remain, and establish touch chains for those who cannot hear one another. Eels become active before many residents have seen the rain. Grazers clamp close or are torn away. Waders leave familiar stances for temporary feeding grounds. Flue-wings disappear into ducts, and the deep veils alter their slow filtering as reservoir pressure changes.

During the worst of it, much human communication becomes very simple: *seal holding*, *wait*, *child accounted for*, *pump unchanged*. A hand pressed repeatedly to a shoulder may mean only *still here*. Outside, storm crews work among material that is simultaneously becoming flood debris, construction feedstock, and animal habitat.

When the water finds the old channels, return does not occur in a single order. Films spread from refuges no map records. Wall-fur emerges from pores and drain joints. Eels test the new openings. Waders arrive at outfalls whose masonry is new but whose flow is familiar. Fliers come back to exposed food and warm roofs. People reopen doors, argue over altered access, set out cooking vessels, and begin naming the latest arrangement of surfaces by the old addresses.

By the next interval of ordinary sunlight, a single wall may contain ceramic deposited the previous night, the shells of animals killed during the storm, a film younger than the last rain, and the vibration of a pump station older than every person in the district. Behind it, a household unpacks possessions carried through several buildings. Beneath the pavement, an eel turns toward a pressure signature it has never needed to see. In the reservoir, a veil contracts, and water rises slightly at the grates.

None of these inhabitants is waiting for the Sinter to become dry, finished, or still. Life there is not the persistence of substance. It is the ability to resume when the substance has been moved.
