## The Inhabitants of the Vane Country

What lives in the Vane Country is not, at first, best understood as a list of species. It is a set of habits: waiting beneath a lip of stone, arriving with the dusk wind, drinking without wetting the ground, making a nest inside something designed for another purpose. Its residents are fitted less to the country’s average climate than to its brief differences—warm rock beside cooling alloy, a damp gasket beside a dry basin, a fault that releases too little water to measure accurately but releases it for forty years.

Most of the permanent residents are small enough to spend noon within the width of a shadow. This is not a figure of speech. A fracture two fingers wide may contain a lizard, the lizard’s retreat, a spider’s daylight chamber, and several generations of pale insects living along the one handspan where condensation occasionally reaches. A census taken at midday would find almost nothing. One taken after a humid dusk would find the country crowded. One taken after three rainless weeks would conclude that much of the population had died, when in fact it had retreated, dried, gone to seed, or simply stopped moving.

The first occupation at a leak is usually too small to see.

Dust adheres to the dampness. With it come airborne cells, fragments of lichen, insect remains, pollen, and the faint organic residues that collect anywhere water repeatedly appears and disappears. A dark film develops at the margin between wet and dry. Joint flies graze there—soft, colorless insects that crowd the cool end of a pipe fitting until the metal seems to have grown a fringe. Most of their lives are spent within a few body lengths of the place where they hatched. When a humid wind comes, a small proportion develop wings and leave. Nearly all of those are lost. A few reach another joint, another sweating seal, another narrow continuation of their world.

Cup beetles are harder and more mobile. They are matte black, flat enough to shelter beneath the rolled lip of a collection cup, and able to cling to alloy that appears too smooth for purchase. On dew mornings they gather around the rims in dark, orderly lines. They drink from films too thin to drip. When disturbed, some simply release their hold and fall into the fractures below, then spend hours climbing back. Others are drawn into feeder screens and die there. Maintenance crews have occasionally cleaned out cups apparently clogged with black sand, only to find that the sand had legs.

A leaking gasket therefore does more than provide drinks. It grows food. A seep may support more visible life than a temporary pool, because the pool is somewhere animals visit, while the seep is somewhere other animals can remain.

Above them, the spiders have made a map of the evening wind.

Their webs are not distributed evenly through the fin fields. They concentrate along saddles, beside gaps in vane ranks, and downwind of projecting brackets where the lowland current slows and turns. A cable and a natural fin may carry opposite edges of the same web. To a person, the arrangement looks incidental until several evenings have been spent watching where the moths arrive.

The spiders work most actively while the silk is still silvered with dew. They repair broken lines, drink from the threads, and cut away sections loaded with dust. Some webs catch almost nothing for nights and are left in place; others are taken down after a single abundant hour. The spiders themselves retreat before full sunrise into screw housings, hollow brackets, and the narrow ash-gray interiors of fractures. Birds rob them of wrapped insects. Lizards eat the smaller spiders when they venture too low. The webs nevertheless persist because the wind is more generous than any local leaf or flower.

That wind brings moths from distant lowland vegetation, flying beetles whose proper homes may be days away, winged ants, seeds, and tiny drifting things that never establish themselves at all. Most do not become residents. This does not make them unimportant. They are food, then droppings, then dust, then part of the film around a leak. The Vane Country is fed partly by places it never resembles.

The plants live on a longer patience.

Some of the black lichens are likely older than the entire installation. Wetting changes them briefly from black to olive; for a few minutes they are active, and then they are stone-colored again. A wiregrass shock that appears no more substantial than a bundle of straw may be decades old, its living base buried in a fracture and its green growth confined to the short periods when growth costs less than dormancy. The little red-lobed succulent is translucent when full and duller when its stores are depleted. At noon it closes upon itself so tightly that a court can seem to lose a whole small population of plants without anything having died.

After substantial rain, the yellow flowers emerge from dust films with very little margin for delay. They are visited by whatever the wind has supplied: small beetles, moths, joint flies on their rare winged journeys. Pollination must often fail. Enough succeeds. The seeds that result do not wait in a continuous seed bank so much as in thousands of separate mineral pockets, each with its own chances of being wetted, eaten, washed deeper, or left untouched for another year.

The country’s chief burrowers are blunt-faced, slate-colored animals commonly called husk rats, after the litter outside their more frequented entrances. They are poor engineers of solid rock and excellent engineers of everything people have left incompletely sealed. They cannot excavate the welded fins, so they appropriate drains, pipe trenches, failed cement, and spaces behind inspection plates. Where an old channel narrows, they open a side chamber. Where it collapses, they go around through a fracture. Their nests are lined with wiregrass and occasionally with felt stripped from perished pipe insulation. Their seed stores are small and dispersed; a single wet intrusion does not destroy them all.

They can survive for long periods without drinking free water, taking moisture from insects, succulent tissue, and the little that remains in seeds. At a reliable leak they drink anyway. Their passages serve other residents after them and sometimes while they are still in use. Lizards occupy the outer chambers. Spiders sit just inside the entrances. Ashwings investigate abandoned ventilation holes for nest sites. A maintenance drawing may show a drain ending cleanly against stone; the husk rats’ version continues around it, divides, and emerges three courts away.

The ashwings themselves are only conditionally resident. Some remain through dry years, making short, economical flights between fins and living on whatever the wind still brings. Others disappear from whole basins and return later. Their broad wings are made for using moving air rather than laboring against it, and at dusk they often launch from the same few projections, turning into the lowland current to take insects before dropping back toward the shelters.

They nest in pipe sockets and hollow insulator pedestals, lining the mineral hardness with grass, spider silk, and whatever soft industrial remnant can be pulled free. A nest is not only a nest. Dropped food, shed feathers, dead nestlings in bad years, and accumulated droppings make a small nutrient deposit beneath it. Beetles come. Lichen establishes on the stone below. In this way even a successful brood leaves a patch of fertility behind.

The birds breed according to conditions rather than a dependable season. When the dusk wind is strong and insect arrivals are frequent, insulators that have been empty for years may all be occupied. In poor periods the ashwings simply do not attempt it. Around the nests, mates and parents communicate with soft bill-clicks and brief exposures of the pale undersides of their wings. A bird arriving silently at an insulator may be answering a signal no human visitor would recognize as one.

The largest hunter is known in maintenance notes as the court fox, though the name suggests a closer acquaintance than anyone has ever had with it. It is fox-sized, low through the shoulders, and dust-gray, with broad, furred feet and a tail carried near the ground. It does not patrol its territory in any regular way. It revisits.

A drinking place may be ignored for several nights and then watched for hours. The hunter is not usually waiting to drink. It is waiting for the loss of attention that drinking causes. Husk rats are its substantial prey; lizards and grounded birds are taken when opportunity permits. Its range crosses several basins and saddles, and it may be absent from a court long enough for the smaller animals there to become busy and conspicuous again.

The silence attributed to it is not mysterious. Many of the country’s inhabitants can detect a footfall or a displaced current of air before a person could see anything move. For a moment the spiders stop adjusting lines, the beetles cease ticking against metal, and the rats withdraw without having appeared to run. Then the small noises resume, sometimes several courts away.

All these lives are easiest to read at Leak 9, though South Court is not an oasis. From a few steps outside, it looks only slightly less bare than the surrounding passages.

The bushes there cast shade deep enough to cover a lizard. Their litter is sparse: a few curled leaves, seed coats, insect wings, a feather caught near the roots. Repeated dampness binds this material into the pipe-trench dust. It is not soil as a continuous layer. It is soil as an event that keeps recurring in the same place.

The roots follow the trench, exploiting both its looser fill and its unreliable moisture. Beneath the bushes, the rock stays dark a little longer after dawn. Joint flies occupy the lower stems and the wet margin of the seep. Spiders attach lines where the branches interrupt the wind. Husk rats approach through a drain rather than cross the open court. Ashwings come down cautiously, drink if the drop rate allows, and retreat to the insulators. Gray lizards spend the hot part of the day in separate fractures but converge on the same small area before sunrise.

The amount of water involved is trivial. Its reliability is not.

If Leak 9 were sealed, the court would not become lifeless at once. The bushes would first shed leaves. The damp film would shrink and break into isolated patches. The flies would disappear from the surface, though some would persist deeper in the joint. The lizards would still come for a while, following routes established over seasons. Eventually the court would retain only the inhabitants that could afford its new, longer intervals between wettings. Seeds and roots might wait underground for another failure in the pipe.

This is why the amendment on the pump house is more than an indulgence toward a few bushes. In maintenance terms, Leak 9 is an accepted loss. In the court’s terms, it is a small climate. The instruction **LEAVE IT** draws a boundary around a dependency whose full extent no maintenance map could conveniently show.

People remain the briefest and most consequential visitors. When they come, they breathe hard on the staircases, leave new dust on old surfaces, and pass noisily through an ecology that otherwise communicates by touches, clicks, and absences. Their repairs create a patchwork of local histories. A clamp may end one population’s water supply; a replaced cup may become a nest site; a cleared trench may open a rat road; an abandoned shelter may acquire spiders before its door has finished rusting.

Nothing about the resulting arrangement is harmonious in any simple sense. Birds steal from spiders. Rats empty seed stores and are themselves taken at burrow mouths. Most wind-borne arrivals die without reproducing. A successful repair can be a drought. Yet persistence has never required harmony here, only enough repetition for lives to close around an opportunity.

Before sunrise, the gray lizard at Leak 9 comes up through the same warm fracture and settles beneath the lowest bush. She is neither the owner of the court nor its sentinel. She is merely one of its most punctual inhabitants. Above her, an ashwing clicks once from a hollow pedestal. Somewhere below the court, pressure changes in the pipe.

The lizard is already waiting when the drop begins to swell.

She takes it with a small, exact movement, without haste. Behind her, something withdraws into the drain. Under the bushes, the joint flies begin to stir.

And the next drop begins.
