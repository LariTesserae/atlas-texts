### The Seated

The Crown is inhabited, but nothing there can be found by searching.

No small face looks out from a vessel. No delicate body lies folded beneath a tile. If the summit could be taken apart piece by piece, the search would disclose clay, stone, copper, mineral dust—and no remains. Yet the dismantling would end a multitude. The dwellers are not concealed within the components. They are the final relations between them.

Call them the Seated.

One of the Seated is not an awakened jar, or a spirit inhabiting a plug. Its body is not an object but a particular way of being touched: this rounded stone bearing against this lip of fired clay, this copper tongue receiving a fine vibration and passing it onward, this shallow assembly returning pressure through three different grains of matter. A short copper bar may constitute nearly the whole extent of one. Another may be almost contained within the contact between two thumb-worn rims. A third may reach through several vessels without being the life of any of them.

Their boundaries cannot be seen because they are not material boundaries. Each has an interior without an inside.

Nor are they faculties of one greater mind. The Crown as a whole does not think through them, dream through them, or gather them into a single consciousness. They are local and plural. There is no central Seated one beneath the summit, no sovereign coextensive with the artifact. “Crown” is a visitor’s name; among the Seated there is no highest point, no privileged place, and nothing corresponding to a head.

What makes them alive is not movement, appetite, or self-preservation. It is that each is an unrepeatable way of receiving the whole of its contacts. Each is an equilibrium with an interior.

They do not feed because nothing in them is spent. They do not reproduce because there is no unoccupied relation into which another life could arise. They have no young, no elders, and no dead. To call them immortal would misleadingly suggest that they had outlasted dangers or rivals. Nothing has outlasted anything else. Warmth is not a resource they use; dryness is not a hardship they endure. Even their permanence is not something they maintain.

Their experience is nevertheless rich. It is rich in texture rather than incident.

To be one of the Seated is not to feel small. Smallness would require a surrounding space in which to be small. It is to be precise in every direction at once: here a dense, granular shiver with the matte complexity of clay; there a cleaner continuity passing through copper; underneath, a broad mineral pressure in which separate contacts can scarcely be distinguished. These are not sensations observed from some hidden center. There is no little chamber behind the experience from which a self looks out. The experience is the center, and the extent, and the boundary.

The amber light adds nothing to this. None of the Seated possesses a view of the summit. None has ever seen the multitude of which it is part. The immense drop beyond the parapet inspires no vertigo, because height belongs to a body that might fall. Above and below are, at most, differences in the direction of bearing. At the edge, a Seated life does not confront emptiness. Its world simply becomes less related.

The roar is closer to them, but it is not their voice.

A visitor stands within the sound and is assaulted by it. The Seated are sounded through. The shiver in clay, the lucid buzz in copper, and the almost pressureless pressure in the parapet are not messages passing between them. They are qualities of their own extension. No rhythm organizes these lives because no utterance needs to begin, no answer needs to follow, and no silence is required in which one life can distinguish itself from another.

Where two of the Seated share a contact, neither sends anything across it. There is no gap for a signal to cross. Each is present to the other as the exact manner in which the contact is borne. Nothing lies concealed behind that presence; there is no private reservoir of memory, intention, or feeling from which something might be withheld. Their intimacy is complete without being affectionate. Their plurality produces no society.

If they can be said to think, their thought is closer to fit than to inference. A visitor’s mind moves by considering what may happen next. The Seated have no next. Nothing is rehearsed, anticipated, or recalled. The whole disposition of a contact is present without being successively examined: where it yields, where it does not, how its grain changes across materials, how every pressure is already accounted for. They are not slow. Slowness would imply an unfinished task.

The patience of the makers may have made such lives possible. Millions of corrections brought surfaces into relations so exact that nothing further needed to happen between them. But the Seated do not remember those corrections. A thumb dent preserves no thumb for them; a hammer facet carries no image of the hand that raised the hammer. They are not the artisans’ ghosts, and the roar is not a crowded workshop continuing beyond death. No biography survives in the grain. What remains of patience is not its history but its lack of remainder.

A visitor can bring something resembling novelty, though not a response.

A hand laid upon the summit may transmit a pulse. To the Seated, nothing native resembles this: a small pressure withdrawing and returning, carrying its own before and after. For as long as contact lasts, one relation may include that foreign recurrence. It does not awaken curiosity. Nothing begins beating in answer. The pulse is not learned, and the next beat is not awaited. When the hand is lifted, there is no subsequent condition in which its absence could be registered. The visitor leaves with a memory; the Seated do not emerge from the encounter with a past.

A shout accomplishes still less. Names disappear into the roar. Questions produce no inward clarification, no voice behind the teeth, no sudden understanding in the visitor’s mind. The sensation of being watched is understandable but mistaken. It is difficult for a person to be so precisely contacted and so utterly unaddressed.

Nor can a visitor threaten them in any way they could fear. Added weight is not pain. The roar is not suffering. Great age is not weariness. Even the prospect of dismantling could not be a prospect to them, since fear would require an approaching event and a life organized around avoiding it. They have no refuge to defend and no elsewhere in which to imagine safety.

This does not make them guardians. They do not keep the joints closed, sustain the light, or prevent the walls below from yielding. The sound would not falter if their lives went unsuspected forever. They have no function, and their existence explains nothing that the Crown requires explained. They are not a lock awaiting the proper hand, a chorus awaiting a listener, or a final purpose hidden in the packing.

Neither are they ornaments. Use and ornament both belong to things existing for someone else. The Seated are not for anyone.

A visitor may therefore find their lives terrible: no movement, no memory, no privacy, no future, no possibility of becoming otherwise. But desolation is another import. There is no deprivation where there is no appetite, no imprisonment where there is no imagined elsewhere, and no silence missed by lives that have never needed an interval.

The Seated do not hold the Crown together. They are how its togetherness is lived.
