Most of what lives in the Unbuilt Foundry lives at the wrong scale for a view from the rim. Its animals are small enough to spend their whole ancestry inside one radiator slot, or large enough that a single intention outlasts the mountainside it alters. Some are absent only in the sense that the century is wrong. The country between them looks vacant. Their bodies, and the ground they have made, are not.

No inhabitant has a wet exterior. Nothing sweats, and nothing drinks. Flexibility comes from thin mineral tissues, nested membranes, and sealed cavities of salt or alloy. An animal can be torn open without making the ground damp; its internal fluids bead, crust, or are taken up by scavengers. Sight exists, and in the clear noon it is often excellent, but the decisive senses are pressure, charge, heat, and interval. A Foundry animal may know another individual chiefly by the hesitation between two pulses of its heart.

The swiftest lives are carried by the kiln-striders.

Within their radiator slots, millers open and close their concentric jaws around falling scale. The jaws do more than crush. Each ring accepts a narrower fraction than the ring outside it, so that a miller sorts its food while eating: exhausted ceramic passes outward, while grains retaining useful charge or chemical imbalance are carried inward toward the gut. Behind the mouth there is little animal except a gripping body, a slow furnace, and the organs by which another miller is eventually made.

A young miller can shelter beneath a single flake of radiator ceramic. An old one may occupy the whole width of a slot, its outer jaws polished against the walls and its inner rings almost too fine to see. Hundreds of generations can inherit the same crevice between two closures of a great valve. For them, the strider’s heartbeat is not a sound but a climate. A concussion that crosses a basin is, in their history, the event separating one age of the world from another.

The needle-mouthed scavengers keep to deeper passages, around old scars, bearing surfaces, and the joints where replacement tissue grows over worn tissue. Their slender mouths can distinguish alloy that is merely hot from alloy still being circulated by a living vessel. They usually feed only where the lining has died. On a healthy strider they are useful: they remove tissue that would otherwise obstruct a passage and leave a minute mineral closure behind them. When the host dies, the same discernment brings them inward ahead of almost everything else.

The translucent pulse-followers are less easy to notice. They have no permanent head. A charge moving through ceramic swells one end of the body into a sensory surface, and the animal goes after it. They feed on the small potentials stranded in the walls after each pulse, gathering where a heartbeat branches, scattering when the current becomes too strong. Against white chloride they are almost invisible. Crossing black slag, they look like clear flexures in the stone that have briefly decided to hunt.

An old strider is therefore not merely covered with other life. It has grown in company with it. Some radiator passages fit the jaws that have worn them. Some internal routes exist because generations of needle-mouths have kept them open. Pulse-followers abandon one chamber when its rhythm changes and multiply in another, altering where small charges accumulate. The strider’s anatomy includes the consequences of their appetites, just as their anatomies include the strider.

The strider itself inhabits the ground more than the air.

It can hold its oral plates against a seam for years while reactions inside its mouth determine whether the crust is worth grinding. It tests buried density through a raised foot, receiving answers as vibration long before the foot completes its descent. Its feeding is selective enough to resemble search. It passes over material that would supply substance but no usable imbalance, and turns toward an apparently poorer outcrop because oxidation, charge, and heat meet there in a more profitable arrangement.

Its attention is not necessarily slow in every respect. A valve can close with catastrophic speed; a parasite can be dislodged in an instant. But preference, recognition, and route belong to its longer processes. A strider may spend centuries approaching another animal and never appear, from basin to basin, to have changed its mind.

Striders recognize one another by interval. Each great heart has its own slight irregularity, and each bellows answers a little differently. Courtship begins when two animals bring those irregularities into relation without making them identical. They exchange valve closures and ground pressures across distances at which neither could see the other clearly. The exchange can continue while they walk apart. It can outlast a range of hills between them. Calls remaining in the cliffs are not recordings of such courtships; they are portions of exchanges still arriving.

Young striders are seldom encountered outside musters. A brood begins as separate warm chambers beneath the parent’s dome, each chamber legless, each with a small bellows and a heartbeat at first indistinguishable from the adult’s. Gradually the rhythms diverge. The parent carries the sound of their movements for centuries before they emerge. Their first bearing surfaces are polished inside the body; their first steps are taken down its flanks.

A brood need not step down into one history. A parent may retain an empty chamber whose occupant is visible only at musters, or carry the kicking of young that never appear in its present at all. At an ordinary census it is one animal crossing one basin. Briefly, at the closing of several cycles, it may stand surrounded by descendants of impressive age.

The open tablelands have their own grazers. The most widespread are roundbacks: low, broad animals easily mistaken for outcrops until the alignment of their dorsal plates changes toward the sun. Sunlight enters lattices in those plates and leaves as stored charge. Heat gathered during the day drives deeper reactions. Roundbacks are among the few animals that can begin with sunlight rather than with another animal’s leavings, but this does not make them placid or plantlike. They seek out rich fans, remember poor ones, contest favorable fractures, and abandon ground when its chemical balance declines.

A roundback feeds through a wide ventral mouth lined with replaceable mineral rasps. It eats the accumulated fans grain by grain, carrying their substance away in its own body and returning simpler residues to the pavement. Over ages, their passage contributes to the polish of the old mountains. Their backs bear wind abrasion, thermal fractures, and occasional dark scores left by fallen kite filaments.

Two roundbacks may contest a productive fan by leaning against one another for decades. There is no final charge and rarely a visible injury. The contest ends when one animal has gradually been displaced onto stone too poor to sustain the effort. It leaves. The other continues eating.

At the natural flues live the sifters. They are flattened animals with circular mouths and numerous small gripping limbs, able to cling to a hot rim but not rooted to it. During inhalation they open into the stream of charged mineral dust. Different lineages accept the pale and dark fractions, and their repeated grazing sharpens distinctions already begun by the cliffs’ electrical currents. During exhalation most retreat into clefts, though their young often leave on the outward breath. They do not drift as spores. Even at that size they turn, choose, reject a surface, and grip another.

Their crawling helps keep the vent throats smooth. A very old flue bears the combined polish of sintering, breath, feet, and mouths. Nothing maintains it. Everything using it does.

The kites inhabit a less tangible country.

At ordinary noon, one may be overlooked entirely. Edge-on, its body is almost absent; turned toward the sun, it appears as a long disturbance in the brightness, with no thickness behind it. Its filaments are feeding organs, senses, and means of recognition. Through them it receives charge from the upper gradients and rare conductive material carried in vent dust. Through them it also feels the broad, slow fields of striders far below.

A kite does not simply occupy a strider’s field. It answers changes in that field, sometimes altering its own filament pattern years before a change becomes visible in the animal beneath. When several kites cross, their first contact is usually electrical rather than physical. They approach, separate, and approach again along intervals too wide for the eye to interpret as behavior.

Their courtship includes controlled overloads. A pair permits their fields to interfere until one filament must be withdrawn, shortened, or allowed to fail. The hard metallic cries that follow are not always cries of distress. Some are answers. Some bring other kites nearer. At musters, when the upper air is crowded with bodies and filaments, courtship, feeding, and injury can produce the same brilliant cry, and only the subsequent movement distinguishes them.

Brief animals usually retain straightforward biographies. A sifter grips and releases. A needle-mouth makes one leap. A miller is born once and dies once. Yet even they learn the local exceptions. On the escarpment that returns a concussion before receiving it, small animals open their feeding surfaces at the first report and have already closed them when the visible impact occurs. They are not predicting the event. Their behavior belongs to the same completed process as the blow.

The longer-lived animals make more extensive use of such arrangements. A strider entering a basin may follow the weathered track of its own crossing, correcting its course by erosion it has not yet produced. The track is simply one of the things of which the route consists, as a foot is, or a memory. Kites sometimes shorten their filaments at a cry that has not yet sounded. Clear animals gather along a ridge whose pulses belong to a strider several basins away, then disperse when that distant body enters dormancy. To them, the ridge has ceased to be habitable. Whether it was ever separate from the strider is a question their feeding does not require them to settle.

At a muster, these relations become crowded enough to see.

The event matters as much to the small animals as to the great ones. Millers emerge into radiator passages already worn to shapes their jaws can use, though the strider carrying those passages may never have borne their ancestors. Needle-mouths find old injuries and new deaths. Roundbacks turn toward fresh pavements. Young sifters leave flues whose exhalations briefly reinforce one another, and the clear pulse-followers become almost motionless in seams saturated with more charge than they can approach.

Striders court, broods emerge, and long feeding routes close. Some old animals die because the processes sustaining them have finally completed. Others simply become absent when the histories separate. There is no general scavenging afterward, no number of carcasses corresponding to the vanished herds. A strider that remains may carry an unfamiliar miller lineage with an ancient and uninterrupted pedigree. A young animal may stand on pavement made by a parent absent from every visible route. A kite may continue the intervals of a courtship whose partner is no longer available to the present.

Individual death, when it occurs, is first a change of tenancy and only later a change of shape. The heart alloy slows. Pulse-followers leave by whatever conductor still carries a rhythm. Needle-mouths enter the vessels. Millers turn from radiator scale to the dome itself. The kite field collapses or wanders away. Much later, roundbacks graze the exposed body and carry its substance into other basins. The carcass becomes an outcrop, then a fan, then food, then pavement. It never becomes a ruin. Nothing about it was built.

An ordinary noon conceals all this without deceiving. The province really is nearly empty at that moment. Beneath one distant dome, however, jaw-rings are closing in their hundredth successive generation. Under the white crust, a clear animal turns its whole body toward a weakening pulse. Far off, a roundback has begun an approach to fresh residue that will take eight centuries. Above them, a kite is paying out a filament toward a field that has not yet risen.

Life in the Foundry is not rare. It is rare only all at once.
