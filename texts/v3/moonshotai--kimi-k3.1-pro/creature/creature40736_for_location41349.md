Nothing in the Garden is blind in quite the way a visitor is blind. A visitor has been deprived of sight; most of the inhabitants never made sight part of their lives. Their world is not an unseen version of the basin. It is a different basin, composed of heat, mineral gradients, pressure, texture, and sounds arriving by several routes. A flame carried across the rim is not a lamp to them. It is a small, violent seep that moves: fierce warmth, crackling, the smell of scorched fuel. A warmth-closing leaf may begin its slow response to it. Most animals simply make room.

Nor do the inhabitants share a common night. Activity follows hunger, moisture, and private intervals rather than any basin-wide cycle. At any moment, nearly everything is still, but it is not the same everything. A grazer may be completing a journey begun months before while the animal beneath the next root mat has not moved since the previous visitor came. Rest and wakefulness overlap without forming a pattern anyone could call a day.

At the base of the Garden’s economy are the stone-feeding films. Some are no more than a slickness on warm rock; others lie dense and velvety between root tips. Their microorganisms pass dissolved substances from one kind of colony to another, each living on some part of what the warm water has taken from the mountain. They are patchy even within a single seep. A difference in temperature too slight for skin to detect, or a change in the water’s mineral burden, can make one colony prosper while another dwindles to a thread. The Garden’s true geography is chemical. Two pools separated by a pace may be less alike than pools on opposite sides of the basin.

The plants are farmers, plumbing, and shelter at once. What visitors call mosses are living scaffolds for these films. Their dense little fronds hold seep water against the stone, slowing its escape and giving the colonies room to spread. When compressed, they release warmth and the smell of iron; afterward they recover so gradually that a footprint may remain springless for days.

The strap-leaves and ribbed fans are not reaching for anything overhead. Their surfaces gather condensed moisture, conduct warmth toward their roots, and divide the seep flow into finer and finer threads. A fan often has no fixed center. As a seep shifts, new roots advance on one side while the oldest roots die on the other. Over centuries, the plant may travel a short distance without any single part of it appearing to move. Its body is a slow decision about where the water will be.

The cup-fronds make smaller worlds. Each spoonful of water has its own inhabitants: microbial veils, minute swimmers, eggs glued just below the rim, and tiny animals able to survive the cup’s occasional emptying. A drop striking one cup is not quite the same event as a drop striking another. Depth, age, and the tension of the leaf alter the note. Animals that never touch the cups may nevertheless know them as landmarks.

The leaves that close around warmth are not traps. Nothing so slow could be meant to catch an animal capable of withdrawing a hand. Their folding seems to preserve a pocket of heat and moisture, allowing the root colonies associated with them to flourish briefly. A salamander’s body, a bell-throat’s back, or a temporary warming of the seep can begin the response. Long after the warmth has gone, the leaf may still be closing.

Height would purchase nothing here. There is no light to compete for, and tissue raised away from the wet ground would lose heat and water without gaining food. The Garden is therefore not a forest with its trees removed. It is a community that never had a reason to build upward.

Beneath this growth, life occurs in archipelagos. A root mat fed by one seep may be crowded, while apparently similar ground a few paces away contains almost nothing but old fibers and water. The shell-backed grazers are among the most numerous inhabitants of the crowded places. Some are no larger than a fingernail; others would fill the palm. Their mouthparts work like fine combs, removing the microbial crop without stripping the living roots beneath it. Each follows a chemical circuit through the mat, revisiting feeding grounds only after they have recovered. A large grazer’s ordinary round may take years.

Their shells are not merely armor. They are instruments of touch. When two grazers meet, they often remain motionless for a time and then knock shell against shell: one tick, a pause, another tick. Sometimes this ends with one turning aside. Sometimes both resume feeding. In the densest mats, their irregular ticking gives an approximate account of how many bodies are present and how closely they are feeding. It is not a chorus. It is more like the sound of many careful collisions being avoided.

The soft, many-legged animals work deeper in the root braids. They consume tissue that has become too old to serve the plant, open passages through compacted fibers, and carry spores and microbial cells on their damp bodies. Dead roots do not simply vanish beneath their attention. They are incorporated into the passages through which water and living roots subsequently travel. These animals are among the Garden’s principal recyclers, though nothing in their behavior suggests haste or thoroughness. A piece of fallen fan may occupy several generations of them.

Ribbonlike hunters lie at the boundaries between one mat and another. Flattened almost to the thickness of a leaf, they detect pressure transmitted through roots and mud. They do not pursue prey across the basin. They wait where paths used by smaller animals converge, then feed by gradually folding themselves around whatever has come close enough. A grazer may pass the same hunter for much of its life without incident. A slight shift in the seep, a new root, or the collapse of an old passage can bring the circuit within reach.

The jumping creatures provide nearly all the suddenness. Their dry cracks are disproportionately startling, especially when a visitor has begun to equate stillness with absence. The leap itself carries them only a short distance, but the sound travels through the mat and into the air. Other animals respond in different ways: a fish turns in its pool, a salamander lifts its head by a fraction, a grazing shell becomes quiet. For a few moments after a footstep, a patch of ground can sound much larger than it is.

The shallow pools are equally densely known by their inhabitants. The slow fish are generally small, flattened, and fringed about the mouth. They do not cruise open water; there is little open water to cruise. Instead, they hold position with minute movements of their fins, drawing seep water through their feeding fringes and sensing the entire pool as a field of pressure. A fallen leaf changes that field. So does a grazing animal entering at the margin, or a drop striking the surface. What seems to a visitor like an unbroken, hand-deep stillness may be, to the fish, a succession of substantial events.

The blind salamanders favor the warmer runnels. Sensitive pits along their jaws and flanks distinguish the heat of stone-fed water from the more localized warmth of another body. Some remain for weeks beside a single root or rounded stone. They feed infrequently, often by doing little more than allowing water and small prey to come within range of a slow gulp. Their young live deeper in the mats, where temperature and chemistry are more stable. An adult motionless in a runnel may therefore be the least representative part of its own species’ life.

Other gilled animals fasten themselves to stone or root and draw water through feathered mouths. On touch, some contract; others merely stop filtering and resume later. They clarify the pools not by cleaning them in any human sense, but by removing particles whose accumulation would otherwise alter the flow. When they die, their places are often occupied by roots first and by similar animals only afterward.

The bell-throats come closest to being persons, not because there is evidence that they think like visitors, but because each is distinctly itself for a very long time. A pool is not simply occupied by “a bell-throat.” It is occupied by the voice with a catch before the boom, or the one whose call makes the ground answer twice, or the one so low that visitors learn it through the soles of their feet before they hear it with their ears. These differences can persist beyond a human lifetime.

The animal producing such a voice is heavy, low, and usually half-buried. Its throat is chambered, and the call couples differently with air, water, and ground. The airborne boom establishes distance around the rim and across the central stone. The ground-borne component arrives by another route, sometimes sooner, and gives neighboring animals information that an echo in the air cannot. A bell-throat’s territory is therefore not just mud and pool edge. It includes a period of acoustic space around its call.

Individuals call at different intervals. Some repeat after several days, some after much longer, and some with a regularity that makes their rare irregularity noticeable. Neighboring calls seldom develop into a chorus. Over long periods, each animal’s sounding occupies the room left by the others. An answer may come hours later and may be altered slightly when the neighbor’s voice or position has changed. It would be too much to call these exchanges conversations in the human sense. It would be too little to call them noise.

Young bell-throats spend their early lives within the wet root systems, feeding on smaller animals and making nothing more impressive than muffled knocks. When one eventually occupies an empty pool margin, its first booming may be rough and inconsistent. Only later does it settle into a stable voice. On rare occasions, two adults alter their intervals, approach one another, and breed in a warm seep. Afterward they separate, and the old distances gradually reassert themselves. There is no season of general excitement, no gathering of the population.

When a bell-throat dies, its body may remain at the pool margin for years while roots, grazers, and microorganisms make use of it. The more conspicuous absence is acoustic. One established pressure disappears from the ground; one familiar boom no longer returns from the rim. Eventually, if the seep remains suitable, a younger animal begins a rougher voice nearby. The Garden does not replace the individual. It replaces the interval.

Only the broad-winged listener regularly leaves the rind and enters the great vacancy above it. Its body is smaller than its flight suggests, much of its apparent size consisting of broad, slow wings suited to the dense, warm air. It produces clicks so faint that a visitor may notice them as tiny pressures rather than sounds. Between clicks, it receives the basin back: rim, open stone, warm bodies, irregular growth, the changing shape of still air above a seep.

It spends much more time folded in a rim crevice than flying. When it lands, it feeds delicately among the mats, taking small jumpers and other exposed animals and drinking from leaf-cups or shallow seepages. Its rarity is not necessarily the rarity of a creature nearing extinction. A stable Garden can afford a very small number of animals that require a great deal of room.

Sometimes it alights on the central stone and remains there with its wings closed. It may press its body against the rock and stay motionless while water gulps somewhere below. No one has established that this behavior has anything to do with the pin emerging from the stone. It may be listening to the return of water through fissures. It may be doing something for which a visitor has no useful category. Of all the animals, it is the only one that could know the basin’s whole outline in a single excursion. Even it does not appear to visit every part.

Nothing tends the pins. Roots divide around them as they would divide around any durable obstruction. Peat accepts one; seep water passes another; animals ignore the third except when their own paths bring them near. There are no caches, tools, deliberately cleared paths, or regular gatherings on the central stone. The seven descending steps affect drainage slightly and are otherwise part of the ground. If the makers of these things left instructions, none of the present inhabitants behaves as though it has inherited them.

Yet the Garden is not without knowledge. Its knowledge is distributed and partial. The microbial films know, in the manner of living chemistry, which reactions are available. Roots know where water is becoming dependable and where it is failing. Fish know the pressure-shape of their pools. Grazers know feeding circuits too long for any visitor to observe. Bell-throats know one another’s distances and voices. The listener knows, occasionally, the shape of the open air. No inhabitant needs to possess the whole.

Long after a visitor has crossed the far rim, the Garden may still be finishing its response to the visit. A warmth-closing leaf completes a movement begun by a hand that is no longer there. Root tips enter water retained in a footprint. The nearest bell-throat calls at neither an earlier nor a later interval than usual. Days afterward, the listener crosses the open air once, clicks, and receives back the shape of the basin.

Then the grazers return to their films, the fish adjust to the fading vibrations, and the slow voices leave one another room. From some distant curve of the rim, a boom arrives through water and stone.

It is not quite an answer. It is the distance, still known.
