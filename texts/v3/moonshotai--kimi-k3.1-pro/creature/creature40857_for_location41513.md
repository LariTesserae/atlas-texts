Nothing large makes its whole living in the Lower Basin. Even the bats, the most numerous animals visible to a visitor, must leave it to feed. The true units of habitation are not chambers but surfaces: a microbial film no thicker than paint, a hand’s depth of silt, the underside of a collapse block, the wet crystals along a mineral dam. From the maintenance route, a hall may look deserted. At the scale of a hand, it is crowded.

At the warm inflows, the food web often begins as a stain. Rust-coloured and cream films spread over stone, some drawing on the chemistry of the spring water, others working through organic matter carried in from elsewhere. They are not merely growths on the pasture; much of the time, they are the pasture. Snails graze them with inconspicuous persistence, leaving pale, branching trails that cross and disappear as the film recovers. Some of the snails are no larger than grains of rice. Others move openly across the terraces, their shells stained by the same iron that colours the stone. A snail may spend its whole life within a few pool rims. When it dies, little is wasted: the soft body enters the sediment community, while the shell may remain on a ledge, fill with silt, and eventually be glazed into the rim.

The silt is fuller of animals than it appears. Amphipods flick through it and vanish beneath debris; isopods work more heavily among softened twigs, guano particles, and discarded organic matter. A handful set down in a tray may continue to shift for several minutes. Flatworms lie flattened beneath stones, almost indistinguishable from wet mineral surfaces until they move. Insect larvae occupy crevices between calcite crystals, some so narrow that the animals seem at first to be irregularities in the stone. New travertine, for all its fragility, offers them a multitude of shelters. Every low dam also makes several kinds of habitat: a filmed overflow lip, aerated water immediately below it, and quieter sediment in the pool behind.

The crabs are the basin’s deliberate pedestrians. They pick their way along margins that would collapse under a boot, pausing to scrape at a film or investigate something lodged in the crystals. Their pallor is often broken by mineral dust or a little rust-coloured staining. At an unfamiliar vibration, they back into the water or fold themselves beneath an overhang. An abandoned moult may remain on a ledge long after the animal has gone, delicate and perfectly crab-shaped until seepage begins to coat it.

In the pools, the pale fish live by a different arrangement of senses. They hang facing the current, nearly motionless except for the small corrections of their fins. Their eyes, where reduced to dark points, are poor instruments for understanding a chamber, but the chamber is not primarily something they need to see. They respond to pressure, contact, and the close disturbance of prey. A lamp moved above the water may cause no reaction; an insect larva struggling at the surface brings an immediate turn. They take amphipods and other small animals from the water and sediment, and in turn leave remains for the crabs and scavengers. In the central chambers, where low dams and old intakes divide the water, a fish’s whole effective world may be one clear pool and its narrow overflows.

Above the western pools, the bats are the great commuters. Their roost is a concentrated, restless mass of bodies, the apparent thatching of the ceiling continually altered by small shifts and disputes over position. In the breeding season it is also a nursery. At dusk the animals pour toward the outer connections to hunt; later they return, bringing the surface back with them in the most useful form possible. The roost is therefore not merely a sleeping place. It is a point where food gathered over the plateau is steadily imported into the dark.

What falls beneath it supports another crowded province. Fungal threads and bacteria work through fresh guano. Beetles and mites subdivide it further. Long-antennaed crickets move across the ledges, feeding where material collects, while spiders wait in crevices and along the edges of the richest patches. These communities are patchy, following the shape of the ceiling above and the routes of seepage below. One ledge may be almost bare; another, a few metres away, may be busy at every scale a lamp can resolve. Water carries fine material downward, and the subsidy continues into the pools. An insect caught above ground may thus contribute, several removals later, to the food of a sediment crustacean or a fish beneath the limestone.

Green life occupies only the few places where light is granted. Near the roof fissures, ferns, dark mosses, and descending roots form sparse, moisture-loving colonies. They are not the beginning of a cave forest; each exists close to a strict boundary. The old lamps create smaller and more artificial versions of the same condition. Algae and stunted moss gather around them in green halos, with tiny animals using the shelter and additional food where they can. When a lamp fails, the halo fades. When a new one is installed, another colony slowly begins. The maintenance crews, without intending to, decide where these little illuminated ecosystems will exist.

Near the outer connections, the population is more varied and more accidental. Leaves, twigs, silt, and occasional surface animals arrive with cooler water. Some arrivals survive for a time; others become food almost immediately. A waterlogged branch caught against stone can support a succession of occupants as it softens and is worked over. Farther inside, such imports become rarer, and the lives of the residents depend more heavily on microbial films, recycled material, and what the bats bring home. Along the Hot Fault, habitation becomes still more a matter of margins. Films colonise raw warm surfaces; small animals occupy gravel, crystal spaces, and the undersides of blocks. They live on ground that has not entirely settled whether it is mud, water, or stone.

People are the largest regular visitors, but they belong to the basin only in shifts. They arrive with lamps and ear protection, communicate by hand signals near the pumps, replace bearings, clear screens, read gauges, and leave. Their map of the place is one of maintained routes, safe footing, pipework, and access. The animals’ map is almost entirely different: seep lines, guano fall, current, shelter, and the thickness of a film. Close to the vibrating pump plinths, animal life thins; a short distance away, the sediment may be densely worked again.

Even the industrial works become habitat. Films develop in wet intakes until crews remove them. Snails use damp mineral surfaces around concrete. Spiders shelter in protected recesses, and lamp halos come and go with maintenance decisions. An abandoned ladder, rail, or shed is not simply unused: it offers shade, attachment, a trap for sediment, or a surface on which new stone can begin.

Most residents will never cross a whole chamber. A snail may have one terrace, a fish the water between two dams, a cricket the reach of one guano ledge. Water, seepage, and bats stitch these small territories together. Meanwhile, the mineral construction touches all of them without announcing itself. Shells and moults are left on rims; grazing surfaces become glazed; a seep shifts, and a community moves a few centimetres. That small movement, repeated throughout the basin, is how life keeps pace with the rising floor. This is not a community installed in a finished cavern. It is one continually finding room on the surface of a place still being made.
