## The Inhabitants of the Upper Flood

Most of what lives in the Upper Flood cannot easily be counted as a collection of bodies. Life there is more often a succession of surfaces: a film on volcanic glass, a root inside a fracture, a mat of old leaves supporting younger leaves above it. Even a large garden may be less a population than a persistence. It remains in place by repeatedly becoming its own support.

On the high, lit stone, the turf plants are small, but the turf itself can be very old. Its living surface is only a thin jade skin. Beneath that lie bronze leaves, dead roots, trapped dust, and clear mineral crust, all compressed into a layer that thickens almost imperceptibly. There is no founding trunk and no necessary center. A patch can be torn away by a fresh fracture without troubling the rest. The mat advances laterally, root by root, and its oldest members have long since ceased to be distinguishable as individuals.

Where a crest rises into the warm rain, the turf narrows to wet seams in the rock. Even there it does not quite become a land plant. It is aquatic life enduring a different arrangement of water, drawing what it needs from films on stone and from droplets that may remain among its leaves for a long time before rejoining the flood.

Farther down, the stalk forests outlive their first attachment to the mountains. A young plant roots in stone. As it ages, its lower growth loses its greenness and acquires a silica rind, while new crowns rise above it. Other stalks lean against the remnant. Roots cross roots; fallen fronds lodge between them; veils attach to both. After enough generations, the original anchor may be cracked, buried, or dead without causing the stand to fall. Much of what holds the forest upright is no longer alive, but little of it is unused.

The finely divided fronds present an immense amount of living surface to water that brings them almost nothing by current. Their fertility is therefore mostly local. Material released by one leaf is likely to be taken up by another before it has settled far. Warm seepage contributes new minerals; pollen, dead tissue, and the thin living films on old surfaces are used again and again. Most reproduction occurs at touching distance. A slope can be densely green with the descendants of only a few arrivals.

Long-distance renewal is rarer and often follows damage. When a heavy layer settles and opens a gap, light reaches growth that may have done little for a very long time except persist. Some plants then put out delicate fertile threads or release a small quantity of spores. The open water above a gap can briefly become faintly dusty with them. Most are intercepted by the nearest veil. A few descend into lower shade. Almost none cross an entire pass unassisted.

The rootless veils make the distinction between inhabitant and habitat still less useful. A young veil is nearly all water: a little translucent tissue spread across the mesh of an older plant. As it thickens, it catches mineral dust and fragments of dead growth. Filamentous decomposers enter the accumulated material. Smaller green things establish themselves on its upper surface. Eventually the original veil may constitute only a minor part of the mass it helped to gather.

By then, tearing it does not necessarily kill it. One portion remains as suspended soil; another sinks onto a lower storey and begins again there. The layer has no single root to sever and no vital center to expose. It survives damage by being incompletely organized.

There are smaller animals within these gardens: soft, colorless beings in the water-filled spaces of the decomposing mass, and minute feeders among the films. They do not make the forest seem populous. A suspended garden may contain a great deal of living tissue and still offer no visible movement. It is possible to look through several storeys of crowded growth and see nothing that appears to be an animal at all.

Against this background, the translucent grazer seems almost conspicuous, though a large one could rest on a spread hand. Beneath an old leaf, it is little more than a clear margin and a broad adhering foot. Its interior shows as a faint line of green, gray, or rust, depending on what it has been scraping. Its outline can be mistaken for a blister of water trapped under the leaf.

It eats the film rather than the leaf itself: microorganisms, fine organic matter, and the thinnest living part of the mineral accretion. Behind it, the leaf is temporarily clearer. Its path is therefore easier to find than its body, and even the path does not last. New film spreads across the cleaned surface while the grazer is still elsewhere on the same plant. A trail that seems to lead toward an animal may instead be closing behind one.

Its eggs are few, clear, and fixed separately beneath old growth. The young often begin life on the same leaf or an adjoining one. Nothing drives them outward except the gradual exhaustion of suitable film and the slow failure of their support. One animal may spend its entire life within a few connected crowns. Other forests, apparently identical, have no grazers at all. Their old leaves simply thicken beneath uninterrupted films.

The ribbon-finned swimmer lives by a different solution: distance.

A mature animal may be longer than a person is tall, but there is little bulk to it. Its pale body seems to continue into the fins, and the fins into the water. The head is blunt, with two small eyes and a mouth too slight for cropping mature leaves. It can move more quickly than anything else in the forest, but speed is not its ordinary condition. More often it hangs above the canopy, or advances by undulations so long and slight that the water appears to close around it without having been pushed.

It feeds sparingly on fertile growth at the frond tips, loose fragments of veil, and the minute life gathered in both. It does not remain to strip a plant. A few fronds bend; a narrow pale shape passes out of sight; the canopy resumes its former stillness.

Its solitude is not necessarily fierceness or fear. It may simply be arithmetic. There are too few swimmers for encounters to be common, and too much open water between the places where they feed. Their rarity also gives them an importance out of proportion to their appetites. Spores, fertile dust, and the eggs of very small animals can adhere to their fins and skin. A swimmer crossing a saddle may carry material farther in a day than still water would carry it in centuries.

It is the nearest thing the Upper Flood has to weather: an infrequent event that moves something from one place to another, then leaves the stillness apparently unchanged.

Below the reach of light, life thins rather than becoming fantastic. There are no luminous gardens in the basins. Where warm seepage meets the floodwater, pale and rust-colored films spread across fresh stone, sustained by dissolved minerals. Their living portion is often only the uppermost skin; beneath it lies the record of earlier films, already becoming crust.

The dark bottom receives some fronds only after they have served as hosts, shelves, and trapping surfaces higher up. Soft, colorless animals work through pockets of compressed vegetation, feeding on decomposer films and on one another’s leavings. Many are no thicker than root-threads. Long stretches of angular stone separate one inhabited pocket from the next. Most of the deep floor remains close to what it was when the rock first fractured.

Night brings no second multitude. The grazer continues to feed by contact. The swimmer hangs almost motionless or becomes a pale absence in the dark. Decomposition goes on without any change in pace. Nothing answers the mineral ticking of old stems, and nothing glows beneath the canopy. Darkness removes color and distance; it does not reveal a hidden flood.

At the outer margins, life becomes discontinuous along with the water. The last rooted growth occupies fractures kept wet by drifting droplets. Beyond it are thin films on warm stone, then spores and other viable specks carried within the rain itself. Some of these may leave the flood and return without ever having touched a surface. The air is not a second, busy realm above the first. It contains humidity, a little reproductive dust, and bare summits where life has not secured a hold.

For all this, the Upper Flood is not slow at every instant. A film can spread across a fresh fracture comparatively quickly. A swimmer can cross open water between one noon and the next. A veil that has sagged for centuries can lose its support in an afternoon. What makes the region seem motionless is the spacing of such events and the time required for their consequences to disappear. A broken stem may tick once; the garden it supported may continue settling long after the sound has gone.

The largest inhabitants may therefore be continuities rather than organisms: a turf mat with none of its first plants remaining, a forest standing among its mineralized dead, a veil garden in which tissue and soil can no longer be separated. They have no center and make no decisions. They persist because damage is usually local, and because almost everything they lose can be used again before it settles beyond reach.

None of them is prepared for the distant drainage of the flood. There is no dry-land form waiting inside the turf, no buried seed intended to outlast the emergence of the mountains. These are not incomplete inhabitants of some later world. They are complete inhabitants of this interval.

For now, a film enters a new fracture. A clean crescent widens beneath an old leaf. Somewhere above the canopy, a pale fin turns once and is gone. Count bodies, and the Upper Flood is nearly empty. Count persistence, and it is full.
