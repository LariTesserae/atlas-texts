## The inhabitants of the Clay Lung

Life in the Clay Lung is arranged by the duration of wetness. Some creatures require permanent water; others can endure a dry interval as eggs, larvae, or dormant specks in a seam; still others survive by keeping above the basin altogether. These territories are not fixed. A clay lip may be shelter during one stage of the cycle, a hunting perch during another, and an impassable dry ridge by the end. The animals know these transitions better than they know the visible shape of the chamber.

### The small multitudes

The foundation of the community is the living film. It is thickest behind the pan lips and in the runnels, where organic dust and sediment come to rest. Microscopic growth binds this material into a thin, dark pasture. Midge larvae work through it, some making fragile tubes from grains and silk. Their burrowing loosens one patch of sediment while their tubes consolidate another. A passage may remain open because larvae are continually disturbing it, while a neighboring passage slowly closes beneath their abandoned cases.

Water fleas gather above these feeding grounds in clouds so fine that they seem like disturbances in the water rather than animals. A falling drop disperses them; afterward they collect again, not quite in the same place. They rise and sink with faint currents, crowd beneath submerged edges, and provide a steady ration for small fish and hunting insect larvae. When a pan is isolated, its fleas may become denser for a while. When it reconnects to deeper water, the cloud thins as predators find it.

Springtails occupy the uncertain boundary between water and clay. They feed on spores, decaying fragments, and the organic sheen left by receding water. On the surface they are almost weightless, dimpling the reflections without breaking them. A fish strike or a heavy drip can throw dozens onto the surrounding clay. Most return along the wettest route, but some remain stranded in drying seams, where they become food for other small scavengers.

The eleven rushes and six moss patches are important out of all proportion to their size. Midges rest on the rush stems before and after flight. Spiders use the stems as anchors, and beetles sometimes cling there with only part of a bubble showing. The algae trailing nearby shelter newly hatched animals that would otherwise be exposed in open water.

The moss patches are humid refuges as well as obstructions. Springtails and minute larvae persist within them when nearby clay has dried. By slowing one seep, a patch may keep a lower cup filled long after it would otherwise have emptied; by diverting another, it may prolong the wet season of a second patch some distance away. The animals living around each seep thus experience slightly different weather.

Almost nothing large accumulates as waste. Instead, there is a continual fine fall of wings, droppings, cast skins, eggs, and tiny bodies. Most of it disappears into the films between one wetting and the next.

### Fish, beetles, and the watchers in deep water

The transparent fish spend much of their time suspended without apparent effort. Only the quick articulation of the backbone and a brief opacity around the gills make a turn visible. They feed through short, precise darts, taking larvae from the film before the sediment has time to settle again.

When rising water connects the pans, the fish spread into places that are inaccessible during quieter periods. Some find rich, nearly predator-free feeding grounds. Others are caught when the connection fails. Their young are especially difficult to see among the algal threads, where they remain until they are large enough to venture beneath the open surface.

The fish react strongly to vibrations transmitted through clay. Before a visitor can identify any change in the drain, they may cease holding position and begin moving between runnel mouths. Not all of them escape the falling water. A shrinking cup can contain a surprising number, flashing over one another in water only a few fingers deep.

Water beetles travel more independently. Their bubbles flash beneath pan lips as they dive, and their wing cases make hard little scrapes when they climb over clay. They can cross distances that confine purely aquatic animals, sometimes flying during active periods and sometimes blundering into webs or walls. Their aquatic larvae hunt among the same sediments frequented by the fish, so the beetles are both competitors and prey at different stages of life.

The flat water scorpions belong chiefly to the sump. They do not pursue food through open water. They cling at slight angles, wait, and close their forelegs on whatever comes within reach. Against dark clay they can resemble a curled fragment of organic matter until that fragment moves. At times they rise toward shallower water to breathe, then return to their motionless stations. Their slowness makes them easy to overlook and difficult to exhaust: a fish may waste energy fleeing a danger that has not changed position for hours.

### The newts and the animal beneath the causeway

The pale newts spend the quieter intervals inside wall channels. Their presence is often first indicated by a head at a wet opening, motionless except for the throat. Increased dripping draws them farther out. They enter the shallows along routes that appear too narrow to connect with anything, yet a newt disappearing into one wall may later emerge several paces away.

In open water they hunt by short sideways snatches. Young fish are taken near submerged edges; springtails are picked from the surface or from clay just above it. The newts are most exposed when the pans are fullest, but they rarely seem to stray far from a possible retreat. As the water turns toward the sump, some withdraw immediately. Others remain until the shallows are nearly empty and must cross bare, glistening clay to reach a seam.

The forearm-long salamander is seldom seen entire. Beneath the causeway, its body is divided by shadows and clay feet into separate indications: part of a head, a length of back, a tail that might be another animal altogether. Its stillness resembles that of the water scorpions, but its scale changes the meaning of it. When it shifts, sediment moves along a broad front.

It becomes most active during drains. As the smaller pools shrink, fish, beetles, newts, and occasionally a frog too slow to leave are concentrated into little remaining water. The salamander moves among these pockets with unhurried thoroughness. The roar of the siphon conceals whatever sound its passage makes. Afterward there may be tail marks in the sediment, a cleared patch of film, and several cups from which every visible moving thing has vanished.

Between drains it returns beneath the causeway. Its long periods of inactivity are not empty of consequence. Fish feeding near that side of the basin remain wary, and small animals crossing its shelter do so quickly. It is the nearest thing the Clay Lung has to a single dominant predator, though for much of the time it has less visible presence than a cloud of water fleas.

### The frogs’ other geography

The clay-brown frogs divide the deeper cups among themselves, but their territories cannot be drawn accurately on the floor. Cup depth, water level, and connection with the buried jars determine how far a call travels and where it seems to originate. A small frog in a favorable vessel can dominate the basin. A larger animal calling from a shallow pan may sound faint and remote.

Two frogs only a pace apart may scarcely answer one another, while frogs on opposite sides of the chamber exchange calls as though sharing a pool. Their social geography is therefore partly acoustic. Physical nearness does not always mean audibility, and apparent distance may be an effect of the clay rather than the position of the animal.

Spawn changes these relationships again. A cup filled with jelly loses some of its hard resonance; drops no longer strike it with the same clear note, and the calling frog’s voice seems duller. Tadpoles then graze within that altered vessel, consuming film and adding their own waste to the sediment. By the time the young frogs leave, the cup is not quite the same feeding ground or instrument that received the spawn.

Not every brood succeeds. A vessel suitable in one cycle may be scoured, isolated, or too heavily hunted in the next. Enough cups remain safe often enough, however, and enough frogs use different depths, for the population to survive its losses. When adult midges rise during wet weather, the frogs feed close to the same cups from which they call. Hunting, breeding, and sounding the chamber are not separate activities so much as different uses of the same small territory.

### Life above the water

The long-legged crickets occupy a basin that the aquatic animals never experience: dry ribs, lath angles, and narrow runs along the shell. They scavenge organic dust, dead insects, cast skins, and whatever soft material remains within reach. Their droppings and occasional fallen bodies return this material to the water below. They also convert the basin’s sound into a ceiling phenomenon, their calls traveling through spaces where no frog voice carries cleanly.

Their silence is consequential because it comes early. Long before drops become numerous, the crickets have responded to some change in air movement or moisture. To an attentive observer, the sudden openness overhead is more informative than the first shine on the clay points.

The spiders live between these upper and lower worlds. Their lines join structures that have no ecological connection for most animals: rush trough, causeway, wall. Webs set near emerging midges can become crowded in a single active period. Afterward the silk holds wings, legs, and small translucent husks, a temporary record of how much life has passed through apparently empty air.

As condensation increases, many spiders climb toward drier anchors. Some abandon the lower parts of their webs; others repair them immediately after the worst dripping has passed. Their positions change so regularly that the state of the silk can indicate the stage of the cycle even when no water is moving.

### The weather they inhabit together

A full active period sorts the whole community without ever gathering it in one place.

First the crickets stop. Newts become visible at wall openings. Fish make their restless movements near the runnels. When the drops begin, water-flea clouds repeatedly scatter and reform. Frogs answer from their cups; midges rise from the wet films; beetles renew their bubbles and cross between pools. Spiders take some of the midges before they ever reach frog height, while other adults feed the frogs or fall back onto the water.

As the runnels fill, animals that were separated by dry clay come suddenly within reach of one another. Newts enter pans occupied by fish. Beetle larvae encounter concentrations of prey. Fish spread into newly connected water. For a brief period the basin is rich in crossings.

The siphon reverses this expansion. Beetles wedge themselves beneath lips. Newts make for the walls. Fish that can reach deeper water do so; those that cannot become crowded in isolated cups. Springtails collect in irregular crescents along retreating margins. The frogs stop together, and beneath the causeway the salamander begins to move.

The drain is therefore not simply a disaster or a cleansing. It is a migration, a stranding, a feeding opportunity, and a redistribution of sediment. It removes some animals from places where they had been abundant and delivers others, living or dead, into the sump. When the deep note releases, survivors are already reoccupying the basin.

No creature maintains the Clay Lung deliberately. Yet the larvae loosen and bind its sediment, the fish limit their numbers, the moss retimes its seeps, and the frogs alter its cups with spawn and grazing young. Spiders and frogs reduce the adult midges; the salamander changes which animals survive isolation; fallen ceiling life feeds the films below. These effects are not always beneficial, and they do not produce a perfect equilibrium. Cups fail. Broods are lost. A passage opened by insects may dry one refuge while a casing-blocked passage creates another.

A listener on the clay seat can hear nearly the whole chamber and still locate almost nothing correctly. The animals, each knowing only a narrow part of it, know when to move.

By the time the first cautious frog calls again, resettlement is well under way: water fleas thickening beneath a quiet surface, a newt withdrawing into its seam, a spider descending to inspect its silk, transparent fish returning to the runnel mouths. Beneath the causeway, the salamander becomes still. The Clay Lung is occupied once more—not exactly as it was before, but by the same restless company, slightly altered by another weather.
