## The Population of the Height

Nothing is native to the Unfinished Height.

There is no concealed biosphere in the fractures, no rare organism waiting beside the warmer vents, no intelligence that needs to be attributed to the moving stone. At almost any instant, the province contains no one. Its population is zero. That does not make it empty; it merely means that emptiness and activity are not opposites here.

The closest things to residents are arrangements with a little duration.

A mineral patch beside a vent may persist long enough to acquire a history. Needles appear first in the hottest suitable recesses, then plates extend across them, then a darker coating closes over both. In cross-section, the patch may look layered like a tiny forest preserved in stone. But its layers record changes in gas, not generations. The silica does not descend from the sulphur. The metallic bloom is not a final stage toward which the others were tending. Nothing has been learned, inherited, or carried onward.

When the vent shifts, the patch ends without dispersal. It does not colonize the next warm seam. There is no dormant remainder waiting in the dust. Another patch may appear elsewhere under similar conditions, but it will be a new accident, not a continuation.

The larger forms have the same kind of tenure. A ridge is less a body than a difference in height maintained for a while. A hollow crown of cliffs is a temporary relationship between uplift and support. When that relationship fails, the material remains, but the form does not go anywhere and cannot exactly be said to die. It simply ceases to be a distinct event.

The Height therefore has growth without life, succession without ancestry, and great age nowhere written as weathering. It contains many things that last. It contains nothing that lives by lasting.

If a living traveler ever arrived, it would have to bring a little elsewhere with it: the chemistry, moisture, atmosphere, nourishment, and protection required to remain itself. The province could offer stone and warmth, but nothing with which to continue a body. While the traveler’s supplies or enclosures held, it would be alive in the Height without being of the Height. It could not establish itself by staying longer. Even its route would have to be measured again after every interval of stillness, because stillness elsewhere would not guarantee stillness along the way home.

Such a traveler would not found an ecology. It would make an expedition.

Against this background, the built traces are more startling than ruins would be. A ruin implies a former whole: a building, a settlement, a pattern of use from which the surviving fragment has broken away. These marks imply no whole. Each may be complete in itself, the entire material record of one pause.

What makes them exceptional is not workmanship but selection. Stress can open a straight fracture across a plain. Cooling can divide glass into regular plates. Vapour can deposit crystals in exquisite order. But five slabs stacked largest to smallest represent one configuration chosen from among many possible configurations. Something intervened in the ordinary distribution of stones and then stopped intervening.

The marks are consequently less like possessions than relations made visible.

The stack establishes *above* and *below*. The arc of wall makes a near side and a far side without defending either. The doorway distinguishes passing through from going around, although both arrive at the same empty slope. The three stones against the fracture introduce sequence into ascent. The line of blocks interrupts randomness without explaining why. The shallow rectangle draws a boundary around nothing that has been found.

Their sophistication lies almost entirely in *on*, *across*, *between*, *through*, and *within*. The stones may be local. The relations are the only clear imports.

Of those who made them, very little follows. Placement does not reveal anatomy. The steps do not prove that their maker climbed them. The doorway does not establish the maker’s height, and a wall described as knee-high supplies the observer’s knee, not necessarily the builder’s. Nothing requires hands, eyes, flesh, or mechanisms. Nothing rules them out. The absence of tools means only that no tools were left; the simplicity of the work is not evidence of simple thought.

Even calling the makers “they” may assume too much. One visitor returning at widely separated intervals, several visitors belonging to no common period, or unrelated arrivals independently using the same elementary forms could all leave a similar record. There is no demonstration that any maker saw another maker’s work. There is no repaired wall, enlarged settlement, accumulated refuse, or developing style from which to infer transmission.

The safest plural is not visitors, but visits.

Perhaps each arrangement was the whole of one visit: a pause, a selection, a placement, and then nothing further that the ground preserved. A cairn might have marked a position, though no map remains to say what made the position important. The doorway might have been practical within some activity now impossible to reconstruct. The rectangle might have bounded an object, an area of observation, or an act that left no other trace. To call their makers surveyors, priests, artists, or settlers would be to people the darkness with my preference for a story.

Nor can the marks be put into a reliable order. Intactness does not make one recent. Damage does not make another ancient. A leaning wall measures uplift since its construction, not the length of its abandonment. The split doorway records displacement, not a later change in purpose. A cairn standing on rock younger than its own stones cannot be dated by the terrace beneath it. Survival distinguishes the lucky sites, not the important ones.

There is thus no stratigraphy of intention. The province preserves a few acts without preserving the intervals between them.

If their makers ever returned, they left no certain repairs. If the visits stopped, there is no way to distinguish that from an absence between visits longer than the life of the current landscape. Nothing supports the idea of a vanished civilization, with its implied cities, generations, and decline. The evidence is both more definite and much smaller: on several occasions, something capable of choice was present long enough to arrange stone.

The honest census is therefore very short. No native life. No demonstrated resident intelligence. Scattered mineral growths without descendants. Landforms whose identities are temporary. And a handful of placements indicating visits that cannot be counted, ordered, or confidently connected.

The Height is not a country whose inhabitants have disappeared. So far as its traces show, inhabitation has never taken hold there. It has occurrences instead of generations, endurance by accident instead of inheritance, and placement instead of memory.

Somewhere, five slabs may still stand largest to smallest. Nothing tends them. Nothing is necessarily expected to return. Their survival is not a promise or a memorial. It means only that, for the moment, the latest fracture happened elsewhere.
