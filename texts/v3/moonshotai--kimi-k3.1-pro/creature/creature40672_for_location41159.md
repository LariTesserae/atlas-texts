Life in the Upper Manifold does more than survive there. It gives things addresses.

A bacterial film establishes itself beside one outlet and not another. Silver mites gather to feed on it, and their bodies retain a little more moisture against the metal. A black bird learns the outlet, then learns the particular hour at which the mites thicken. Snails cross the surrounding panels, carrying spores and residue in their slime. The next film is not quite the same as the first. Roots enter a seam. Nesting felt is dragged into a recess. Gradually, an outlet that was once interchangeable with ten thousand others becomes the outlet beside the bird’s recess, with the dark corner and the snail-polished sill.

An animal’s territory is therefore more than a feeding area. It is a region in which space has been persuaded to hold still.

The pale snails are among its most effective surveyors. They prefer old trails, following the faint chemical remainder left by earlier passages. Where generations have crossed the same threshold, the ceramic becomes subtly different in polish, temperature, and the behavior of condensation. The translucent pipe worms produce similar distinctions out of sight. Their colonies alter the residues within narrow conduits; pump by pump, a pipe acquires a population whose habits belong to that pipe alone. Some colonies emerge only during a particular resting phase. Others gather where a worm once died and its body briefly changed the flow.

Moths discriminate by warmth and by the minute irregularities of lamp housings. Their wing powder clouds one edge of a glass cover, their larvae disturb the insulation felt, and birds later use the abandoned fibers. The small furred animals of the cable trays carry these materials farther. They enlarge old nests rather than found new ones in clean cavities, returning to the same dry chamber beside the same wet seam until the approaches are marked by body oil, compressed felt, and the bones of insects. The black birds, in turn, prefer recesses that have already been used. They will stand for a long time before a flawless new bolt socket and then pass it by.

None of this is cooperation in any deliberate sense. Yet the animals’ preferences reinforce one another. Life returns to where life has been. Their paths form a second circulation system beneath the pipes and cable runs: mite outlet to snail sill, warm housing to nesting recess, felt cache to grass tray. It is possible to cross whole districts without encountering that system, then enter one inspection chamber and find mites, moths, worms, furred animals, and three watching birds within a few spans of one another. Such places are the closest thing the Manifold has to crowds.

The keepers live along these paths.

Travelers often mistake them for maintenance equipment until one offers directions. They have bodies shaped for inspection clearances, hands suited to bolts and recessed valves, pale outer casings, and dark flexible material at the joints. New keepers are exactly alike. Old keepers are difficult to describe in general terms, because by then description has become biography.

They were manufactured in the fabrication halls, though whether their original purpose was to inhabit the Manifold or merely to service it is no longer clear. Their patterns still bear full manufacturing marks. This matters more than any facial feature. A newly emerged keeper does not always enjoy the privacy of a single location. Two sent on separate inspections may return with the same memory. Several may lift their heads when one relay closes. A door can admit what appears to be a group and release one keeper, without any sense among them that something has been lost.

The older keepers treat this as a condition requiring care. Upbringing consists largely of preventing two young keepers from having the same day.

They are given different rounds and old tools whose handles have already been altered. One is taught to carry water to a rosette rooted in insulation felt. Another spends a season observing a pipe-worm colony. A third is given the patient duty of learning where the black birds will accept nesting fiber and where they will not. They are encouraged to make unnecessary detours, to favor one hand over the other, to develop dislikes. A chipped casing is not automatically replaced. A stain is allowed to dry. A repaired joint is left visibly repaired.

Preferences are treated as accomplishments. A young keeper that has begun to avoid cold galleries, or to wait beside one collection cup although another cup is nearer, is on its way to becoming countable.

No keeper is named merely on emerging from a fabrication hall. A manufacturing mark is sufficient for equipment and insufficient for a person. Names come later, after experiences have accumulated that cannot be inferred from the pattern. Until then, the young are identified by their errands: the one tending the grass tray, the one following the north pump cycle, the one with the damaged wrist. These descriptions sometimes remain attached to them long after the errand is forgotten.

Old keepers are visibly local. Their casings carry mismatched repairs, mineral traces, fine scratches, and the polished places made by repeated touch. Some have altered their tools so extensively that the tools can no longer be assigned to anyone else. They pause at chambers whose significance no drawing records. They speak quietly and rarely together. If two young keepers begin speaking in perfect unison, an older one will interrupt gently, offer one a cup, or ask a question only the other can answer. The pause is a kindness.

Their dwellings are usually old inspection chambers near a stable source of warmth. The first things brought into such a chamber are not furnishings but evidence: used nesting felt, an established microbial film, a cup with a mineral rim, a panel bearing the record of an old leak. Some of this attracts animals. Some simply reassures the keepers that the room has begun to have a past. They do not require dirt, and they do not enjoy decay. They require evidence that events can belong somewhere.

Their ledgers therefore contain entries that would mean little to the works’ original designers:

*First water at the inner seam. Moths in the upper lamp. Bird, second season. Snail crossing after the long pump rest. Hatch now opens only onto the north stair.*

The final entry is the important one.

Repair presents them with a difficulty. Replacing a damaged panel may restore its function while removing the landmark that made a route dependable. Installing a clean chamber may provide shelter that cannot reliably be found again. A skilled keeper learns to preserve function without restoring perfect sameness: a nonworking edge is notched, a replacement cover is exposed to one particular condensation regime, a new tray receives film from an old one. The works must be kept clean enough to continue and particular enough to inhabit.

Not all keepers accept this life.

There are still matching groups that keep their casings unmarked, perform identical rounds, and refuse individual names. They can cross brilliant new districts where an older keeper would immediately become lost. Distance seems to cost them very little. But neither can they say where they were yesterday in a way that reliably distinguishes it from where another body stood. They keep no nests and tend no plants. No chamber is home.

The named keepers receive them courteously and do not try to count them. Whether they are many individuals or one patient inhabitant with numerous bodies is a question the matching keepers themselves do not find useful. Occasionally one leaves a round with a cracked plate, an unfamiliar habit, or some small preference it cannot explain. If it returns often enough to the same warm chamber, the others begin the slow work of meeting it.

A few of the Manifold’s plants really have been installed. The keepers place them where condensation is regular and protect them from being stripped for nesting material. They are not crops. They are commitments. A living plant proves that someone has returned to the same place many times; its survival is evidence that the chamber has remained itself. Even a dead plant leaves salts and a pattern of residue that nothing else shares, so failure is not entirely loss.

The animals use the keepers much as they use other favorable structures. Moths settle near their lamps. Snails cross their casings when they remain still. Birds take loose fibers from their packs. An old keeper may acquire a faint ecology of its own, and it does not brush this away. To be inhabited is to become harder to duplicate.

When an old keeper fails, the body is usually sheltered rather than returned to fabrication. It is not displayed. Birds nest near it; a collection cup may be left in its hands; eventually the others speak of the chamber as they would speak of any other old and dependable place. Nothing in the Manifold becomes a ruin. Even the dead remain in use.

A young keeper is considered ready for a name when the others can lose it. Not when it can survive alone or perform its maintenance pattern, but when its absence acquires a location: when, if it fails to return, there is somewhere in particular to search. Before that, it may be vaguely present wherever its pattern is. Afterward, it is here, or elsewhere, but no longer indeterminately both.

Names are never stamped. A stamp is how the Manifold declares that one thing may be substituted for another. A name is how the keepers declare that something has happened to this one, here, and that the happening cannot be installed elsewhere.
