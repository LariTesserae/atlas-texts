Life at the Hollow Crown is concentrated into a few intensely occupied surfaces. Between them, the mountain is almost uninhabited. This gives its residents strangely different ideas of home.

A seep frog may spend its whole life within a patch of garden smaller than a cloak. Its world has definite borders: the dry side of a stone, the deep moss beneath one fern, the margin of a pool where the algae are softest. Beyond those borders lie other frogs’ pools, or stretches of rock where its skin would quickly lose moisture. It does not experience the Crown as an immense summit above an immense view. It experiences shelter, wetness, the approach of footsteps through vibration, and the changing taste of the air before rain.

On humid evenings the males take up positions beneath the ferns and around the stone cups. Their white throats swell; their small bodies tighten with the effort of calling. Each frog advertises itself at a scale far beyond its actual territory. The shaft confuses distances and multiplies directions, so that a chorus of a dozen animals can seem to come from everywhere along the cliff. Yet the frogs appear to understand one another perfectly well. Calls are answered, repeated, shifted, and occasionally abandoned when another frog is too close. Their abundance is crowded into inches.

Their young inherit an even smaller world. Among the tadpoles, the pool is not a puddle but a whole range of feeding grounds: a dark algal margin, a sunlit patch of stone, a depth sufficient to hide beneath. They graze the same surfaces again and again, keeping the thin growth from closing over itself. Some pools hold tadpoles of several sizes, while others, a hand’s breadth away, remain empty. A difference in shade or seepage decides which is which.

The midges make abundance less dependable. For days there may be only a few, taken one at a time by spiders and frogs. Then the right combination of warmth and moisture brings them up in dense columns. Suddenly the garden is full of food on the wing. Spiders feed beneath the hooded flowers and in the angles of fern stems; frogs climb onto wet stone and snap at whatever passes low; the air above the rim becomes interesting to the swifts. A hatch is not a season. It is an event, sometimes over almost as soon as it begins.

The copper-colored bees keep a different schedule. They belong to sunlight and to the scarlet flowers. Where the frogs respond to humidity, the bees respond to warmth, appearing on open rock and working methodically among the nodding blooms. Pale pollen collects on them until their bodies look dusted with flour. The flowers depend on these visits, though their roots stand in water and the bees must cross bare, dry intervals to reach them. When cloud closes over the garden, the bees’ day contracts. When a raptor comes, it ends instantly: the flowers seem to swallow them, and only the red hoods remain visible above the moss.

The small furred herbivores are known chiefly by their workmanship. A sedge is clipped cleanly. Several stems vanish. Later, a bundle appears in a crack where moving air can dry it without carrying it away. These are not random leavings but provisions, gathered during brief abundances and set aside against cold, cloud, and days when venturing onto the open garden would be unwise. The animals themselves move along routes that barely exist: under a ledge, behind a root, through a cleft too narrow to admit a watching eye. A warm patch of flattened moss, a pellet, the quick withdrawal of a whiskered face—these are usually all the evidence they offer.

Their clipping changes the garden. Some sedges are kept low while others, growing in less accessible pockets, rise and seed. Droppings return nutrients to places where soil is only beginning to form. Seeds stick to fur or pass through the animals and emerge along their hidden paths. Without intending to, they carry small portions of the garden beyond its wet center, though most of those seedlings fail where the moss ends and the silver rock begins.

The moss is the garden’s real architecture. It holds water long after the exposed stone has dried, makes cool cavities for frogs, and gives fern roots something in which to catch. Its surface looks soft and uniform from a standing person’s height, but at the scale of a mite or a spider it is broken country: towers, shaded gullies, soaked hollows, dry ridges. Whole lives pass between one green cushion and the next. When moss finally binds enough mineral grains and old organic matter to make soil, the broad-leaved herbs enter. When a mat dries too far or slips from its ledge, that small history ends, and another begins in the seep behind it.

The ancient tree stands apart from all this hurried occupation. It may be the oldest distinguishable inhabitant of the Crown, already gripping its cleft when the shelter was built. Almost none of its effort has gone into height. It has thickened instead: wood laid down slowly, bark furrowed, roots forced along the outside of the rock until stone and root seem to be bracing one another. Its needles hold water after the garden’s broad leaves have begun to dry. Tiny spiders use the stillness among them. Lichen occupies the older bark without noticeably inconveniencing it.

Nothing about the tree suggests deprivation alone. It is not merely a large tree prevented from becoming large. Its form is a complete answer to the place—an answer so slow that weather, which transforms the Crown within an hour, can scarcely be the right measure of its life. A handline rots; the tree adds another almost invisible increment. The shelter settles; a root extends the width of a grain of sand.

The swifts live at the opposite extreme. Hundreds of them are packed into dry holes down the inner wall, yet for long intervals the Crown appears to have no birds at all. Within the cavities, however, the colony is present as rustling, warmth, droppings, old feathers, and the insistent sounds of nestlings. The young know the shaft first as resonance. The wind-note reaches them through stone; neighboring birds shift and cry; the open entrance changes from silver to dark.

Once on the wing, their home expands beyond any useful boundary. They feed meters from the cliff but may range far along the ridges before returning. When a hatch draws the colony out, individual decisions become almost impossible to follow. One bird turns, another banks to avoid it, a third climbs, and suddenly the whole flock has folded into a new shape. Their cries strike the inner wall and return sharpened. Then the insects thin, the flock breaks apart, and the sky is vacant again.

Not everything that lives at the Crown lives upon it. The pale raptors belong to a territory measured in cliffs and valleys. The Crown is one passage through that territory, valuable because swifts rise from it and small herbivores occasionally misjudge the safety of an open ledge. The raptors’ ownership is intermittent but complete. For the few seconds of a crossing, no other animal behaves as though its ordinary concerns matter. Bees disappear, swifts become a dark scattering against the wall, and the frog chorus loses its confidence. Then the pale shape clears the rim, and possession of the place returns to the smaller lives.

People seem never to have attempted that kind of permanent possession. The shelter was not quite a house; it was a refuge sufficient for a pause. The pecked channel and basin show that someone understood the seep well enough to improve its usefulness without trying to command it. The peg-holes show respect for the traverse. Nothing suggests fields, storage for a household, or the patient accumulation of rubbish by which a settlement usually announces itself.

The hammerstone in its niche may be the clearest evidence of how they regarded the place. It was not discarded. It was left where a returning hand could find it. Whoever those visitors were, their relationship with the Crown included the expectation of another journey, but not necessarily a longer stay. Now they are among its inhabitants only in the way that a habit can outlast the people who practiced it.

The present residents are governed by intervals: wet and dry, warmth and cloud, hatch and hunger, exposure and cover. A clear morning can make the Crown seem deserted, though the wall is full of swifts and the moss full of frogs. A cold night silences almost everything, yet the hay stores remain in their cracks, the bees wait out the darkness, and the tree continues holding drops it has not yet released. Life here is rarely visible all at once.

On a still evening, with the swifts back in the wall and the scarlet flowers closed over their small concealments, the whole population seems to have withdrawn into stone. Then a white throat swells beneath a fern. The first call is absurdly large for the body that makes it. Another answers from across the seep. The shaft takes both voices, joins them, and returns something deeper.

For a little while, the frogs inhabit not only their few wet stones but the entire hollow mountain.
