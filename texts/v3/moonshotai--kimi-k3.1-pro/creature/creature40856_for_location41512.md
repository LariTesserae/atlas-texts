## What Lives There

A census of the Unfinished Highland would fail before it found its first specimen. The pale column beside a court might be a separate plant, a temporary organ of the floor, or the surviving portion of something much larger whose other tissues have already been withdrawn. Two vaults touching at their bases might be hydraulically independent. A tower and a reservoir kilometres apart might respond to the same wound. There are living forms in abundance, but very few reliable individuals.

The most widespread inhabitant is the smooth green skin that makes the courts look empty. It is not turf, and there is no crowd of lesser plants beneath it. It is an extended living surface, photosynthetic where the light reaches it, pale and densely vascular below. Its flatness is not inactivity. Within it, the anatomy of a wall, basin, or arcade remains possible without yet being present. It can lie unchanged for years, holding water and tension across immense distances, and then differentiate so rapidly that the floor appears to have been merely the folded state of something tall.

The reservoir linings have another kind of life. In daylight they may be little more than glossy skins; in the black cisterns they become thick, folded, tannin-dark tissue, able to tighten as their water is taken from them. Their pools are not habitats in the ordinary sense. Nothing swims in them, roots at their margins, or hunts beneath their surfaces. The water is held, pressurized, and surrendered by the body surrounding it. When a basin is emptied, its lining does not necessarily die. It contracts, seals its smaller pores, and waits in the dark.

A detached piece of that lining can become the smallest convincing independent organism in the highland. Lodged in a wet fracture, it curls, closes its wounded edges, and makes a cup. The cup may remain no larger than a scar, or it may send vessels into the surrounding tissue and begin drawing enough water to darken the stone-like fibers around it. If the connection is later severed, the cup may survive alone. If the connection remains open, its separate existence becomes difficult to prove.

Columns, buttresses, ribs, and leaf-vaults are not so many species standing above this basal life. They are ways of spending accumulated substance and pressure. A column carries water upward until its flutes are full of vertical movement. A hollow vault catches daylight on its outer surface while condensation runs through the shaded grooves within. A buttress takes load from a floor that is becoming something else. None of these forms is necessarily progressing toward a final, stable version of itself. An arch that exists only long enough to cast one shadow is not therefore an unsuccessful tree or a ruined building. It was complete as an event.

Their endings are equally unlike ordinary senescence. Some harden and bleach, remaining standing after their living tissue has retreated. Others are green and apparently vigorous when their vessels begin to close. Such a structure can sag and die without rot, its substance being taken back through connections invisible from outside. Conversely, a splintered arcade with no intact crown or root may still be abundantly alive. In the highland, a silhouette is poor evidence of survival.

At the rim, the root curtains are the only inhabitants that appear to reach beyond the body of the place. They do not search for soil. They live at the boundary between hot vapor and open sky, gathering moisture directly from moving air. Their feeding becomes visible only when droplets swell at their tips and the finest roots bend under the weight. Some strands thicken into wet, vascular cords; others remain almost transparent. A torn length may dry into inert fiber, but a piece carried across a fresh surface can sometimes resume growth, leaving no clear distinction between a root lost and a new plant established.

Even the buried highland is not uniformly dead. Much of it has been crushed into structural fiber, but living threads persist around some of the old water chambers. Without light, they grow slowly or scarcely at all, sustained by whatever moisture and sap still reach them. A fresh split can expose such tissue to air and daylight. It may green along the wound, or emerge white and begin building without passing through any recognizable seedling stage. The visible region is perpetually young, but some of its continuities may be considerably older than the forms they produce.

The highland’s separate propagules are inconspicuous beside all this vegetative renewal. Its wind-dust has no bright floral display to accompany it and no scent meant to attract a visitor. It is most likely to escape during brief dryness, when a drained rind contracts and a seam opens above warm, rising air. Most of the dust is lost to cloud or water. The little that reaches a wet wound encounters a suitable beginning: exposed tissue, available moisture, and no waxy surface to cross. Waterborne propagules are equally passive. They do not swim or choose a landing place. The same violent release that destroys a district may carry them into another.

But fragmentation is more important than either. A fallen piece does not always reproduce the organ from which it came. Cistern tissue may send up a rib; a rib may first spread into a low green sheet; a length of arcade may root along its underside while its upper surface becomes a line of small chambers. What persists is not a shape but the capacity to make shapes, modified by pressure, hydration, and the condition of the wound.

This makes competition difficult to distinguish from self-maintenance. A rising district may draw water from another until distant walls slacken. If the two are separate plants, one has deprived the other. If they are continuous, the body has sacrificed a limb to feed a growth. Cooperation has the same ambiguity. A new reservoir may stabilize several towers without sharing anything so deliberate as aid. It simply alters the conditions under which all of them stand.

There is no visible food web laid over these exchanges. Light, water, pressure, and recoverable tissue are the principal resources. Nothing is grazed, and no wreckage is processed by a procession of scavengers. The faint fermentation inside sealed sap may hint at microscopic tenants, but they do not give the highland a second visible ecology. At every scale visible from its courts and rim, the eater and the eaten have been replaced by the donor, the recipient, and the possibility that both are the same organism.

After a collapse, the number of inhabitants may therefore appear to increase even as living substance is lost. A single lattice becomes a hundred rooted splinters. A reservoir lining divides among several cracks. A column survives but no longer shares pressure with the floor that once supported it. To ask whether this was reproduction, dispersal, pruning, or merely damage is to demand a distinction the highland does not consistently provide.

Its silence is not the silence of a place awaiting life. The pools hold no concealed swimmers; the pale vaults shelter nothing; the open courts are not empty ground between living things. They are living things in one of their least differentiated states. When the far horizon lifts, no hidden population is building a city. The population is becoming visible by changing shape.
