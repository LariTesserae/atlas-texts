Nothing lives in Basin Four in numbers sufficient to explain the noise. The impression of crowding is an error of scale. A few engines can fill kilometres of cold air, while an entire community of moss animals can be contained, frozen and invisible, within a cushion no larger than a hand. The basin’s visible life is sparse; its persistent life lies close to the ground and is often difficult to distinguish from the dead material beneath it.

The plants of the upper benches are the nearest thing to permanent residents. Sphagnum does not merely cover the ground. It builds slowly upward, each shoot living at its tip while the older stem below darkens and dies into peat. A hummock is therefore a population standing on its own remains. In favourable years the tips add a little growth; in poor years they may appear to do nothing at all. Their rust, olive, and grey patches record differences in moisture and shelter too slight to read from the rim.

Sedges bind the wetter margins with rhizomes, drawing a tough horizontal network through the root-felt. Crowberry keeps its small, leathery leaves close to the shelter of the mat. The dwarf birch is easy to mistake for a scattering of recent seedlings, but height is no measure of its age. A plant no taller than a boot may have a thick, woody base and many seasons behind it. Exposure has made it wide rather than tall. Its buds and leaves must accomplish what they can in the short, uncertain interval when growth is possible.

On the frost-shattered eastern stone, lichens add another, still slower population: pale scales and dark crusts occupying surfaces where almost nothing else can gain a hold. Around abandoned workings, colonisation proceeds without any appearance of pursuit. A sedge establishes at the lip of a drain where snow collects. Moss finds a sheltered ledge on a peat block. Years later, another fragment of rail becomes difficult to see. There is no green advance across the basin, only innumerable small closures.

Beneath this living skin, most of the ground consists of former inhabitants. Sedge stems, moss leaves, roots, and other fragments lie preserved in layers. The peat is not a vast store of life waiting to resume, though viable spores and resting stages occur in suitable places. Much of it is simply an archive in which decay has been delayed. Bacteria and fungi continue their work where temperature, water, and oxygen permit, sometimes so slowly that a recognisable stem can outlast the working lives of the people cutting around it. When the mill processes peat, it is handling the accumulated remains of communities whose growth no human observer could have watched directly.

The smaller living animals belong chiefly to the surface moss, litter, and wetter seams of peat. When a little water thaws, nematodes move through the films around particles; rotifers and tardigrades become active among the moss leaves; mites and springtails resume their obscure business in the root mat. Not all survive freezing in the same way. Some endure it, some tolerate drying, and others persist through eggs, cysts, or sheltered refuges. Their active intervals may be brief and separated by long apparent lifelessness. A spoonful of thawed moss can contain more visible movement than the whole basin floor shows from a service road.

The warm ground beside the peat mill concentrates these possibilities. It is the closest thing the Drum has to a garden, although no one planted it. Moss near an outlet may be wet and vividly green while moss a few strides away remains locked and subdued. A dark film gathers where seepage stays liquid. Small arthropods find their way into the litter, and in the brief thaw a few insects emerge around the seep. There is no abundance beyond that margin. It is as if summer had been reduced to the size of a doorway.

Nor is this little oasis secure. It depends on waste heat, pipework, and the continued operation of the mill. A change in drainage can move it; a shutdown can freeze it. Its green cushion may represent many years of growth, yet the conditions supporting it are industrial and temporary. The crews may value it less as vegetation than as evidence: damp ground, warmth, and seepage are things to notice because they indicate what the machinery is doing.

The raven uses the basin without belonging to it. From the highest power pole it can watch the shelter doors, the roads, and the active face without entering the worst of the noise below. It may have learned that human activity occasionally produces something edible, but there is not enough dependable food to support many scavengers. When it calls, the sound is often taken by the wind or absorbed into the larger note rising from the workings. Its silence from the ground makes it seem more aloof than it is.

The fox is even less committed. Its tracks along an abandoned drain describe a journey, not a territory. It crosses the service road, passes beyond the lamps, and leaves the basin with nothing to show whether it was hunting, scavenging, or simply taking a firm route through difficult ground. Neither fox nor raven forms part of a visible animal community. They are brief connections between the workings and the wider tundra.

People occupy the Drum more continuously, but less permanently, than either. Their homes are elsewhere. On the basin floor they inhabit shifts rather than houses, and their continuity depends on handover. The main shelter holds the small domestic equipment of that continuity: somewhere to dry gloves, somewhere to write, a logbook, a kettle perhaps, and the flashing telephone light. Individuals arrive and leave; attention to the sump, the pumps, and the active face is passed on.

No worker experiences the whole basin equally. It is divided into rounds, isolated pools of lamp light, and routes known by their hazards. One person may know the pump floor chiefly through vibration and another the conveyor through changes in its impacts. Someone who has worked there for years may know the workings intimately without often having seen them in daylight. To a visitor, the widely separated lamps mark random objects. To a crew member, each marks a task and a particular approach through darkness.

Experience does not make the noise recede, but it makes differences within the noise meaningful. A new vibration, a changed interval between impacts, or a note that no longer belongs to the expected machinery can matter more than a gauge reading. At close range, where speech is impossible, gestures and lamp signals become economical and local. Inside the shelter, at handover, the basin briefly becomes speakable: what has frozen, what has begun to thaw, what sounded wrong, what has been adjusted, and what must not be left unwatched.

Older crews remain in the same fragmentary way. Their stakes, rails, stores, and roofless hut wall survive without providing the outline of a former settlement. They did not leave a town behind. They left alterations, tools, and work that subsequent people either continued or allowed the cold to preserve.

The machines are not alive, yet they dominate the basin as no animal could. They consume fuel, shed heat, make weather around themselves, and determine where water can remain liquid. Their requirements set the rhythm of human attention. Each creates a small habitat of vibration, rime, exhaust, or warmth. They also possess none of the other inhabitants’ capacity for suspension. A moss shoot can survive a long interruption in its growth; a stopped pump may freeze into the very condition it was meant to prevent. That difference explains much of the Drum’s apparent urgency.

Thus Basin Four has no hidden city and no single creature at its centre. It has a thin plant population standing upon the remains of older ones; microscopic awakenings in temporary water; one dependent patch of warmth; a raven now and then; a fox crossing out of the dark; and people whose individual stays are short but whose relay of attention can continue for years.

They all keep different time. The small animals come awake with thaw. The fox measures its visit in tracks. The crews measure theirs in shifts and handovers. Pumps and engines run according to fuel, ice, and maintenance. Birch reckons in decades, moss in the slow burial of its own stems, and peat in durations that make the abandoned workings look recent.

From the rim, none of these inhabitants is visible. Only the engines announce themselves. The rest of life in the Drum is not silent, exactly; it is simply too small, too cold, or too slow to answer them.
