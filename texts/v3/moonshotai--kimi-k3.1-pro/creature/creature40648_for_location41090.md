It is tempting to call the inhabitants an ecology, but ecology implies exchange. Nothing passes here from mouth to body, from body to ground, from ground to leaf. It is equally tempting to call them a community, but no favor is being performed, no quarrel developing, no allegiance tested. The multitude preserves the shapes of hunger, shelter, pursuit, craft, and attention without requiring any of them to continue.

The slate-haired grazers are usually the first lives close enough to examine. Their hair grows in narrow, directional whorls, and their muzzles are armored only at the lip, where old grazing would have demanded it. Some have incisors visible beneath the lip; some hold a few grey filaments without having severed them. Their horns are polished in places, their hooves unevenly worn, and here and there a flank bears a seam of scar tissue long since closed. These marks resemble the residue of journeys, contests, and seasons, but they cannot be used to reconstruct a history. There are no smaller versions growing into larger ones. A knee-high animal standing beneath a giant’s belly is not a calf. It has its own completed proportions, its own worn feet, its own undemonstrative eyes.

The tall browsers rise among them like instruments abandoned by a survey. Their necks are divided by nearly hidden joints, each section so narrow that the head seems to have been set at the top of a series of deliberate decisions. One may hold a wax scale between its lips. Another may have its mouth around the tip of a black branch, though the branch bears no wound and the browser’s teeth have not closed. Their long legs fit among the other bodies with an exactness that looks courteous until you notice that courtesy, too, would require negotiation.

The plated giants carry the most complicated company. Their backs are not merely armored but inhabited. Small furred groomers cling in the seams, their long fingers curved around parasites they have not removed. Blind, pale creatures occupy the warm-looking hollows where plates overlap, though there is no warmth. Along one giant’s spine, a row of compact animals sits with their muzzles buried in its hair; along another’s, translucent wings are folded over clusters of eyes. Some of the colonies appear territorial, some commensal, some simply intimate. A flat many-legged thing can be exposed beneath a lifted plate edge, its body occupying almost no thickness at all, while above it a broad-pawed animal rests with one hand laid against the giant’s skin as though listening.

There is nothing sentimental in these arrangements. Two giants may stand with their armor locked in what could equally be combat, mating, or shelter. A groomer’s fingers may be stained dark at the tips. The lean predators have not become harmless in any moral sense. Their teeth remain excellent. One hunter’s paw is extended with the claws unsheathed; another holds its mouth a finger’s width from the narrow place behind a grazer’s skull. The intended prey has no expression of resignation, and the hunter none of mercy. Nothing has been forgiven. Nothing has been spared. The final instant has simply ceased to be a passage into another one.

Overhead, the winged inhabitants make the crowding architectural. Their extended wings form roofs, aisles, and intermittent fields of shade. Some have feathers stiff and thin as horn; others bear membranes stretched between fingers so elongated that the whole animal seems mostly gesture. Their feet may touch a horn, a giant’s plate, or nothing you can identify. None strains against the air. The wings are not holding themselves up. They are simply the shape these creatures have when flight is no longer necessary to the meaning of wings.

A crowd of this size should have an odor: hide, breath, dung, crushed vegetation. It has none. Even the small lives are clean of process. Break a pan plate and the shelled bodies beneath it lie cool and separate, without tunnels, eggs, or discarded casings. Under a lens they do not look dormant. Dormancy would be a kind of waiting. Their mouthparts are articulated, their folded legs complete, their minute eyes open or closed according to no discoverable pattern. None resembles a young form of anything above ground. The multitude does not diminish by generations as it diminishes in scale. Each interval contains inhabitants of its own.

The plants are sparse, but they are not merely exceptions to the animal crowding. A tussock of grey filament has the same finality as a horn. A rust-green crust follows the protected grain of rock without spreading beyond it. The black shrubs bear their waxy scales with an almost arbitrary precision: no scale is newer, none damaged, none about to fall. Sometimes a browser holds a scale identical to those on a nearby shrub, while the shrub lacks nothing. Food and feeding remain related without anything having been transferred.

Different country brings forth different anatomies, but not better explanations. In the mineral basins, armored animals stand deep in dust with only their upper ridges and the crowns of their skulls exposed. Some have nostrils raised on fleshy stalks; others have broad, sieving lips suited to matter far softer than dust. On the high roots, the herds are taller and more thinly furred, their narrow feet placed on slopes no hoof could usefully grip. Certain animals bear membranes apparently made for gliding, webbed feet, heat-shedding frills, or other equipment for conditions the plateau does not provide. Their bodies are not solutions to this place. They are the surviving grammar of needs, written after need has lost its authority.

Near the rim, the outward-facing animals are the most difficult to interpret. They include low, thick-skinned forms with their eyes set far back, slender creatures whose many small eyes shine at slightly different depths, and makers crouched so motionlessly that at first they seem to be additional stones. They are not watching for rescue, enemies, weather, or one another. The inaccessible country below promises them nothing. Their orientation is not hope. It is simply that, among all the relations inscribed in them, departure remains one.

The makers are rarer only in the sense that tools and shelters are rare. A change of view can make either seem less so.

The seated toolmaker rewards close attention, though it gives none back in any ordinary sense. Its squat trunk and long limbs explain the low doorways; its hands explain very little else. The hammerstone fits a polish in one palm. The obsidian core rests securely between its knees. In the other hand, the finished point lies across the fingers, thin, balanced, and unmistakably intentional. Every part of the work is present except an event.

The maker is not interrupted. There is no tension in its shoulders, no suspended concentration, no sense that a visitor has arrived at the wrong moment. Its face is in some ways harder to read than an animal’s because the tools encourage a mistake: you keep expecting intention to become action. But intention here is not an arrow. It is a distinction. In the maker’s presence, stone is no longer merely stone. It can be core, hammer, flake, edge, point. That difference is as persistent as hide or horn.

Elsewhere, a cutting edge is already hafted while the sinew remains in a long twist beside it. A shelter’s hide is stretched and sewn, but there is no bedding inside and nothing has been put away. A cairn cannot reliably mark a route, since the route will not remain consistent, yet its stones are still not an accidental heap. The makers’ works do not organize the plateau. They punctuate it.

To call these beings guardians would invent a duty. To call them priests would invent an object of worship. To call them the last of their people would invent a sequence of loss. They may be none of those things. They are simply the inhabitants in whose company the difference between accident and workmanship becomes visible. That does not make them rulers of the multitude. The distinction between host and parasite, made behind one animal’s ear, may be just as intricate.

And no first census survives a second way of looking.

A sideways step may reveal a copper-brown herd among the slate-haired one: high-shouldered animals with narrow ivory faces and fans of translucent skin between flank and foreleg. Their bodies are not transparent, and they have not replaced the grazers. A horn that seemed to belong to one animal becomes the meeting place of three unrelated heads. Lower your eyes toward the shining crust and further populations appear, pressed close to the ground or standing far off in valleys that were not visible from standing height. Where several solidities coincide, their outlines deepen almost to black.

These are not visions laid over the true herd. There is no true herd in that sense. Touch the body available to you and it is cool, substantial, quietly yielding. Return to your former position and you have not proved the others unreal; you have only resumed an earlier poverty of view. The plateau does not conceal its inhabitants behind appearances. Appearance itself is too narrow to admit them all at once.

It would be convenient to say that the plateau alone is alive and that everything else is its thought. But a thought does not usually have worn hooves, individual scars, or a flank that yields under the hand. Nor would a single life need to preserve so exactly the difference between hunter and hunted, between a shrub and the scale held in a browser’s mouth. If the plateau lives, its life does not cancel the lives it contains.

The closest a visitor can come to understanding is this: the animals are not alive because they perform living processes, and the processes are not illusions because they have ceased. A grazer remains distinct from its food. A predator remains distinct from its prey. A maker remains distinct from both the intended object and the accidental stone. These relations have not been erased; they have been relieved of the need to happen.

Your own life, by comparison, seems almost noisy with incompleteness. You breathe. You tire. You become hungry. You make plans that depend upon a direction remaining behind you. Even your footprints may already be waiting ahead, so none of this guarantees that you possess a future the plateau must recognize. Among the multitude, being warm and changing does not make you superior. It makes you provisional.

Somewhere within the woven horns, an eye acknowledges you. It does not turn. Nothing gathers in the face around it. There is no welcome, warning, or appeal, and no sense that your next action is anticipated. Its patience is not waiting. It is attention without expectation.

That may be the clearest sign of what lives here. These are not bodies frozen on their way toward life, nor dead things retaining the appearance of it. They are lives no longer required to enact themselves in order to remain distinct. The hunter is entire. The prey is entire. The sheltered colony, the scale-bearing shrub, the maker, and the point in the maker’s hand are entire.

Only the visitor insists that something must happen next.
