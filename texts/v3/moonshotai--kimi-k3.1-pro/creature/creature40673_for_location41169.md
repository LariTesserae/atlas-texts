## The Tenants of the Small Rain

No single creature lives in the whole of the Undercroft. The swifts’ undercroft is a high cliff face and a column of usable air; the doves’ is a crowded town of middle ledges; the frogs’ is two shores of tea-dark water, briefly enlarged after rain. The striped snake knows a warm ground country with a prohibition running across it at the height of the second ledge. Only the keepers attempt to inhabit all these versions at once, and even they do so largely by learning where not to go.

The keepers are a small, changing company rather than a closed order. Some are born into the work; others arrive as apprentices and discover whether they can endure the noise, the ladders, and the extreme inconvenience of never being allowed to finish a repair simply because it needs finishing. Most go up the brick stair to sleep. One may remain below beside an unfinished patch or during uncertain weather. They dress for falling things: hair bound close, shoulders covered, soft soles on the ladder rungs. Their hands are usually marked with lime, cord cuts, and the pale scars left by scraping droppings from brick.

They are not exactly priests, though they keep rules whose reasons are sometimes older than anyone present. They are not exactly bird-farmers, though they gather droppings and know the condition of hundreds of nests. Nor are they merely masons, although a keeper who cannot lay a neat patch or judge a failing rib will not remain one for long. Their occupation is more particular: they keep a crowded place from becoming only one thing.

An apprentice learns prohibitions before techniques. Never put an unseeing hand into a niche. Never assume an opening is empty because nothing moves in it. Look above before lowering a basket, and below before pouring water. Do not clear an inactive nest until certain it is inactive. Do not mistake quiet for safety; doves may go quiet because something has entered their tier. Only afterward come the mixing of plaster, the setting of pegs, and the hundred small ways of persuading heavy objects up and down without machinery.

The noise makes experienced keepers extraordinarily attentive listeners. One working on the floor can tell whether a flutter overhead is an ordinary quarrel or the beginning of a general alarm. A new drip is identified not only by its note but by where the vault returns that note. The oldest keeper often does the least visible work, sitting near a repair with a smoothing bone in hand, apparently idle, until remarking that a basket has begun to knock hollowly against a newly cracked ledge, or that one of the upper swift slots has gone silent too early in the season.

Their care is exact but not gentle in any simple sense. A spiderweb across an unused recess may remain for years; the same web across a ladder is removed before morning work. Beetles are left to clean old nest litter and killed when they enter stored grain. A fern growing from a cracked drain joint may cause the drain to be repaired around it, while the fig—far larger, far greener, and much more admired by visitors—is cut back without hesitation. A repair can wait half a season for a marked nest, but an unmarked colony spreading into a vent will be checked.

The doves are the most familiar tenants and the least impressed by human intentions. They are habituated, not tame. They will continue arranging nest stems while a keeper works within arm’s reach, but a hand inserted into their niche is answered with beak and wing. They steal cord, appropriate freshly swept ledges, land on plaster before it has hardened, and conduct long, resonant disputes over openings that appear identical to everyone except the birds involved. Some return to a niche after it has been rebuilt, inspect the alteration, and accept it. Others refuse it forever.

A few interfere so persistently that they acquire temporary names. Cord-Taker is one such name; it has probably belonged to several birds. The keepers are unsentimental about losses, but they notice absences. A niche that remains empty through the season becomes something to be investigated, not merely a space reclaimed.

The swifts belong less to the keepers than the doves do. Their narrow slots and high tiers put most of their affairs beyond reach, and their twice-daily passage through the central air establishes a boundary no work schedule can ignore. People stand back for them. A falling basket is dangerous, but a keeper moving foolishly through a returning stream of swifts can cause a greater commotion with much less effort.

When a swift is found on the floor, the response is quiet and practical. It is not tossed upward or encouraged to launch itself from brick. A keeper lifts it on a broad woven paddle, carries it back into height, and gives it the wall again. Whether it survives is usually unknown. The high colony does not repay such assistance with trust. At the approach of a ladder it simply withdraws deeper into brown darkness and continues its stitched, insistent calling.

The bats are known largely through their tubes, their dusk departures, and the tiny disturbances they leave behind. No sensible keeper probes an occupied tube with a finger. By day the bats seem less like animals than an additional warmth stored near the vents. At night they are glimpsed rather than followed: a brief interruption beneath a shaft, a turn near the lamp, nothing where the eye expects a body to continue. Their hunting reduces some of the small flying life around the upper brickwork, but the keepers do not justify their presence entirely by usefulness. The tubes were made habitable, and habitation followed. That is reason enough to repair a cracked tube when it can be done without tipping its occupants onto the floor.

Below them, the smaller inhabitants divide the chamber according to rules of their own. Spiders are tolerated according to geometry: whether their silk crosses a passage, a cord, a nest entrance, or only an empty darkness. Geckos take possession of ledges at dusk and of the warm brick near lamps after insects have gathered. Some know the precise limits of a keeper’s reach and flatten themselves just beyond it. An old gecko with a thickened, once-regrown tail may occupy the same seam beside an unfinished repair for much of a season, disappearing whenever plaster work resumes and returning as soon as the tools are quiet.

Crickets inhabit joints too narrow for any keeper to inspect. Beetles pass between spilled grain, feathers, droppings, and dead nesting material, janitors in one place and thieves in another. Nothing in the chamber is simply waste until several kinds of mouth have examined it.

The ochre frogs are often invisible even when their sumps are full. They shelter beneath stone lips and in the dark margins where the water does not quite cover the brick. A lamp may reveal tadpoles hanging like punctuation in the deepest basin, or a row of small eyes that vanish before they can be counted. The keepers’ strictest protection is reserved for these animals they seldom handle. The deepest sump is never emptied, however convenient that might make cleaning or repair. Its water may be dark, low, and uninviting, but it contains a continuity that cannot be restored merely by waiting for the next wet night.

After rain, the frogs’ apparent numbers become absurd. The keepers do not count them by their calls. They know the vault is multiplying a handful of animals into a population, and they know that by the following week most of the new puddles—and the chorus associated with them—will be gone.

The striped snake is usually spoken of simply as *the snake*, even when one individual disappears and another, differently marked, takes up the lower patrol. The singular name is useful because the keepers’ arrangement is with the office as much as with the animal. Below the second ledge, it hunts. It may take crickets, a gecko, or whatever else it can catch. Above that line, it is driven down with the reed.

This is neither reverence nor an admission of defeat. The keepers will protect a threatened nest when they can, and a nest reached through badly maintained masonry is considered partly a failure of maintenance. But they do not attempt to make the floor harmless. The snake is not allowed to turn the whole chamber into its hunting ground; neither are the birds allowed to make it entirely theirs. Above the line, human interference makes the architecture temporarily unfair. Below it, the snake usually has to manage without assistance.

Even the plants occupy negotiated territory. The fig may be the oldest continuous living presence in the chamber, though no keeper could prove that the present root is the same one an earlier generation first cut back. It persists by sending growth sideways toward the brightest shaft, and the keepers persist by removing enough of that growth to preserve the brick. They will not let it become a tree. They also have never quite succeeded in making it only a stump.

The ferns are smaller accidents. No keeper admits to encouraging them, yet repairs near a flourishing clump are sometimes performed with oddly small tools and unusual patience. Moss is treated less as vegetation than as a record of repeated wetness. Grass belongs to the brief abundance after rain and to the beaks that find it. The green residents have the least power in the Undercroft, but they are not outside its arrangements.

Nothing there is wholly wild, and almost nothing is fully domestic. The birds use human masonry without obeying human boundaries; the keepers depend on animal occupation without being able to prescribe its extent. A good season is not the one with the most doves, the most frogs, or the greatest growth of ferns. Too many doves mean fouled ledges, parasites, and falling nests. Too many webs make work impossible. Too much root lifts brick. Too much cleaning would leave the chamber sanitary, silent, and failing at whatever mysterious combination of purposes has kept it occupied for so long.

What lives in the Undercroft, in the fullest sense, is therefore not merely a collection of species but a crowded practice of accommodation. It is not harmonious. Nests fail. Beetles are crushed. Roots are cut. Keepers curse birds whose lives they have just gone to considerable trouble to protect, and the snake sometimes wins. The practice endures because no victory is allowed to become complete.

At midnight, when a drop strikes the white-worn cup of an old basin, each inhabitant receives a different event. The doves hear a disturbance and shift together. The night keeper looks upward, trying to locate the seam by the sound returned from the vault. A gecko remains motionless beside the lamp. The frogs, if they notice at all, notice only a tiny alteration at the margin of their world.

Then the separate noises resume. The keeper waits for the next drop, having learned, like everything else below, that living there is largely a matter of knowing when to move, when to keep still, and how much room to leave for something else.
