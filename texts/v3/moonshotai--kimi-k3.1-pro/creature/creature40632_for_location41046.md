## The Inhabitants of Vael

Life in Vael does not cover the mountain. It runs through it.

It follows the warm side of a pipe, the seep beneath a retaining wall, the updraft over an engine house. It spreads along ledges where droppings and windblown dust have accumulated, then stops abruptly at bare stone. Between one inhabited seam and the next there may be a kilometre in which nothing moves except condensate gathering at the lip of a leaf.

The first occupants are usually too small to see. Rust-colored films spread through warm fractures where sulfur and iron enter the water. Grayer growth collects around pipe intakes and in the lightless ends of cisterns. Where a leak persists, these films trap mineral dust; moss takes hold in the dust; fern spores lodge in the moss. A leak no wider than a needle can, given twenty years, produce a vertical garden.

Nothing about this process is gentle. Roots enter joints and force them apart. Nest material holds moisture against iron. Creepers find their way around door seals and into ventilation slots. The people who tend Vael’s gardens therefore spend nearly as much time cutting them back as encouraging them. To garden in Vael is to decide how much damage a habitat is worth.

The pale cistern salamanders are among the oldest residents of these made habitats. Most are no longer than a hand, though water wardens occasionally find old ones lying beneath an intake screen, thick as two fingers and faintly violet along the spine. Their eyes are small and black. The young are almost invisible until they move.

Some populations have been isolated for generations. Their ancestors may have arrived as eggs on wet rope, cleaning tools, or the boots of wardens transferring water stock from one district to another. When a cistern is emptied, the salamanders are gathered into covered vessels and counted out; afterward they are counted back. The count is not especially reliable. It merely makes people careful.

A newly cleaned cistern may contain no salamanders for years. An old one is seldom entirely still. Even in an abandoned district, a watcher beside the water may eventually see a pallid shape detach itself from the dark below the intake and rise toward some disturbance too slight to ripple the surface.

The engine moths are born elsewhere. Their larvae live in wet compost beneath the flue gardens and in the black root mats of old courts. The adults emerge with transparent wings marked by a few lead-colored veins. They seek warmth, and possibly something in the vibration of machinery; no two engine keepers agree on the matter. What is certain is that a generator which has run at the same temperature for twenty years will have its moth beams, its moth corners, its customary pale drift above the hottest housing.

The adults do not eat. They gather, mate, and die within the machine rooms. On cold mornings the floor beneath a flywheel may be paved with wings. These are swept into pans and carried to the gardens. Very little organic material in Vael is permitted to remain waste.

The small split-hooved grazers make paths between all these places. Their hooves have hard outer edges and softer central pads, equally useful on crystal ledges, worn masonry, and the raised rivets of iron stairs. They are not tame, but neither are they indifferent to people. They learn market hours, the location of kitchen refuse, and which garden gates are merely symbolic.

Their trails are constant revisions. A band may use the same ledge for years, then abandon it after a frost, a rock report, or some change no surveyor can identify. They do not foresee alterations in distance, as romantic accounts from lower cities sometimes claim. They simply walk the mountain every day. Their map is newer because their hooves remake it nightly.

Surveyors therefore watch them without sentimentalizing them. A splitfoot trail is evidence, not instruction. It states where an animal went recently. It does not promise that a person carrying a chain can follow.

The great gliders, called bellwings in most districts, occupy the upper levels of the same network. They are dark above and pale beneath, so that when they bank over the cloud gulfs they seem alternately to vanish and flash into existence. Their nests are bulky constructions of root fiber, shed hair, insulation, stolen cord, and wire. Condenser crews have learned to inspect a rookery after the disappearance of small tools.

Bellwings take moths on the wing, scavenge around markets and slaughter yards, and forage along lower cliffs. Their droppings, together with discarded food and the remains of unsuccessful nests, create some of the richest ledge soils in Vael. The greenest patch beneath an engine house is often directly below its least approachable masonry.

An active rookery complicates maintenance. Fins must be cleaned around nests, hot surfaces screened, and young gliders prevented from entering cable runs. Occasionally a crew keeps one bank of an otherwise obsolete condenser warm until the last young have left. This is not recorded as sentiment. It is entered as the cheaper alternative to having the entire rookery relocate into a cable station.

The small predators live where these routes intersect. They have several local names and no accepted census. Mostly they are known by eyes at a cable anchor, a disturbance among nesting bellwings, or the remains of a young splitfoot beneath an iron stair. They appear to hunt and scavenge opportunistically, and their ranges extend well beyond municipal space. Tracks have been found at both ends of routes that no longer joined. This has done nothing to make their taxonomy clearer.

Against all this occupancy, human Vael can seem provisional. Yet people are the greatest makers of habitat on the mountain. Their leaks water it, their exhaust warms it, their machinery gathers its moths, and their refuse feeds its courts. Even an unsuccessful foundation may become useful: a sheltered basin, a rookery wall, a place where dust can deepen into soil.

The human inhabitants have habitat preferences of their own. They cluster near reliable heat, covered water, and a long-running engine. Silence is not always comforting. In an outer ward, the absence of mechanical sound can make a street feel less like a street and more like a place merely resembling one.

Most residents combine several occupations. An engine keeper may also dress bearings, tend an exhaust garden, and serve on a survey chain. A mason may know cistern law better than a magistrate. Addresses are given by practical references—Second Cistern, the west bore, above Repack Four—as often as by street names. Private events are dated in the same way: a birth two winters before the bridge bearing was replaced; a marriage in the year the western stair acquired its additional landings.

Water customs are exact without being ceremonial. Clean water is not poured away for show. Wash-water goes to roots. A visitor who leaves a cup untouched may cause more quiet distress than one who arrives unexpectedly. In the washing halls, people clean themselves and their clothes with methods developed to make each stage of dirtiness useful somewhere else.

Children learn the survey rules as games before they understand them as warnings. They count landings, recite pressure-door sequences, and memorize routes in short repeated verses. Many work songs use the same structure: a phrase can be interrupted by a compressor stroke or a shouted measurement and resumed without losing the verse.

Beyond the last anchor, that habit of resumption becomes practical. One member of a survey pair remains with the reference point while the other pays out chain. They speak at intervals, not continuously, and describe ordinary things—the condition of a boot, the number of steps, a bird’s nest, what will be eaten on return. The companion’s task is not simply to wait. It is to help keep the route back an experience shared by two people rather than a private recollection.

Vael’s residents are not calm about every irregularity. They close roads. They abandon houses. They argue fiercely over whether a report has been exaggerated or minimized. But they distrust heroism. A traveler who goes beyond the maintained network alone is not considered brave so much as potentially expensive to recover.

Nor do they agree about the engines. Some speak of vibration, some of repeated measurement, some of the expectations of everyone who has ever crossed a street and found it where it should be. The arguments can continue through an entire maintenance shift. At the end, the same bearings are greased and the same flywheels kept turning.

When people leave a district, the other inhabitants do not necessarily follow. The moths disperse as the machinery cools. The splitfoots remain until the browse is cropped out. Bellwings may use a cold array for another season, then another, while deep heat keeps a sheltered garden alive beneath it. Cistern pales persist longest of all. Survey parties use the word *occupied* for such places. *Inhabited* is reserved for districts to which someone can still reliably return.

At the edges, even occupancy becomes difficult to interpret. Fresh nest material has been found on two cliffs that a route described as one. Grazing sign has appeared in a quarry closed for a season. There may be ordinary explanations: an unrecorded carrier, a repeated face mistaken for the same face, a door left open in error. The outer surveys record the evidence without choosing the most satisfying explanation.

Animals are not immune to uncertainty. Their trails are valuable because they are recent, not because they are infallible.

At the longest civic interval, all these separate lives briefly enter the same human schedule. The flywheels come into rhythm. People pause with a hand on a doorframe or rail, feeling the concussion through iron and bone. The splitfoots raise their heads. Bellwings fold their wings along the warm condensers. In covered cisterns, the pales sink away from the faint vibration in the water.

No one pretends that this makes the inhabitants of Vael one society. Afterward, the predators will hunt, the roots will continue opening stone, and the water wardens will still quarrel with the gardeners. But for a short interval, their routines overlap.

Surveyors measure Vael with chains. Its other inhabitants measure it by return: moth to engine warmth, grazer to ledge, bellwing to condenser, worker to doorway, warden to the same dark cistern.

The city is not merely the stone contained within its anchors. It is the collection of places to which something living can come back.

During the common beat, all those private surveys seem, for a little while, to describe the same city.
