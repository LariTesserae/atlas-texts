Most of the Held Sea’s population lives inside its country.

The warm capillaries in the basalt contain more living matter than the open water above them. Single cells and short, colorless chains occupy spaces too narrow to admit a visible film, oxidizing sulfur and iron, repairing themselves against pressure, and dividing only when the local chemistry permits. Others live on their metabolic leavings. Some pass their entire existence within a few grain-widths of mineral, neither dormant nor exactly active, but persistently alive at a rate that would be difficult to measure from outside.

There are animals even in the fractures. Most are smaller than a grain of rice: flattened, many-jointed creatures that can extend themselves through a narrowing crack and then lock their bodies against a pressure pulse. They have no eyes. A few possess no mouth in the usual sense, only a row of absorbent surfaces pressed against the rock. One may spend its life within several metres of stone while its food, wastes, and offspring are exchanged with the whole sea through moving water.

The piston rhythms reach these animals as weather. So do the timing pulses in the bedrock. A station shutdown, if one occurred, would not initially mean silence or darkness to them. It would mean that a pressure which had always arrived had failed to arrive.

The exposed films are thinner still. Grazing and circulation remove them almost as quickly as they form, so a populated ledge looks precisely like a barren one. There are no blooms in the water. The engines carry away concentrated cells and dissolved nutrients before either can gather into a local abundance. The sea’s clarity is therefore misleading. Its water is not sterile; its food is merely too sparse and too evenly dispersed to cloud it.

Nothing native to the Held Sea lives in the air. No animal comes to the surface to breathe, and there are no flying hunters, floating breeders, or colonies on the clear polar ice. The surface is an upper boundary crossed by weather, not a habitat. Even the largest animals remain below it.

### The combs

The hand-length combs are the animals most likely to be encountered in open water, though “likely” can still mean one brief appearance in a day.

A comb has no head worth distinguishing. Its transparent body surrounds a narrow gut and a nerve cord; the rest is mostly water held in shape. Its silica paddles are visible only when they turn at an angle to the light. The animal’s flash is reflected daylight, nothing more. Below the reach of daylight, it does not flash at all. No comb makes light of its own.

The paddles are used less for swimming than for exact adjustment. A comb turns itself across a current, rises into a shear layer, or allows itself to be carried backward while filtering. The engines have already supplied the motion. The animal’s task is to spend as little as possible resisting it.

Its whole body is a crude pressure ear. Differences in vibration along the nerve cord allow it to distinguish the direction of a station, the echo of a major scarp, and the approach of a turbulent boundary. Different basin populations have slightly different paddle rhythms and body stiffness, each suited to the local combination of current and sound. They do not need to recognize these differences as differences. A comb carried into an unfamiliar basin simply behaves badly there: it feeds at the wrong angle, turns too late, and is more likely to enter an intake or an acoustic antinode from which its tissues cannot escape uninjured.

Their young are shed into the circulation in numbers that would seem wasteful anywhere else. Almost all disappear. A few remain within the same fixed gyre for their entire lives, not because they have chosen a home but because the water has chosen one for them. A population can thus be ancient and local without any of its members ever deliberately returning.

An intake takes many. The same stations that distribute their food also gather and destroy them, indifferently. Around an outflow there may occasionally be enough combs to produce a faint graininess in a shaft of light. By the standards of the Held Sea, this is a crowd.

### The grazers and the station animals

The cliff grazers are more substantial, but not necessarily easier to notice. Their pale silica shells are divided into irregular, angular plates. When their jointed limbs fold inward, the seams close and the animal becomes another fresh-looking chip on a fracture lip.

Some are no larger than a thumbnail. The oldest open-cliff individuals can be several metres across, though an animal of that size may be the only grazer in an entire valley. It feeds by pressing a fine rasping surface against the rock and removing life at nearly cellular thickness. It does not excavate the basalt or produce grit. After it passes, the ledge is still bare, sharp, and black.

A grazer’s range is often determined less by food than by tolerable sound. Permanent antinodes can injure its soft joints; narrow cancellation bands provide routes along the cliffs. Its long circuit may therefore look inexplicable to an observer following the visible terrain. It crosses apparently rich fractures without stopping, then travels far along an unpromising edge because that edge lies within a survivable interference pattern. When the limbs close, the shell helps it clamp down and wait out pressure that would otherwise tear it loose.

They grow slowly and reproduce rarely. The young are lighter, softer, and more transparent than the adults. Currents carry them away from the parent’s fracture; a very small number settle where an unoccupied grazing route can support them. There is no competition for visible territory because there is no visible territory to defend. Two grazers can pass on opposite sides of the same slab and never encounter one another.

The stations support their own sparse versions of this life. Small, flattened grazers attach to the cool outer steel of intake towers and heat exchangers. Some close their shells in phase with a pump stroke. Others live only on the leeward sides of rivets and braces, where cavitation is slightly less severe. None forms a visible encrustation. A cable remains a cable, a gauge remains legible, and a lamp’s cage remains bare metal.

The lamps attract nothing. Food arrives with current, not light, and no native animal uses a filament as a landmark when the station’s sound can be sensed through its whole body. A caged service lamp may burn beside an intake for years without gathering so much as a halo of combs.

### Baskets and black hunters

The basket-bodied filterers look less like animals than like a way of holding water still.

A basket is an open tensioned body: pale silica ribs, transparent connective tissue, and broad spaces through which the current passes. Some are tens of metres long, but there is little dense flesh in them. They have no projecting head and no obvious limbs. Fine adjustments in the ribs alter their resistance to the water. By making those adjustments over hours or days, a basket can remain almost motionless relative to a fixed current while travelling immense distances with it.

This is why they appear moored. One may hang beside an escarpment with no cable, root, or attachment, its orientation changing by less than a body width over a season. A chart that treats a large basket as a stationary obstruction can remain useful for generations, even though the obstruction is alive and will eventually die.

The mesh retains microbial cells, comb larvae, and small organic fragments. Larger combs can usually back out. The basket does not pursue food or create a feeding current of its own unless damaged. Its whole economy depends on the engines continuing to bring clear water through it at the expected rate.

The black hunters live at the places where such expectations intersect.

They are lean, narrow animals with small eyes and bodies patterned by long pressure-sensitive folds. Most remain near a particular meeting of currents, or just beyond an acoustic barrier where disoriented animals emerge. A hunter may occupy the same few body lengths of water for years. It has no nest and usually touches no bottom.

It does not hear prey as a separate sound above the engines. That would be impossible. Instead, it detects changes in a familiar field: a comb momentarily altering an echo, a basket distorting a shear boundary, a wounded animal failing to hold its accustomed position. The hunter waits with most of its body slack. When it attacks, stored tension is released in one short movement. There is no prolonged chase. Either the current and the strike have placed food within reach, or the hunter returns to waiting.

They do not hunt in packs. Two using the same intersection generally occupy different depths or feed on different prey. Their blackness is conspicuous only in the upper daylight; at depth, their more important invisibility is behavioral. For almost their entire lives, they do nothing that requires them to be noticed.

### The hushbacks

The hushbacks are organized around a different possibility: not making a signal within the roar, but altering the roar itself.

The largest exceed a hundred metres, though much of that size consists of resonant architecture rather than dense muscle. Long cavities in the skull and flanks are divided by flexible septa and lined with tissues whose tension can be changed with great precision. A hushback can tune these structures to absorb narrow frequency bands. Its body is therefore both an ear and an acoustic shadow. It hears the industrial spectrum in extraordinary detail, and it can remove selected parts of that spectrum from the water around itself.

In ordinary daylight, one may appear simply as a long gray-white body with a blunt head and slow, economical movements. It feeds by opening a seam-like mouth across a prey-bearing current and remaining there for weeks or months, straining combs and other small material. Its size does not indicate a correspondingly huge appetite. Much of the animal is chamber, membrane, and support, and its circuits between feeding grounds can take years.

Those circuits are not migrations in a seasonal sense. They are repetitions within a fixed geography.

A hushback knows stations as distinct voices and scarps as characteristic returns. It can signal by thinning one band, restoring it, then thinning another in a measured sequence. To an unequipped listener, the effect might resemble a slight irregularity in otherwise overwhelming machinery. To another hushback, it can carry identity, position, reproductive condition, or an answer to a previous pattern.

They do not travel in pods. Two animals maintaining a long exchange may be on opposite sides of a basin and may never see one another. A reply can arrive when the first animal has already moved on. Closeness, for them, is not primarily a matter of distance between bodies. It is the ability to recognize and answer an alteration in a shared sound.

Calves must learn to control their resonant cavities. Until then, they remain near an adult, often within a silence they did not create, and become disoriented when the full engine band returns. Regional signal patterns are also learned rather than simply appearing with maturity. This gives hushbacks something comparable to dialect. Their longest exchanges have no obvious immediate function. Whether these are memory, courtship, or a form of history is not evident from outside. They leave no artifact with which to settle the question.

If a hushback has a name, it is not a sound. It is a characteristic way of making one part of a station less audible.

Other animals do not necessarily experience a passing hushback as shelter. Some combs turn away when a familiar orientation band disappears. Hunters may stop feeding until their acoustic surroundings become legible again. At close range, the absence can be so spatially distinct that the surrounding pressure seems to acquire a near side and a far side, but this is not universal peace. It is a temporary alteration of the map.

The hushbacks themselves show no special understanding of the stations as manufactured objects. They do not tend them, defend them, or treat them as anything other than fixed and dangerous features of the world. An intake is to be avoided. A generating hall is a voice with a particular harmonic structure. The purpose of the machinery is irrelevant to the use they make of it.

When a hushback dies, there is usually no witness. The body sinks into clear water, and somewhere a familiar pattern fails to recur. The band it used to thin remains present. The roar fills the space where an answer had been.

### Lives without succession

None of these animals builds a shelter that outlasts its body. There are no nests, shell middens, burrow plains, or reefs begun from old skeletons. A carcass is a brief concentration of food, not the foundation of a new habitat. Tissue is scavenged, cells are returned to circulation, and the remaining hard parts stay isolated on bare rock or are carried away.

This sparsity is not the appearance of an ecology still recovering from a catastrophe. A valley with one grazer is not waiting for more. A clear current is not an empty stage awaiting plankton. A ledge without a visible mat is not uncolonized. Each is already inhabited at approximately the rate its energy supply permits.

Life has not stopped changing, either. Paddle rhythms, shell chemistry, pressure tolerance, and hushback resonance differ between basins and continue to be refined. But adaptation does not lead toward visible transformation of the surroundings. The animals fit the fixed geography; they do not remake it. The only ecosystem engineers large enough to alter the Held Sea are the engines.

That dependence is total. For nearly every animal, the industrial roar is not a recent disturbance imposed on an older natural quiet. It is the condition under which its senses, feeding habits, and routes developed. A stopped station would first be a vanished landmark, then a failed food supply, and only later a change in the water itself. Silence would not restore the sea to them. It would deprive them of the world by which they orient.

A visitor could therefore hang above a populated fracture and see almost nothing: black glass, one pale chip that has moved since the last inspection, a comb turning once in the light. Farther out, a basket holds its apparent mooring. A black hunter remains folded at a current boundary. Then a hushback passes somewhere beyond clear sight, and one engine band goes missing. The grazer stops, not because peace has arrived, but because an edge of its world has briefly disappeared.

The band returns. The grazer resumes. Nothing has been added to the ledge.

The engines speak continuously; almost everything alive listens. The hushbacks answer by removing a little of what has been said.
