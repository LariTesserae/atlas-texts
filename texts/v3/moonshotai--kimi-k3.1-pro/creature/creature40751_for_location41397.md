What lives here does not fill the chamber. It makes a few slight alterations to its surfaces.

The open water has no visible population. Nothing crosses from wall to wall, nothing hangs suspended in the pale volume, and nothing rises from the floor to investigate a disturbance. Life keeps to stone and sediment. Even near the light, where it is most evident, it appears less as growth than as a subdued change in color: olive where the limestone would otherwise be pale, a darkening on an upper face, a low softness in a crack.

The algal films are the slightest of these presences. Some are thin enough that they can hardly be separated visually from the rock beneath them. They occupy the surfaces that receive the most indirect daylight, spreading not in lush sheets but in discontinuous, patient coverings. Under the gray-green water their color has no brilliance. They photosynthesize without producing any visible bustle, taking what the weak illumination and sparse dissolved materials allow. A film may extend by an almost invisibly small amount, then lose cells elsewhere. Its boundary remains apparently fixed, not because nothing happens there, but because gain and loss are both so slight.

The mosses have more substance. They form low, close patches near the brightest wall, their small shoots crowded together rather than drawn out into trailing growth. Sediment lodged in irregularities gives them a little purchase. Nothing in the water bends them or combs them in one direction; they have no current to answer. Their stillness is therefore complete but not rigid. They are living tissue held in cold water, growing slowly enough that their shapes seem geological. Each patch has an edge, but it is not an edge one could watch advancing.

In a few cracks, short algal filaments occupy the narrow space between the mosses and the films. They do not stream outward or connect the patches. They remain where fine grains can collect and where light can still reach. Together, all these green things make a loose map of illumination. Where the light weakens, they thin. Beyond it, the rock returns to bare granularity, vein, and pit.

The sediment holds the less visible portion of the ecology. It is not a rich bed, and nothing turns it over. No burrows interrupt its surface; no feeding tracks cross it; no buried body alters it from within. But among the fine grains, microbial cells make use of what little organic material becomes available: remains of the attached growth, minute contributions from elsewhere in the aquifer, substances already several times transformed. Decomposition proceeds at the pace permitted by cold and scarcity. Some constituents return to the water; some remain among the grains. The process neither clouds the silt nor gives it the appearance of fertility.

This is the chamber’s small cycle, though it is not perfectly closed. The fissures remove a little and supply a little. Cells grow, die, and are broken down. Material changes hands without ever accumulating into abundance. The green surfaces provide the sediment with almost nothing; the sediment provides almost nothing in return. Almost nothing, continued for a very long time, is enough to maintain the sparse life that exists.

There is consequently very little behavior here. Life has metabolism, sensitivity, growth, and death, but no searches, pursuits, migrations, or encounters that could be watched unfold. Nothing grazes the films into patterns. Nothing shelters beneath the slab. Nothing responds to the machinery’s note. The ecology does not present itself as a sequence of events. It presents itself as a condition.

That condition can be mistaken for suspended animation, but the distinction matters. The stones persist by changing very little. The living patches persist by replacing themselves. A patch may look identical over a long interval without being composed of quite the same cells, just as the clear water can remain clear while water and minerals continue their minute exchange. The visible sameness of the chamber is not an absence of processes. It is processes held within extremely narrow limits.

The machinery belongs to those limits without belonging to the ecology. Its pumps and heat exchangers do work elsewhere; their labor arrives here as cold, pressure, and a low vibration carried through limestone. To the mosses and films, this is not a message or a disturbance. It has no more evident meaning than the calcite veins in the wall. It is simply part of the physical constitution of their world. The industry does not cultivate them, protect them, or know them. They continue within conditions produced for other purposes.

No caretaker visits the patches. No population depends on the chamber’s being recognized as a habitat. Its life is neither a remnant of some richer community nor a promise of one to come. There is no evidence that fish once used the open volume, that animals once worked the floor, or that the present sparseness is a loss. The chamber is not waiting to be recolonized. It is not recovering. Its poverty of life is an old, stable expression of light, cold, stone, and available material.

And yet it is inhabited.

Its inhabitants amount to a few low olive cushions, films almost indistinguishable from discoloration, short filaments in sheltered cracks, and microscopic cells dispersed through sediment that looks undisturbed because they are too small and too few to disturb it. They do not give the room a center. They do not animate its emptiness. They remain marginal even within their own home.

But they are the only things here that maintain themselves by making more of themselves. The limestone rests near equilibrium; the regulated water circulates; the distant engines convert power into continuous work. The living films do something quieter and categorically different: they use a little light and a little matter to continue being alive, then to replace, almost exactly, what dies.

Near the pale opening, the mosses keep their small holds on the wall. The algal surfaces remain dark olive under the diffuse illumination. Below, the sediment receives and releases its minute supply. The pumps continue without variation. Nothing crosses the chamber, and nothing needs to. Its life is already where the light, the stone, and the cold allow it to be.
