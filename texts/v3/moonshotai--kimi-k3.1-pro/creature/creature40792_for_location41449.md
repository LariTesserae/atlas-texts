Nothing is hiding in the city.

No shy people withdraw into the towers when approached; no mineral sleepers lie folded beneath the cisterns; no solitary spirits keep the bridges. Such inhabitants would require a body that could enter a room and leave it, an inside and an outside, a life distinguishable from its shelter. What lives in the rift is not separable from the place in that way.

The basin trees are its most conspicuous lives. Their crowns stand along the rim and above the upper roof basins, but a crown is only the most easily seen portion of something whose outline cannot reliably be traced. Roots leave a trunk, enter the pale substance of a wall, divide behind galleries, and emerge tiers away as columns, conduits, or the woody cords of a bridge. A tree on the rim and a root-grown arch far below it may appear unrelated and yet be continuous by sap. There is no gap across which they are mysteriously united. Along every local stretch the connection is ordinary: root joins root, tissue passes into tissue. Only the whole body refuses to fit into a single shape.

No part of such a tree needs a map of the rift. A root surface responds to moisture, resistance, and dissolved mineral; a leaf responds to light, heat, and wet air. Each local relation is intelligible. The contradictions belong to an observer trying to assemble all those relations from outside.

The leaves are not waiting to become anything else. They are broad, mature working surfaces, held indefinitely at the fullness for which another forest would have a brief season. Their wax gathers water without letting it linger destructively; their veins return what their roots supply; their tissues are renewed without altering the outline of the leaf. There are no buds crowding the axils, no stored provision against cold or drought, no flowers offering color or scent to another kind of body. Nothing in them is an invitation. Their green is not display but work.

The window ferns have not colonized the city in the sense of arriving too late for its history. Fern and window are parts of the same persistent arrangement. The fern’s roots hold fine mineral where seepage emerges; the opening admits the light the fern uses; the thickened growth around that opening preserves the chamber’s shape. Moss performs a similar office along walls and balustrades. Its cushions are boundaries maintained between wet stone and dense air. Cells are replaced at their margins, but the margins do not advance. There is no pioneering moss and no climax moss, no bare surface awaiting its turn.

In the streams, the translucent algae are the clearest sign that clear water is not empty water. Their ribbons lie along the undersides of currents, neither swimming nor drifting, taking light where light reaches and exchanging dissolved substances with root films downstream—or upstream, according to an entrance that may also be an exit. Still finer lives occupy the wet mineral rind and the interiors of conduits: microbial layers too thin to cloud a pool or fuzz a step. They receive what leaves and roots release, break down stray organic matter, and return its substance to circulation. Nothing is left over long enough to become detritus.

At this scale, counting inhabitants becomes misleading. A trunk is not necessarily an individual. Neither is a pool, a root mat, or a ward.

Consider one of the returning streams. Its course links seepage in black stone, a moss cushion, the roots of a basin tree, a court pool, a ribbon of algae, a fall through several galleries, and at last the fissure from which its upper reach emerges. Beginning anywhere in that course is arbitrary. What persists is not a particular quantity of water but an entire closed exchange: leaf surface, root membrane, mineral boundary, flowing channel, and living film, each maintaining the conditions under which the others continue.

That circuit is closer to an inhabitant than any visible tree is.

It has no heart and no single seat of activity. It has no skin, though it has membranes and chemical boundaries. Its identity lies in the relations that remain satisfied: this substance retained, that one released; this pressure held in a conduit, that one dispersed in a fall; this quantity of moisture carried into foliage and returned through condensation. Some circuits are contained within a single court. Others extend through whole wards or include tissues visible on opposite sides of the gulf. They overlap without necessarily merging. One may share stone with another, exchange spores with a third, and pass water through a pool that belongs to several different continuities.

The city need not therefore be one vast organism. It may be many, though not many that can be counted by trunks or enclosed by walls.

Its architecture is the nonliving and partly living support of these continuities. Some of the pale ribs are old, mineralized tissue; others remain threaded with active root. Stone dust and deposit make them rigid, while dissolution keeps them from sealing the passages they contain. A cistern is not a vessel made for water so much as one of the shapes water, root, and crust can jointly sustain. A bridge is not primarily something to cross. It is a span that keeps separated portions of a living circuit in relation. A gallery is a region of shade, seepage, deposition, and return. The stairs belong to no one’s ascent; their regularity is the regularity of the growth and thickening that made them.

This does not make the life passive. A great deal is happening in every leaf, root surface, algal cell, and wet film. Matter is taken up, transformed, released, and taken up again. Spores travel through the circuits, but they are not heralds of coming forests. Where one enters an established tissue, its substance may contribute to renewal; where it cannot be received, it is broken down and returned to the exchange. Reproduction has ceased to produce expansion. It has become one of the forms of maintenance.

There is consequently no visible nursery and no visible graveyard. Cells end; their materials do not accumulate into a history. Roots remain vigorous without becoming exploratory. The great trees cast no seed beyond the rim because there is no unoccupied elsewhere toward which their lives are directed. Even competition has been reduced to the preservation of boundaries. Nothing is about to replace anything else.

Nor do these lives have habits in the animal sense. There is no hour at which the courts become active, no season of release, no sleep beneath the continuous roar. The heat is not an ordeal they endure, and the fixed daylight is not a prolonged morning to which they are temporarily responding. They have no need to anticipate cold, darkness, drought, pollination, or dispersal. Their physiology has rates, but no calendar.

The sound belongs to this activity without being its language. The falls are not announcements, and the resonant shafts are not voices. No circuit calls another through the white roar; nothing listens for an answering pattern. If vibration is registered in root and conduit, it is registered as pressure, one condition among others, not as music or meaning. The deafening city has no sonic social life. Its noise is circulation made audible.

Whether any of the greater circuits possesses experience is not something the place displays. There is no gesture from which to infer intention, no preference beyond the chemical preferences written into its form, no memory except the persistence of that form. Yet if anything here is aware, its awareness could not resemble a survey of the rift. No part stands apart from the whole to contemplate it. Such awareness would have to be distributed and entirely local: light continuing in a leaf, resistance continuing in stone, water returning through a passage, mineral crossing a root boundary. Not a thought about eternity. Not even, necessarily, a thought about self. It would contain no expectation of being interrupted and no knowledge that its geography was impossible. Impossibility is a judgment made from outside.

Nothing there is waiting to be discovered, because discovery would have no place in its continuation. Nothing is guarding the empty courts, because emptiness is a human description of rooms without tenants. To the lives of the rift, the courts are not vacant. They are open exactly as they must be for water, light, root, and mineral to maintain their relations.

So the city is neither abandoned nor secretly peopled. The basin trees are not residents planted beside its streets; the streets are among the things their rootedness does. The ferns have not taken possession of the windows; fern and window are two aspects of the same stable opening. The algae do not haunt the returning streams; their chemistry is part of the return. Even the oldest pale arcades, where no green tissue is immediately visible, remain involved in exchanges that have not ceased.

No one will appear on a parapet. No one will cross a bridge to meet a visitor. There is no hidden population whose absence explains the silence beneath the roar.

Nothing lives in the rooms. Life is what the rooms are doing.
