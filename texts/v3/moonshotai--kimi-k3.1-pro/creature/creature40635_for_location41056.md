### What Lives in the Ninth Sump

At first, each grove seems to contain several kinds of life.

The copper-coloured cushions appear to be one organism, something close and patient spread over the rock. The black canes seem to be another, rising through the cushions with their fans held above them. The ivory filaments on the pipe lagging look like a third, and the translucent tissues hanging underneath might be something else again: a root system, a reproductive membrane, or merely the groves’ decay.

But the distinctions become less certain the longer they are examined. Cushions thicken and put up canes. Canes return, through older tissue, to the same buried mats from which the hanging sheets descend. Ivory threads emerge along wet seams in those sheets and climb wherever a film of moisture makes climbing possible. A fan may outlive the cane that raised it; a cane may remain standing long after the root that sustained it has become mineral.

It is easiest to call each grove a colony. It may be more accurate to call it a body that has never needed to be compact.

Its organs are arranged around a chemical boundary. The pale root tissues spread where hot, iron-bearing seepage meets the cooler water delivered by the pipes. They do not simply absorb the seepage, nor do they feed directly on the engines’ condensate. They occupy the narrowing region where the two waters alter one another. Energy is available along that alteration, and the grove draws what it can before the boundary closes into scale.

Consequently, the wettest place is not always the most alive. A warm pocket may hold clear water and remain bare. A small, discoloured hollow beneath a joint may support growth so crowded that its separate surfaces cannot easily be distinguished. The grove needs water, but water alone is only permission to wait. It must also have the reaction beneath its roots.

Above that boundary, the different tissues perform their slow work.

The cushions receive the drips. Their surfaces are divided into thousands of shallow, overlapping cups, tough enough not to be broken by an ordinary falling drop. Water seldom shines among them for long. It darkens the copper tissue, passes into narrow intervals, and disappears downward. During dry periods the cups close and become hard at their edges. The whole cushion then looks less like living matter than a stain fired onto the basalt.

The canes rise by increments too small to deserve the word *growth* unless observed across years. A joint darkens, hardens, and acquires its fine mineral bloom. Another may not follow for a long time. Their flexibility is not used in the still air; there is no wind to move them. It allows them to survive the weight of water, the fall of a fragment of lagging, the slight shift of a pipe heating and cooling beside them.

The fans are not leaves in any useful sense. They do not turn toward the three lamps. A light brought close and moved around them produces no response. Their pleats open according to wetness, not illumination, and their overlapping arrangement has more to do with the paths of drops than with the capture of anything from the air. In some, the upper surfaces are almost soot-black; in others, aubergine gives way to umber at the margins. The undersides can be bone-pale. Where a fan has been repeatedly wetted and dried, its folds acquire a fine rust-coloured outline, as though each opening had been recorded.

The sheets beneath them are not traps. Nothing is caught in them except water, dust, and occasionally a tiny particle of their own tissue. A lamp behind one reveals a dense, uneven ramification of threads, some clear, some clouded with iron. The finest are too delicate to survive handling. The oldest have become rigid and remain in place only because the chamber offers so little to disturb them.

When a relay closes, none of this stirs.

The pump’s beginning belongs to the machines. The pipes cool, condensation gathers, and still the grove remains folded. Its response begins only when water actually reaches it, and then it proceeds in the order of arrival. One cushion darkens while another, an arm’s length away, stays dry. A root sheet takes on weight. The outermost pleat of a fan relaxes. Hours later, a second drop follows the same route more easily because the first has prepared it.

There is no general awakening. A wet cycle may belong to one circuit more fully than to another, so that one grove opens while a second merely dampens and a third receives nothing. Over several days, provided the roots have retained enough living tissue, the fans unfold. They do not spring apart. Their movement is closer to a gradual loss of resistance. The chamber’s smell becomes sharper near the wetted beds: wet iron, warm mineral tissue, and that abrupt vegetal note like celery cut with something medicinal.

Then the pipes warm.

The grove does not immediately close. It uses what water remains in its cushions and sheets, losing it unevenly. The highest fans fold first in some beds, the oldest first in others. Eventually the canes support only narrow, dark seams. The visible life becomes still again, though its roots may continue their almost undetectable work at the seepage boundary.

Reproduction is difficult to separate from extension. A new cushion close to an old one may not be a descendant at all, only another region of the same body becoming visible. Spores and fragments have no animal carriers and no useful wind. When they travel, they usually travel downward in water. Most fall within the grove that produced them. Most of those that reach bare basalt accomplish nothing.

A genuinely new grove requires a sequence of accidents: moisture must repeatedly find the same place; the place must lie close enough to suitable seepage; the first tissue must survive the intervals between wettings. After that, establishment may still look for years like nothing more than a copper discoloration around a fracture. The black canes come later, if they come at all.

The groves therefore differ less in species than in history.

In the oldest beds, living tissue stands on the intact remains of its former surfaces. Fallen fans lie whole beneath the canes, neither scattered nor chewed. Lower layers have collapsed into brittle, glassy laminae stained with iron. The bed is still growing, but it is also becoming a deposit. Its thickness is not a count of years. It is a record of periods in which the grove could afford to build tissue and later let it fall.

Near the second engine, the spanner has become an incidental part of such a record. Roots cross its handle, divide around the head, and meet again beyond it. They are not extracting anything purposeful from the metal, and there is no struggle in their embrace. The spanner simply presented a dry obstruction during one interval and a wet support during another. The grove has included it without changing its opinion of it.

Elsewhere, a bed may consist almost entirely of cushions with only a few stunted canes. It is impossible to say from appearance alone whether it is young or frequently deprived. Youth and scarcity produce similar forms here.

The dry lattice beneath the failed pipe is more difficult still. Its canes remain upright, its fans tightly folded. Some of the outer tissue has mineralized, preserving the arrangement with unreasonable fidelity. It may be dead. It may contain a living thread too deep and too inactive to declare itself. In the Ninth Sump, dormancy and death can share an appearance for years.

Nothing in the groves startles. Nothing withdraws from a footstep or turns toward a brought lamp. There are no burrows among the roots, no nests in the old fans, no small mouths opening in the warm pools. The absence of animals is not an incompleteness the life seems to strain against. Nothing requires pollination, dispersal by fur, or the clearing work of scavengers. Tissue falls where it grew. Water moves what water can.

Nor are the groves parasites upon the engines in any simple sense. They do not use the current arriving through the armoured cables. They have no access to the purpose for which the coolant still circulates. They take neither instruction nor nourishment from the machines’ intentions.

The engines are their weather.

A reliable circuit makes a reliable climate, and life gathers within its narrow outline. A weakening circuit produces a grove with folded margins and long intervals of dormancy. A failed circuit leaves architecture without rain. If an engine stops altogether, there is no immediate catastrophe. The last condensate drips. The remaining water is used or lost. The fans close, the cushions harden, and the grove continues to resemble itself while its edges slowly cease to be tissue.

Most of the chamber will never be inhabited. The broad, dry intervals of basalt are not territories awaiting conquest; they are simply places where the necessary coincidences have not occurred. Life in the Ninth Sump has not spread across the available floor. It has accepted a few small, repeatable conditions and remained answerable to them.

Somewhere beneath a cooling pipe, a drop strikes a copper cushion and vanishes.

There is no sound from the grove, no visible gratitude, no sudden opening. Days later, if enough other drops have followed, one fold in one black fan may relax by the width of a finger.

That is how the Sump shows that something lives there.
