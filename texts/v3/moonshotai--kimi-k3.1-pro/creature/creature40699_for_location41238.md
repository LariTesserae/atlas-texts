No keepers live among the machines, and none of the animals is waiting for one. The Level’s fauna is not a workforce that has forgotten its instructions. It is a crowded, reproductive, fallible community whose appetites happen to leave the world around it unchanged. None of them knows what a pump is for. Their maintenance is appetite seen from far enough away.

On the ceramic curbs and walking plates, the finest of them form the **rind**. An individual is usually no larger than a grain of rice, though some districts have broader kinds, almost the width of a fingernail. Each has a low, flexible shell divided into shallow facets and six or eight legs folded completely beneath it. Packed together, they look like casting texture. Only their breathing, and the occasional pale line where one shell slips under another, betrays them.

They feed with a ventral mouth softer than the glaze beneath them, drawing up condensation and the colorless films that collect where feed aerosols have settled. They cannot rasp away the intended surface. Even their oldest individuals leave no scratches.

The rind has no queen and no collective mind. Its apparent coordination comes from hundreds of similar responses occurring close together. A descending shadow makes each animal draw in its legs; pressure through the plate makes it sidle into the nearest vacancy. A foot comes down on bare ceramic while the texture stands in a narrow ring around it. When the foot lifts, the ring closes unevenly, some animals returning at once, others remaining still until a drop of water strikes nearby.

Their clear eggs are laid along wet molding ridges. The hatchlings shelter beneath larger shells, not because they are guarded, but because there is moisture there. Many are washed into channels and eaten. Enough remain beneath the same few square hand spans of ceramic to replace the adults that dry, are crushed, or wander into the mouths of other things.

The **lids** are easier to overlook and harder to forget once seen breathing. They occupy the warm sides of engines, cisterns, and pressure housings. Some are palm-sized; others are broad as shields. Their backs are matte gray or blue-black, divided by shallow creases, and their margins follow corners so closely that they appear to have been installed. Around those margins, however, the flesh is soft. A close observer can see it pulse.

A lid clings by eight short legs and a broad, muscular belly. Its mouth is underneath. Without fully releasing its hold, it can extend a translucent feeding tongue into seams and condensation traps, taking paste, rind animals, and the soft organic scale that would otherwise narrow an aperture. When it has absorbed too much heat, it detaches with a quiet wet release and walks to a cooler surface, often the base of an exchanger. There it flattens, blood spreading through the vessels just under its back, and remains until its body has cooled. Then it seeks warmth again.

They compete for favored housings. The contest is usually silent and almost motionless: two lids press margin to margin for hours, each gradually deforming the other. One may lose a strip of flesh and leak dark blood into the condensation. The wound sometimes heals as a pale scar; sometimes the animal weakens and is eaten before it can find another resting place.

Their young are born wrinkled and nearly colorless. For a while they remain beneath the mother, giving her outline a second, thicker gasket. Later they separate, inflate their backs with tissue fluid, and become small covers in their own right.

In the watercourses, the **braids** are not colonial animals, though they can be grasped, divided, and poured like one. Each member is a finger-length eel, dull pink or translucent gray, with a narrow red line of blood visible along its side. Individually, an eel swims badly in open water. It does best with other bodies against it, using their resistance to push itself forward while its tiny mouth works over skin, submerged ceramic, and the soft scale gathering there.

A braid has no head and no permanent membership. Animals continually move from its center to its surface and back again. At a grating, it narrows until the eels pass nearly single file; beyond the obstruction, it thickens. When one is injured, its neighbors may turn and feed on it without disturbing the general form. The water closes, the braid continues, and nothing about the channel records the loss.

They spawn in the shallow rectangular pans where the current is weakest. The eggs are clear, almost invisible except when they collect in the corner of a drain lip. Eels eat most of them. They also eat rind hatchlings, drowned fliers, and the tender young of their own kind. Their apparent diligence in keeping the watercourses open includes a great deal of ordinary predation.

The dark cores inside vent plumes are made of **vent flies**. Their larvae live as transparent commas in the pans, grazing beside the young eels and being grazed upon in turn. Adults emerge at wet edges and rise into the warm vapor, where they feed on the finest suspended nutrients. Each remains within a narrow band of temperature and moisture, adjusting constantly to avoid other wings. The swarm’s shape is the shape of the usable plume.

Warm rain beats them down by the thousands. Their bodies enter the same channels that carried their larvae upstream. Elsewhere, more adults are always emerging, so the plume never appears to diminish.

Above the digesters move the **sails**. These are the large, slow-winged animals, some with wings broader than a person’s outstretched arms. A sail’s body is surprisingly small: a compact knot of muscle, gut, and warm blood suspended between sheets of living membrane supported by fine cartilage. Beneath the body hangs a folded feeding organ. In flight it opens into a wide, delicate skirt on which nutrient aerosol condenses. Every few circuits, the sail draws the skirt into itself and licks it clean.

Their wingbeats are infrequent. Mostly they adjust a fingertip of cartilage, spill a little air, and continue around the same gyre. At roost they fold along cistern ribs and resemble bundled insulation until a side expands with breath.

Sails bear one young at a time on open thermal-buffer roofs. The newborn’s wings are heavy, wet, and useless. For days it lies against the parent’s body while the parent feeds it regurgitated concentrate and pulls it back from edges with a hooked fold of wing. Later, the young animal follows the adult into the gyre. The bond weakens as its flight strengthens. An old sail may eventually become too ragged or stiff to stay aloft; then it comes down into a service court, where the ground animals pay it little attention unless it can no longer move.

The pale animals standing flank to flank in the roofed courts are commonly called **sleepers**, although much of their stillness is simply rest, rumination, or watchfulness with the eyes half closed. A full-grown sleeper stands no higher than a person’s knee but may be broad as a low bed. It has six splayed legs, soft wide feet, and a heavy, naked back. Its head hangs below the shoulders, with small dark eyes and a mouth adapted to close over low paste outlets. Its skin is gray where thick, pink where folded, and always warm.

Sleepers take in more bulk feed than any other open-air animals. They also lick films from pans, swallow stranded eels without apparent notice, and drink steadily. Much of what they consume leaves them as a thin slurry that passes through court gratings into recovery ducts. Their broad flanks serve as temporary heat reservoirs. After feeding, they often stand against cooler exchanger walls until the warmth has drained out of them, then return to the group.

The group is not a disciplined herd. Animals arrive and leave singly. Some consistently seek the same companions; others prefer an edge. Young sleepers are often kept near the center, where the surrounding bodies shelter them from rain and wandering needles. Adults nudge them awake when feed begins to flow. They can be careful with their great weight, but they are not reliably gentle. Competition at an outlet may leave a young animal limping, and a startled sleeper can crush a smaller creature against a housing without ever appearing to notice.

Rind animals live on their backs, feeding on dried paste and loosened skin. Vent flies gather around the moisture of their breath. None of this is cooperation. The sleeper tolerates what does not hurt it, and the smaller animals take what they can reach.

Close up, the courts smell faintly of warm hide and living bodies. The sound is mostly breath, the soft compression of feet, and water moving beneath the floor.

Below the walking plates live the **needles**: narrow, hairless hunters, usually no longer than a forearm, with eyeless heads and whiskers long enough to test a clearance before the body enters it. They can flatten until they seem to have no spine at all. Their claws tick faintly on cable trays. They follow vibration toward concentrations of rind, take young eels from open ducts, and consume feed that has leaked into places too confined for larger animals.

Needles are solitary except when mating or carrying young. A exposed needle remains motionless for a moment, its whole skin appearing to listen, and then pours itself toward the nearest dark aperture. They are fallible hunters. Some starve after chasing prey into a gallery whose temperature they cannot tolerate. Others are found by renderers while still warm, trapped beneath a deck they had entered easily when smaller.

The **renderers** are present almost everywhere but become visible mainly around death. At rest they resemble wrinkled, bone-white knots packed into sump walls and drain recesses. Each is a flattened ring of jointed legs surrounding a mouth. When carrion chemistry reaches them, they unfold and follow it.

They do not cooperate, though dozens may work over the same body. They spread clear digestive fluid, drink the softened tissue, and carry what they swallow into the collection system. Most Level animals have little true bone; their support comes from flexible shell, pressured tissue, and wet cartilage. Even these materials eventually soften. A dead eel may vanish within its braid. A dead rind animal disappears beneath its neighbors. A sleeper takes longer, sometimes days, and for that time its court contains an unusual concentration of small, purposeful movement.

The renderers’ judgment is chemical, not infallible. Occasionally they begin on an animal that is merely immobilized. It may wake convulsively and scatter them, surviving with raw patches in its skin. It may not. The Level corrects neither mistake.

Other sleepers sometimes investigate a dead companion, nudging it or standing beside it until the smell of feed draws them away. Whether this is recognition, confusion, or something that deserves to be called grief is not evident. The event can occupy an individual completely. It does not become a story retained by the court.

Across the continent, these convenient names divide into many local kinds. The permanent-rain provinces have long-legged rind that feeds beneath a continuous water film and lids with broad sucking bellies for vertical exchangers. Sails in the white condensation banks are often so pale that only their feeding skirts remain visible. In hotter, deeper districts, the sleepers’ place is taken by longer, lower animals that rarely stand in groups and spend much of their lives half immersed in pans. These are not new adaptations spreading through the Level. They belong to its old, stable inventory, as locally fixed as the weather.

The Level’s unsettling youth does not extend to individual bodies. Animals age. A lid’s legs splay and its old scars pucker when it breathes. A sleeper may lose an eye, grow wrinkled around the mouth, and take longer each time it rises from rest. A sail can spend much of its adult life compensating for a wing torn when young. Nothing restores them to specification. Renewal means replacement, not repair.

Nor is the equilibrium a moment-by-moment peace. A pan may temporarily fill with larvae. A failed feed outlet may leave one patch of ceramic bare while animals crowd another. A hot housing can kill every lid attached to it. A court may hold too many young sleepers for a while, and hunger can make them push, injure, and drive one another out. Individuals vary, learn routes, form preferences, make mistakes, and suffer the consequences.

What does not happen is accumulation. Excess animals meet finite feed, exposed apertures, predation, or temperatures their bodies cannot survive. Empty places are entered from adjacent crowds. Dead tissue returns through the sumps. No successful behavior becomes the foundation of a later abundance. No injury becomes a ruin. The narrowness of the world absorbs every deviation before it can become a direction.

To stand quietly among them is to discover how much event can be contained in apparent stillness. The rind closes around a shadow. Somewhere nearby, a lid releases its hold and begins its slow walk toward cooler metal. A braid divides at a grating. High overhead, a sail folds its feeding skirt and turns again above the same digester. In a court, a young sleeper presses harder into the warm flank beside it. Farther down, something dead is being softened into what will feed something not yet born.

None of these lives points beyond itself. That does not make them less animal, or less tender, or less brutal. The Level remains the Level not because nothing within it changes, but because hunger, rest, birth, injury, affection, and death have all become parts of the same unchanging fullness.
