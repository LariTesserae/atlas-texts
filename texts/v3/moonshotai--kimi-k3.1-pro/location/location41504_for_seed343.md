## The High Thatch

The highest country in the region is also the flattest.

From the basins below, the High Thatch appears as a dark, nearly level line above the weather. Clouds accumulate beneath it. Storms break against its sides. On clear days, the lowland rivers can be seen from its rim as pale threads laid across a country so distant that their movement has been swallowed by scale.

Yet once the rim is crossed, there are almost no heights. The summit is a vast, shallowly undulating plain, days across, with long views interrupted only by isolated stands of vegetation. Its mountains were worn smooth before the whole region was lifted. What remains is less a range than the exposed stump of one: ancient pale stone, rounded shoulders, drainages so old and broad that they no longer seem to flow anywhere. There is little scree and almost no exposed fracture. Even the great cliffs at the edge have softened into convex buttresses and long, root-choked slopes.

The land has not stopped changing, but its changes no longer alter its outline.

Above that geological body lies a skin of plants. Nearly everything visible is alive or was recently alive: the springy surface underfoot, the low domes, the vast fluted stalks rising at intervals across the plain, the curtains hanging down the cliffs. Bare stone appears mainly at the rim, and even there it is freckled with crusts and threaded through by roots. What looks like soil is not soil but old leaf bases, root felt, fine animal waste, and the compacted remains of earlier generations of the same covering.

It is tempting to call the Thatch a forest until one crosses it. A forest implies crowding. Here there is almost nothing in the middle scale: few shrubs, little tangled understory, no accumulation of trunks and branches to obstruct the view. There is the continuous living floor, and there are the masts—enormous plants with hollow, fluted stems and crowns of stiff, spoon-shaped leaves—and between them, extraordinary intervals of air.

A traveler may walk for hours toward a mast stand without its apparent size changing. In the other direction there may be no upright object at all, only the grey-green floor rising gently toward the sky. Most of the landscape’s volume is vacancy. Its openness is not the openness of grassland, exactly, because the ground is thick, architectural, and full of concealed depth. It is more like walking across the roof of an enormous, sparsely furnished building.

In places the roof really is hollow. Roots and old leaf bases have bridged ancient depressions without filling them, leaving galleries beneath the surface. Some are cramped and wet; others open into high, dim halls whose ceilings are supported by a few living arches. Almost nothing loose litters them. Material is quickly carried away, consumed, or incorporated. Warm air rests in the deeper chambers. Drops fall from root tips with long intervals between them. A footstep overhead arrives as a dull, wandering concussion.

The Thatch does not look wet enough to support itself. There are no substantial rivers on top, and permanent open water is rare. Rain comes irregularly, often in isolated cells that drench one basin while leaving the next untouched. For much of the time, the exposed surfaces are merely cool and leathery, and the air above them can be dry enough to roughness the throat.

But the whole country is arranged to intercept small amounts of water and keep them. At night the high leaves cool enough to gather condensation. Dew runs down their veins into fluted stems. Fine root nets comb moisture from clouds that brush the rim. Broad cup-plants hold a few buckets or a few thousand liters, according to their size, each tank darkened by tannins and crowded with life. After rain, brief streams follow old drainage lines for an hour or two before disappearing into hollow stalks, root cisterns, and deep cracks in the stone.

Thus the surface can appear dry while, a few body-lengths below, everything is damp. The scent changes accordingly: crushed stems and dry fiber in the open; wet pepper, old rain, and the ammoniac sharpness of insect colonies under the mat.

The days are warm rather than hot. Rising air from the lowlands and heat stored in the dark living floor soften the altitude’s cold, though the nights grow cool enough to bring dew. The light itself is ordinary. No foliage glows; no colored haze hangs permanently between the masts. At noon the shadows are short and nearly black. Rain turns the world grey. Morning and evening gild the plants in the usual way. The country’s strangeness has to endure plain daylight.

Then there are the animals.

Most of the time, very few are visible. A handful of small things move along a stem. Something long vanishes beneath a leaf. A distant browser, tall and grey, crosses an interval between mast stands with birds clustered along its back, and for hours afterward nothing larger than a hand appears.

But the stillness is visual only. A palm pressed firmly to the thatch feels ticking, scraping, and minute impacts. The root chambers contain larvae beyond counting. Every water cup holds its own crowded suspension of swimmers, hunters, eggs, and transparent things feeding on one another. Wings and folded limbs occupy the hollow stems. Small creatures inhabit the leaf axils in layers, each layer preying on or parasitizing another.

The loudest are the drumflies. Their young develop in the wet root felt, feeding on sap and decay. As adults they gather in the masts, where their mating calls are reinforced by the hollow chambers of the stems. A chorus begins with isolated dry notes, like seeds striking pottery. Other stands answer. The calls multiply until distance stops helping; a mast several kilometers away can seem to be sounding directly behind the listener.

At full chorus, speech is useless. A shout at arm’s length disappears. The sound becomes a pressure in the teeth and breastbone, and the thinner leaves can be seen trembling with it. Even between choruses there is rarely silence: cup frogs boom after rain, stems tick as they warm, wings pass in dense soft gusts, and somewhere there is always the dry labor of mandibles.

The masts benefit from the uproar. Their flowers often open during the choruses, when vibration shakes pollen loose and sends collected dew down the stem flutes. The drumflies carry pollen and spores between stands. Other flying animals follow the flowering, and predators follow them. Noise, water, pollination, and hunting have become parts of the same mechanism.

The animals are not merely residents of the vegetation. They are its circulation. On stone so old and a surface with so little true soil, nutrients cannot be left lying where they fall. Insects carry them into roots. Fliers transport them between isolated cups. Scavengers dismantle carcasses and distribute the pieces through the mat. Tiny aquatic animals are stranded, eaten, or absorbed when their pools shrink. Almost nothing is allowed to remain useless for long.

When rain does cross the interior, the concealed abundance becomes visible all at once.

First the floor darkens. Shallow pools gather in the oldest hollows, reflecting an ordinary grey sky. The masts begin to drip from heights, each crown releasing separate curtains of water. Then come the emergences: winged insects lifting in such numbers that they resemble smoke without a fire; frog-like animals climbing from root chambers toward the filled cups; flocks of flying grazers arriving to feed on fresh pollen and new growth.

For a short time the open country can be crowded. Wings pass in rivers with clear air between them. Hunting birds and long-jawed gliders fold into the swarms. Serpentine climbers ascend the cup-plants. Large browsers move toward softened shoots, their footfalls booming through hollow sections of the floor. The frogs and drumflies call over one another until the rain itself is only one component of the noise.

Then the shower passes. The temporary streams vanish. The swarms break apart into the masts or descend beneath the thatch. Predators remain for a while, working through the injured and stranded. Within a day, the plain again looks almost uninhabited, though the cups are full and every hollow contains eggs, feeding, pursuit, and escape.

Human work is scattered so thinly across all this that it can seem incidental.

At long intervals there is a dew station: four poles cut from a fallen mast, a steep roof of woven root fiber, a grooved catchment feeding a fired-clay basin. Elsewhere, a sleeping platform has been wedged into the fork of a dead stem, or a short ladder of pegs leads to a naturally dry chamber. A cord bridge crosses a place where the thatch sounds dangerously hollow. A stone cairn, no taller than a knee, marks a route that has otherwise disappeared.

Many of these things are abandoned. Living roots pass through their lashings; small plants occupy the roofs; clay jars hold mosquito-like larvae and old rainwater. There are no roads in the usual sense, no villages on the open plain, and no large dressed stones. The tools implied by the structures are simple: flaked rim stone, bone awls, fire-hardened splints, hand-twisted cord, patient scraping. Nothing here looks nailed, sawn, or wheeled into place.

Those who use the Thatch travel by agreements about weight and sound. They tap the floor before trusting it. They avoid cutting green masts, which are reservoirs as well as nests. They do not camp beside a newly filled cup, because too many other things come to drink. During the loudest choruses they communicate by touch or gesture, and when crossing unfamiliar ground they keep a cord between them—not against losing sight of one another, but against the disorientation of having every direction roar at once.

From a high mast, the whole region appears finished and vacant: an ancient level skin, a few widely separated crowns, cloud at the edges, and a great deal of untouched sky. There is no visible river, no city, no herd darkening the distance. The mountains have long since been worn beyond drama.

Yet the floor under that stillness is warm with hidden movement. The empty galleries carry footsteps and dripping water. The stems are crowded with waiting voices. It is possible to stand in that immense openness, see almost nothing alive, and be unable to hear one’s own breathing because of the life surrounding it.
