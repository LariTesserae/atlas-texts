## The Unfinished Engine

High inside a young volcanic mountain, only a short distance below its summit, there is a cavity no larger than a small room. It could be crossed in nine paces if it were empty. It is not empty.

The mountain around it has barely finished becoming rock. Its outer slopes are raw clinker and volcanic glass, without gullies, rounded stones, or sorted beds of ash. At this altitude the air is thin, and the summit has never known snow. Nothing about the exterior suggests the chamber: no entrance, no plume, no opening large enough to admit a finger. Only a slightly warmer seam in the stone betrays it.

Inside, the heat is immediate and merciless. The rock is not molten, nor hot enough to glow, but it would scorch unprotected flesh at a touch. The gases trapped in the pocket are hot, pressurized, and utterly waterless. There is no vapor hidden in them, no dampness in the dust, no water locked into the minerals. Even the native animals use no water in their tissues; their internal chemistry moves through sulfur-bearing oils and waxy fluids. A droplet of rain would be more foreign here than fire.

The chamber is less an open cave than a crowded interstice. Its walls are intergrown blades of silica, iron sulfide, and dark glass. Some are thin as leaves, others thick as a hand, all sharply terminated. Fresh fractures shine where the rock has split; older surfaces—older by decades, not millennia—are furred with new crystals. The floor is a compacted mass of sulfide grit, broken needles, and mineral skins that have peeled from the roof. Nothing has traveled far enough to become sediment. Every fragment lies close to where it was made.

Space is the rarest material present. Crystals grow through crystals. Fine tubes of gas wind between them. Mineral crusts overlap in dense, brittle shingles. And over nearly all of it, packed into every depression and crevice, are animals.

Nothing here has been built, yet the pocket runs with the grim regularity of an industrial plant. Heat deep in the mountain drives dry gases upward. Natural constrictions meter their flow. A narrow throat hisses continuously; another, lined with friable mineral, grinds its own walls into powder. Somewhere behind the chamber, a skin of volcanic glass flexes under pressure and returns a low concussion to the gas-filled passages. Stress gathers in the silica-rich ribs until charge leaps through conductive dust in a brief, hard spark.

The resemblance to machinery is not a matter of intention. It is a matter of accounting: heat enters, pressure is contained, gas is throttled, material precipitates, waste accumulates, and electrical imbalance is discharged. Millions of microscopic reaction sites operate in parallel, laying down fresh mineral one layer at a time. There are no tools, conduits, engines, or artifacts—only geology doing accidentally, and very efficiently, what industry does by design.

It is extremely loud.

The main sound is a roar too broad and steady to seem like wind. Beneath it lies a pressure-note felt in bone rather than heard. Higher sounds crowd above it: the needle-hiss of escaping gas, the dry rasp of falling grit, the minute cracking of crystals as they grow and bind neighboring surfaces together. The animals add their own dense patter of shell against shell and leg against stone. Because the chamber is so full, it does not echo like an empty room. The noise returns immediately, thickened by every surface. Loudness here has no suggestion of spaciousness.

There is almost no light. The rock gives off heat without visible radiance, and no sky reaches through the mountain. Only the occasional electrical discharge interrupts the darkness. For an instant, crystal edges stand out white and razor-sharp, the animals appear as black seeds packed between them, and every narrow passage becomes a corridor of violent contrast. Then the chamber closes back into blackness.

Nothing native relies on sight.

At the base of the food web are chemosynthetic microbes living within the mineral pores. They occupy the steepest chemical gradients, taking energy from reactions among sulfur, iron, and the hot gases. They form no meadow, mat, or visible covering; there is nothing photosynthetic, nothing green, nothing with leaf or root. Their abundance is measured in the richness of the films on which larger creatures feed.

The most numerous animals are flat, many-legged grazers ranging from the size of a lentil to that of a thumbnail. Their shells are dry composites of silica and sulfide, their joints sealed with flexible wax. They cling closely to the crystals, mouths working slowly over the reactive films. In some places they lie several bodies deep, the lower ones feeding while the upper ones cling to their backs. During the electrical flashes, parts of the wall seem to contain more shell than stone.

Other animals thread through the narrow spaces between them: wire-bodied predators that wait beside grazing trails, blunt scavengers that worry at molted shells, and minute larvae living in the sulfide dust. Their behavior is not especially strange. They feed, compete, shelter, mate, molt, and die. They build nothing—not nests, not burrows, not stores. The existing pores provide all the shelter they need, and their dead remain where they fall until scavengers and chemistry reduce them.

Because sound in the open passages is so overwhelming, much of their communication travels through solid mineral. A grazer drums one shell against another. A predator detects the tiny, repeated vibration of feeding mouthparts. Mates answer through the shared crystal on which they stand. The chamber’s geology is also its sensory network.

The rules of the place are few and tightly coupled. Heat makes pressure. Pressure makes flow. Flow deposits mineral. Mineral constricts and redirects flow. Life occupies the chemical differences maintained along the way. Almost nothing is decorative, and almost nothing is separate.

Yet the noise and crowding give a false impression of haste. The chamber changes slowly. Its roar may keep the same pitch for generations. A crystal needle can take a century to thicken by the width of a hair. The great shifts occur when deposition finally narrows some deep gas passage and pressure finds another route: then, over years, the bass note changes, one group of pores cools, another becomes reactive, and the dense populations gradually redistribute themselves. Even the animals, though capable of sudden movement, usually proceed with deliberate steps through the crush.

There is no day, season, weather, or flowing channel by which to measure time. Time is deposition, molting, and the slow alteration of sound.

Eventually, the chamber will cease to be a chamber. It will not collapse or erode away; it will be filled by its own production. Crystal by crystal, its remaining spaces will close. Long before then, pressure will have opened newer interstices elsewhere in the summit rock, and the animals will have followed the shifting chemical frontier through passages too fine to see.

For now, the pocket remains unfinished: a small, lightless fullness high inside a raw mountain, crowded with sharp stone and patient bodies, roaring steadily as heat turns gas into mineral and life persists in the narrow margins of that manufacture.
