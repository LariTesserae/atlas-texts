### The Bright Seep

Beneath a young sheet of volcanic glass lies a cavity with no entrance. It is perhaps seven paces long, though most of its meaningful distances are smaller: the width of a water film, the spacing of crystals, the translucent shell of an animal too slight to cast a shadow. From above, the place is almost nothing—a hairline of clear mineral in the black ground and a warm patch where rain dries too quickly.

The cavity formed when trapped water flashed to steam beneath the cooling crust. The roof lifted slightly and held. Everything inside is consequently fresh. The walls retain the fine, concentric hackles of fractured glass. Slender blades of silica stand along one fracture, their edges so sharp they vanish when viewed directly. There is no sand, no gravel, no soil, and none of the rounding that comes from long contact with water. Even the floor seems not yet to have acquired a floor’s patience: broad planes of smoke-gray glass end abruptly in narrow fissures, and a misplaced hand would find edges rather than dust.

Heat comes up through those fissures in slow, uneven pulses. The air is hot enough to make breathing an effort, though it is not still. Over the warmest stone it trembles; nearer the roof it becomes humid and almost perceptibly heavy. Water enters at the rear as a thread of seepage, spreads through capillary cracks, and gathers in one shallow pool along the wall. The pool is barely deep enough to cover a hand. Its surface does not ripple so much as tighten and relax when another pulse arrives.

Elsewhere, water is present mainly as shine. Beads condense on the marginally cooler ceiling, join, and remain suspended for a long time. When one finally falls onto hot glass, it breaks into several smaller drops that skid briefly on their own vapor before disappearing. At the rear fissure, a faint mineral exhalation marks where liquid becomes steam. Along the roof, that steam becomes liquid again. The chamber has weather, but all of it occurs within a few body-lengths.

By rights, it should also be dark. Instead, at midday, it is dazzling.

The clear seam visible on the surface continues into a lace of opaline silica throughout the roof. Its structure is too fine to see: galleries and repeating voids laid down at molecular dimensions, with a regularity no human fabrication has yet learned to grow under such hot, dirty, fluctuating conditions. Daylight enters the seam and is divided, guided, and released through thousands of mineral faces. It does not arrive as a beam. The whole cavity simply fills with white radiance.

The light is bright but not gentle. Glass edges flash. Condensation shines like a second, scattered ceiling. The pool is transparent to its deepest crack, and even the pale steam above the rear fissure becomes briefly visible. Shadows are shallow and multiplied, as though no single part of the chamber were willing to take responsibility for them.

Nothing here was built. There are no fitted joints, conduits, markings, or tools—no sign that anything has ever entered. Yet the mineral lattice behaves with the refinement of an unmade instrument. Where it concentrates light, evaporation increases. Where evaporation cools a wet surface, dissolved silica comes out of solution and extends the lattice. The growing crystal then alters the path of the light. This is not thought, and there is no intention behind it. It is simply feedback: light changing water, water changing stone, stone changing light.

Most of the cavity remains empty. One pool, a few standing blades, a sparse scattering of clear beads on the floor, and long intervals of bare glass. The emptiness gives each presence disproportionate importance. A new droplet on the ceiling can be followed for minutes. A thin amber stain at the waterline marks the entire visible extent of anything resembling growth.

There are no plants, nothing green, no roots or fibrous mats. The color comes from simple microbial films metabolizing hydrogen and sulfur where hot water meets fresh mineral. They advance and recede with the seep, sometimes leaving a dry, powdery margin, sometimes returning as a slick so thin it appears only when the light strikes at a low angle. Their ecology is measured less in coverage than in chemistry: a slight clearing of sulfur here, a trace of iron deposited there, a narrow zone made habitable for something else.

Animals are rare enough that movement counts as an event. In the cooler throat of the feeder fissure live a few translucent groundwater crustaceans, none larger than a grain of rice. For long periods there is no sign of them. Then one may emerge along the pool’s margin, testing the hot water with delicate limbs and grazing a path through the microbial film too narrow to see except by its interruption. Minutes later it is gone. Once, perhaps, an empty molt remains on the bottom, colorless and perfectly articulated, catching the light like a second animal that has forgotten to move.

For all this activity, the chamber is mostly quiet. The pool does not lap. There is no wind beyond a nearly imperceptible breathing through the finest cracks as the surface above warms and cools. Silence lasts long enough to seem complete. Then comes a single drop, a small glassy note; or a tick from the young roof adjusting to heat; or the faint hiss at the rear fissure, so soft it seems closer to a sensation on the skin than a sound.

The place will not remain as it is. The same deposition that distributes daylight is slowly sealing the water pathways. The pool will shrink into a chain of wet pockets. The pockets will become opal, preserving their last water as microscopic inclusions. Eventually the bright seam may close as well, leaving only a dense white lens beneath the black plain.

For now, though, the processes still overlap. Heat lifts water; water writes silica across the roof; the roof returns that writing as daylight. A drop gathers, falls, and vanishes. Somewhere in the feeder crack, one small animal changes position. Then the hot, brilliant emptiness settles back into silence.
