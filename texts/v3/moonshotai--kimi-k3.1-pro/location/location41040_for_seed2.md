## The Low Reservoir

The first thing to know about the Low Reservoir is that nothing in it was drowned. There was no valley here, no city, no reef. The floor was cast, the walls were raised, the roof was sealed, and only then did the intake gates open. Water is the youngest part of the landscape.

It now fills a container so large that its far walls are mostly administrative facts. Cargo submarines measure crossings in days. The deepest sumps lie far below the outside sea surface, while the roof is high enough overhead to resemble a pale, structured sky. Charts divide the Reservoir into pressure provinces, lighting districts, and circulation basins. There are routes across it, but no casual journey from one side to the other.

It is a sea whose container happens to have been built.

There is no natural bottom. A sounding line strikes prestressed concrete, steel plate, or the grating of a ballast gallery. The long, open plains of the floor slope at deliberate grades toward drainage trenches. Expansion joints cross them like roads, though nothing travels along most of them. Low ridges turn out, on approach, to be foundation buttresses or banks of buried heat exchangers. Steeper escarpments have regular faces, inspection ladders, and painted depth marks. A dark line on the horizon may be a cable trench hundreds of kilometres long.

The Reservoir is still sharply itself. Concrete corners have not been rounded. Welds remain visible as bright seams. Rubber gaskets are black and clean between flanges, and commissioning numbers can still be read on the pump housings. Rust exists mainly where construction equipment scarred the paint. At a few high-pressure outlets, cavitation has already bitten pale crescents into the steel—the first erosion in a place that has not yet had time to develop ruin.

For all that construction, the dominant material is empty water.

One can leave a pumping station and travel for hours without seeing another object. The floor sinks into blue-grey distance; the roof disappears into its own diffuse light. Occasionally a single support column passes, enormous at its base and yet too slender for the space around it. Freight beacons stand days apart. Whole factory hulls, when they finally appear, look less like cities than like ships accidentally holding still. Almost everything is made, but almost nothing is occupied.

The water is cold and clear. In the deep return channels it stays only a few degrees above freezing; elsewhere it is merely cool enough to make exposed skin ache. Factory outflows create broader regions of relative warmth, though even these are seldom warm. There is a slight mineral and metallic character to the water near new installations. Inside the rare airlocks, the accompanying smells are wet cement, lubricant, old canvas, and the sharp cleanliness of electrical ozone.

It is not abyssally dark. Broad lighting fields in the roof, supplemented by suspended lamp arrays, maintain an even working daylight. The effect is closer to a bright overcast morning than to an illuminated room: few hard shadows, little glitter, distances gradually dissolving into suspended haze. Trusses occasionally cross the illuminated ceiling and cast shadows large enough to resemble weather. At night the light is reduced rather than extinguished, leaving the open water pewter-coloured and the stations marked by rows of ordinary white windows.

The steadiness of the light makes the emptiness more legible. A school of fish can be seen approaching from far away. So can a submarine. Most of the time, neither is there.

Sound arrives first.

The entire Reservoir carries a low mechanical note, felt through a diving suit or a vessel’s hull as much as heard. It is the combined labour of circulation pumps, turbines, pressure regulators, and machinery beyond the walls. Near a major station, that note becomes a physical roar. Valve gates close with concussions that travel through the floor. Cooling water pours through exchangers in continuous thunder. Freight engines produce slow, rhythmic beats, and contracting metal ticks loudly when a warm outflow shuts down.

In open water, sounds lose their apparent distance. A maintenance horn may seem close enough to touch and still be hours away. Sonar pulses, cable strain, animal calls, and the hollow ringing of struck structural steel overlap without ever becoming a single ambience. Local silence is unusual enough to be a warning: it generally means a shutdown, a failed pump, or a gate closing somewhere upstream.

The Reservoir’s purpose is practical—water storage, industrial cooling, transport, and controlled production—but its operation has created weather.

Warm discharge rises from factory hulls and spreads beneath the roof in broad, slow layers. Cold return water moves along the floor. Between shifts, when pumps are throttled and gates change position, these layers slide over one another. Their boundaries are sometimes visible as faint optical distortions, with threads of particles gathered along them. Internal waves travel outward from the great basins. Gyres hundreds of kilometres wide turn so slowly that a drifting animal may spend its whole life inside one without crossing its edge.

A current storm begins with a pressure boom. Then suspended bubbles draw into long ribbons, loose equipment pulls against its restraints, and fish turn together into the strengthening flow. Small submarines leave exposed routes and shelter behind buttresses. Hours later the storm may end simply because a distant control room has completed a load transfer. Control rooms issue forecasts, but the water body is now large enough to complicate them. A warm plume can stall where none was expected; a returning cold front can arrive early after days of unnoticed acceleration along the floor.

Life entered through intakes, stock transports, and hatchery releases. It has not covered the Reservoir, but it has found its productive places.

Vegetation remains sparse. Thin green films occupy lit expansion joints and the sunward faces of roof supports. Around nutrient outfalls there are narrow bands of tougher algae and a few managed lines of kelp, cropped short by grazers and maintenance crews. Most of the floor is bare. There are no weed meadows stretching to the horizon, no forests rising through midwater. The dominant colours are concrete grey, painted steel, pale light, and water.

Animals, by contrast, can arrive in astonishing numbers.

Clouds of small crustaceans gather beneath certain lighting fields. Silver schooling fish assemble above them in bodies large enough to dim the water as they turn. Squid hunt along the edges of the schools, while crabs and eels work the trenches below. Filter feeders position themselves downstream of nutrient-rich outflows, and larger predators follow the slow migration of prey between pump basins. Encrusting animals have begun colonizing the newest surfaces, though their growth is still shallow enough that painted warnings show through.

This abundance is gathered rather than spread. There may be nothing but cold open water for a day, then a passing school containing millions of lives, then emptiness again. Some spawning aggregations now form around warm discharge plumes. Scavengers gather near freight locks. Predators have learned the vibrations associated with gates opening, and grazers follow the maintenance lighting cycles. The engineers regulate the currents; they do not fully regulate what the currents have made possible.

The rules here are therefore simple but not absolute. Daylight is a power setting. Tides are often pump schedules. Depth is a pressure jurisdiction. Sound outruns sight. Still water usually means that a machine has decided to make it still.

Yet from midwater, none of that is immediately visible. There is only cool light, the distant engine-note, and perhaps one tiny station suspended far off in the haze. A school passes like brief weather. After it has gone, the Reservoir returns to its immense manufactured vacancy.

It is not a wilderness, and it is not yet a ruin. It is a new, industrial sea—still sharp from fabrication, already loud with weather, and gradually being revised by the animals living inside it.
