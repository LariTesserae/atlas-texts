## Crownwater

At the summit of an ancient highland, where the mountains have been worn until height is almost their only remaining feature, there is a sea.

The plateau stands above the clouds, in air too thin to carry sound well. The sky never brightens beyond a gray, directionless twilight. At the surface, the world is nearly silent and nearly invisible. A body length underwater, it is black. Several body lengths down, it is loud.

Crownwater occupies a whole summit valley and extends through broad submerged openings into two adjoining basins. It is a province of water, not a pond: a marginal country of low vaults, a reach of isolated pillars, and an open middle whose floor lies beyond sight. Yet there is no dramatic shoreline, no cliff of rock, no gravel bar or bank of soil. The original mountains merely determine the altitude. Nothing of them is exposed.

The sea’s bowl was grown.

A consortium of plants, so old and thoroughly grafted that it now behaves as one body, has sealed and built over the entire basin. Its retained tissues form the bottom, the rim, the pillars, and the submerged halls. Everything solid here is cellulose, pith, resin, living sap, or plant tissue aged until it resembles black horn. Old surfaces are dense and smooth, with the slight yield of wet leather. Active surfaces wear a close olive velvet. Openings have no cut edges; they are rimmed by growth that thickened, met other growth, and stopped.

It is built in the way a tree is built: cell by cell, without hands or tools, with no final separation between the maker and the thing made. Nothing looks machined. Nothing looks accidental, either.

The totality of the vegetation does not make the place crowded. Most of Crownwater is open water. One can swim for a long time through darkness and touch nothing at all. Then a pillar arrives—first as a low vibration, then as a pressure against the water, and only at close range as a surface beneath the hand. The pillars stand hundreds of paces apart, some solitary, some joined by submerged arches large enough for the largest animals to pass through. The floor between them is almost flat, interrupted only by broad, shallow depressions whose sides have been rounded beyond any memory of fracture.

At the surface, widely separated leaf crowns spread nearly black leaves beneath the weak sky. None of the crowns touch. They gather what little light there is and send its products down through the pillars. In return, the submerged roots draw nutrients upward. There are no dense underwater thickets and no drifting mats of weed. Vegetation is total here as substrate and architecture, not as visual saturation.

The water is cold enough to numb exposed skin, but it never freezes. Its temperature is nearly the same from surface to floor, so there are no thermal layers, seasonal overturnings, or cold currents unexpectedly giving way to warm ones. Bands of microscopic cilia and the slow sweeping of submerged fronds keep it circulating along closed paths. No river enters; none leaves. Water lost to the thin air is condensed and returned by cold surface tissues, while the roots regulate its salts.

There is no silt. No sand collects in the depressions, and no fallen leaves accumulate beneath the crowns. Wear still occurs, but new tissue replaces it exactly where it forms. Loose material is intercepted by root hairs and taken apart before it can become sediment. If Crownwater were illuminated, its water would be remarkably clear. Darkness, not murk, makes it opaque.

The fauna are abundant, but abundance here is not crowding. It is a property of the whole region rather than of any one view. Many kinds of animals live in the basins, and their total populations are large, yet a swimmer may encounter only a single pale grazer on a pillar, or a distant shoal passing as a brief disturbance in the surrounding sound.

Small, flattened grazers move over the plant skin in widely spaced files, keeping feeding pores open and preventing the pillars from sealing themselves over. Sieve-bodied swimmers travel in long, loose formations, straining spores and fragments of living tissue from the water. Eyeless, many-finned animals rest inside ready-grown cavities, sensitive to the smallest change in pressure. In the open middle, much larger creatures follow routes so old and regular that the routes function as geography. They are seldom seen, even by Crownwater’s standards, but their calls are always somewhere audible.

Sound supplies the detail that light withholds.

Water passing through hollow pillars produces a continuous drone, deep enough to be felt in the ribs. The submerged arches add a lower rushing note, while fine currents crossing their lips make a soft, piercing hiss. These are not instruments; nothing was fashioned or tuned. They are simply the sounds made when an old living form and an unchanging current meet.

Against that background, the grazers contribute faint sizzling and close, brittle clicks. Shoals pass as showers of tapping that approach, spread around a listener, and recede. The large animals of the open water call with single knocks, long descending groans, and occasional booms that return from the rim after an unsettling delay. Near a major pillar the combined loudness can become physically uncomfortable. Even in the emptiest water there is no fully silent interval.

Because the sources are far apart, the sound often suggests a more crowded place than the water actually is. One may float in apparent solitude while hearing life from an entire basin. Animals navigate by this acoustic geography: the note of a pillar, the dull reply of the floor, the open middle’s long echo, the rim’s layered roar. A call identifies not only the caller but the surfaces around it.

Nothing here needs technology, and none has appeared. The animals do not pile stems, cut branches, or carry objects. Shelter is a cavity the vegetation has already grown. Food is plant tissue, spores, suspended organic matter, or another animal. Even the empty water is part of the constructed environment, its volume and composition maintained by the living basin. By biomass, Crownwater is overwhelmingly plant. By voice, it is overwhelmingly animal.

And yet, for all its motion, it does not change.

Sap rises; water circulates; grazers advance and shoals turn. A leaf expands while another thins and is reabsorbed. A grazed surface regrows at the rate it is eaten. New animals replace dead ones without altering the population. The rim neither advances nor retreats. The open water does not fill with sediment, and the pillars do not thicken into a forest. Construction and erosion have not ceased; they have become equal and opposite.

Crownwater is not frozen. It is changeless in the way a standing wave is changeless. Individual voices enter and leave the chorus, but the underlying chord remains. There are no young districts and no ruined ones, no frontier of fresh growth and no abandoned center. The whole region possesses the same completed age.

To float in its open middle is to feel the altitude leave the body. The thin air, the dim surface, and the flattened mountains vanish. There is only even cold, black water, and sounds arriving from distances that cannot be judged. Somewhere a pillar receives the same current it has received for ages and answers with the same low note. Farther away, the rim returns an animal’s call.

Between them lies an immense, carefully maintained emptiness, with nothing left that needs to become something else.
