The plateau is young only by the calendar of stone. Dark lava flows, stacked one upon another, have been lifted into a country higher than most mountain summits, yet frost and running water have barely begun to soften them. Escarpments break in straight, splintered lines. Cliffs descend in columnar steps; gullies are narrow slots rather than open valleys; the scree below consists of angular blocks that knock sharply together when disturbed. Beyond these edges, the tableland runs past every useful horizon. A traveler can walk for weeks without leaving it. Snow may cross the interior while the western rim stands in clear sunlight, and a cloud bank can take days to drift from one province of the plateau to another.

The height is felt in the body before it is understood by the eye. Newcomers move slowly, stop often, and learn not to mistake visible cliffs for nearby ones. The air is thin and cold. Even in the brief warm season, shaded fissures retain old ice, and for much of the year breath whitens at noon. Under a clear sky the sunlight is plain and strong: black stone, pale lichen, white frost, distances difficult to judge. South-facing slabs may warm enough to shed their rime while the air around them remains freezing. At night the cold returns quickly, entering walls and clothing with equal patience.

Water is scarce as scenery but persistent in detail. There are no generous rivers on the upper plateau. A few shallow streams appear after the occasional cold rains of summer, run furiously for an hour or two over bare rock, and vanish into fractures. More often, moisture arrives without falling. Damp air climbs the escarpments from the lower country and condenses on the outer cliffs. Hoarfrost gathers in sheltered grooves; beads of water darken overhangs; at midday, brief silver threads run from sun-warmed stone. Where one lava flow rests upon another, seeps sometimes emerge along the buried contact. Their pools may be no wider than a doorway, rimmed with ice even while their centers remain unfrozen. The smell of wet basalt is rare enough to be noticed from several steps away.

Soil exists mainly as dust in cracks. Vegetation never becomes a cover. Lichens spread in gray, rust, and yellow stains across old surfaces, while low cushion plants occupy hollows where wind cannot quite reach them. Sedges and small, thick-leaved herbs gather at the seep mouths in narrow, intensely green margins. There are no trees on the open tableland. A shrub tall enough to cast a shadow at noon is worth remembering as a landmark.

Yet the plateau is full of animals. Their abundance is partly a consequence of its size: each herd may require an enormous range, but the range exists. Heavy, double-coated grazers move along routes worn almost imperceptibly into the stone, spending seasons out of sight before returning to the same passes. Smaller furred animals inhabit the scree in astonishing numbers, emerging to feed on seeds, insects, and whatever the herds leave behind. During the short thaw, seep pools cloud briefly with insect life, and birds arrive to take advantage of it. Other birds nest along the columnar cliffs in dense, tiered colonies. Fox-sized scavengers follow both herds and settlements; raptors hang for long periods above the rim, moving so little that they seem fixed to the sky.

Life gathers most tightly where a dependable seep, a migration route, and the head of the only easy pass come together. There, the city has grown.

From above, it is surprisingly small: a dark, squared thickening at the edge of the plateau, easily mistaken for a exposed band of rock. Inside, it is crowded almost to the point of continuity. Houses share walls and foundations. Upper rooms rest partly on older roofs; later roofs serve as paths to newer dwellings. Retaining walls rise through several generations of construction, their lowest courses massive and smoke-stained, their highest still fresh from splitting. Passages narrow until a person can brush both sides with outstretched elbows, then open without warning into small courts containing a cistern, a stair, or a cluster of animal pens. Some streets are little more than covered clefts between buildings. Others cross rooftops, where vents, drying racks, and low windbreaks leave only a crooked route through.

Everything essential is made from the plateau. Freshly split basalt is blue-black; older walls weather toward brown and gray. Blocks are fitted closely with stone mauls, antler picks, wooden levers, and ropes braided from hair or sinew. Clay from a few weathered pockets serves as mortar and sealant. Roofs are corbelled or laid from broad slabs, their seams packed against frost. Doors are layered hide on wooden or stone pivots. Metal is too scarce to shape the appearance of the place. The city’s achievement lies not in machinery but in repetition: generations of hands splitting stone along the same natural weaknesses, raising rubble ramps, adding one course at a time.

Its most important public works concern water. Shallow gutters cross roofs and run beside stairs. During rare rain, channels that have been dry for months begin to whisper, carrying water through settling basins and into covered, clay-lined cisterns. South-facing walls are grooved to catch the melt from hoarfrost. Cistern courts are among the few spaces deliberately kept open, and access to them shapes the layout of whole neighborhoods. Even the drains have a second use, guiding the occasional flood away from foundations before it can freeze there.

Heat is managed with the same closeness. Dung from the herd animals, dried and stored in roofed stacks, is the ordinary fuel. Fires are small and deeply set. Thick walls hold their warmth, and in winter some households keep animals in lower rooms, accepting the smell and noise for the heat that rises through the floor. The city lives on milk when available, dried meat, hides, hair, and stored foods brought up the pass. Pack trains descend with hides and finely worked stone vessels, returning with grain, salt, and precious timber too valuable to burn.

For all this concentration, the city is mostly quiet. There are no iron-shod wheels and little continuous traffic. Thick walls break up sound, and hide boots make almost no noise on stone. Sounds arrive singly: the clack of a hoof in an empty lane, the scrape of a door, three measured blows from a mason, a child’s voice quickly hushed by distance. After fog, water can be heard dripping in several courts, each cistern at a slightly different interval. At dusk the cliff colonies produce a vast dry rustle of wings, then settle ledge by ledge into silence. The usual smells are dung smoke, cold hides, animal musk, and, close to the water works, the clean mineral odor of damp stone.

Change comes slowly enough to resemble permanence. Frost opens a joint by grains. A herd path deepens over centuries. Lichen advances across a wall beyond the memory of the people living behind it. The city itself grows by repairs, subdivisions, and inherited courses of masonry; no builder expects to see a great public work begun and completed. A rockfall or an unusually wet summer may mark a generation, but between such events the days differ mainly in wind, cloud, and the position of animals on the passes.

At evening, the city becomes difficult to distinguish from the fracture it occupies. Thin smoke flattens above the roofs. Frost returns to the outer steps. Somewhere below a stone cover, water ticks into a cistern. Beyond the last wall, herd trails lead onto the dark tableland and disappear into distances large enough to contain separate weather. The stone remains sharp, the settlement remains pressed close around its water, and both are still being made so gradually that stillness is the easiest name for it.
