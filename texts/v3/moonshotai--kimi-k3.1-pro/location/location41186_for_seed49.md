## The Sinter Basin

The Sinter lies in the floor of an old sea, though no open sea remains in sight. For thousands of kilometres the basin has been drained, terraced, built over, and built under, until city and terrain have become the same object. It is immensely broad and almost without elevation. A canal can run for a day’s travel without a lock; a difference in ground height that would go unnoticed elsewhere is enough here to determine which districts flood and which become foundations.

From above, in ordinary daylight, it could be mistaken for a mineral delta. Its colours are wet slate, bone white, rust, tar black, and the yellow-brown of old salt. Only the regularity of the channels gives away the construction. Closer inspection reveals that nearly every apparent ridge is a roof, every crevice a conduit or street, and every patch of open ground merely a space between uses. The sun rises and sets normally. At noon there is hard white glare on wet ceramic; toward evening, the basin turns the colour of heated pottery. The strangeness is not in the light.

Water is present everywhere, but not usually as a flood. It stands in shallow, black channels, seeps around door seals, beads on ceilings, and fills the pores of the ground. It rises through pavement as a warm film and disappears again before it can gather depth. Rain arrives in brief, heavy cells, barely cooler than skin. Even the still water in cisterns trembles continually with pump vibration. Nothing is dry in the uncomplicated sense of dust and loose grains; “dry” means only that water cannot presently be seen.

The city’s material looks older than its technology. Walls are made from a dense, dull substance somewhere between concrete, fired clay, and basalt, full of shell fragments and dark aggregate. Corners are rounded. Steps sag at their centres. Canal margins have been polished into smooth, fluted profiles by centuries of warm water and suspended grit. In the oldest drainage reaches, erosion and deposition roughly balance: what a current removes from one bend is laid down along another. The Sinter is weathered without being ruined. Wear is part of how its pieces fit.

At street level, there is very little street. Passages run beneath aqueducts, behind heat exchangers, and through the occupied thickness of piers. A bridge may also be a reservoir cover, a freight conduit, and the floor of several hundred rooms. Arches are packed with roosting animals and cable bundles. Vertical shafts contain counterweighted transport, warm rising air, maintenance rails, and colonies of small winged scavengers. Overhead connections subdivide the sky into narrow, changing pieces. Even the air seems fully used, occupied by droplets, noise, exhaust, and fine mineral dust.

There are no plants. No reeds soften the canal margins, no roots enter the masonry, no moss holds the shade. What looks from a distance like a patch of green growth usually proves to be copper staining; what looks like brown fur on a wall is often a dense colony of tiny jointed animals. Food comes from closed synthesis vats and chemical culture houses deep inside the city. Nothing like a farm interrupts the built surface, and there is no countryside beyond it in which one might be hidden.

The Sinter’s most advanced mechanisms are not conspicuous objects. They are properties of the otherwise drab material. Microscopic constructors travel in the water supply, able to loosen a wall into grey pellets or deposit new structural ceramic grain by grain. Field footings transmit weight and vibration into the deep substrate, allowing supports to be replaced while the occupied mass above them remains standing. A building can therefore be dismantled around its inhabitants without quite being demolished. Its outer rooms may depart as freight-sized blocks while new partitions accumulate behind them.

This makes the city hyperactive without making its underlying geography especially changeable. Construction fronts move through districts like squalls. In the morning a frontage is intact; by midday it is a haze of constructor activity and exposed ribs; by night it has become a pumping station or a stack of sealed dwellings. Bridges withdraw into their abutments. Pavement opens into a canal. A passage that has existed for fifty years is filled in an afternoon because the pressure balance two districts away has altered.

Yet the great pump stations remain where they have always been. The principal channels return to their old courses. Addresses outlive their buildings, and routes outlive their surfaces. Maps show drainage divides, load nodes, and pressure phases before they bother with street names. A door may be temporary; the vibration signature of the pump station used to locate it is not.

Because the basin is so large and warm, it produces weather of its own. Heat and evaporation rise from millions of roofs and cisterns, forming low clouds that drift along the built plain. Some rain cells follow thermal corridors for days, growing darker as they gather moisture from successive districts. A storm front can be visible long before it arrives: first a grey lid over the remote city, then vertical columns of mist above the heat plants, then a moving line along which every wet surface briefly shines.

The noise never really stops. At the bottom is the stroke of the deep pumps, too low to hear cleanly, transmitted through floors and bone as a succession of pressure changes. Above that are freight impacts, water hammer inside walls, the granular hiss of constructors, the crack of field footings accepting a new load, rain on ceramic, and the bellows and shrieks of animals. In dense districts, human crowds and machinery occupy the same narrow resonant spaces until there is no acoustic distance anywhere. The loudest thing is not a single machine but the impossibility of getting away from all of them.

Outdoor workers wear helmets as much for hearing as for protection. Long conversations are conducted by touch, induction, or in the brief lulls when equipment changes phase. Local quiet means being able to distinguish one pump from another. During storms, speech becomes pointless even through helmets; loose water jumps on flat surfaces, and the roar seems to issue from the material of the basin itself.

Life has adapted to all of this with startling completeness. The food web begins in thin microbial films, colourless or rust-red, nourished by warm mineral seepage, industrial heat, and organic leakage from the synthesis houses. They look like stains until they are disturbed. Pale eels move through seepage channels and storm drains, steering by pressure pulses. Flat, shell-backed grazers cluster so thickly on wet walls that the walls appear to shiver when they feed. Long-legged wading crustaceans gather at pump outfalls, lowering broad sieve mouths into the aerated flow.

Smaller flying animals roost in exhaust ducts and emerge in dense sheets whenever demolition exposes a colony of grazers. Larger filter animals inhabit the deep reservoirs; their movements are sometimes detectable only as a slow rise and fall of water at street grates. The maintenance systems classify some species as obstructions, others as useful cleaners, and most as unavoidable. Shells, carapaces, and drowned bodies are continually incorporated into new ceramic, giving freshly deposited walls their faint speckling.

A major storm brings every cycle into coincidence. Warm rain falls so hard that its rebound forms a second rain above the pavement. Drainage reverses, and shallow streets become brief, violent canals. Emergency load fields take the strain with a series of deafening cracks. Whole blocks shed their outer material into the water, where it is broken down and carried toward new floodworks. Eels pour through openings that were solid an hour before; waders follow them; the flying scavengers vanish into shelter. For a while the district appears to be coming apart.

Then the water finds the old channels. The ancient curbs return, perhaps made from entirely new matter but wearing the same rounded profile. Animals recolonize the wet surfaces before the last construction haze has cleared. By the next interval of ordinary sunlight, there is steam above the roofs, activity in every opening, and no obvious boundary between damage, repair, and growth.

The Sinter is neither drowning nor approaching completion. It is old in the way a delta is old: its form persists while its substance is carried away, replaced, and carried away again.
