## The Low Choir

The Low Choir is not a single cavern, though it lies entirely beneath stone. It is a province of connected hollows: broad basins separated by low, worn divides; collapsed dales filled with old clay; gently tilted flats where the ceiling withdraws beyond the reach of every echo. Nowhere does the land rise far. Cool air gathers in its depressions and moves through them in slow, invisible streams.

There is no sky, and darkness here is not a time of day. It has texture and weather. In the still basins it feels close and absorbent; along the passes it moves with the drafts. A few animals make brief chemical lights, but these illuminate almost nothing. A blue-green stitch appears beside a pool, reveals a curve of wet shell and several hundred startled legs, and goes out. Afterward the darkness seems more complete. Most inhabitants do not navigate by sight. The country is known by pressure, vibration, scent, and the shape of returning sound.

The stone is predominantly old limestone, blackened in places by manganese and stained rust-red where iron-rich moisture has dried. Its violence is long past. Fallen blocks have lost their corners. Gravel has been rounded, sorted, and rounded again. Flowstone descend in thick, blunt folds rather than delicate needles, and the rims of ancient pools have been dissolved and rebuilt so often that they resemble healed bone. Even the animals’ routes are old: shallow polishings across rock, worn not by intention but by countless generations following the same easy grades.

Water seldom announces itself as a river. Most of the Low Choir is merely damp, and much of that dampness is intermittent. Moisture condenses on cold overhangs, gathers along calcite seams, and falls as isolated drops with long intervals between them. Some basins hold only a skin of wet clay. Others contain pools no larger than a body, their surfaces crowded with respiratory openings. After a prolonged wet period, several seeps may join into a stream that runs for a short distance, talking loudly over shell gravel before disappearing into a sink.

Between these events there are dry intervals. Dust settles over the higher flats. Microbial films tighten into dark, leathery crusts. Animals withdraw into cracks, bury themselves in sediment, or seal themselves inside their own shells. Yet the dryness never becomes barrenness. It is dormancy at enormous density.

Nothing photosynthesizes here. There are no roots in the silt, no fronds beneath the seeps, not even a green stain on the stone. The food web begins with films of chemosynthetic microorganisms living where sulfur and dissolved iron emerge from the rock. When wet, the films are slick and faintly iridescent; when dry, they wait. Their growth feeds the raspers, and the raspers feed nearly everything else.

The raspers are the first animals noticeable underfoot, though “noticeable” is inadequate. In favorable country they are the ground. Flat, many-legged, and armored in overlapping plates, they sit shell to shell across boulders and sediment, scraping the microbial films with mineral-edged mouthparts. A single one makes a faint tearing sound. A million make a continuous hiss, like rain heard through a wall. Their shells click as they jostle. Their droppings become pale mud. Their dead accumulate in layers so thick that younger animals graze across the backs of ancestors without touching stone.

Above them is another population, and above that another.

Small winged animals crowd the lower air, feeding on organic dust, larvae, and the fluids exuded by densely packed colonies. Their bodies are soft, their wings nearly mineral-transparent. Individually they weigh almost nothing; together they make walking difficult, coating every surface they settle on and turning exhaled breath into a struggle. Larger fliers move higher, where the air is less congested, hunting by abrupt changes in the wing-hum below.

The overhangs carry sessile animals with hollow, calcified breathing organs. From a distance—and in some imagined light—they might resemble clusters of pipes. Nothing about them has been constructed. Each tube is part of a body, and each body filters the passing air. Their exhalations produce flutes, rattles, and low reedy drones. On the walls behind and beneath them live other animals: blind coils in wet seams, flattened hunters under shell flakes, brood chambers packed so tightly with young that an apparently solid surface can suddenly begin to seethe.

Even the largest grazers are crowded landscapes. They move slowly on six thick legs, their broad backs armored with concentric mineral growths. Smaller animals occupy the grooves of that armor. Winged larvae shelter beneath their throats. Their passing leaves wide swaths of cleaned stone, quickly occupied by raspers following behind. At intervals the great grazers stop, inflate folded chambers in their chests, and release notes so low that they are felt before they are heard.

Those notes are the foundation of the Low Choir’s sound.

Above them comes the hiss of grazing, the ticking and knocking of shells, the wing-roar, the fluting of the overhang colonies, the shrieks of contested pools, and the percussive impacts of animals dropping from walls to avoid predators. The old basins gather and return all of it. Eroded hollows focus certain frequencies; porous shell deposits swallow others. A call may cross a basin, strike a far curve of limestone, and return altered several seconds later, where another colony answers it as though it were a living neighbor.

At full activity the noise is not merely loud. It is oppressive, a sustained physical presence. Loose grains tremble. Water shivers in its cups. The distinction between hearing and touch begins to fail. What seems to an unaccustomed ear like one unbearable roar is, to the inhabitants, a densely partitioned medium: different species occupy different pitches, rhythms, and directions of vibration. Many perceive through their feet or through the whole inner surface of the shell. They live inside a signal too crowded for silence, but not too crowded for meaning.

The sound also changes the water.

When condensation has thickened on a roof and several colonies enter the same deep rhythmic phase, droplets begin to jump from the stone. At first there are only a few additional falls. Then the vibrations coincide across a wider surface, and a whole patch of ceiling releases its moisture at once. Fine rain descends through air that was still a moment before.

Nothing planned this. It is simply an old entanglement of physics and life. The animals call; the calls dislodge water; the water opens egg cases, softens dry films, and wakes buried young. Generations have become timed to the coincidence. After such a chorus-rain, the basin changes pace within minutes. Raspers unseal themselves. Temporary streams turn milky with larvae. Hunters converge from drier ground. The winged population rises in a dense, glitterless cloud, and the noise doubles before the first drops have finished falling.

Not every basin sounds alike. The shell flats are dominated by a high, abrasive hiss, with sudden isolated cracks traveling through them like stones skipped across water. The deeper sinks hold bass notes for a long time, until the air seems too small to contain them. Along the low divides, several choruses can be perceived simultaneously, each out of phase with the others. Farther away, sound ceases to identify individual places and becomes a regional condition: a continuous subterranean weather arriving from beyond any visible horizon.

Silence is the exception, and it is never peace.

The great soft-bodied predators make almost no noise. Their broad feet deform instead of clicking; their sensory filaments trail without scraping; their flesh absorbs many of the echoes that would otherwise reveal them. Prey animals detect the disturbance they cause in the chorus—the broken rhythm, the unanswered call, the small pocket where familiar reverberation has gone wrong.

A hush then spreads outward.

It can cross a crowded flat faster than any animal can run. One group stops calling, then the next, then colonies beyond sight of the original disturbance. For a few seconds the Low Choir becomes audible in an entirely different way: individual claws, wet breathing, the tick of a cooling shell, a drop falling far overhead. These small sounds are almost intolerably exposed. Somewhere within them, the predator is feeding.

Then calling resumes at the edges. The chorus closes inward, raggedly at first, until the basin again conceals its separate lives inside the roar.

Light plays only a marginal part in all this. Some larvae flash when injured. A few courtships involve dim sparks passed from one body to another. At least one hunter counterfeits those signals, holding a cold point of light just above the mud and waiting for something curious enough to approach. But illumination is too costly, too revealing, and mostly unnecessary. The Low Choir has not organized itself around being seen.

Its openness is therefore easy to misunderstand. There are wide spaces, distant divides, and floors that extend far beyond the reach of any glow, yet almost none of that space is empty. It contains layers of animals, suspended organic dust, drifting spores of microbial films, cool descending air, warmer colony-breath rising in narrow currents, and sound crossing sound until the atmosphere itself seems occupied.

Nor is the land static beneath this activity. Grazing wears the limestone. Acids in wet sediment dissolve it. Mineral-rich pools lay down new rims, and shell debris slowly raises the flats. Collapse still occurs, but rarely and locally; for the most part, removal and deposition have approached an old accommodation. The Low Choir is established without being settled, balanced without being quiet.

Nothing here has been built, and nothing needs to have been. Its pipes are breathing organs, its drums are hollows in stone, its roads are polish left by repeated bodies. Yet the whole region participates in a circuit so close it can appear deliberate: stone supplies chemistry; chemistry feeds films; films become animal bodies; bodies fill the basins with vibration; vibration releases gathered water; water wakes the films again.

The circuit falters during dry periods and resumes unevenly, basin by basin. It has no central rhythm and no final crescendo. From one of the low divides, however, when several distant choruses rise together and the first condensation-rain begins ticking on shell and stone, the Low Choir can seem less like a landscape inhabited by animals than like a single immense metabolism with a limestone skeleton.

It does not await dawn. It does not remember surface weather. In the cool dark, crowded almost to solidity, it continues making water, sediment, hunger, silence, and its enormous many-layered voice.
