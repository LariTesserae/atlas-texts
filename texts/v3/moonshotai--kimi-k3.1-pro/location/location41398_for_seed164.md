## The Upper Works

At an altitude where a more ordinary continent would be frozen, dry, and quiet, the Upper Works is wet, green, and roaring.

The cold is clean and penetrating. Metal rails are painful to touch, and a visitor’s breath would hang white in the air. Yet water rarely freezes. It seeps under pressure from the young rock, heavy with dissolved minerals, and keeps moving through open faults, hillside mires, and narrow industrial channels. Every exposed surface seems to be condensing moisture: cliff faces shine, bridge parapets drip, and the leaves gather clear beads that never quite freeze.

The strangeness is not in the light. The light is ordinary overcast daylight, ample and shadow-soft, slightly blue on wet steel and warm enough in tone to distinguish green from gray. It is possible to read small print outdoors. The sun is only a vague brightness behind high cloud, and that brightness does not travel. Morning does not advance; afternoon does not arrive. The lamps inside the factories burn because their circuits are closed, not because darkness is coming.

The land itself is a vast, newly uplifted shield of ash-gray stone. Nothing about it has been rounded. Escarpments terminate in uncompromising edges; clefts plunge between walls that fit together in the imagination like the two halves of a broken bone. Fresh scree lies in angular heaps, each fragment keeping its original sharpness. Road cuts expose glassy blue-green interiors, as though the rock had been opened recently and had not yet decided what color to become on contact with air.

There are no soft foothills at the margins. The shield simply rises, folds into high blade-like ridges, and then drops through kilometer-high walls into lower banks of cloud. From a summit, distance arranges itself in layers: bare silver pavement nearby, black ravines beyond, then blue mesas, gray ranges, and finally long geometric suggestions in the haze. The continent is broad enough to contain several separate weathers. They seem less to pass across it than to be installed in different provinces. In one basin, drizzle falls continually from a low ceiling. Elsewhere, a white river of fog occupies the same valley, its surface continually renewed and never displaced. On a far horizon, a rain shaft stands beneath its cloud, joined to the ground by a gray waist. It never crosses the intervening ridge.

The wetness is most intimate at close range. Stone weeps in fine vertical streaks. Water gathers beneath overhangs, runs along the natural grain of fractures, and collects in fault-line pools with dark, perfectly steady margins. Some pools are only hand-deep mirrors; others widen into cold marshes whose bottoms cannot be seen through the green-black growth. Thin waterfalls drop from clefts without wearing back their lips. Pale mineral rims mark the level of every reservoir, and those marks neither rise nor become buried beneath new deposits.

Wherever water lingers, the vegetation becomes extravagant.

Black moss forms deep, resilient upholstery over the rocks. Above it rise wax-leaved ferns, bronze-backed fronds, thick ribbons of green foliage, and hollow sedges with dark red joints. In sheltered mires, low branching plants interlock into thickets, their leaves overlapping in several wet tiers. Many are almost succulent, storing water against the cold; others have narrow, translucent edges through which the flat daylight passes. Their colors tend toward bottle green, iodine brown, and deep wine, with occasional patches of astonishing pale blue where submerged stems catch the sky.

The lushness is concentrated rather than continuous. A seep may support a hundred paces of dense growth and then end at a dry fracture. Beyond it, the stone is completely bare. A marsh can fill the foreground while the rest of the visible province contains nothing but rock, air, and one distant chimney. The dominant substance of the Upper Works is not water or vegetation. It is interval.

Nothing here has evolved against being eaten. There are no thorns, no barbed seeds, no nectar-rich flowers. Nothing browses the thickets; nothing pollinates them. The plants reproduce by spores, division, and running roots, though there is no visible succession from one association to another. Beneath the oldest growth lies a thin black felt of preserved tissue, not the deepening soil of an ordinary wetland.

There are no insects over the pools, no larvae beneath their surfaces, no fish disturbing their reflections. No birds cross the gorges. There are no people, either. The absence of animal life is not immediately obvious because the landscape is so loud; only later does one notice that the noise contains no call, wingbeat, footstep, or voice. The air smells of wet mineral, ozone, cold green sap, and a little machine oil—never of musk, droppings, or decay.

And yet the Upper Works is urban.

Its central district can be crossed on foot in less than an hour. Broad paved streets meet at right angles between brick pumping houses, concrete turbine halls, warehouses with sawtooth roofs, and substations fenced in riveted iron. Gutters empty into ceramic-lined drains. Electric tram rails run flush with the pavement. Elevated pipes cross the streets on dark gantries, ticking faintly as water moves through them. Some narrow buildings resemble tenements until one looks through their wired windows and sees stacked condenser plates or ranks of batteries instead of rooms.

Beyond the last block, the city does not exactly stop. Its organization continues without its buildings.

Surveyed avenues run out across empty rock as paired cuts in the stone. Numbered power poles march toward addresses separated by tens or hundreds of kilometers. A solitary pumping station may stand at the center of an otherwise unoccupied plain, with paved approaches on all four sides and street signs at a crossing no vehicle needs to make. Farther on, a freight depot occupies the lip of a gorge; beyond that, nothing but a humming transmission line and the enormous distance to the next installation. It is one settlement by infrastructure, not by proximity.

Nothing appears ruined or abandoned. Paint remains intact. Rust exists only as fixed orange halos around certain bolt heads. The loading platforms are clean, the windows unbroken, the cistern covers closed. There are no belongings because there are no inhabitants to own them. Low electric freight carriages move through the streets without drivers, carrying castings, spooled wire, ceramic insulators, and drums of mineral salts. At the factory gates, equivalent streams of material return to be melted, drawn, crushed, or dissolved again. It is production without accumulation: industry whose final product is the continuation of its own arrangement.

Water and machinery form a single anatomy. Seepage feeds high reservoirs; reservoirs feed penstocks; falling water turns turbines in the cold lower halls. Pumps lift part of the flow back toward the summit cisterns, while immense condensers draw more moisture from the passing air. Deep engines supplement the circuit with heat brought up from far below the cold surface, and white vapor issues steadily from the stacks. Vegetation crowds the warm, wet discharge channels but stops abruptly where the mineral balance changes. Pipes vanish into the shield and emerge days later in another district, still carrying the same pressure.

The whole system can be heard long before much of it can be seen.

At great distance, the industrial provinces merge into a low note transmitted through wet rock, felt in the teeth and in the soles of the feet. Closer, the note separates into components: the slow concussion of piston pumps, the high insistence of turbines, water hammer moving through a buried main, fans driving air across condenser fins, freight wheels striking rail joints, transformers humming behind their fences. Open spillways add a broad white roar. In narrow clefts, falling water and machinery reinforce one another until the sound seems to have physical weight.

The emptiness does not soften it. There are too few buildings, too few groves, too little absorbent ground to catch and scatter the noise. It travels outward across bare pavement and returns from distant cliffs as a delayed, simplified version of itself. Nothing rises for a shift change. Nothing winds down. There are no alarms, because there are no emergencies to announce.

That is the underlying law of the Upper Works: nothing is still, but nothing happens.

Water travels without lowering its source. Turbines rotate without wearing their bearings. Freight moves without congestion or arrival becoming an event. Fronds bow in the steady exhaust of the fans, but no new frond unfolds and no old one falls. Rain strikes the fresh scree without shifting it. Clouds retain their shapes because vapor is continually streaming through them. For every flow there is a return; for every gradient, some engine or seep maintains the difference.

The rock remains young because its sharpness is never negotiated away. The city remains operational because operation does not lead anywhere. The marshes remain lush without spreading. The daylight remains ordinary without becoming morning or dusk. A wheel has a next position; the world does not have a next state.

At the outermost curb, where the pavement ends above a wet, green fault and only the transmission wires continue into the empty distance, the scale of the place becomes clear. Behind lies the full noise of the city. Ahead there may be no building for a hundred kilometers, though the wires hum as confidently as if the next district were just beyond sight. Water gathers on the railing. The cold does not deepen. Far off, the stationary rain continues falling into its appointed basin.

The Upper Works is not frozen, and it is not waiting. It is running at full volume, in equilibrium.
