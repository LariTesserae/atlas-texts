## The Black Catchment

The high country has no sky. Its cirques, passes, and summits are submerged, and above the summits the water continues into darkness. There is no surface to break, no shoreline from which to judge the extent of the flood. The cold holds the water just on the liquid side of freezing. If this place began in catastrophe, it has long since outlived that condition. Its channels are established, its gardens mature, its ruins worn into the grammar of the terrain. Nothing here is waiting to drain.

The Catchment occupies a whole mountain province: several drowned cirques separated by blunt ridges, hanging valleys suspended over a central gorge, and long slopes of debris descending into water too dark to measure. The rock is mostly black schist, split into broad, sloping plates, with ribs of pale quartzite standing through it. There are no fresh scars. Even the highest summits have lost their sharpness. Water and ice have rounded their corners, and the exposed stone is fluted in long, parallel grooves. Old moraines survive as low banks beneath the current. Around them, gravel has been sorted into ribbons—coarse black fragments along the outside of each bend, fine pale sand in the lee, silt deposited in thin, sigmoid fans.

Height and depth do not agree here. The water grows heavier as one ascends. Pressure gathers over the ridges and becomes greatest above the summits, so that climbing is also a kind of descent. The central gorge, though far below, is comparatively buoyant and still. A stone dislodged there may settle slowly; the same stone carried over a pass will be driven downward with enough force to strike sparks from quartz.

This inversion gives the region its weather. For part of each long cycle, currents collect in the low basins and run uphill. They move through gullies, pour over saddles, and disappear into the oppressive water above. Later the water returns as rain: colder, denser water falling through the surrounding liquid in ropes, sheets, and enormous slow drops. For a while these remain distinct from what they fall through. They bend around outcrops, strike the vegetation, and burst silently into smaller descending masses. The rain may continue long after the uphill current has stopped.

Vegetation follows the mineral seeps and the sheltered sides of the old moraines. Where it takes hold, it is extraordinarily lush. Veilgrass rises in crowded, supple columns, each plant putting out dozens of broad ribbons that fold lengthwise against the current. Beneath it grow thick cushions of stone-moss, their outer layers hard with trapped silt and their interiors soft, branching, and almost transparent. Cup-leaf spreads over the larger boulders, opening shallow, pale vessels that catch mineral dust during the ascent and close when the rain begins. In the oldest cirques, fallen fronds have accumulated into black, springy beds many layers deep. New growth rises through them while the oldest tissues are slowly compressed below.

None of this depends on sunlight. The plants draw on the bitter seepage from the rock and on the faint electrical charge produced where water shears across schist. Their growth is very slow. Their abundance is the abundance of great age and repeated survival, not of a brief warm season. Cold would split ordinary tissue; theirs remains dense and flexible, and ice tends to form as a clear external rind rather than within the stems.

Lushness is local. Beyond a grove there may be nothing but bare fluted stone and open black water. Entire high slopes carry only a few hairlike tufts in their deepest fractures. The central gorge is emptier still, its visible edges unsoftened by growth. A dense stand of veilgrass, an isolated hut, and a single quartzite pinnacle may be separated by distances in which no other presence interrupts the dark. The region is full of processes, but sparse in things.

There is no animal life in the water, the sediment, or the hollow stems. Nothing grazes the old gardens, burrows through their fallen layers, or carries their spores from place to place. Reproduction belongs to fragmentation, release, and accident. The uphill current lifts spores and broken growing tips; the rain redistributes them. Most come to nothing on scoured rock. A few lodge in seeps and begin the slow work of making another grove. Plants die through burial, freezing, uprooting, or the gradual failure of a mineral seam. When a grove collapses, nothing consumes it. Its tissues remain for a long time in the cold, darkening and compacting until a later current takes them apart.

The darkness is nearly complete. At the turning of the water, the upper blackness sometimes acquires a dim iron-grey cast, just strong enough to distinguish a nearby ridge from open liquid. This is the closest thing to day. At other times there is no reliable visual horizon, and the pale quartzite is no easier to find than the schist. Distance is more readily known through pressure, resistance, and sound.

Especially sound. The Catchment is loud without being continuously deafening. Water moving through the high passes maintains a deep roar that seems to come from the rock as much as from the current. Sand drawn over stone adds a lower grinding note. Ice forming in submerged fractures ticks, tightens, and then breaks with hard reports that travel far beyond their source. Large beds of veilgrass hiss as their ribbons close; loose stems knock together with a dry, irregular percussion. The central gorge returns these sounds late and often from the wrong direction.

When the rain arrives, the separate noises lose their boundaries. Heavy water drums on the moraines and booms against the submerged cliffs. A stone cell may begin to hum. The whole mass of a ridge can carry the vibration, so that the rain seems to be falling inside it. Yet none of the sound is a call, a warning, or a response. Nothing approaches because it has heard something else. Nothing flees. The apparent movements in the darkness are only water, ice, vegetation, and stone.

Built things are rare enough to be mistaken at first for accidents of erosion. In one cirque there is a single corbelled cell tucked against the lee of a moraine, its courses hand-fitted without mortar. Its doorway is low and narrow, and its threshold has been worn into a shallow hollow. Elsewhere, seventeen stone steps climb the side of a submerged spur and end beneath an overhang. No platform survives above them. Farther along the ridge stand three small cairns, widely separated, each pinned together by a green corrosion of hammered copper.

Occasional artifacts lie near these places: a stone bowl with a pecked interior, a flaked adze dulled by silt, a length of plant-fiber cord preserved in cold mud. There are no machines, no extensive metalwork, no continuous road, and no pattern dense enough to suggest a town. Whatever settlement once existed has not left a center. The makers left no explanation of why the doorways all face away from the ascending current, or why the stair was built to terminate in blank rock. Their works are now simply among the obstacles around which sediment gathers.

The cycles continue through them. During the ascent, root mats strain silt from the water and the high gardens release their spores. Clear ice forms in shadowed fractures, wedging open seams that will later admit more roots. During the rain, old fronds are stripped away, gravel shifts one ribbon farther around a bend, and another grain is taken from a step or doorway. Deposition replaces much of what erosion removes. The gorge remains open; the moraines remain low banks; the gardens migrate almost imperceptibly along their seeps. Each cycle leaves the province changed in detail and nearly identical in form.

Then the upper darkness closes. Somewhere beyond sight, a pass begins to roar again. Loose spores lift from the basin and start their long ascent toward the summits, where the pressure is greatest and the water is most black. The old stair stands in the gathering current, silt pouring gently over its worn edges. Above it, below it, and in every direction around it, there is only more water.
