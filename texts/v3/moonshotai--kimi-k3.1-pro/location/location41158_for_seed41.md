### The Breathing Hollow

Beneath a shelf of rust-colored caprock, where the surface is little more than hot dust and scattered trees, a narrow collapse opens into a single chamber. It is perhaps seventy paces long, with three shallow alcoves and a roof high enough to disappear into dimness. No passages continue from it. The hollow is a complete place: descended into, crossed, and surrounded.

The heat is its first boundary. It rises from the floor in dry, steady waves and remains even before dawn, when the opening above has cooled. The walls are old limestone, fluted by water that no longer runs openly. Every edge has been softened. Ochre calcite skins the lower stone; higher up, the rock is gray and velvety with age. There is almost no fresh rubble. Whatever falling and breaking made this room happened long ago, and the present processes—wear, deposition, the pressure of roots—have settled into a near balance.

It is not a wet cave. Much of the floor is covered with warm, fine powder, and whole stretches of wall will not yield a drop. Moisture has particular addresses.

At night the exposed caprock cools while the deep stone stays hot. Warm vapor rising from seams below condenses on the cooler roof, gathering in beads along the old flutes. Toward morning these release, one at a time. Some fall directly into small natural basins; most land on roots. Occasional storms replenish the system, sending brief brown threads down the walls. Between storms, the same water seems to circulate from rock to plant to animal to air, seldom reaching the floor.

The vegetation follows these descending routes rather than spreading evenly. From the roof opening hang roots of the surface trees, pale and smooth where they enter, darkening and dividing as they find damp stone. They form dense curtains, some fused into columns, others loose enough to stir when the hollow breathes. Beneath them grow ferns in close, dripping colonies: bronze fiddleheads, bottle-green fronds, small round leaves with waxy surfaces. Their tiers overlap so closely that a drop may pass through five plants without ever touching rock. Moss pads the junctions. Fungal felts, cinnamon and ivory, occupy the deeper shade.

Between these colonies are islands of completely bare stone. This makes the lushness more pronounced. One can stand on warm dust with nothing at ankle height and, an arm’s length away, be unable to see through the layered growth.

The light never becomes strong. At noon, reflected sky enters the roof slit and gives the central colony a muted pewter glow; the alcoves remain nearly black. Colors are consequently subdued, but surfaces are not. Wet leaf, dry calcite, polished root, mineral dust, and the soft bloom of fungi each hold the dim light differently. Near the basins the air smells of iron and crushed stems. Elsewhere it smells faintly sweet, like warm bark and fermentation.

The hollow first appears uninhabited because so much of its life is motionless and because none of it calls.

Then a patch of fern resolves into snails, dozens of them, their shells the color of old bone. Beetles graze the fungal films in crowded, shining lines. Small crickets move through the root curtains without singing; they touch antennae, or drum so lightly against a stem that the message travels through the plant rather than the air. At dusk, moths detach themselves from the warm roots where they have rested wing to wing. Their rising is visible before it is comprehensible: a pale loosening of the surface, then hundreds of separate flights.

Rust-colored geckos wait head-down among the fern ribs. Slender snakes occupy the upper roots, so still that their scales resemble irregularities in the bark. Bats enter after dark and take moths near the roof without a cry audible in the chamber. Below, centipedes and soft-bodied hunters work through the fallen leaves. Nothing is large. The abundance is in numbers and layers, in the number of lives that can occupy a single root junction or the space beneath one curled frond.

The loudest ordinary sound is a drop striking a leaf. Sometimes there is the faint tick of a beetle losing its foothold, or the dry brush of wings against wings. The moss, root mats, and packed growth swallow the rest. Yet the quiet is not stillness. At dawn the dripping colonies seem to loosen and brighten; by midday heat drives many animals into the stone joints; at dusk the upper chamber fills with flight. A carcass left on the floor is reduced and carried away with astonishing speed. Fronds are continually being eaten, roots continually extending by almost imperceptible increments.

There are a few built things. A low arc of fitted blocks surrounds one basin, though part of it has been engulfed by roots. Four worn steps descend from it and end against a blank wall. Farther away lies a single threshold-like slab, half buried in powder. No complete structure remains, and nothing here has been maintained. The masonry has weathered to the same softness as the natural stone. Mineral crust and roots now hold it together as much as they pull it apart. Whatever purpose the basin once served, its present one is clear: it catches roof-water, and a dense little world has gathered around that fact.

Rare rain changes the whole arrangement. For an hour or two the separate damp places connect. Basins overflow into shallow runnels; dust darkens; the heat lifts a smell of iron and wet leaves through the chamber. Animals crowd the margins, some drinking, others hunting those that come to drink. The sound is still only a whisper. Then the threads fail. Water retreats first from the floor, then from the open stone, remaining in root cups, deep basins, and the bodies of the colony. Dark marks on the walls record levels that may not be reached again for years.

Heat comes from below, light from above, and water mostly from the narrow exchange between them. Life occupies that exchange. From the surface, the hollow is only a fissure exhaling warm air and a few roots disappearing into shade. Below, in the near-silence, thousands of small movements continue, none of them needing to become loud in order to fill the room.
