## The Basin Made Once

Everything in the basin is handmade, including the things that have since become alive.

It lies low and open beneath a white, sourceless noon: a valley-sized expanse of shallow lakes, water stairs, reed islands, clay causeways, and groves separated by broad reaches of empty pavement. There are no hills. The highest points are roof platforms and garden terraces, seldom more than a few trees tall, and from them the land appears almost level. Water and vegetation interrupt the whiteness without ever conquering it. A narrow canal leads to a wide, vacant court; beyond the court stands one dense grove; beyond the grove, a solitary shelter and a long sequence of stepped pools. The distances are generous. Nothing crowds anything else.

There is no natural floor beneath it. The black soil of the planting beds was compounded from crushed charcoal, fiber, and broken pottery. Below that lie mats of split cane, packed clay, timber grills, older pavements. No excavation has ever reached bedrock. There is no bedrock to reach. Even the low rim at the basin’s farthest visible edge is built, a long bank of hand-pressed blocks continuing beyond sight. Wild country does not begin on the other side. There is only more made ground.

The materials retain the irregularities of simple work. Clay slabs bear finger grooves and the shallow drag of stone scrapers. Plaster was smoothed with palms rather than floats. Cords were twisted from bark fiber; wooden pins secure joints where a metal nail would otherwise be expected. At close range, nothing is perfectly straight. Yet the work is extraordinarily crisp. Canal copings have sharp corners. Reeds end in pale, freshly cut planes. The edges of spillways have not rounded, though warm water slides continually over them. Roots enter masonry without forcing it apart. Nothing crumbles, sags, silts up, or acquires the softening mantle of age.

The basin is not recently completed. Its freshness is not a matter of elapsed time.

Occasionally, the handiwork repeats too exactly. The same thumbprint—a hooked ridge, a small scar interrupting the whorl—appears on blocks a day’s walk apart. Two widely separated pavilions lean by the same barely perceptible amount. A knot in a causeway’s foundation recurs in the roots of an island tree. It begins to seem that the region was not produced by making many objects, but by making a few objects properly and allowing the act of making to continue without further supervision.

The light never changes. No sun crosses the sky; there is no visible sun to cross it. Brightness comes evenly from the white heights and, in the shallow courts, seems also to rise from the pale floors beneath the water. Shadows collect directly beneath things as soft, saturated pools. Leaves glow at their edges. Wet plaster is painful to look at for long. There is no dawn blue, no afternoon gold, no approach of weather.

Heat fills the light. It is humid and steady, hot enough that cloth remains damp against the body and sweat does not dry. The air smells of wet clay, green sap, crushed reed, and the heavy sweetness of white flowers. Condensation beads on shaded vessels and on the undersides of stone seats. In the groves, the glare diminishes but the heat does not. There is no cool current moving through the shade, no promise of evening.

Water is everywhere near the surface. It stands in broad mirrors only ankle-deep, turns slowly in darker basins, and threads the pavement in incised channels. Vertical walls sweat. Beads gather beneath steps and fall without a sound. A foot placed beside a causeway may squeeze clear water from what looked like dry jointing. The level of every pool remains constant, though water enters and leaves them.

Not all of it obeys slope. On one long stair, a thin sheet climbs each riser, gathers at the top, and disappears into a seam, while the pool below neither rises nor falls. Elsewhere two canals cross at the same level, their waters passing through one another without mixing: one remains green with reflected leaves, the other colorless over white clay. If a hand is drawn through the crossing, both currents wrap around the wrist and resume their courses.

The water does not babble. Even where it drops the height of a person, it makes no more noise than fabric being drawn across fabric.

Vegetation arrives in concentrated excess. Between groves there may be nothing but white ground and a single line of water; inside them, leaves overlap until the paths become green tunnels. Tall reeds hold feathery heads above the canals. Stilt-rooted trees stand over black pools. Broad, ribbed leaves shine with condensation, ferns fill every shaded joint, and vines hang in heavy loops from platforms that were perhaps built only to support them. Lotus-like flowers float with their petals fully open. Their scent is almost edible in the heat.

Close examination reveals the same double nature found elsewhere. A fern’s central stem has the spiral seam of twisted cord. Bark bears faint impressions of basket weave. The veins on the underside of a leaf converge into something like a potter’s thumb-mark. Seedpods appear to have been pierced with a bone awl. But these are not sculptures. Cut stems bleed sap. Leaves tear. Roots inhale and exhale in the mud. Flowers attract insects, and fruit is sweet. At some point the workmanship ceased to be representation and acquired metabolism.

The animals are equally alive and equally numerous, though the openness of the basin keeps them from seeming crowded. Silver fish turn in loose shoals over pale floors. Transparent eels occupy the seams between pavement and water. Tortoises rest on the lower steps, their shells marked with concentric grooves like coil-built vessels. Red-legged marsh birds move through the reeds. Small blue frogs cling beneath leaves, and dragonflies hang far apart over the open courts. Occasionally a dark, otter-like animal slides from one canal into another, or a soft-hoofed browser steps out of a grove to crop the same bright shoot at the water’s edge.

They respond to disturbance. Birds abandon a fishing place when approached; fish scatter from a shadow; a tortoise withdraws its head. Nothing about them is clockwork. Yet their abundance never becomes a multitude, and their activity never becomes noise. The frogs do not chorus. Insects are visible in hundreds but produce only the faintest dry hum. Wings clap once, far away. At long intervals a marsh bird gives a single wooden note, and no other bird answers.

Most of what a visitor hears is therefore their own body: breath, wet cloth, a foot separating from clay, water disturbed at an ankle. These sounds do not echo. The empty courts take them in, and the quiet closes again.

At first that quiet feels like suspension before an event—a storm, a migration, the return of the makers. Nothing arrives. The basin is not waiting.

Nothing in it is motionless, but nothing in it changes.

Water circulates; gills pump; animals feed; leaves open toward the light. These movements are features of the place, as color and texture are features. They do not accumulate into history. The lake does not become shallower. The grove does not spread across the empty court. Fruit does not rot into soil, banks do not slump, and sharp edges never lose their arrises. Heat does not build toward afternoon. There are eggs in certain nests and old shells beneath certain trees, but they do not establish a before and an after. They belong to those places in the same way the nests and trees do.

The basin can be touched, entered, even briefly damaged, but it cannot be revised. A corner broken from a clay slab remains solid and heavy in the hand. When the slab is next seen, however, it is whole. The fragment does not vanish. The design has simply been completed again. Footprints remain while they are being made; the walked causeway behind is unmarked. A picked flower may continue to wilt in a traveler’s fingers while its likeness stands open on the stem.

Erosion would be a form of revision. So would decay, succession, construction, and ruin. The basin admits none of them for long.

Its simplest marks seem also to be its deepest rules. A groove made idly in clay remains a groove. The same groove cut with the patient, repeated gestures of a channel-maker may begin to gather dampness. A correctly tied knot can remain under tension across an entire reed island. Carelessly copied forms stay inert; exact ones sometimes continue beyond their makers. This may account for the impossible extent of the work. A settlement need not contain multitudes, or possess engines, if reality has learned to repeat what a few hands knew how to do.

The scattered shelters offer little explanation. They are open, airy structures of cane and timber, each far from the next. Some contain a stone adze, a bone needle, a shallow bowl, coils of cord laid out as though someone has just stood up. There are no heaps of waste, no worn thresholds, no smoke stains, no crowded center from which the basin might have been governed. The tools may have built it. They may also be part of what a completely built landscape requires in order to resemble something built.

After enough walking, the openness stops feeling like ordinary distance. It becomes interval: the space between repetitions of one held act. Pools, gardens, stairs, and empty courts are not stages toward a center. There is no center to reach, no far edge beyond which original earth resumes. The region continues in bright, wet equilibrium, made all the way down and alive all the way through.

Behind the traveler, the causeway is clean. Ahead, a drop of warm water climbs a white step without sound. A red-legged bird watches from the reeds, then resumes fishing.

The light has not moved.
