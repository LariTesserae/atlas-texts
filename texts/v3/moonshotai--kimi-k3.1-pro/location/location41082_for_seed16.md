## The Thrum

From above, there is almost nothing to find: a low salt pan, white at noon and violet-gray before dawn, with a faint dark seam crossing it like a hairline crack in pottery. The seam is warmer than the surrounding ground. Dust sometimes rises from it in a thin, straight thread, though there is no wind.

Beneath it lies a single hollow, and within the hollow, a town.

The chamber is only a few hundred paces across, its floor set a little below the surrounding plain. It has no towers and no deep descending levels. Everything is low, compressed, and slightly sunken, as though the town had been built inside a closed hand. Most of its volume is empty air. Buildings cling to the perimeter in separated groups, leaving a broad central vacancy where nothing grows and nothing stands. The streets do not converge on a center; they bend around the absence.

Even so, it is unmistakably urban. There are lanes with curbs, shared stairways, small courts overlooked by doorways, workshop yards, storage chambers, and public kilns. A covered walk crosses one cluster of roofs on piers no thicker than a person’s waist. Clay conduits run beneath the paving and emerge as flues along the walls. Some were made to carry hot air; others are fitted with gratings and traps for drifting sand. There are no wells, cisterns, or rain channels. Nothing in the town’s infrastructure acknowledges water.

Its construction is patient and handmade. Walls are dry-laid from dark stone and ochre mineral brick, with seams closed by black plant resin. Stone hinges support clay shutters. Fiber lashings, tightened long ago and now hard as horn, hold together racks and ventilating frames. In the workshops lie hammerstones, drilling bows, grinding slabs, and blades of flaked glassy rock. The most elaborate mechanisms are counterweights, treadles, and shutters lifted by a strong draft. Handles are polished where hands once closed around them, but no present hand maintains anything.

Nor does the place look freshly abandoned. Its ruination, if it can be called that, finished long ago. Fallen blocks have been rounded into cobbles. Thresholds are shallowly dished, corners softened, and every sharp inscription blurred by the passage of dry grains. Dust has settled into stable slopes against the walls. Where roots entered mortar joints, they thickened and now hold the masonry together. The town is old without being fragile.

Daylight enters through the salt seam after travelling along a crooked mineral fault. By the time it reaches the chamber, it is only a dim pewter glow. At its brightest, noon resembles late dusk. The salt ledges remain chalk-pale, while doorways stay black and the upper surfaces of walls seem to dissolve into shadow.

The dimness gives no relief from heat. Heat rises through the floor, radiates from the walls, and accumulates beneath the roof until the air has a faint visible grain. It smells of hot ceramic, crushed pepper, salt, and the bitter oil exposed when a stem breaks. Nothing is damp. Even in the deepest fissures, the dust remains loose and powder-dry. There is no condensation during the less-hot hours, no hidden film of moisture, no water locked in the vegetation.

Yet along the warm outer walls, growth is luxuriant.

Black-green leaves open in overlapping fans, each edged with a fine bronze seam. Wine-dark fronds hang from the covered walk in curtains thick enough to conceal whole doorways. Between them lie cushions of olive-colored filaments and stands of jointed, ochre canes. Their tissues are filled with wax and oil rather than watery sap. Their leaves feel substantial but not soft: polished, tough, and slightly warm. White roots etch branching lines across the salt, drawing sustenance from minerals and from the chemical differences between the hot floor and the air above it.

This lushness is accumulated time rather than hurried growth. A curtain of fronds may be the work of centuries. New leaves appear slowly, but the chamber is old, and almost nothing removes them. Dead growth does not rot into softness. It hardens into dark lace, splits under thermal stress, and is gradually abraded to powder. Roots reclaim part of that powder; the rest joins the dust in the streets.

From the central vacancy, however, the vegetation is only a narrow, intricate fringe. Most distances here contain nothing. There are long intervals between roofs, between stems, even between drifting motes. A seed released at one wall can cross nearly the whole town before touching anything. The hollowness is not an absence within the place. It is the place’s largest feature.

No animal life interrupts it. There are no insects among the seed heads, no birds in the flues, no burrows beneath the paving, no nests in the sheltered roofs. Pollen travels without carriers. Seeds disperse without being eaten. Nothing responds to a footstep except the town’s echoes.

And the town is frequently loud.

As the salt pan heats above, the air in the chamber expands and begins a long exhalation through the conduits. First comes a low tone beneath the paving. Then separate flues join it, each at its own pitch, until the whole hollow produces a grinding, resonant roar. It is not deafening, but it is far too large for the visible town. The central emptiness gives the sound back, deepening it.

The draft lifts sand from the floor in narrow sheets. Fronds turn edge-on and stream toward the walls. Counterweighted shutters rise, hesitate, and knock against their stone stops. Dry seed capsules begin to rattle. At one particular resonance, many of them open together with a noise like handfuls of gravel thrown onto tile. Pale seeds and yellow pollen enter the dim light, hang for a moment in the hot air, and are carried across the hollow.

That timing is no longer accidental. Pods that open during the great exhalation cast their seeds farthest; generation after generation has favored valves sensitive to the town’s note. The old ventilation system has become an ecological clock. When the pressure reverses, other plants release a finer dust of spores into the returning current. Between these events, smaller processes continue: roots split mineral grains, clay pipes tick with expansion, sand climbs and leaves a ramp, and oil-heavy leaves slowly turn their reflective undersides toward the hottest wall.

By the end of each cycle, some material has been worn away and nearly as much has been deposited or bound into roots. Streets shift by fractions. Wall lines hold. The town and its vegetation are not destroying one another, nor have they reached stillness. They have settled into an active balance.

When the exhalation weakens, individual sounds emerge from the roar: a late pod snapping, a shutter tapping twice, a stone contracting somewhere under the floor. The heat remains. The dim growth lifts again along the walls.

The Thrum is not waiting for rain, repair, or inhabitants. This hot, hollow, loudly vegetated condition is not a stage in its recovery. It is what the place became when it finished becoming itself.
