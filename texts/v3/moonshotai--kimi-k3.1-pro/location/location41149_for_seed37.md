## The Unfinished Hollow

The Unfinished Hollow lies less than a body’s depth beneath the soil, sealed in a lens of pale marl. It is not the entrance to a cave system. There are no passages leading away, no deeper chambers, no promise of an underworld. Its whole extent would fit inside a small room. A root-thin seam sometimes connects it to the surface, though the seam does not always open in the same place.

Calling it a cave gives it too much history. The walls have not been smoothed by water or time. Their fractures are fresh and angular; their edges crumble into loose grains that have never been transported, rounded, or deposited anywhere else. Here and there the marl is still deciding between sediment and stone. Clear calcite buds in the damp seams, while other seams remain soft enough to take the impression of a passing snail. At one end, a thin crystalline rib is slowly knitting the wall shut. Beneath it, the hollow continues to open.

Yet the chamber is full of daylight.

Not a beam from the seam, not phosphorescence, but broad, clean brightness, white with a little gold in it, brighter than an overcast noon. It has no visible source and brings no warmth. The air remains cool, with a mineral freshness that touches the back of the throat. Wet clay smells faintly sweet; dry grit smells of crushed shells. Shadows are shallow, silvery things. Some arrive a moment after the animal that casts them.

Most of the hollow is emptiness. The floor forms a shallow, uneven dish, interrupted by a few widely separated flakes of raw stone. There are no heaps of debris, no crowded growths, no tangle of roots. The central space is so vacant that the small occurrences around its margins—a descending droplet, a turning mite, a grain loosening from the wall—acquire the importance of weather.

Water never gathers into a stream. After rain above, one section of wall darkens, and moisture advances through it in branching stains. At other times condensation appears without any seep: single beads on the undersides of stones, a cold film on the calcite, three or four shallow pockets in hollows no larger than spoon bowls. The largest pool would barely cover a palm. Its surface is often crowded with the reflections of creatures that have not yet come to drink.

Drops fall at long intervals. Each makes a small, clear tick, with no echo unless the echo comes first. Now and then a drop pauses on the floor, draws itself into a perfect sphere, and returns to the ceiling. The damp crescent beneath it remains.

Vegetation is almost an accident. There is one hair-fine green filament beside the seep, a scale of liverwort no bigger than a fingernail, and a pallid rootlet that has entered through the seam and stopped, as though listening. Nothing else is visibly green. The pasture of the hollow is instead made of colorless microbial skins, thinner than reflections, spreading wherever damp mineral and light coincide.

Animal life is abundant, though never crowded. Glassy springtails graze the clear skins, leaving looping tracks visible only when the clay darkens. Blind mites investigate their shed husks. Threadworms move through the wet grit like living cracks. Tiny snails carry shells so transparent that their bodies show inside as curled, moving commas. Under two separate stones, pseudoscorpions wait with their small claws open, hours apart in temperament and appetite. A milk-pink salamander, no longer than a finger, occupies the cool margin of the pool. It is the largest animal and the least often seen.

Rain starts the quickest cycle. The seep spreads; the microbial skins thicken; springtails emerge from pores in the wall and begin to feed. Worms rise toward the new moisture. The snails follow, and behind them come the predators. As the wall dries, the whole procession contracts toward the remaining water. Then, for a while, almost nothing moves. Even at its busiest, the hollow retains its distances: one hunter beneath a stone, three snails on a wall, a brief constellation of springtails around a drop. Abundance is revealed by attention, not by mass.

Only three things suggest construction. Near the northern curve of wall stands a thumb-high crescent of fitted flakes, without mortar. Farther away, a narrow slab lies across level grit like the threshold of a doorway that has never enclosed anything. Beside the pool are three straight incisions, parallel at their beginnings and gently diverging before they end. All are scattered, partial, and inexplicably fresh. Nothing here bears a chisel mark. There is no worked metal, no device, no object shaped to perform a task. The glass-snails occasionally leave grit along the crescent, but they do not build it. The structures feel less like the remains of builders than like the hollow’s first thoughts about walls.

The slower cycle concerns the stone itself.

At irregular intervals, the brightness becomes denser. The walls turn luminous, the floor loses its grittiness, and without any sound of collapse the chamber is simply solid marl again. Not filled: solid. The animals remain visible for that interval as minute, lit silhouettes suspended within it, uncrushed and continuing their small movements. Then hollowness returns.

Afterward there is usually a little more space. A new flake lies on the floor, sharp as the instant of its appearance. A pool has shifted by the width of a hand. The crescent may possess one additional stone. Rarely, the threshold stands slightly farther inside the chamber than the chamber is deep.

This is how erosion proceeds here. Stone is not patiently worn away; it intermittently agrees to have been absent.

Other sequences are similarly unreliable. A wet ring appears before water gathers within it. Tiny tracks cross the grit and end at a solid wall from which, much later, a snail emerges. The salamander’s shadow sometimes completes a hunt while the salamander is still motionless beneath its ledge. Nothing in the hollow can safely be called ancient. Even the remains may precede whatever they remain from.

And yet the place is not chaotic. Moisture gathers and retreats. Clear skins spread and are grazed. Young animals hatch, hunt, molt, and hide. Crystals close one seam while another opens. The scattered architectural marks persist through changes that should erase them. All these processes continue in near silence, beneath light that never dims.

The hollow is a landscape rehearsing its own existence: dampness learning weather, raw sediment learning an edge, animals learning an ecology, empty stone learning the difference between a wall and a room. When the last visible drop draws itself back into the ceiling, the cool brightness remains. For a moment nothing stirs. Then, somewhere inside the solid-looking wall, a small claw ticks against a surface that has not yet opened.
