## The Included City

From outside, the Included City is easy to overlook: a cold bead of water clinging beneath a black ceramic sill, smaller than a grain of rice. It does not drip, evaporate, or gleam. Magnification reveals no towers inside it, no tiny streets, only water with a slightly complicated refractive edge. Now and then the bead produces a hard, dry tick, though nothing around it has moved.

To pass through that edge is not to become smaller. It is to be *included*.

Inside, the bead contains an entire low basin, wide enough to have near and far districts, weather of its own, and a horizon interrupted by construction. The interior does not feel miniaturized. Extent here is a relationship rather than a quantity the outer world can measure; the city remains small only to things that have not been given a place in it.

The basin lies under plain white light, much like ordinary daylight in a flooded industrial hall. It is strong enough to show the blue-grey floor, cast soft shadows beneath ledges, and pick out silver distortions in moving water. There is no abyssal blue gloom, no neon, no constellation of luminous organisms. The boundary admits illumination and repeats it through the interior without visible lamps. The result is almost mundane: clear working visibility, true colors, readable surfaces.

Water occupies everything. It fills the streets, rooms, locks, conduits, and apparent sky. There are no dry roofs or air-filled service tunnels. Even the acoustic chambers contain water, held in a different mode of stillness. Streets are not canals cut through a city so much as routes along which the surrounding liquid is permitted to move. Doors are pressure decisions. Windows are changes in refractive index. A wall may be solid ceramic on one side and, on the other, only a firmly maintained instruction that water must not pass.

The water is cool rather than bitterly cold, with the controlled chill of a shaded cistern. It is exceptionally clear. Distance is judged not by murk but by the accumulation of edges: one transparent wall behind another, pipework seen through a foundation, terraces repeated in curved reflections until the near city seems to be standing inside several versions of itself.

Its topography is modest. The floor tilts gently through three broad terraces into a central depression. The structural stacks are low and wide, bundled together rather than rising as isolated towers. There are no hills, cliffs, or distant high ground. But the lowness of the place should not be mistaken for softness. Everything is young.

The basin was unfolded recently and is still unfolding at its margins. Floor panels meet at unrounded edges. Fresh cuts have pale interiors. Expansion joints are crisp enough to catch the light in continuous white lines, and narrow faults terminate in sharp glassy splinters. There is no soil, no deep silt, no bed of rounded rubble. When a surface begins to wear, its material is usually recalled and printed again before age can become texture. Even the oldest-looking districts have the clean severity of something newly cut.

The urban fabric is packed so tightly that, from certain angles, it first appears to be one solid mass. Blocks share walls; walls carry channels; smaller channels run inside the linings of those channels. Pump chambers fit beneath switching yards, switching yards beneath reservoirs of deliberately still water, and transit passages thread through the thickness of access ramps. Roofs serve as foundations for other systems. There are no ornamental voids and almost no permanently open space.

A street may be only a narrow seam of current between two active surfaces. The nearest equivalents to plazas are calibration volumes: temporary rooms of unmoving water that exist for several seconds, perform one measurement, and are then occupied by three different infrastructures. Crowdedness here does not mean disorder. Every volume has been assigned, often more than once.

The most advanced construction is therefore easy to mistake for an optical fault. At peak load, districts are allowed to occupy one another out of phase. A conduit passes through a hall without piercing it; for part of a cycle, both are completely present but mutually unreachable. When the phases slip, ghosted pipework appears inside a floor, or a second set of terraces seems suspended within an already solid block. Two currents cross in the same volume without mixing. These are not projections. A probe striking either structure meets real resistance. The city has not solved its shortage of room by making infinite room. It has simply learned to fill the overlaps as well.

Several cycles keep the basin in constant motion. A fast pressure tide runs through the street-seams, reversing at the gates with blunt concussions. A slower thermal tide sends cool water along the floors while slightly warmer water returns above the roofs. At longer intervals, dense brine descends through the basin in wavering vertical ropes: rain without air. Where those ropes strike the lower terraces, they spread into thin, cold floods, operate banks of valves, and carry dissolved feedstock toward the construction fronts.

Along one margin, a bright seam advances across the ground. Behind it stand new channels, couplings, steps, and interlocking walls, all still ringing faintly as they set. Elsewhere, a district may be unmade without collapsing. It loses detail from the outside in—first its address markings, then its joints, then its surfaces—until the structure becomes a faint milky streak in the current and is carried away. Nothing remains ruined for long. Nothing is allowed to become picturesque with decay.

All of this is loud. Water delivers sound to every surface. The pumps maintain a low, many-noted drone that seems to come from the floor itself. Pressure gates crack like stones struck together. Fresh ceramic ticks and pings as it hardens, and the descending brine makes a tearing hiss where it is combed into separate streams. During a phase change, the packed basin gives a prolonged groan, followed by hundreds of small impacts as overlapping systems reclaim their clearances.

The outer boundary returns whatever sound the city does not absorb. Echoes arrive late, sometimes in the wrong order. A gate may appear to answer before the original impact reaches a listener; a construction front may be accompanied by the thunder of a process already completed somewhere else. These returns are not entirely accidents. The city stores short instructions in reverberation, and certain repairs begin only when their commanding echo comes home. There are no voices in the noise, and nothing that could properly be called music, but the infrastructure is continually answering itself.

Vegetation is sparse enough to be surprising when encountered. A dark olive seam of threadleaf may occupy one fracture and leave the next hundred bare. A few translucent fans root where the brine floods lose speed. Whole terraces support nothing botanical at all. The plants are functional rather than decorative: threadleaf roots make otherwise invisible stress fractures apparent under the ordinary light, while the fans remove a particular metal from the return water. They bend and flatten in the currents, but nothing grazes them. Their growth is cropped by construction, never by mouths.

There is no animal life. Nothing swims between the blocks, burrows into fresh seams, or drifts as larvae in the brine. There are no citizens in the streets and no bodies for whom the city’s passages were scaled. Nor is this an abandoned habitat. It was built without the assumption that a city requires inhabitants. Its shipments consist of material and instruction; its vehicles carry no passengers; its addresses identify operations, not homes. Every moving presence is a gate, a carriage, a current, or a field doing work.

At the fullest part of the cycle, the separate processes briefly coincide. Brine ropes fall through the white light. The margins print new ground. Phased districts pass through one another in dense, transparent layers. The few plants lie flat against their seams. Temporary plazas vanish, every current acquires a conduit, every conduit a second use, and every returning echo a destination. For one instant there is not even an unused pocket of still water.

Then the pressure tide turns, and the city finds its narrow clearances again.

Outside, beneath the ceramic sill, the bead gives a single tick. It looks once more like a drop that has not yet fallen.
