## The Held Sea

The highest country on the planet is underwater.

The Held Sea lies over a shield of black basalt whose average elevation would place it above the cloud decks of smaller worlds. Its great escarpments rise higher than most mountain ranges, yet none rises high enough to make an island. From the thin, cold air above, all that altitude is invisible: there are only steel-gray swells, a white sun, and water continuing to the curvature of the planet.

The light is not strange. Near the surface it is simply daylight, sometimes hard and white, sometimes softened by cloud. It catches the rust-red paint on a depth gauge, the pale silica in a rock fracture, the translucent body of a passing animal. Farther down, it darkens honestly with distance. There is no compensating glow in the water, no luminous vegetation, no blue fire along the bottom.

The water itself is brine held close to freezing. Its cold is less like weather than like a condition of matter. Ice exists, but mainly as clear, permanent skins in sheltered polar hollows and deep joints in the rock. Elsewhere, pressure, salt, and the sea’s ceaseless circulation keep it liquid. Nothing steams. Nothing smells strongly. Even at the surface, spray falls back before wind can carry its scent far.

Beneath it, the land looks unfinished.

The basalt has not been worn into hills. It terminates in blades, conchoidal lips, and sudden vertical faces. Volcanic glass shines along fractures that have never been rounded by sand. Veins of white silica stand proud of the darker rock, not because they are harder than the surrounding stone after ages of weathering, but because the surrounding stone has scarcely been touched at all. There are almost no pebbles. There is no soil, no mud, and in most places no sediment finer than a thin bacterial dust. A slab the size of a city may lie exactly where it fell, its upper edge still sharp enough to split a shell.

This freshness is not the aftermath of a recent catastrophe. It is the normal appearance of the place. The crust is young, the relief is high, and the processes that would ordinarily soften it have never been allowed to gather momentum.

The sea is vast enough to possess weather of its own. At the surface, cyclones wider than continents turn over particular basins. Beneath them stand internal waves, shear zones, and gyres whose boundaries can take years to cross. But these systems do not travel. A storm remains above the same trench; a current follows the same black escarpment; a wall of turbulent water forms and reforms along the same line. Navigators chart them as they would chart mountains.

The Held Sea has weather, but weather is a map rather than a forecast.

For all that water and movement, the overwhelming impression is emptiness. The sea is too clear to have visible depth at close range. A swimmer may turn through every direction and find no bottom, no surface, no animal, no structure—only daylight grading into distance. On the bottom, one white grazer may occupy an entire fracture valley. A pump station may be the only constructed object within a thousand kilometres. Even the largest gyres carry almost nothing visible in them. Emptiness is not an interval between features here. It is the principal feature.

But no part of the Held Sea is quiet.

The sound arrives through rock, water, equipment, and bone. Its lowest component is a planetary vibration, too deep to seem wholly audible, against which the body feels continually braced. Through that runs the regular concussion of enormous pistons. Above both is the tearing hiss of cavitation: intake mouths, turbine blades, pressure veins opening and closing in the current. At the surface, the noise comes out of the horizon rather than from any identifiable direction and makes loose spray tremble on the swells. At depth, there is no possibility of shutting it out. The whole body becomes a listening surface.

Near an engine, the pressure can injure unprotected tissue. Far from one, it can still be worse. The young basalt carries vibration efficiently, and the fixed basins and scarps form standing interference patterns. Some otherwise empty regions are permanent acoustic antinodes, where the roar exceeds anything beside a machine. In others, narrow bands cancel, leaving routes along which the sound is merely overwhelming.

The sources of all this are absurdly small against the landscape.

A typical station is a few hundred metres of black steel fastened directly to a cliff: intake towers, a riveted generating hall, cased flywheels, heat exchangers, and squat electric pumps. Ceramic insulators support cables that disappear into boreholes. Caged service lamps burn beside pressure gauges. Valve wheels stand behind hinged grilles. Nothing floats, nothing glows without a filament, and nothing appears beyond the capacities of a strenuous, well-organized industrial civilization.

Heat from the young crust drives the turbines. The turbines drive generators and circulation pumps. Water is taken in, forced through the machinery, and returned to the sea. Each station’s output is governed to match the others; timing pulses pass through the bedrock. Their combined work keeps the great currents in their fixed courses, distributes heat and dissolved minerals, and prevents the sea from separating into stable layers that could begin to evolve on their own.

The engines are not transforming the world. They are preventing it from changing.

Between stations, construction becomes even fainter: a ladder bolted to an otherwise untouched face; a numbered plate above a fissure; a square hatch with no path leading to it; a concrete anchor holding no cable. There are no cities, ports, roads, or continuous fields of wreckage. The structures are not picturesque ruins. Their machinery still turns. Their marks remain legible because there is almost nothing present to obscure them.

Nothing in the water is green.

There are no weeds on the ledges, no forests in the shallows, no floating mats, no photosynthetic dust softening the basalt. The foundation of the ecology is microbial and nearly invisible. Simple cells live within warm fractures and in films thinner than tarnish, oxidizing sulfur and iron made available by the young crust. Some enter the circulation and become a sparse, colorless food supply. Grazing keeps the exposed films from ever becoming a visible covering.

Animal life is abundant in the planetary census and rare in any glance.

The most numerous open-water animals are transparent, hand-length combs, each little more than a gut, a nerve cord, and rows of beating silica paddles. Most of their body is the water they filter. One may cross a shaft of daylight, flash along its edges, and vanish; hours may pass before another appears.

On the cliffs move pale, jointed grazers with shells deposited from the same silica that fills the fractures. When they close their limbs, they resemble fresh chips of stone. Larger, basket-bodied filterers drift in the permanent currents, some tens of metres long, moving so precisely with the water that they appear moored. Lean black hunters occupy the fixed intersections where prey-bearing currents meet. A hunt is brief, widely separated from the next, and usually witnessed by nothing.

Nearly all of them navigate by the industrial roar. The stations are acoustic mountains; the scarps are echoes; the interference basins are roads and barriers. Where vision fails, the machinery provides a complete, unchanging geography.

The largest animals, the hushbacks, use it differently. Long resonant cavities in their skulls and flanks allow them to absorb narrow bands of engine noise. They do not try to call above the sea. They edit it. A signal is a patterned thinness in the roar, an absence passed from one distant body to another. At close range, a hushback’s presence can make the all-surrounding pressure seem suddenly to acquire a near side and a far side. One layer of sound goes missing. For a few seconds, in a world without quiet, silence becomes a shape.

Nothing here is motionless. Pistons reciprocate, animals feed and die, storms rage in their appointed basins, and the sun rises and sets. But almost nothing accumulates. Heat, salt, microbial production, grazing, and mechanical work remain in close balance. Currents do not wander into new courses. Bare ledges do not become reefs. Rock flour does not gather into plains. A scratch made on an exposed ledge can outlast civilizations elsewhere without becoming part of a younger deposit.

The stability is not the stillness of ice. It is an equilibrium with enormous throughput—a held note rather than a stopped song.

From a high ledge near the surface, the whole character of the place can be taken in at once: black glass dropping into clear gray water, a short red cable disappearing into a drill hole, ordinary daylight on edges that have never been softened. Far off, a pale animal crosses the emptiness and is gone. The cold presses inward. The engines fill the skull. Nothing responds, and nothing needs to.

The Held Sea feels like the first instant after a world’s formation, prolonged by machinery until that instant has become a climate. It is a fresh fracture held open by sound: immense, nearly vacant, and so stable that even its deafening violence has become a form of stillness.
