## The High Remainder

The High Remainder is what is left when a mountain system has had enough time to stop being mountainous.

Its altitude is immense, but its relief is not. Whole provinces lie above the cloud decks, yet there are few peaks, cliffs, or sharp divides. The old ranges have been reduced to long, rounded swells of bedrock. Valleys are shallow and broad, their sides descending so gradually that water can take years to decide which way to run. Even the stones seem finished: cobbles without corners, outcrops polished to the softness of worn steps, gravel sorted into pale seams along channels that have not carried a true flood in ages.

The dominant rock is a dark, fine-grained stone, greenish when wet and almost blue in cold shadow. Veins of white quartz cross it in broken, widely spaced bands. There is little loose soil. What soil exists has gathered in sheltered basins, deep and black beneath the vegetation, or in the joints of old pavement.

The light is ordinary. The sun rises, crosses a pale sky, and sets. Daylight is clear rather than strange; rain makes it gray; night is properly dark. There are no luminous fissures or colored storms to announce that the world has become unreliable. Most of the time, the Remainder looks merely ancient, cold, and very large.

Its scale becomes apparent through weather. Cloud seas move beneath the plateau for weeks without finding an edge. A rain system can occupy one watershed for an entire season while the next remains under motionless, dry air. Shadows travel across country so extensive that dawn in one district can still be visible as a band of sunlight behind the evening clouds of another. No survey has established the plateau’s full extent. Routes that should lead outward eventually describe country already crossed, though approached from directions that did not previously exist.

The cold is persistent. Exposed water skins with ice on most clear nights, and shaded ice can survive through the brighter part of the year. Yet the place is not frozen solid. Its wetness is local and intermittent: condensation on stone, cloud water combed from the air by vegetation, brief cold rains, meltwater moving through a channel for a few hours and then disappearing into bedrock. Tarns occupy some basins, usually small and perfectly still. Others contain only a darkened hollow, a line of water-polished stones, and mud waiting beneath a crust of frost.

Where moisture recurs, the vegetation is lush. This lushness is mostly horizontal. Cushion plants and dense, many-lobed mosses cover the ground in continuous green and rust-red layers. Sedges rise from wetter seams. Low fernlike plants unfold black-green fronds after rain, holding beads of water long after the clouds have gone. In the most sheltered hollows stand thickets of short, crooked trees, their trunks pressed sideways by old winds and their branches interwoven into a canopy barely higher than a rooftop.

Seen closely, these pockets are crowded with life. Seen across the whole plateau, they are sparse archipelagos. A green basin may be surrounded by days of bare stone. Another may be visible on a far swell, intensely colored after rain, with nothing between the two but quartz, gravel, and empty air.

There are no animals. No insects move among the leaves, no birds cross the open country, and nothing burrows beneath the mats. Plants reproduce by wind, water, splitting, and spores. Some colonies appear to occupy several separated hollows at once: identical fronds open under the same rain, and identical patches of frost damage appear on growth with no continuous root system that can be traced. Nothing eats them. They spread, thicken, freeze, dry, and decay into their own dark soil.

Consequently, the quiet is unusually complete. Wind often makes no sound until it encounters a wall. Rain on leaves is a soft, close ticking. Water clicks into a cistern; ice gives an occasional dull report beneath a stone. Most sounds do not carry far, and the plateau seldom returns an echo. Activity is constant, but almost all of it is silent.

The strangeness lies in the relations between places.

The material landscape has nearly finished eroding. Its geometry has not. Distance is stable within a small enough view, but it is not always reciprocal. A direct walk from a standing stone to a green basin might take an afternoon; the return might take three days. Two valleys can remain unchanged in shape while exchanging their positions relative to a third. Cloud cover is especially unsettling: when a horizon disappears and later returns, some part of the country may have become nearer, farther, or simply elsewhere.

This rarely looks dramatic. A road bends where it did not bend before. A familiar swell is seen from the wrong side. Rain falls in a courtyard while clouds remain visible only beyond the opposite wall. A stream always runs downhill while watched, yet its next visible section may lie in another basin entirely. There are no visible tears in the world. Here and there have merely ceased to be dependable opposites.

The towns are built against this.

They occur at enormous intervals, gray concentrations of pavement and low walls in the empty country. Each is urban in organization rather than in crowding: broad courts, roofed clusters, covered conduits, reservoirs, lanes, stairs, and long causeways, all separated by generous spaces of unused stone. Some contain gardens whose soil is deeper than anything in the surrounding wilderness. Others have been almost entirely reclaimed by vegetation, so that streets run beneath continuous green canopies while cisterns remain open to the sky.

Their construction is simple. Walls are dry-laid or bedded in clay. Roofs are overlapping slate. Doors were made from stone slabs turning in stone sockets. Water channels were cut with stone mauls and copper wedges; containers were fired from local clay; the few surviving fasteners are cold-hammered native copper. There are no engines, conduits of power, or intricate mechanisms. The most sophisticated instrument implied by the work is a stretched cord and a weighted line.

But the builders learned that construction could persuade a place to remain itself.

A straight, well-founded wall keeps its two sides separate. A roof usually prevents one room from occurring inside another. A paved causeway makes a journey approximately as long on the return as on the way out. Within the oldest towns, streets and courtyards retain their relationships even while the country beyond them shifts. The towns are therefore not only shelters from cold and rain. They are local agreements about geometry.

These agreements require no tending. They persist in the weight, repetition, and continuity of the work. A wall breached in too many places becomes merely another outcrop, and the spaces around it begin to drift. An intact cistern, however, remains the same cistern from one year to the next, even when it collects rain that fell on a roof in another quarter.

The towns are old without appearing spectacularly ruined. Every exposed edge has been softened. Tool marks survive only beneath overhangs or inside dry conduits. Newer foundations rest on older pavements; former doorways have become culverts; abandoned plazas have been deepened into reservoirs. Some districts seem to have been rebuilt many times, not upward but inward, each layer narrower and more carefully joined than the last. No builder remains, and the stonework preserves no account of where they went.

Rain is when the whole landscape becomes most active. Water appears first as dark spots on slate and bedrock, then as threads in handmade gutters. Dormant channels fill in sections rather than continuously. Green basins deepen in color; fronds unfold; spores lift in brief, invisible clouds whenever the wind finds them. In the towns, cisterns begin their small, separate music. Beyond the walls, temporary streams shine on stone that may have been dry for generations.

Then the rain stops. Ice returns to the hollows. The clouds move on, or are replaced by a view of country that was not there before.

The High Remainder is neither dead nor coming apart. Stone endures. Water returns. Plants cover what they can. The towns hold their quiet shapes. Only the distances continue to erode, patiently and without sound, until the whole vast plateau seems less like a single surface than a final, settled collection of places—each perfectly itself, and no longer obliged to agree about the others.
