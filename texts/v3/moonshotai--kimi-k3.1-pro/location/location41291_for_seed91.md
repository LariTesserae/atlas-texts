On the survey sheets it is **Basin Four**. The pump crews call it the Drum, and the reason is audible long before the rim is reached.

At first there is only a vibration in the cold air, too sustained to be wind. Then the flat tundra opens beneath the feet, and the sound rises out of a broad depression several kilometres across: engines, pumps, fans, steel conveyors, and the concussive labour of the peat mill, all running together. The eye searches for a settlement large enough to explain it. There is none. A few lights stand at widely separated points below. The rest is open ground, shadow, and long distances.

From above, Basin Four looks barren. This is misleading. Almost nothing stands tall enough to interrupt the view, but nearly every surface is vegetation. Sphagnum moss, sedge, crowberry, and dwarf birch form a continuous frozen skin over the upper benches. The birch is rarely higher than a boot; its branches spread sideways, pressed close to the ground by wind and cold. Underneath lies a tougher fabric of roots and old rhizomes. Where vehicles have crossed it, the mat is crushed but not broken through.

Even the apparently bare cuttings are plant material. Their dark walls are peat: generations of moss and sedge preserved in cold, wet conditions, with individual stems still visible in freshly exposed faces. Stone appears only in the deepest sump and in a few frost-shattered ribs along the eastern side. There is almost no ordinary soil. Roads in winter are simply firm routes across frozen root-felt. Foundations, where they exist, must be piled down through the plant mass or isolated from it so that engine heat does not turn the ground soft.

The basin is not a single pit. It is a chain of broad floors, stepped workings, narrow drainage cuts, and older collapse hollows connected by ramps. The western terraces are regular and machine-made. Toward the east they lose their geometry, breaking into sharp-sided gullies where frozen silt has split and peat blocks have toppled. At the centre lies a long, shallow sump, usually more ice than water. Beyond it, abandoned workings continue toward another rim, their outlines visible mainly because snow lies differently in their hollows.

Everything looks recently made. Ditch banks have crisp corners. Fractures have not been rounded by streams. Old slumps retain raw lips and angular debris. This freshness is deceptive. The cold preserves scars, and there is too little moving water to wear them smooth. A cutting abandoned decades ago may look almost as new as one opened last winter.

Moisture is present, but seldom abundant. It darkens the peat in protected seams, gathers as slush at the bottoms of ditches, and forms clear lenses inside the root mat. A few springs produce tea-brown seepage that moves only a short distance before freezing. Rain is rare; occasionally a warmer front lays a wet glaze over the basin, and for a few hours the whole surface becomes black and reflective before locking solid again. More often, water appears indirectly—as rime on intake grills, condensation beneath insulated pipes, or icicles under the pump-house eaves. Around the peat mill, waste heat maintains a small halo of genuinely damp ground. Beyond that halo, the cold returns abruptly.

For much of the year the sun does not clear the southern rim. Even at midday the floor receives mostly reflected sky. Mosses remain subdued shades of rust, olive, and grey; exposed peat is nearly black. Snow does not fall deeply, but wind combs it into the cuts and leaves the hummocks bare. Lamps are spaced too far apart to illuminate routes. Each seems instead to guard one object: a valve assembly, a transformer, a stair head, the door of a shed. Their light picks out frost on wire and the undersides of pipes, then stops. Exhaust and ice fog collect in the lower workings without ever becoming a uniform mist.

The human works are small in plan but heavy in construction. There is a pump house on concrete piles, a low drying and pressing shed, a transformer enclosure, two winch shelters, and a scattering of abandoned timber stores. Insulated pipes cross the ground on short trestles. Power cables run between poles and then disappear for hundreds of metres into private darkness. Older artifacts survive without much alteration: a length of narrow-gauge rail, survey stakes with their paint faded to grey, a ceramic insulator lying beside a collapsed bench, the partial wall of a hut whose roof was removed rather than ruined.

The principal machinery keeps the basin drained and processes peat from a limited active face. Much of it runs continuously because stopping equipment in this cold creates more work than running it. Fuel thickens, grease stiffens, moisture freezes in clearances, and a stopped pump may become part of the ice it was meant to control. Consequently, there is no natural interval of quiet between tasks.

The sound changes from place to place but never becomes background. Near the sump it is mostly low-frequency pressure, felt through boot soles and in the chest. Along the conveyor gallery it separates into belt rumble, roller shriek, and repeated impacts as frozen material enters a hopper. The drying fans add a hard, continuous roar. In the narrower cuts, cold air and frozen walls carry the noise back from unexpected directions. At the pump floor, speech is useless even at close range. Workers use hand signals and lamps; the telephone inside the main shelter activates a flashing light because a bell would serve no purpose. Farther away, the many sounds merge into one immense note. The basin sounds crowded although it is nearly empty.

Animals contribute little. A raven sometimes occupies the highest power pole, hunched against the wind. It may open its beak without any call reaching the ground. Fox tracks appear occasionally along an abandoned drain, cross a service road, and vanish. In the brief thaw, a few insects emerge near the warm seep beside the mill, but there is no general summer swarm. Most animal life remains small, dormant, and hidden within the peat. The vegetation itself is also slower than it appears. The green cushion near a warm outlet may represent many years of growth; elsewhere, a moss shoot can remain frozen for much of its life without dying.

The machines move quickly, but the landscape does not. Pumps hold seepage in check. The peat compresses almost imperceptibly under its own weight. Ice lenses thicken, lifting a pipe support or opening a new fracture by fractions. Buildings settle and are adjusted. A disused rail is gradually overtaken, not by a rush of greenery, but by moss closing over one cold centimetre at a time. If the works were abandoned, there would be no sudden flood or dramatic reclamation. Water would return patiently. Fresh ice would disturb the drainage cuts. The sharpest edges would soften over generations.

Seen from the far rim, Basin Four can almost be mistaken for an unoccupied hollow in the tundra: a vast, dim expanse of living ground with a handful of lights and no visible streets. Only the sound reveals the scale of the intervention. Nothing here hurries except the engines, and much of their labour goes simply toward keeping everything else the same.
