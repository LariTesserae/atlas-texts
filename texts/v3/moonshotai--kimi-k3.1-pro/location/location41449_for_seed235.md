From the outer rim, the region looks like a forested mountain range that has split open and stood itself on end. The rift is broader than a valley, its escarpments descending in flutes and buttresses of nearly black stone. There is no visible floor. Instead, the cliffs carry tier upon tier of what appears to be a city: pale arcades recessed into the rock, roofless courts, narrow bridges, stairways, cisterns, and clusters of towers whose windows are choked with ferns. Forest spills over the upper walls. Root curtains hang beneath the lower balconies. Across the open gulf, more wards become visible whenever the eye adjusts to another layer of distance.

The altitude is extreme, but the air has refused every consequence of altitude. It is hot, dense, and almost drinkable. Moisture gathers on stone and leaf without ever making the shaded places cool. The heat is not confined to springs or sunlit ledges; no vent, fire, or sulphurous stain accounts for it. It simply belongs to the place. Mist stands in the deeper streets and around the bases of the great cliffs, but it is not thick enough to hide them. It only silvers their edges.

The light is equally unreasonable in its plainness. This is ordinary, slightly hazy daylight, with honest greens, wet purple-blacks, chalky mineral whites, and shadows of familiar depth. Nothing glows. The sky is not split or burning. The whole impossible region could be surveyed under the mild light of a late morning after rain—except that “late” and “after” are assumptions made by the eye. The shadows never lengthen. The mist never thins. Every surface retains the freshness of a downpour that has neither just ended nor quite begun.

The cliffs themselves are immensely old in appearance, though not dilapidated. Their fractures have been softened by water. Seepage has left rust-red and olive stains down the black stone, and pale mineral rinds have accumulated around the permanent springs. Channels fit their streams exactly. Ledges are rounded without being rubble; there is no fresh scree beneath them. Whatever violence opened the rift has left no loose edges. Even the tallest root-grown walls seem to have passed beyond the stage at which they might merely collapse.

At close range, the impression of construction becomes stronger and its history less plausible. Streets run behind colonnades and open unexpectedly onto sunken plazas. Staircases connect cistern terraces with long, covered galleries. There are doorways without doors, drains without grates, bridges with parapets, rooms grouped around light wells. Yet there are no joints between blocks and no tool marks on the thresholds. The pale structural material is a fusion of old root, mineral deposit, and stone dust, grown into seamless ribs. Living roots pass through it and emerge as columns, vaults, or bundles of conduits. Floors are not paved so much as repeatedly thickened.

The city is urban in organization, not in origin. It is neither an abandoned settlement nor a disguise adopted by the forest. Nothing in it was designed, and nothing in it is quite shapeless. Roots followed water through the cliff; water followed the spaces between roots; mineral crust preserved the most persistent passages. Over durations that no longer have any meaning here, those preferences produced streets, rooms, bridges, and wards. No chamber contains furniture. No path is worn by feet. There has never been anything to occupy the city in the ordinary sense.

Its density is greatest in the middle heights. There, canopies overlap roofs, roots occupy the walls behind other roots, and pools lie above rooms whose ceilings are also the bottoms of further pools. Broad, waxy leaves shed beads of water into fern-filled court basins. Lianas as thick as buttresses cross the narrow lanes. Moss makes vertical walls appear upholstered, while translucent algae ribbon the undersides of clear streams. Even the open plazas feel full: full of reflections, of water noise, of views down through arches and up through layered greenery. The region offers long vistas, but almost nowhere offers a simple surface.

Higher up, the architecture loosens into forest. Buttressed trees stand in immense roof basins, their crowns making a broken green horizon along the rim. Lower down, the wards become more mineral: hanging cisterns, dripping galleries, root bridges stretched across blue shadow. Still lower, where a valley floor ought to begin, the terraces are silhouetted against pale sky.

This is not an optical trick of mist. Looking far enough down the rift becomes indistinguishable from looking out through it. There is no dark bottom and no point at which the reversal visibly occurs. Every district has depth beneath it, including districts that can plainly be seen beneath one another from across the gulf.

Water obeys the same local and incompatible geography. It enters the city as seepage through the high stone, collects in leaf basins and court pools, spills through root conduits, and falls in sheets from gallery to gallery. Every separate reach runs downhill. Nevertheless, some streams return to their own springs. A canal disappearing beneath a lower plaza emerges from a fissure several tiers above its entrance, without any visible ascent. A stairway can descend past three balconies and terminate in the courtyard from which those balconies project. The connections never change. This is not a labyrinth rearranging itself; its impossibility is dependable.

From different overlooks, the same bridge may appear on opposite sides of the same tower. It is not a pair of bridges, and neither view is false. The wards simply cannot be assembled into one consistent solid. Space seems not so much broken as healed around several contradictory instructions. Water, roots, and daylight all follow the resulting seams without difficulty.

The vegetation is lush, but nothing about it suggests impending growth. There are great mature leaves and none unfolding, thick moss cushions and no advancing fringe, trees full of vigor and no fallen trunks opening gaps for successors. Flowers and fruits are inconspicuous or absent; nothing here is shaped to court a pollinator or tempt a browsing mouth. Leaf, root, spore, and mineral exchange continue, but succession has closed. The ecology has no next stage.

No bird crosses the open rift. No insect hangs in the steam. The pools contain neither fish nor larvae, and the sheltered stone bears no webs, burrows, nests, tracks, or droppings. The absence is more than quietness or emptiness: no part of the city has ever been adjusted by an animal body. Its steps are regular because growth is regular, not because anything once climbed them. Its pools are full because water returns, not because anything drinks.

And the place is deafening.

Each seep alone would be a small sound, but there are thousands of seeps, threads, spills, and falls. The great open drops produce a continuous white roar that fills the rift. Narrow shafts take the impact of water and return a bass pressure too deep to locate. Arcades boom with fixed pitches. Sheets falling into shallow basins add a hard, bright hiss, while countless leaf drips make a dense granular texture close at hand. On the bridges, the sound seems to press inward from the open air. Within the courts, it becomes enclosure.

There is no animal call within it, no answering voice, no irregular gust giving it phrasing. Nor does distance soften it in a reliable way: the broken geography carries the roar of remote wards into nearby galleries, so far water and near water arrive together. The sound never rises toward a crisis and never relaxes afterward. Echoes do not die away; their sustained resonances are simply part of each district’s chord. Eventually the noise seems less like an event than a second atmospheric pressure, as material as the heat.

That is when the deeper stillness becomes apparent. The mist does not drift. Pool levels do not change. The outline of each waterfall remains constant, though the water within it is moving. Shadows stay fixed across steps and leaves. No fresh rockfall appears, no wall darkens with new damp, no root extends visibly toward a seam. Dissolution and mineral deposition maintain the same channels. Condensation returns to the roots as quickly as foliage releases moisture. Nothing accumulates, and nothing is used up.

This is not a snapshot or a petrified world. The plants are alive; the water runs; heat passes through stone and air. But every process is enclosed within an equilibrium so complete that the place as a whole has no subsequent condition. It looks weathered because weathering belongs to its form, not because it is currently decaying. It looks freshly rained upon because wetness is one of its permanent materials.

It is tempting to call the city abandoned, but abandonment would require inhabitants and a before. It is tempting to call it petrified, but the green tissue is supple and the water is warm. Even “impossible” is only partly right: impossibility suggests strain, a contradiction about to give way. Nothing here is straining. The contradictions have worn as smooth as the water channels.

The loudest sound announces nothing. The highest terraces overlook depths that are also heights. Water falls forever without emptying a single basin. Under plain daylight, in the hot green air, the city remains exactly as impossible as it needs to be.
