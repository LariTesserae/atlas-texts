## The Unfinished Crown

At the Unfinished Crown, the mountains are not old. They are not even, in the ordinary sense, completely solid.

The region occupies a cold, rarefied height beneath a small, ash-white sun. The sun gives enough light to distinguish pale rock from black sky, but little more. Shadows are broad and uncertain. Stars remain visible through the day. From the upper terraces, the view opens across hundreds of kilometers: blade-like plateaus, isolated mesas, deep wells of shadow, and, at the limit of sight, another range standing almost on edge as it emerges from the dark.

The openness is immense, but nearly everything in it is interval. Bare stone predominates. Gardens appear far apart, like black stains on the heights. A structure may be visible as a single cubic fleck on an otherwise empty spur. The cliffs fall farther than sight can follow, through motionless veils of mineral dust. No common base to the region has been established. Some of the gulfs appear to have bottoms only when viewed from particular ledges.

Dryness here is not a climate. It is a condition of matter.

There are no streams, no clouds, no ice in the cold shadows, no water vapor in the thin gas. The rock contains no hydrated veins. Dust never cakes or clings. Nothing rusts, nothing mildews, nothing becomes damp before dawn. Even the word *dry* is slightly misleading, because it suggests the absence of something that might be brought back. Water has no part in the Crown’s chemistry or its history. The cold is consequently very clean: not biting, not clammy, simply patient.

Into this thin air comes a sound that should be impossible.

It is a detonation that has not ended, a continent-wide roar with a crushing low note beneath it and a fine ceramic screaming threaded through the top. At times one can distinguish crashes, vast tearing noises, the rattle of a billion hard leaves—but these are only textures within the larger sound. It arrives in stone, vegetation, tools, and bone at the same instant. It does not need an atmospheric path. Covering one’s ears suppresses the small nearby ticking of dust and nothing else. A sealed hut is exactly as loud as an exposed summit.

Yet almost nothing can be seen moving.

A cliff may require ten thousand years to rise by the width of a hand. A new plateau may remain intangible for the whole lifetime of a garden. The sound is not evidence that these processes are happening quickly. It is as though the entire labor of an age—every fracture, every displacement, every grain forced into a new arrangement—were audible in the present, while the eye was permitted to see only the nearly motionless result. The Crown looks patient and sounds catastrophic.

Its geology has the rawness of a first attempt. Escarpments end in fresh, glassy edges. Fractures are sharp enough to divide light. There is no talus beneath the cliffs, no rounded gravel in the depressions, no soil softened by long weathering. Erosion has scarcely begun. The world is still too busy insisting on its surfaces to wear them away.

Along an active seam, the strata sometimes continue a short distance beyond the rock as hairlines in empty air. They are visible only when the dim sun strikes them sideways. A hand passes through them. Much later—after generations, after whole gardens have hardened into mineral thickets—there may be a cold ledge where the lines were. Later still, old records become unreliable: maps show the ledge preceding features that were once built beside its absence. New stone does not simply accumulate. Gradually, it becomes mandatory.

Near the outer seams, distance is also unfinished. A summit can remain visible at the same apparent size through days of approach. Two peaks may exchange which is higher without either moving; the alteration appears first in surveying cords and in the pitch of the roar. A route across bare rock can be longer on the return journey without passing through different country. The empty spaces beyond an active edge are not black, white, or misty. They are simply places in which no dependable direction has yet been established.

Rooted ground is more stable. So, often, is stone that has been made into an enclosure.

This may explain the gardens.

They grow where the rock has become consistent enough to hold roots and where the great sound is especially strong. Seen from across a gulf, each garden is small. Inside one, the vegetation is extravagantly lush. Dark felt-leaf cushions overlap in deep, springy beds, each cushion composed of thousands of soft-looking scales. Above them stand hollow reeds twenty meters tall, fluted like organ pipes and banded with pale silica. Between the reeds, fan-shaped fronds open in dense tiers, while looping filaments climb over old trunks and bind whole outcrops into a single trembling mass.

None of it contains sap. The leaves are flexible lattices of carbon, glass, and dry conductive mineral rather than vessels full of fluid. Their metabolism is a slow traffic of charge through solid tissue. Pressure from the great note deforms microscopic structures in leaf and stem; the deformation frees energy, and the roots use that energy to strip sustenance directly from anhydrous rock. The dim sun supplements the process, but it does not account for the abundance. Abundance comes from the roar.

The gardens are therefore not refuges from the sound. They are its most articulate places. The low mats hiss with a fine, dry friction. The reeds boom through their hollow cores. At certain harmonies, whole thickets of fan-leaves begin to ring, each plant adding a small, hard tone to the overwhelming note.

Growth is extremely slow. A frond may take a century to unroll. But nothing grazes it, and dead tissue does not rot in any familiar way. Fallen stems remain for ages, gradually becoming hollow mineral sheaths. Living reeds rise through the skeletons of earlier reeds; root mats spread over the preserved impressions of root mats older still. The lushness is not rapid proliferation. It is accumulation without interruption.

Reproduction waits on resonance. When the great sound settles into a frequency suited to a particular garden, charged spore-dust lifts from the foliage without wind. For a few hours the plants glitter as if emitting smoke. The dust crosses open stone in thin veils, most of it drifting into gulfs or settling where the rock is not yet stable. A very little reaches another seam.

There is no animal life to carry it. There are no insects among the fronds, no burrows beneath the roots, no winged shapes above the cliffs, no scavengers at the dead stems. Nothing nests, hunts, browses, or leaves tracks. No animal life exists here at any scale. Apart from the almost imperceptible motion of growth and the occasional release of spores, the only deliberate marks on the region are artifacts.

These are so sparse that they seem at first like mistakes in the landscape.

A corbelled hut stands alone beside a garden, built without mortar from slabs small enough for one or two hands. Farther on, three stones have been stacked at the edge of a drop. A dry stalk ladder leans against a ledge. A length of cord, twisted from felt-leaf fiber, joins two pegs on an otherwise featureless terrace. Elsewhere, a stair of seventeen steps ends against a blank wall. At eye level on the wall, the outline of an eighteenth step is faintly visible, though there is nothing there to touch.

The tools are correspondingly simple: hammerstones, flaked scrapers, stone drills, abrasive blocks, cord spindles. No engines, no metal fittings, no roads beyond short prepared paths. There are no villages, cultivated fields, middens, or graves. Whatever makers passed through did not establish a population. They established a few places.

Some of the huts have a peculiar virtue. Inside them, measurements tend to hold. A cord stretched between two pegs remains the same length. The doorway keeps its distance from the rear wall. A stone left in one corner is there when the surrounding view has altered. This may be why closed shapes were built, or it may only be why those particular structures survived. Nothing explains the method. The huts do not reduce the sound.

A usable map of the Crown would have to be part atlas and part musical score. Stable gardens possess recognizable harmonics; active seams produce dissonances that shift over centuries. Straight lines are the least reliable routes. Rooted terraces, old enclosures, and cord-marked paths offer better passage, though none is guaranteed. Sharp edges are not necessarily dangerous, and smooth ones are not necessarily old. Height is recorded with a date, because height without a date is only a temporary opinion.

The light changes almost as slowly as the land. The white sun shifts by degrees across generations. Dust raised by a new surface may hang for years before settling. When the great note deepens, a seam somewhere becomes tangible, a garden releases its spores, and one portion of the horizon draws nearer without visibly moving. There are no sudden seasons, no floods, no violent renewals. Even reality’s failures are patient.

From a hut above one of the upper gardens, the whole contradictory character of the place can be taken in at once. Below, the reeds stand crowded among their mineralized ancestors, black foliage trembling with stored ages. Beyond them lies many kilometers of nearly empty stone. A second hut appears close enough to reach with a thrown pebble, though no surveyed path agrees on the distance. Past the nearest cliff, faint bedding lines extend into nothing, sketching a terrace that may one day be able to bear weight.

There is no drip, no wingbeat, no rustle of prey or predator. There is no weather coming. And still the sound fills every available space, deafening without haste.

The Crown is not a dead world, nor quite a world waiting to begin. It already has ecology, architecture, history, and patterns of inheritance. What it lacks is completion. The roar continues as the cold heights decide, over unimaginable durations, what will be above, what will be below, and what can finally be said to exist.
