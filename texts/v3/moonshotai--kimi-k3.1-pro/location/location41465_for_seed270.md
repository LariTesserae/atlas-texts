## The Grading Pocket

The pocket lies beneath a plain so old and low that distance has almost ceased to make a difference to its shape. The dry mineral crust—pale salt, graphite, powdered volcanic glass—rests in long, imperceptible swells. There are no streambeds, no damp hollows, no banks left from any former abundance of water. Every few kilometres, a ceramic terminal or a short rib of iron emerges from the dust. These do not immediately suggest ruins. They are too scattered, and everything between them has had time to disappear.

The pocket itself cannot be seen from the surface. Its only opening is a breather slot beneath a glassy crust, too narrow for a hand. Sound comes out of it: a hard, regular ticking under a much finer noise, like gravel being poured continuously into a metal drum.

Inside, the space is eleven paces long and three across, nowhere high enough to stand. At human scale it would be a maintenance void; at the scale of its inhabitants, it contains high ledges, open feeding ground, covered approaches, and a deep central basin. Even the basin is shallow. Nothing in the pocket rises far above anything else. Its ceiling is a smooth skin of brown mineral, and its floor has settled into low lenses of black and ivory dust. The place seems to have been approaching levelness for so long that levelness has become one of its natural laws.

It is cool inside, cool enough that the remaining metal feels painful to hold. Only the motor housing carries a narrow zone of warmth. The air is absolutely dry and smells of crushed stone, ozone, and a faint solvent sharpness. Two caged work lamps provide plain white light, bright enough to read by. Nothing glows on its own. Wings and bodies cross the lamps, and their shadows move normally over the walls.

There is no vegetation: no roots in the cracks, no moss at the opening, not even a coloured stain that might be mistaken for algae. A dark film grows around the electrical terminals, but it is not alive. It is an energy-rich mineral deposit formed where leaking current acts on the finest dust. This nonliving film, renewed whenever the circuit is closed, is the beginning of the food web.

The machine that makes it possible is the only substantial structure in the pocket. It was once an industrial grader: an electric motor, an eccentric shaft, and three perforated screens intended to separate powdered ore by size. Its housing is pitted and rounded, its labels worn to pale arrangements of scratches. A cracked gauge lies some distance away, half incorporated into the floor. A few bolts, a porcelain saddle, and the armoured power cable account for nearly everything else visibly built. Yet the grader still runs. Current arrives through the cable from somewhere beyond the pocket, and the screens continue to grade material whose original purpose has been forgotten.

At first, the floor appears to move only because the machine is vibrating. Then the walls begin moving in a different rhythm.

They are covered with buttonbacks: flat, dry-shelled animals, most no larger than a lentil, with dull grey or copper-coloured cases. They pack the feeding ledges until their shells overlap. They climb over one another to reach the terminal film, scrape at it with paired mouthparts, and retreat into seams to shed, mate, or simply be out of the crowd. A clear space the width of a fingernail closes within seconds. There are buttonbacks in the motor’s cooling slots, buttonbacks in the hollow gauge, buttonbacks layered several deep along the upper wall. Their combined footsteps make a faint hiss beneath the machinery.

They are not small robots, despite their metal-flecked shells and their attachment to the circuit. They grow, hunger, compete, and make mistakes. Their flexible tissues are waxy rather than wet; their chemistry uses no water. A crushed body leaves a grey oil-smear, mineral grit, and a sharp smell, but nothing resembling blood as a wet-world animal would know it.

Beneath them, the coarse fraction from the grader forms low ridges. Wirebacks hunt there. These are finger-length predators with many narrow legs and blunt, scissoring jaws. They bury themselves until only the upper line of the shell remains visible, then follow the tapping vibrations of feeding buttonbacks. When one surfaces, it does so in a straight, abrupt rush. A shell cracks. Hundreds of prey animals recoil at once, and the disturbance travels across the ledge as a visible wave.

The air is inhabited too. At intervals, some of the buttonbacks emerge in a winged stage. Their dry, translucent wings are shed after mating and become part of the pale floor dust. During a major flight, the ordinary white light becomes granular behind thousands of bodies. Smaller scavengers move through the fallen wings and old shells, reducing them to flakes; still smaller animals live among those flakes. There is almost no unoccupied medium in the pocket. The ledges are crowded, the dust is crowded, and the air periodically becomes crowded.

The noise is out of all proportion to the size of the place. The motor supplies a continuous hum. The eccentric shaft knocks. The screens chatter against their worn frames. Beneath and around these sounds are shell friction, wingbeats, feeding taps, the small detonations of predators striking, and the dry fall of dust from the ceiling. The low mineral roof returns everything. Inside, a shout would disappear into the general industrial roar.

That roar has a structure. Every forty-one seconds, the grader reaches the heavy part of its stroke and the screens slap their frames. The pocket’s weather follows this event. First comes a pressure pulse through the breather slot. Then dust drops from the ceiling in thin veils. Winged animals rise before it falls, while the wirebacks stop wherever they are. For several seconds the feeding grounds appear almost empty. When the dust settles, the buttonbacks return in a dense front, and the predators resume hunting along its edge.

Longer changes in the current produce slower migrations. Feeding aggregates shift from one terminal to another; abandoned ground darkens briefly with new mineral film before being discovered again. The grader sorts the continuous fall of wings, shells, mineral powder, and its own abraded particles. Coarse material becomes hunting cover. Finer material spreads across the basin. The finest dust escapes through the slot, preventing the pocket from filling.

Nothing in this activity makes the place young. On the contrary, the activity is what has worn it into its final form. Feet and vibration have rounded the ledges. Shell dust has softened the artifacts. The machine produces, the animals consume, predators concentrate the remains, scavengers pulverize them, and the grader sorts them back into ground. Individuals live quickly; the arrangement among them is immensely old. It changes constantly without changing much.

Above, the plain remains silent and nearly flat. Beneath it, the relay closes again. The screens slam down, white light vanishes momentarily behind wings, and the entire hidden floor seems to rise at once.
