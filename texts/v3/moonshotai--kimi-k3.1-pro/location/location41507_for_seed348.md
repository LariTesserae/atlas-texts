## The Low Works

The Low Works occupies a shallow depression in a dry mineral plain, only a few dozen steps below the surrounding country. It is small enough to see whole: a compact arrangement of roofs, chimneys, pipe bridges, and electrical pylons, bounded on one side by a freight siding and on the other by an old curb of dark stone. Yet it has been laid out with urban seriousness. There are streets, gutters, numbered workshops, a substation, workers’ houses, and a central square scarcely larger than a factory floor.

It is urban without being crowded. The buildings stand well apart, separated by broad courts of packed slag and pale pavement. A pump house faces three empty acres of cracked concrete; a narrow foundry sits beside nothing more substantial than a water tank and a row of insulators. Pipes cross the open spaces on low iron trestles, taking unnecessarily municipal routes—turning at right angles, passing beneath little bridges, entering buildings through formally framed apertures. The whole place seems to have been planned for expansion, then to have reached a final size instead.

Everything is made of vitrified brick, gray cast concrete, dark stone, and iron browned to the color of weak tea. The materials are old but not picturesque. Curbs have lost their sharpness. Thresholds are hollowed into shallow dishes. Brick corners are rounded, rail heads polished, screw slots worn almost smooth. In the basin walls, much older watercourses have left long, soft grooves, though no stream runs there now. Nothing appears ready to collapse. Nothing appears newly repaired. The town has reached the settled condition of a tool that has been used for so long that its remaining shape seems inevitable.

The light is ordinary: mild, shadow-casting daylight in which rust remains red, water remains clear, and distances are easy to judge. There is no infernal glow behind the factory windows and no concealing fog. The air is simply cool, like the air in an unheated workshop. Metal numbs the hand. Breath shows faintly near the vents, where it mixes with the smell of wet iron, mineral dust, machine oil, and warm animal bodies.

Outside the town, the plain is dry. Within it, moisture occurs in small, stubborn jurisdictions. One courtyard lies under a fine drizzle while the next receives none; the drizzle is not passing through, but belongs to that courtyard. Condensation beads beneath every cold pipe and gathers in black sumps no deeper than a hand. Certain walls weep along a single horizontal seam. A shallow channel carries clear water from the pump house to the foundry and back through a return pipe, without ever overflowing its stained margins. There are damp footprints, slick gutters, and pockets of reflected sky, but no marsh and no general mud.

No plant exploits any of it. There is not a weed in a paving joint, not a green thread in a drain, not even a dark nap of mold beneath the dripping eaves. Water leaves mineral rings and nothing else. The absence of vegetation makes the town look scrubbed rather than barren.

Nevertheless, it is overwhelmingly alive.

At first the animals are easily mistaken for industrial coverings. What looks like lagging around a pipe proves to be a chain of folded gray bodies. A tarpaulin heaped in a court opens several dozen mouths. A conveyor belt, apparently standing in an open shed, ripples gently from end to end and reveals legs beneath it.

The creatures range from the length of a thumb to the size of a small cart. All have the same slate-colored, lengthwise-pleated bodies and the same smooth, nearly eyeless heads. Six or more blunt legs carry the smaller ones; the largest move by slow muscular contractions, their many feet ticking in sequence. Their round mouths are lined with pale plates resembling ceramic insulators. With these they clamp themselves to rails, walls, cables, engine housings, or the flanks of larger animals. They are warm to the touch, though moisture collects on their cooler dorsal ridges and runs from them in clear drops.

They hang beneath eaves in layers. They thicken the pipes until the pipes resemble trunks. They occupy the gutters so completely that water travels over a moving bed of backs. Medium-sized animals festoon the power lines, while the biggest stand apart in the courts like pieces of misplaced hose, breathing with deep, regular folds. From a rooftop, the streets appear empty because the creatures match the pavement. At ground level, there is almost nowhere to put a foot. They yield without alarm and close in again, indifferent but not insensible.

The town’s architectural sparseness is therefore not emptiness. Its open spaces are merely spaces between buildings.

No pasture, fungus garden, or hidden vegetation supports this multitude. The animals appear to live directly within the industrial circuit. They gather mouth-first around vibrating flywheels and fasten themselves in dense bracelets to the substation’s insulated cables. The smallest rasp white mineral scale from the condensate basins. Their bodies return clear water and a little warm grit, which the pumps draw away. Nothing is consumed in a way that diminishes the town, and nothing is excreted in a way that accumulates. It is unclear whether the Works was constructed to feed the animals, or whether the animals are the portion of the Works that eventually learned to walk.

The engines supply the low part of the sound: pumps laboring behind brick walls, a turbine’s steady note, heavy belts slapping over wheels, and a single measured hammer-stroke whose force can be felt through the pavement. The animals supply everything above it. Their feet make a dry rushing like rain across sheet iron. Their mouth-plates ring when they attach to metal. When thousands of pleated bodies inflate together, they produce a vast, flat hooting that seems to come from the town’s foundations rather than from any individual throat.

The combined noise is loud enough to make ordinary conversation difficult, though it never quite becomes painful. More disturbing is its constancy. There are no real lulls, no gathering crescendos, no useful direction from which a sound approaches. An echo does not die into silence; it joins the general level. After a while, the commotion resolves into something closer to a chord.

That is the Low Works’ deeper rule: motion is permitted, but alteration is not. A flywheel turns; it does not wear. A piston rises and falls; its cylinder never becomes looser. A drop travels down a window without diminishing the bead from which it came. Animals unfasten themselves, cross a street, and fasten elsewhere, yet the number covering each wall remains effectively the same. Steam issues from a vent in a narrow, bent plume whose outline never frays. The factories neither exhaust their materials nor produce heaps of goods. Every process is balanced by a return.

Local events occur, but nothing comes of them.

The workers’ houses make this especially plain. Their rooms contain enamel tables, narrow iron beds, coat pegs, and shelves for tools. There are no coats, no personal objects, no dust outlining places where objects used to be. Animals occupy the floors and the spaces beneath the beds. The street signs name processes rather than people: **RETURN**, **LOWER INTAKE**, **SECOND COOLING**. If shifts were ever worked here, there is no whistle left to mark their beginning or end.

At the boundary, the pavement stops on a surveyed line. Beyond it lie dry stone, relative quiet, and no animal tracks. The town does not straggle outward. Its dampness, infrastructure, and multitude all end together. Looking back from the plain, one sees only a modest industrial settlement beneath ordinary daylight, with a few roofs shining where water has collected on them.

It is not abandoned, because abandonment would require a departure. It is not waiting, because waiting would require something to come. The Low Works is simply settled: cool in its streets, damp in its hollows, worn into its final forms, crowded beyond counting and empty of consequences. Its engines remain loud, and its immense population breathes without increasing the world by so much as a breath.
