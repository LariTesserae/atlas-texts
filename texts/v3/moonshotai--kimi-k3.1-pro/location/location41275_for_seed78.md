## The High Hollow

By mass, the High Hollow is vegetation. By volume, it is almost nothing.

It is a continent-wide body of intergrafted stems, leaves, roots, and buoyant bladders standing several kilometres above a cloud sea. Stone exists somewhere beneath it, in the old foundations from which the first growth climbed, but none belongs to the reachable landscape. Here, ground is living skin. A cliff is torn sapwood. A ridge is an unopened leaf. A valley may be no more than the interval between two structural ribs, with cloud visible far below through its floorless dark.

The visible organism is so hollow that whole districts are mostly air. Some leaves are broad enough to carry weather, yet the spaces beneath them are vaster still, crossed only by a few cables of tissue. A dropped seed may fall for minutes. A shout may return from several directions, at several different times, without revealing where anything is.

It is a young landscape, grown rather than eroded. From a distance its skyline resembles a new mountain range: black blades, split cones, abrupt escarpments, nothing rounded. Close up, the resemblances fail. Peaks have veins. Cliffs show growth rings where they have snapped. New tissue is wine-dark and slick; mature surfaces wear a close black felt that gathers what little light the year provides. Old growth becomes silver, hard, and papery before being overgrown or breaking away.

There is no soil underfoot, no river gravel, no earthy smell after rain. Even the dust is vegetable: broken fibre, shed wax, powdered husk. The surface springs slightly beneath a step and transmits the movement onward. Where it has been cut, it beads with thick sap that smells green and peppery, then hardens into a clear scab. Nothing has been softened by long weathering. Things are growing, freshly broken, or being consumed; geography has scarcely had time to do anything else.

Most of the year is night. The brief summer is itself only a prolonged, watery twilight, during which the black membranes unfold and the plant stores enough energy to endure the darkness. Almost nothing glows. When the sky clears, stars appear with hard brilliance but give the landscape little usable light. Travellers learn the temperature of surfaces before their shapes: waxed leaf, dry felt, brittle husk, the dangerous slickness of new ice.

The cold is persistent and the air thin. Breath clouds, then freezes in clothing. Sap remains liquid in the living stems, but exposed water usually exists only briefly—as condensation, a small seep, a skin of ice. Warm, moist air rises from the cloud sea through the hollow columns and condenses in the cold upper reaches. Fog moves through the interior like slow drainage. Droplets gather on fine surface hairs, run along veins, and collect in wax-lined pockets.

Thus the High Hollow is damp without often being wet. A sleeve brushed against a leaf may become soaked, while the next kilometre is dry and papery. Occasionally an interior fog bank cools into rain. It falls in brief, noisy showers, dripping from level to level rather than forming streams. There are no true rivers. Water is stored in small basins, carried in tissue, or lost into the depths.

The plant’s slow changes of pressure make these wet places temporary. When an old bladder district fails, a whole tract sags and begins collecting fog. Pools form, sap seeps from strained tissue, and animals arrive. If the damage heals, the basin gradually lifts and drains; if it worsens, the district tears open and its water falls into the cloud below. A pond may therefore be older than a hill nearby, or younger than the footprints beside it. Maps are useful only if they carry dates.

The scale is great enough for separate weather to inhabit separate parts of the organism. One leaf province may be under clear stars while another, beyond a structural rib, is filled with freezing rain. Pressure differences draw wind through the stems even when the outer sky is still. Membranes bow, corded roots tighten, and entire ridges rise or settle by a few handspans. During the dim growing season, buds split and new blades thrust upward so quickly that shelters tilt and handlines pull taut.

All of this is loud.

At rest, the High Hollow produces a low structural note, felt in the chest as much as heard. Wind raises it into a roar. Thousands of stem-openings become flues; loose husks rattle; broad leaves boom like sails. Freezing condensation snaps from edges in long cascading crackles. When an ice-sealed bladder bursts, the report travels through the lattice for hundreds of kilometres. A major collapse can continue for a long time, the initial thunder followed by the diminishing blows of fragments striking lower growth.

Sound often arrives before weather, water, or animals. A change in pitch warns that wind has entered a distant stem system. Drumming beneath the feet may mean a herd crossing a membrane far beyond sight. During the deepest winds, speech is impossible and even nearby animals communicate by touch. In the lulls, smaller sounds emerge: sap ticking inside a stem, wings in a roost, the delicate repeated ticking of ice forming along a fibre.

Animal life is rich but intensely gathered. Around a wet seam there may be burrowing mites in the felt, beetles at a sap scab, swarms of seed midges, and needlewings roosting by the thousand in the warm throat of a hollow stem. On the broader leaf saddles live felt-oxen, heavy shaggy grazers whose wide, soft hooves spread their weight across the membrane. They crop the surface growth, drink from temporary pockets, and move on before the plant’s bitter defensive sap accumulates. Their calls are low enough to travel through the structure as vibration.

Needlewings hunt the midges in clicking, erratic flights, sometimes filling an empty bay with the sound of fine gravel shaken in a tube. Beneath the leaves, long-limbed predators cling to the structural cords. They hunt largely by vibration and do best during storms, when the footsteps of their prey are hidden by the plant’s own noise. Herds bunch together when the deep wind rises; roosting animals fall still; smaller creatures retreat into sap-warmed crevices.

Then one passes beyond the inhabited pocket. For days there may be nothing but black fibre, condensation, and weather. The abundance was real, but separated by enormous distances.

The scattered works of people obey the same sparseness. A camp is usually no more than a woven platform at a slow-growing node, a low windbreak covered with old leaf-hide, a catchment bag, and a few handlines. Tools are bone awls, braided cord, wooden scrapers, and blades flaked from the glassy ribs of dead leaves. There is no metalwork and little reason to carry weight. Open fires are avoided: heat makes the supporting tissue contract, and a hearth can open the floor beneath it. Warmth and light come, sparingly, from fat lamps enclosed in seed cases.

Routes are marked by sound rather than sight. A bound cluster of slotted stems gives a particular chord when the wind passes through it; another chord answers from the next shelter. Between such marks there may be weeks of travel. Sometimes the only sign of previous passage is a broken flute, three knots in an abandoned line, or a bowl frozen into resin. Ruins are not necessarily old. Growth may have tilted a platform, sealed a shelter inside new tissue, or carried its two ends apart within a single season.

At night, the High Hollow can seem almost uninhabited: cold fibre underfoot, one wet seam, stars that illuminate nothing. Then the note beneath the boots changes. Weather is entering the stems somewhere beyond sight. A herd answers across a distant membrane. Close by, a new bud splits with a clean, sharp crack.

Nothing becomes visible. The place simply reveals that its emptiness was distance, not absence—and that all its far-apart walls are alive.
