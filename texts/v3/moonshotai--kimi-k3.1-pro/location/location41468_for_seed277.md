## The Night Engine

The upland is not a mountain range but the raised back of a continent: a vast shield of dark rock lifted above the warmer lowlands, then worn for so long that height no longer means sharpness. Its summits are broad swells. Its valleys are shallow, patient depressions. Cliffs survive only where the rock is especially hard, and even these have rounded lips and skirts of settled debris. Every slope seems to have found the angle it can keep.

From any one place it appears almost empty. Stone continues beyond sight, interrupted by an occasional low rise, a mineral outcrop, a shallow basin. But lightning sometimes reveals the truth of its scale: tablelands overlapping tablelands, storm clouds stacked above separate countries of stone, rain falling in one distance while another distance remains dry. The upland is large enough to make its own weather. Warm basins breathe air upward; the rising currents draw moisture along the old ramps from far below; cloud cells are born, collide, and die without ever reaching the upland’s edge.

The height does not make it cold. Heat seeps steadily through the crust, and the high cloud deck holds much of it in. The rock is often warm to the touch. Wind arrives as a warm pressure rather than a chill. Even the pools, when there are pools, seldom cool completely before they vanish.

Light is scarcer than warmth. A dim star stands behind the cloud for part of the long rotation, but its coming rarely produces anything that could be called morning. At most, the darkness becomes brownish and shapes acquire uncertain edges. Usually there is only black stone under black air. On rare clear occasions, a few stars appear, small and hard and useless for navigation. Deep fissures sometimes hold a dull red glow, though this is heat rather than illumination. Animals that make light do so briefly and close to the body: a mating signal beneath a wing, a chemical flash in a pool, nothing that disturbs the larger dark.

Moisture here is less a visible feature than a recurring condition. Rain is intermittent and intensely local. A storm may soak one basin and leave the next under powdery dust. Between rains, fog creeps through low ground and condenses on exposed mineral crystals. Dew gathers in the grooves of stone, on the waxy leaves of scattered plants, and along the backs of certain beetles. Most of the upland’s water exists as films too thin to reflect anything.

A heavy rain produces brief runnels, then pools. Some pools are no larger than a footprint; others spread for kilometers while remaining only finger-deep. They carry iron, sulfur, and fine black silt. Around their margins, salt rings record each stage of evaporation. Soon afterward, the basin is dry again except for a coolness beneath the surface and a few sealed pockets of mud.

The crust is veined with graphite, magnetite, and conductive sulfides. Warm brine moving through deep faults separates electrical charge; storms add their own violent surplus. As a result, weak currents pass through the stone and its wet films, following paths that branch, narrow, fail, and reconnect over immense distances. Crystal-bearing layers flex under thermal pressure and give off slow vibrations. Some of these are below hearing. Others make a single tick, followed hours later by another.

Nothing designed this circulation, but work is done by it. Ions are driven through chemical films. Minerals are precipitated in repeating bands. Dormant tissues are stimulated. Buried eggs hatch. From a distance, the upland can seem industrial: heat enters below, moisture and charge move through distributed channels, and living matter is produced in pulses. Yet there is no cable, furnace, engine housing, road, wall, or ruin. Nothing has been constructed and nothing has been abandoned. The apparent machinery is simply the fit of geology, weather, and anatomy.

The vegetation accommodates this economy by remaining small. Much of the surface is bare. Thin lichen crusts cling to leeward rock, dry and gray for long periods, turning almost black when wet. In fractures grow sparse ribbon plants with conductive vessels and leaves held edge-on to the wind. Widely separated rosettes cup condensation at their centers. None rises high enough to cast a meaningful shadow, even if there were light enough for shadows.

After rain, the plants do not flare green. They darken. Their surfaces become wet violet, brown, or oily charcoal, and they release spores into the warming air. Then they slow again. A plain may look lifeless while carrying, just above its stone, a fine invisible fall of reproductive dust.

For all this austerity, animals are abundant. Their abundance is simply spaced according to the land. One may cross a broad expanse and see only a handful of moving shapes, while the dust is crossed by tracks, the crevices are crowded with sleepers, and the temporary mud contains more lives than water.

Dew beetles spend the dry intervals pressed against mineral outcrops, their ridged shells collecting what little condenses. Small blind runners move through connected fissures, feeding on lichen and on one another. Flat burrowing things wait beneath dust with their metabolism nearly stopped. When a pool forms, it clouds within hours with larvae, tiny crustaceans, and hunting worms. Dark-legged waders arrive before the water has finished spreading, already aware of the rain through changes in pressure and ground charge.

The largest animals are the slatebacks: low, broad grazers with heat-radiating folds along their flanks and mineralized plates over their shoulders. They scrape crusts from the rock and digest them with the help of symbiotic microbes. A herd may contain dozens of individuals and still appear scattered, each animal separated from the next by long distances. They communicate through slow pulses delivered into the ground. A foot stamps; a far-off body pauses; the herd gradually changes direction.

Their principal predators are long-legged coursers that hunt as much by electrical disturbance as by scent. They can detect the irregular charge of an injured animal, or the agitation that passes through a herd before it visibly flees. Coursers do not howl or call to one another. A hunt is usually only a series of soft claw-strikes, a brief struggle muffled by dust, and then stillness. Later, winged scavengers descend out of the dark, their membrane wings making almost no sound until they fold.

Rain turns these separate lives into a connected event. Water bridges mineral provinces that were electrically isolated. A weak pulse travels through the wet ground; chemical crusts awaken; spores germinate; pool eggs hatch. Slatebacks begin moving toward basins they cannot see. Coursers follow at a distance. Scavengers rise into the thermals above the storm’s path. For a short while, hundreds of square kilometers behave as one responsive body.

Then the water sinks or evaporates. Salts crystallize. Resistance increases. The connections break, and each basin returns to its own slower rhythm.

Because the upland is so vast, these stages never happen everywhere at once. One region may be loud with the brief ticking and clicking of pool life while another remains dormant beneath dust. Elsewhere, a herd is crossing bare stone; elsewhere still, a predator is waiting beside a track that has not been used for days. Weather, feeding, hunting, reproduction, dormancy, and erosion all proceed together, but out of phase.

The prevailing quiet is therefore not inactivity. It is a consequence of how the land transmits its messages. Much of the communication passes through stone rather than air. There are no forests to rustle, no permanent rivers to murmur, and no exposed edges to make the wind whistle. Footfalls disappear into dust. The deep vibration of the crust is felt in the ribs more readily than heard. Rain is the loudest common event, and after it comes a short period of dripping, clicking, and wing movement before the silence closes again.

Occasionally an electrical storm passes over everything. For an instant, white light stands the whole upland up out of the dark: old slopes, distant rain, scattered animals, wet stone shining without color. Thunder arrives late and softened by distance. Then the landscape returns to warmth, pressure, scent, and touch.

Nothing operates the Night Engine, and it has no product beyond its own continuance. Stone stores heat; water makes brief connections; life spends what those connections provide; death returns the materials to the crust. Locally, the system can fail for centuries and resume when rain finds a new path. As a whole, it has persisted long enough for its silences, its sparse growth, and its widely scattered animals to become not signs of deprivation, but the settled forms of a world in balance.
