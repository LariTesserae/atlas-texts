## The High Cistern

At the summit of an industrial tower, with the plain more than two kilometres below and cloud often passing beneath its foundations, there is a single warm room full of water.

The maintenance drawings call it **Header Nine: Biological Polishing**. It is about the size of a village church: forty paces across, twelve metres from its filter floor to the peak of its glass roof. All its boundaries are visible from the entrance lock. Almost nothing inside it is. Pipes, leaves, tanks, cable ladders, root curtains, and radiator fins occupy the chamber so completely that open water exists only as maintenance clearance—narrow, wavering passages in which a swimmer must turn sideways.

From outside, it resembles a rooftop reservoir. Closer, the resemblance becomes difficult.

The glass is only overhead. The sides are open to the altitude: steel columns, louvers, handrails, and beyond them an uninterrupted drop into sky. Yet the water remains within the surveyed rectangle of the chamber. It stands at the open sides in vertical, faintly trembling planes. At the corners it turns cleanly around bronze angle irons, forming a liquid edge almost as sharp as the metal.

There is no transparent wall to discover by touching it. A hand can be pushed through the side of the cistern into dry, thin air. The water offers a cool, elastic resistance, then admits the hand without splashing. Withdrawn, it leaves a shallow depression that takes several minutes to smooth away. Wind against the louvers bows the vertical surface slightly; hours later, it has recovered.

Below the floor, enclosed electric motors drive a set of enormous level engines. Their flywheels appear stationary. Their pistons are connected to no obvious outlet. According to the diagrams, they do not continuously lift or pressure the water. They renew its containment: a measurement, repeated mechanically, according to which the reservoir still has walls. While that measurement holds, gravity is willing to wait.

Inside, the topography is entirely manufactured. There is no bedrock, bank, soil, or silt. White ceramic thresholds rise abruptly between filtration cassettes. Black channels drop into grated sumps. Banks of finned heat exchangers stand like the pipes of a submerged organ, and the spaces between them have become narrow green ravines. A low “hill” is the rounded housing of a pump; a cliff is the side of a settling tank; a cave is the inspection space beneath a gearbox, already half occupied by roots.

Everything is young. Casting seams remain visible in the ceramic. Enamel is unchipped, bolt heads are crisp, and stencilled valve numbers can still be read through several metres of water. No rust softens the joints. No current has rounded an edge. The only conspicuous mark of age is a pale mineral line at the upper surface, so thin and straight that it looks drafted. The cistern is not deteriorating into a landscape. It has been built as one, before weather has had time to object.

The water is blood-warm and exceptionally clear, with a faint green cast borrowed from the leaves. Ordinary daylight enters through the sawtooth roof. It remains ordinary underwater: white on gauge faces, yellow on brass, dark red on the submerged stems. There are no luminous growths or hidden blue depths. On overcast days, plain industrial lamps switch on inside glass globes. Their light is almost unnecessary.

Above the water is a shallow, humid airspace. It smells of warm tile, wet metal, and crushed vegetation, with no suggestion of decay. Droplets condense on the roof so slowly that some remain there for months, swelling beside the glazing bars. Eventually one falls. The small tick it makes on the surface is the closest thing the cistern has to weather.

The vegetation is not an intrusion, although it has the density of a thicket. It grows from porous ceramic root shoes clamped to the structure. Fine, feathered fronds pack the filtration beds. Ribbon leaves coil through pipe racks without bending the pipes. Waxy, saucer-shaped leaves overlap just beneath the surface, while darker stems climb into the airspace and spread across trellises. Ivory flowers open there, almost motionless, within reach of a brass carriage mounted on the roof rail.

Every root bed has a service number. Every pipe doubles as a trellis, every trellis as a brace. From a distance, the chamber looks overgrown; from within, it becomes clear that the crowding is the design. Roots remove dissolved metals. Broad leaves oxygenate the water. Fallen foliage settles into screened hoppers rather than onto the floor, and is carried to warm digesters whose output returns, years later, through the nutrient lines. The plants polish the water, and the water carries the plants’ slow chemistry from one end of the room to the other.

No animal life participates.

There are no fish beneath the leaves, no snails on the tile, no translucent larvae in the sumps. Nothing darts away from a moving shadow. The upper girders hold no nests or webs, and the flowers receive no winged visitors. Once each growing season, the brass carriage advances a few centimetres a day along its rail, lowering soft brushes into the blossoms. It is the garden’s only gesture resembling pollination by touch.

The absence does not feel like an aftermath. Nothing seems to have fled or died out. Header Nine was simply designed without animals, and its stillness has never learned to accommodate their quick, incidental motions.

It is correspondingly quiet. The great engines below are felt more readily than heard: a faint pressure in a handrail, a hum conducted into a diver’s teeth. At long intervals a valve gives one muted click. A leaf may brush a grille. A roof droplet may fall. Otherwise there is no rush of water, because the current is too gradual to produce one, and no voices, insects, wings, or calls. A swimmer pushing through the vegetation is usually the loudest event in the chamber.

That gradualness governs everything. A complete exchange of water requires several decades. A loose leaf, if it does not lodge somewhere, can take most of a year to travel from inlet to outlet. The surface changes height by less than a finger’s width across the operational seasons. One must revisit a flywheel after many hours to discover that its chalk mark has moved. Even growth seems deliberate: a bud thickens for weeks before opening, and a root can spend a season fitting itself around a single bolt.

The cistern was nevertheless planted at full density. Its youth is in its sharp edges and unmarked machinery, not in sparseness. It appears to have been commissioned as a finished world, then immediately placed on clocks too slow for its builders.

Sometimes cloud rises around the tower. The daylight turns milk-white, the open sides vanish, and the warm block of water seems to float without support. When the cloud sinks again, the altitude returns all at once: the tower core falling away beneath the floor, the plain impossibly far below, and beyond the handrails nothing but air. The landscape is minute, bounded, and crowded, yet attached to an almost absurd amount of height.

Its official product is clear water, sent down through a return pipe no thicker than a wrist. But the output seems incidental. Header Nine principally manufactures continuation. Each patient stroke of the level engines reminds the water where its walls are. Each root holds a little chemistry in place. Each leaf adds oxygen to a system that has arranged not to need breath, wings, or hunger.

Nothing here is inert. The machines, the water, and the dense green growth have merely agreed upon the same long interval.
