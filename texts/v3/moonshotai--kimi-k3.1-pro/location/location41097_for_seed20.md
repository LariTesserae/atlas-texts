## The Drowned Nave

High on a young massif, just above the permanent cloud, there is a basin small enough to walk around in an hour and too deep to be a lake. Its walls form a steep, broken oval above the clouds. The sea inside is warm, olive-black, and overfull: it stands higher than the rim in a vertical meniscus, shuddering without spilling. No river feeds it, and no overflow leaves it. Clouds pressing against the exposed water slide aside, leaving long smears of mist on the stone.

The height should make the air cold. Instead, the mountain gives off a slow fever. At the rim the air is humid and blood-warm, smelling of hot iron, salt, and crushed green stone. Heat enters the water through fractures below, so the open basin is pleasantly warm while the depths immediately above a vent can scald. Even at noon the light is meagre. The sky has the colour of wet pewter, and illumination reaches the basin as though it has already passed through several hundred fathoms. Underwater there is no true darkness, only a brown-green dusk in which things become visible by being darker than their surroundings.

Nothing about the rock is mellow. The massif is too young for that. Basalt walls meet in splintered arrises; fresh fractures show blue, silver, and glassy black. There are no gravel beaches, no rounded cobbles, no patient slopes of sediment. Narrow ribs of stone rise from the bottom with wide reaches of empty water between them. White salt and ochre sulphur gather briefly along the hottest seams, then shear away. Scree consists of knives and wedges. When something breaks, its bright new face remains exposed until the thing itself disappears.

The sound reaches everything before any other sensation does.

From far down the mountain it resembles a storm trapped inside the rock. At the rim it stops resembling anything. The deepest voice is the massif tearing along heated joints, a grinding so low that it seems to come from the bones rather than the air. Over this, cooling glass cracks with the repeated concussion of falling masonry. Cavities in the water close with flat, enormous claps. Whole sheets of sea peel away from stone and collapse back with a roar that has no beginning clean enough to identify.

There is no interval short enough to be silence. A hand struck against the rock appears to make no sound at all. Speech is not merely difficult but physically irrelevant. Below the surface, submersion offers no refuge: the water carries the noise directly into teeth, sternum, and the soft spaces behind the eyes. It is less like hearing than like being a hollow space that something else is continually sounding.

Under that sound, the basin builds and demolishes itself.

A line of bubbles may draw itself across open water. Dark glass wells between them; within a breath, a knife-edged fin stands where there was nothing. It may persist long enough to catch the weak light along one face. Then it folds into a plume of black grains, which neither sink nor drift away, but thin until they are no longer included in the place. Elsewhere, the sea heaps into a vertical slab and hangs there, trembling. When it falls, it sometimes falls through the basin and returns as rain against the undersides of ledges.

Nothing weathers gradually. Stone goes directly from sharp birth to fracture to absence. Water likewise skips the gentler states: it is still, or standing on edge, or closing like a door.

Between concussions, the Nave almost obeys geography. Bubbles rise. Depth increases downward. The far wall remains far. Then a major blow passes through the basin, and one relationship comes loose.

It is never everything at once. That is what makes the place so difficult to dismiss as simple chaos.

Before and after may fail, so that an echo arrives first as a pressure in the chest, followed several seconds later by the cliff splitting. Up and down may fail: stones continue to fall while bubbles descend past them like heavy shot. Near and far may fail, bringing the opposite wall close enough to touch although its dim shape never appears to move. Once, depth may simply stop agreeing with descent, and a diver passes the same white mineral seam three times, always with more water overhead.

Everything not involved in the failure continues with pedantic normality. Warmth remains warmth. Salt still stings. Stone remains hard. A swimmer’s body still displaces water. Then the broken relationship returns, usually without restoring the exact arrangement that preceded it. A rock fin is several body-lengths to the left. The partial wall has one more step. The bottom, briefly reachable with a foot, is again somewhere below the reach of light.

The basin’s outer boundary is more dependable. Its footprint remains small. From the rim one can often see the entire steep enclosure at once, including the standing excess of sea above it. But pressure within the water belongs to a much deeper ocean; a short descent can produce an abyssal weight in the ears. The Nave seems to contain depth without requiring room for it.

The nearest thing to a building stands where a buttress most often forms a partial lee: a broken horseshoe of basalt laid in courses, chest-high, with three broad steps descending into unfractured stone. Farther away, a single squared block lies on a submerged shelf. Two upright stones at the rim resemble a threshold only from one angle.

There is no mortar, metal, carving, or portable object. There are no tools and no clear tool marks. The stones are as fresh as the cliffs around them. During some changes, the horseshoe remains solid masonry; during others, it resolves into nothing more than a set of unusually straight fractures in bedrock. If it is a ruin, it is a ruin without a past elsewhere. If it is an imitation, the mountain has learned the appearance of shelter but not its use. It provides no refuge from the roar, which passes through wall, water, and body alike.

Life is startlingly scarce despite the warmth. Thin olive films occupy certain hot seams, each patch separated from the next by expanses of bare glass. A few wine-dark algal threads, no longer than a hand, stream from protected cracks. Whole cliffs may lie between one clump and another. When their supporting stone is unmade, they usually vanish with it; occasionally a similar clump is found already attached to the next new ledge, wet and leaning with a current that has only just come into existence.

Animals are rarer still. A whole wall may carry one chalk-white snail, slowly grazing a film no wider than a handprint. At long intervals, a transparent, eyeless swimmer the length of a finger crosses the open water. It keeps its fins close and moves just before the largest concussions, in the pressure-hollow created by an echo arriving early. It is the only creature that seems to use the Nave’s broken order rather than merely endure it. There are no schools, no birds at the rim, no drifting rafts of weed, and no living call that can be separated from the larger noise.

In the visually quiet intervals, the place has an almost austere spareness: a few black ribs, great volumes of empty water, the small false shelter, perhaps one patch of weed and one pale snail. It resembles a room from which nearly everything has been carried out. But the stillness is only something the eyes are briefly allowed to believe. The mountain continues roaring beneath it.

The Nave does not appear to be approaching stability. It does not cool, fill with sediment, or simplify. Each convulsion returns it to sharpness and dusk. Its youth is not an age it will outgrow, but a condition it keeps recreating.

Seen from far below, it is only one wet black tooth among the high summits. Inside, it is a small ocean rehearsing the moment before an ocean has decided what a surface, a bottom, or a shore is for. The deafening roar is not something happening within the landscape. It is the sound of the landscape beginning again, too violently to finish.
