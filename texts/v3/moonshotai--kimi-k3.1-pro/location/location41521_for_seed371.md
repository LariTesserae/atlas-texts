## The Black Notch

At night, the Black Notch is mostly a noise with a floor.

Wind enters through the upper fracture with a deep, pressurized boom, then accelerates toward the narrow lower end, where it becomes a roar. Gusts comb through millions of stiff leaves, adding hiss, rattle, and a dry metallic scraping. Loose stones tick down the walls. Now and then something larger lets go: a crack, a moment of suspension, then an impact that shakes the ledges and starts a brief avalanche of answering clatters. Thunder arrives from beyond the rim and is thrown back and forth until the whole cut seems to be resounding.

There are no animal sounds between these events. No wings, no calls, no rustling bodies in the debris. When the wind drops, the absence does not become peaceful. Individual drops strike stone with hard, separate notes; wet sediment shifts; roots creak under the weight of the vegetation they support.

Lightning on the rim reveals why everything sounds so crowded. The Notch is small—a hundred paces from its blind upper end to the crack that drains it, and in places only twelve paces across—but its walls rise many times its width. They are not smooth walls. They are columns, blades, offset shelves, and splintered galleries, each ledge carrying another ledge above it. The sky is only a narrow seam of cloud overhead. The ground below is a confusion of angular scree, black sand, deep green cushions, and water shining briefly in depressions.

Then the flash ends, and all of it returns to darkness.

The cut lies at extreme elevation, near the summit of a young volcano. Outside it, the mountain is almost entirely dry ash, clinker, old snow, and wind-scoured rock. The open slopes are too exposed and too cold for continuous vegetation. The Notch should be barer still. Instead, two accidents of form sustain it: its depth shelters the floor from the killing wind, and a little residual volcanic warmth remains in the fractured rock below. The heat is not enough to make the place warm, or even to produce visible steam. It merely keeps some of the deeper fissures from remaining frozen. The air stays cold and thin; exposed edges collect rime. But within the cleft, liquid water is possible often enough to matter.

Geologically, the wound is fresh. Its andesite faces have not been softened by weather. Fractures terminate in sharp lips, and fallen blocks retain the clean, conchoidal surfaces of their breakage. There are no rounded river cobbles and few deep beds of soil. What soil exists has been made locally: wind-borne ash caught among roots, finely powdered rock, and generations of dead plant material compressed into dark, acidic layers. In several places the vegetation and its thin substrate hang together as a single mat, bulging away from the wall. In others, a fresh collapse has removed everything down to blue-black stone.

The plant cover is luxuriant without being tall. Nothing resembles a forest canopy. Most of the growth is below ankle height, though sedges and small ferns rise higher in the warmest, most protected recesses. Moss lies over older moss; clubmosses lace across the cushions; waxy rosettes pack the spaces between them. On stable ledges, separate colonies have merged until the underlying rock can no longer be seen. Their roots form dense, wet bundles through cracks and over edges. Higher up, where cloud water is less reliable, the growth thins to lichen and dark crusts, but even there almost every usable seam is occupied.

In occasional lightning, the place has the misleading appearance of an industrial works abandoned in mid-operation. Jointed lava rises in fluted stacks. Fracture shelves run in long, apparently deliberate lines. Root bundles hang like cable, and water drops from one narrow conduit to another. The whole notch seems arranged into intake, processing, and discharge.

Nothing here has been built. There are no pipes, wires, dressed blocks, foundations, or tool marks. The industrial character comes from the concentration and repetition of natural processes. Cloud is drawn in at the top and wrung out against cold stone and leaf surfaces. Wind drives the entire volume like an unregulated engine. Rock is split, dropped, crushed, and carried downward. Storm electricity supplies the only intense illumination; after a close strike, the wet air smells sharply of ozone. The Notch manufactures cloud water, grit, sediment, and new surfaces for plants to colonize. It has no purpose beyond the continuation of those processes.

It is damp, not drenched. There is no permanent stream and no deep tarn. Most of the water arrives indirectly, as cloud drifting through the upper seam. Droplets condense on the cold walls and on the immense combined surface of the vegetation. For a while, nothing happens except swelling beads and darkening stone. Then some threshold is passed. Drops merge, leaves bow, and several ledges begin dripping at once. A temporary rill threads across the floor, gathers in one or two black basins, and vanishes into open fractures.

Rain occasionally adds a brief excess, turning every shelf into a spout and filling the lower end with a roaring spray. Between these episodes, the outer faces can become almost dry. Moss cushions tighten and dull, ash blows in from the surrounding slopes, and only the deepest layers retain moisture. A pool present at dusk may be a stain by morning. Dampness persists in pockets rather than spread evenly over the place.

The ecology is correspondingly short and severe. Spores and lightweight seeds arrive on the wind; animals have established no part of the community. There is no browsing to open the plant mats, no burrowing to mix the sediment, no insect pollination. The sedges release their pollen to the same turbulent air that distributes fern spores and fragments of moss. Some vegetation spreads when a saturated mat tears loose and pieces of it lodge below. Cool temperatures slow decay, so dead material accumulates beneath the living surface until either water removes it or gravity takes the whole shelf.

This makes the Notch dense in time as well as space. New growth stands on older growth; old growth stands on debris; debris rests on rock that has itself only recently fallen. A ledge can contain several stages of the garden at once. Elsewhere, a fresh scar contains none.

And the place is always revising itself. Fog pours upward past the walls, stops, and drains back down. A gust reverses, and every wet plant releases its load in a sudden glittering sheet. A rill appears, cuts a new line through black sand, then fails. Grit hisses along the floor. Freezing water widens a joint; minutes or weeks later, the joint gives way. A slab falls, carrying decades of vegetation with it, and the impact exposes another surface on which condensation can begin.

Nothing appears from nothing, and nothing vanishes without remainder. Water runs downhill. Fractured rock falls. Plants occupy shelter, reproduce by whatever means the wind allows, and die where conditions turn against them. The turbulence comes from ordinary processes overlapping too closely, too quickly, in too small a volume.

To stand in the Notch would be to feel extremely high and extremely enclosed at once: thin air that does not quite satisfy a breath, cold spray on the face, vegetation pressing densely around the legs, and hardly any visible sky. Above and below lie the dry, nearly empty slopes of the mountain. Between them, for this brief geological interval, the fracture keeps its dark garden running.

Its product is not a stable paradise, but a succession of gardens—each one overcrowded, temporary, and rooted in the wreckage of the last.
