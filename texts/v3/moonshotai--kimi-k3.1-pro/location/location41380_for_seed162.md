## The Unfinished Escarpment

Nothing in the Unfinished Escarpment was built, though almost everything in it works.

It is less a mountain range than a continent raised in one geological inhalation. Its rim stands above a slate-colored sea of cloud, and its interior is broad enough to turn weather aside, breed new weather, and keep several unrelated climates moving across it at once. From above, it resembles a vast piece of dark material struck while hot: blade ridges, splintered plateaus, deep fault basins, and long steps of blue-black stone descending into shadows that do not always correspond to the slopes beneath them.

Everything looks freshly exposed. Basalt breaks in conchoidal shells. Veins of silica shine dully inside fractures. Iron-rich surfaces have only begun to bloom orange-red. Cliffs rise without aprons of rubble, and ravines have walls so sharp that their upper edges disappear against the sky. There are almost no rounded hills, no deep old soils, no valleys softened by long possession. Where stone breaks, the fragments seldom remain for long. They sink, darken, and are gradually taken back into the ground. The escarpment does not usually wear away. It is withdrawn and replaced.

The altitude steals breath, but not warmth. Heat wells continually from the young crust, so even the highest rock remains warm underhand, and there is no snow except as an occasional white mineral efflorescence that resembles it from a distance. The air smells of hot iron, crushed stems, and the faint ammoniac sharpness of life crowded into thin soil. At the vent fields it grows hotter and heavily aromatic, though the vents themselves are only hollow mineral throats deposited in upright ranks. From far off they resemble stacks; close up, each one is visibly grown, its rim layered like the lip of a shell.

The light never becomes strong. A small, brownish sun sometimes shows through the upper haze, more a direction in the sky than an object. Most illumination arrives evenly and without an obvious source, the color of weak tea or old bronze. Nearby leaves are green-black; distant ridges fade through charcoal and violet. There is no reliable dawn, only a slight clarification of the haze, and no true night, only hours when the land becomes harder to distinguish from the sky.

Water seldom organizes itself into anything as permanent as a river. Rain arrives in narrow cells, sometimes soaking one ridge while the next remains powder-dry. It may fall for several minutes or for a day, always with the same fine, subdued hiss. Much of it disappears immediately into warm fractures. Some collects in fault pools whose bottoms are still glassy. Some remains on the surfaces of leaves, held in beads too large and too still for the gentle air.

Before the dimmest hours, the upper foliage cools just enough for condensation. Then whole groves become wet without rain. Moisture gathers along leaf edges, runs down waxy stems, and enters root mats already crowded with waiting mouths. These brief wettings govern much of the ecology. Between them, the escarpment can look almost austere: silver dormant moss, folded fronds, bare mineral dust, isolated pockets of green. During them, the green seems to exceed the available space.

From far above, vegetation appears mainly as seams. From within those seams, it is layered beyond easy counting. Copper-colored crusts spread over the stone. Above them lie black, overlapping leaves adapted to the weak light, then translucent ferns with water bladders at their bases, then tall, flexible stalks that turn their narrowest edge to the warm updrafts. Pale root fans feed chemically where the rock is hottest. Fungal threads pass through all of it, binding living tissue, dead tissue, and mineral grains into a soil only a few fingers deep but tremendously active.

It is difficult to find a handspan occupied by only one kind of life. Mites inhabit the moss beneath leaves whose surfaces carry their own grazing films. Threadlike animals live inside wet stems. Spore-moths shelter in the folded joints of fronds, their matte wings patterned with almost invisible electrical markings. Every temporary pool contains larvae, translucent swimmers, or flat creatures that lie so closely against the bottom that they appear to be imperfections in the stone.

The larger animals are numerous and mostly silent. Herds of low, many-legged grazers move through the vegetation in dense, overlapping processions. Their feet are broad and soft; their flexible backs quickly become dusted with basalt or powdered lichen. They crop in waves, one mouth beginning as another closes, and when several thousand synchronize, the herd produces a faint ticking like cooling stone. Above them, wide gliders hang in the thermal columns for hours without visibly moving their wings. Smaller animals crowd the spaces between stems, under barklike mineral growths, and inside the grazers’ folded armor.

Despite all this motion, the dominant impression is quiet. Wax and moss absorb small sounds. The thin upper air carries voices poorly, and most animals communicate through touch, brief changes of color, or electrical signals conducted through wet roots. Even the weather is subdued. Lightning often appears without thunder; its arrival is felt as pressure in the chest and a taste of metal. A ridge can split open with no more noise than a single deep click. Rain, when it comes, is usually the loudest thing for hundreds of kilometers.

Beneath certain basins, the resemblance to machinery becomes difficult to dismiss. Fault-bounded columns of jointed basalt rise and fall under tectonic pressure, driven by pulses of hot gas from below. Their strokes pump air through networks of natural vents. The moving air dries one basin, draws moist haze into another, and carries spores across distances that no winged animal could cross. Some pumps complete a cycle in minutes. Others take years. Where several operate together, the ground rises by almost imperceptible degrees, as though the basin were breathing.

The groves participate. Tectonic stress charges quartz and magnetite seams, and wet root webs carry the charge away in branching pulses. A dull violet disturbance can pass from grove to grove across an entire province without making the landscape bright. At each pulse, seed valves open, spore capsules contract, and millions of nearly identical reproductive bodies are released into the same moving air. The grazers answer by changing direction. Pool animals begin to divide. For a short time, separate patches of life behave like parts of one dispersed organism.

There are no tools to find, no shelters, roads, cables, or ruins. Even animal trails fade as soon as the vegetation shifts. Nothing has been made into an object separate from the process that made it. The industry here produces no cargo and leaves no inventory. Its output is warm air, charged ground, spores, new crust, new generations, and the conditions for another cycle.

That repetition serves a deeper purpose.

Persistence is not automatic in the escarpment. A form remains stable only while it is, in some sense, continually being enacted. Stone repeats its crystalline stresses. Groves repeat growth, charge, and discharge. Herds repeat their migrations even when the original reason for a route has vanished. Where these rhythms agree, a place may remain recognizable for decades. Where they drift apart, the landscape begins to make mistakes.

A shadow may arrive several seconds before the frond that casts it. Condensation may appear on empty air, outlining a pool that existed in some earlier arrangement of the valley. Two ridges can briefly occupy the same ground, visible through one another and crowded with incompatible vegetation. Distance becomes negotiable: a cleft crossed in three steps going north may have no far side going south. Rain sometimes falls onto bare stone from a cloud that does not form until afterward.

More violent failures create and remove country outright. A fault basin fills with black fronds, then with transparent stone, then becomes a wall. A cliff vanishes without collapsing, leaving its cloud of gliders circling empty air. A ridge emerges already sharp, already warm, already carrying a thin line of moss. There is no rubble because there was no interval of destruction. One version has simply ceased to be repeated, and another has taken its place.

This is why the escarpment remains so young in appearance. Its age is not written in erosion but in recurrence. The oldest things are not objects at all, but habits: a particular shape of pool, a sequence of five electrical pulses, a herd’s tendency to turn left before rain.

Life has adapted to that instability with extravagant redundancy. Some plants put roots into several closely adjacent versions of the same fracture; only one needs to survive. Pool creatures dry into transparent films that can remain viable after the pool, and sometimes the entire valley containing it, has gone. A herd may emerge from a spatial disturbance with more animals than entered it. The additional grazers are not illusions. They are hungry, warm, and capable of breeding, and the vegetation must accommodate them. Conversely, an absence can spread through a food web with terrible speed, leaving dense foliage full of unanswered signals.

The predators are especially attuned to small failures of repetition. They do not merely follow scent or tracks. They watch for a footfall that happens twice, a reflection that hesitates, a patch of moss breathing out of phase with the rest. Their long, narrow bodies are often surrounded by faint afterimages, and it is not always clear which body will be present when they strike.

At the scale of the whole escarpment, this turbulence acquires an unexpected serenity. There is always rain somewhere, always a silent herd crossing a wet seam, always a province pulsing faintly with underground charge, always a ridge becoming something else beyond the reliable horizon. From a high blade of basalt, the land can appear almost motionless: dark steps, green faults, low cloud, distant curtains of rain. Only prolonged attention reveals that none of the silhouettes remain quite themselves.

Occasionally, for a few moments, many of the cycles align. Condensation gathers across an entire basin. Fronds open together. The pool animals unfold. Thousands of grazers lower their heads at once, and the buried pumps complete their stroke on the same electrical pulse that opens the spore valves.

Then the whole escarpment gives one soft, comprehensive click, like a vast thing finding its place.

It does not settle. Another basin is already beginning to disagree with itself. The pumps draw breath, the herds move on, and fresh stone warms beneath the dim sky. Nothing here is finished; finishing is not one of its processes.
