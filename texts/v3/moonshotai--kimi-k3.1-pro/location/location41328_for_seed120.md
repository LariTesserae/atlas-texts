## The Garden in the Highest Brick

At the summit of a mountain with no bedrock, one brick is hollow.

The mountain is a construction from its deepest course to its last: hand-kneaded clay, crushed potsherd, reed matting, timber cribbing, and fiber cord. Whoever made it used stone blades, wooden mallets, baskets, and bare palms. There is no mortar finer than mud, no joint more precise than a thumb could press. Yet the upper works stand above almost all weather. At noon the zenith is dark, and the clouds far below resemble a separate, luminous country. Outside, the thin wind makes only a papery hiss against the clay.

The hollow brick is no larger than a loaf. A narrow vent in one corner exudes hot, wet air and the smell of crushed leaves. Its warmth is startling at that altitude. So is the noise. Put an ear near the vent and the brick sounds like a storm shut inside a jar.

The vent would not admit a thumb. Inside, nevertheless, there is a gorge four paces long.

Both measurements are exact. The brick simply keeps its exterior and interior under different agreements about distance.

Seen from within, the place is less a cavity than a gorge beneath a low green ceiling. Three uneven terraces descend to a basin no wider than a cup, though objects dropped into it sometimes fall for several seconds. The walls rise in soft, sheer folds. Their strata are fingerprints. Palm whorls serve as cliffs; the crescents left by fingernails have become ledges and hanging valleys. In one wall, the impression of a reed basket forms a regular honeycomb of cells, each cell filled with water, roots, or black leaf mold.

Nothing here is a found material. The apparent boulders are lumps of clay bearing tool marks. The scree is broken pottery, every fracture still sharp. The soil is a deliberate mixture of powdered charcoal, shredded matting, ash, and decomposed fiber. Watercourses are split reeds lashed together with cord. Even the deepest-looking crevice ends not in stone but in another made surface, usually one still bearing the stroke that formed it.

Vegetation occupies nearly all of it.

Moss fills the fingerprints first, a damp green pile deep enough to hide the original hand. Liverwort sheets the potsherds. Small ferns spring from the reed lashings, their croziers packed so closely that they uncurl against one another and emerge deformed, glossy, and vigorous. Duckweed covers the basin except where falling drops keep opening black holes in it. A vine with heart-shaped leaves has climbed the same wall three times, using its own earlier stems as support.

There is no clear patch of floor, no open section of wall, no unoccupied interval between leaf and leaf. Every frond holds up another frond. Roots bind the cord that binds the reeds that carry the water in which further roots are floating. The air itself seems fully occupied: by vapor, by green smell, by heat, and by sound. Moving through the garden would mean negotiating with it leaf by leaf. There is room to sit, but not room to turn without being touched.

Water is not confined to the basin. It is a condition of the whole chamber. The clay sweats in clean beads along the lines of the palm prints. The reed gutters fill and overflow one after another, never quite in the same order. Warm condensation gathers beneath the leaves and drops with exaggerated weight. Every few minutes the woven ceiling releases a brief rain, hard enough to flatten the duckweed and set all the terraces trickling.

The first water may have been carried here in gourds. The garden has since taken over that labor.

Its rain does not always fall downward. Water runs toward whichever surface is newest and least finished. Usually that means the floor. Sometimes it is a fresh swelling high on the wall, and then the basin empties upward in bright threads. Drops rise through the ferns without breaking, gather beneath the clay, and disappear into it. The wall swells a little more. A new ledge begins.

Light remains dim through all of this. Beyond the vent, the high day is often white and blinding, but the opening provides a view without providing much illumination. Inside, green grades almost into black. Water gives off most of the visible gleam, throwing weak reflections onto the undersides of leaves.

Yet there are shadows—long, sharp ones, entirely out of keeping with the soft light. Several belong to leaves that have not yet opened. When one of those leaves finally uncurls, it fits its waiting shadow exactly. Others are cast by handprints that have not appeared. Their arrival is usually announced by the sound of wet clay being pressed.

For the garden is loud even between rains.

Drips return from distances too great for the chamber to contain. Reed channels knock against their bindings. Leaves scrape with a dense, leathery insistence. The basin makes a continuous hollow bubbling, as if far more water were moving beneath it than its surface could hold. Underneath all this is the sound of work: stone scraping clay, a wooden mallet landing in slow strokes, cord being pulled tight, baskets emptied, wet palms smoothing a wall.

No workers are visible.

The sounds generally precede their effects. A mallet strikes; a moment later, a dimple appears in the terrace. There is the creak of cord, and only then does a new lasing bind two reeds together. Once, the noise of a basket being set down continues for several breaths before a shallow basket-mark rises in the floor.

Silence, when it comes, is brief and unsafe. The water loses its agreement about down. Roots loosen from their channels. The far wall becomes transparent enough to show another garden behind it, similarly crowded but differently arranged. Then the work resumes with a concussion, and everything returns at once—not always to the position it occupied before.

Nothing in the garden is weathered. There are no rounded pebbles, no softened corners, no silvered wood, no ancient paths worn through moss. Water does not erode the clay; it persuades the clay to swell into further forms. A fallen potsherd is gradually incorporated into a fresh lump. A collapsing wall reveals not an older interior but a newer one, still wet and without fingerprints. The landscape is not aging toward ruin. It is thickening toward an event.

Its unfinishedness is structural.

Whenever a made surface is given its final gesture—its last corner squared, its last smoothing completed, its channel properly closed—it ceases to be part of the interior. It becomes exterior brick. One terrace vanished in this way, and the hollow place grew fractionally smaller. Since then, every edge has been left expectant. Channels begin without arriving. Steps lead halfway toward ledges. Three stone blades lie in a basket with their handles perpetually half-lashed. The garden preserves itself by refusing completion.

Animal life is sparse enough to seem ceremonial. A translucent snail grazes algae from a single shard, leaving a trail that later becomes a thread of seepage. Three midges live beneath the vine leaves. A spider, no larger than a pinhead, has fastened its web between a reed gutter and an unfinished tool handle. The web catches mostly rain, which sets it plinking. Now and then it catches a midge instead. That small struggle is the only visible hunt.

There are no frogs in the basin, no birds in the canopy, no burrowing things under the soil. The roaring place is almost entirely plants, water, and workmanship.

Meanwhile, the garden continues to grow higher without rising.

At irregular intervals the floor deepens by the width of a hand, though nothing in the chamber moves. Through the vent, the clouds are suddenly farther away. Another course has appeared beneath the mountain—not placed on top of it, but inserted below everything that already existed. Height accumulates under the garden like sediment in reverse. The stars remain where they were. The distance to any conceivable ground increases.

Occasionally, between a mallet stroke and its delayed effect, the vent shows somewhere else: not the black zenith or the white country of cloud, but an immense wet palm closing around a lump of clay. In that clay are flecks resembling bricks, leaves, stars, and entire weather systems. Then the delayed stroke arrives. The palm vanishes. The garden is once again at the summit, packed and steaming in its dimness.

Warm rain leaps from the basin toward the ceiling. A fern forces itself through a new handprint. The spider draws up its rain-heavy web. And far below, in a depth that did not exist a moment ago, another hand begins smoothing the clay—carefully, and not quite to the end.
