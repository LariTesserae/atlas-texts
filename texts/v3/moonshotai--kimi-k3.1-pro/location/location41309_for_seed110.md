## The Unfinished Hollow

High in a young volcanic ridge, above the last summit vegetation and above the altitude where water ordinarily remains liquid for long, a narrow seam turns behind an upright slab. The mountain outside is still raw from its own making. Its blocks are angular, its gullies little more than suggestions, and almost no scree has gathered beneath the cliffs. It seems less to be weathering than still deciding what shape to take.

The seam admits a person sideways and almost no weather at all. After two turns, it closes off the sky. Beyond lies a hollow nine paces long and five across at its widest: a single, intimate chamber whose darkness is complete. Eyes do not adjust to it. There is no starlight at the entrance, no phosphorescence in the water, no pale mineral gleam. Within the hollow, landscape is known through warmth, touch, nearness, and sound.

Warmth comes first, even and enveloping, a little like the heat of a held hand. Then the wetness. The air is saturated without being misty; moisture gathers on the lips and eyelashes and does not cool them. There is no hot center to the chamber and no colder edge. The warmth simply occupies the whole volume, as constant at the ceiling as at the floor.

No stream cut this room. Nothing here appears to have been cut at all. The hollow seems instead to be an absence around which new rock has gathered. Its walls have a hard volcanic core, but every inward-facing surface is unfinished: skins of silica, blisters of clay-soft mineral, swollen ribs that approach one another and stop just short of joining. Some descend from the ceiling like the beginnings of columns. Others rise to meet them and remain a finger’s width away. There are no pebbles, no sand, no polished channels, and almost no dust.

The small space is densely crowded with these beginnings. Mineral buds, hollow stone reeds, concentric lips, and thin membranes occupy nearly every hand’s breadth. In places a person can touch wall with both elbows while the ceiling brushes the hair. One crooked route leads through them to the center. The chamber is not large enough to feel vast, yet it contains more surfaces than seems reasonable for its size.

At the center, the continuous mineral floor dips beneath a pool. The water is ankle-deep at its margin and knee-deep in the middle, warm from surface to bottom. It has no inlet that gushes and no outlet that drains. Instead, the whole room weeps. Thin films coat the walls; water beads on overhead stone; seepage gathers in shallow depressions without making streams. The pool is less a separate body of water than the lowest expression of a wetness present everywhere.

Most of the time, there is no sound. Wind does not reach through the bent entrance. There are no insects, no bats, no birds, and no running water to provide a background murmur. A voice is softened by the close, crowded surfaces rather than returned as an echo. Occasionally a drop falls and gives one small, round note. Nothing answers it. The intervals between such sounds are too irregular to measure time, and the silence afterward seems identical to the silence before.

Vegetation is present only in a few isolated clumps, fewer than a dozen in the entire chamber. They grow where seepage emerges between mineral ribs. Each begins as a pale fan of roots and sends up several waxy threads bearing a single folded scale, something between a leaf and a tightly closed sheath. They are plants by local concession: they have roots and something like leaves, but no greenness and no need for sky. Their tissues take whatever they require from the warm, mineral-laden water. None has spread into a mat. None has produced litter. At a distance from them, the stone is entirely bare.

The only animal is a tiny blind water-louse, translucent and no longer than a grain of rice. Even it is rare. A visitor might encounter none, or find one motionless beneath the pool’s edge, its antennae resting against a plant root. Now and then one makes a slow circuit of a few millimeters and settles again. Nothing hunts it. There are no shells, carcasses, droppings, or other evidence that generations have passed through. The hollow supports life, but does not allow life to accumulate.

A few human things are scattered among the mineral forms. Near the entrance, five stones have been fitted into a knee-high crescent, too low to be a wall and too narrow to make a comfortable seat. Beside the pool sits a palm-sized bowl, pecked and ground from stone, containing a shallow measure of water. Two knapped flakes and a coil of hand-twisted fiber have been wedged into a crevice. Four short grooves mark one wall, their edges still fresh.

There is no metal, no fired clay, no joinery, no hearth, and nothing built for shelter. The objects are too sparse to suggest occupation, yet too deliberate to be dismissed. They might have been practical leavings: a cup for warm water, tools carried over the ridge, stones arranged to keep something from rolling. They might equally have been tests—a bowl set beneath a seep to see whether it would fill, a stack made to see whether it would remain, grooves cut to discover whether a mark could endure. Nothing about them explains how their makers worked in the dark.

The strongest impression of the hollow, even without sight, is one of imminence. A column has almost joined. A mineral shell has almost closed. A leaf has almost opened. A drop has almost fallen. The bowl beneath the seep seems perpetually almost full.

But the *almost* never resolves.

This is not ordinary frozenness. The water can be rippled. The little animal moves. Warmth continues to enter a visitor’s skin. Rather, every process has an exact counterpart. Seepage is balanced by absorption; condensation by water returning into the rock; mineral deposition by an equal, quiet dissolving. No channel deepens. No rim rises. The pool does not gain or lose a measurable depth. Even the wet stone’s softness never becomes either mud or harder stone.

Events are permitted. Development is not.

The chamber has no day, season, weather, or succession. Its geology remains newborn without becoming recent, and its stillness is not the stillness of death. Water, warmth, stone, plant, and animal are held in a closed equilibrium in which nothing is finally used up and nothing is completed. The hollow permits disturbances, then returns to the same state without haste.

Outside, the mountain resumes a world of consequences. Water travels downhill. Frost opens fractures. Stone begins its long surrender to slope. Plants unfold, seed, and die.

Inside, high above the valleys, the warm darkness remains crowded with beginnings. It is a spring, a cave, and a garden held together as possibilities, with none required to arrive. It is not waiting for time to start. Its finished state is to remain unfinished.
