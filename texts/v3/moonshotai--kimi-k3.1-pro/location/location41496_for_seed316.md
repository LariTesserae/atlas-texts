## The Last Flat

At the end of its long wearing-down, the world did not become still. It became the Last Flat: a warm, continent-wide shallowness where water, silt, vapor, and aftermath have been ground so finely together that none of them can keep a separate name for long.

There is no dry land in the strict sense, and very little open water. The prevailing material is a gray-olive sediment with the consistency of thick flesh, pierced by warm seepage and overlaid by moving films of brown, opaline, or iron-red water. A foot does not break the surface so much as persuade the surface to close around it. Water rises through the footprint from below while more condenses onto it from above. When the foot is withdrawn, the print may remain for an hour, or seal before the leg has moved.

Nothing permanent stands high. The broadest swells rise less than a hand’s width in a day’s walking. Yet the Flat is not featureless. Its surface is crowded with transient relief: silt blisters, subsiding domes, threads of current, shallow basins that fill by becoming deeper in memory rather than in fact. Rare stones protrude, rounded almost beyond mineral recognition. They weep continuously. Clear water gathers in their pores and runs down them even when they are already submerged.

The old mountains are still here, but only as changes in composition. One crosses a former range by noticing that the mud becomes faintly blue, that the water grows bitter with copper, that for several days the rounded stones resemble vertebrae instead of teeth. Then the taste fades. The mountain has been crossed. Its summit and its deepest buried root now differ in elevation by less than the thickness of a rain skin.

The Flat is vast enough that weather does not simply pass over it; weather is one of its dimensions. A single storm can contain provinces of rain, calm interiors with their own cloud systems, and slow boundaries along which warm vapor folds back into itself for years. Pressure arrives before wind. Wind often never arrives at all. Instead, the whole atmosphere seems to lean, and trillions of shallow water films slide in the same direction without making waves.

The light is dim and sourceless, a tarnished pewter-brown filtered through vapor so deep that cloud and air are only degrees of concentration. There is no visible sun, though sometimes a brighter region occupies one portion of the sky for days. Shadows are pale, brief, and unreliable. Warmth is more constant than illumination. The air rests against skin at almost skin temperature, and condensation forms on anything that attempts to be cooler.

Rain frequently begins without anything falling. The air simply becomes more water. Beads appear in midair, join into hanging lenses, and drop only after they have grown too heavy to remain part of the atmosphere. At other times rain falls through earlier rain, drop striking drop until the shower bursts sideways in glittering horizontal spray. There are no clean intervals between droplet, mist, surface water, and steam.

The crowdedness of the place is not vegetal. There are no forests, almost no mats of floating weed, and long reaches where nothing visibly lives. The Flat is packed instead with occurrences. Every patch of ground seems to contain more states than it has room to express. Mud swells beneath water that is already draining from the mound not yet formed. Bubbles overlap without touching. Currents occupy the same narrow seam in opposite directions, not canceling but taking turns too rapidly to distinguish. Even the haze feels full, as though each clear space were only a transparency laid over another transparency.

And over everything is the roar.

It has no single source and no direction. The deepest part comes through the ground: a continental grinding, not of stone against stone but of saturated sediment continually rearranging its own weight. Above that is the hiss of condensation, the ripping sound of water films pulled into upright sheets, the dense clap of basins collapsing. Small sharp detonations come from silt blisters bursting underfoot. Larger ones arrive as pressure rather than sound, a blow to the chest followed, sometimes much later, by the sight of a distant dome folding in on itself.

The loudness is made worse by its continuity. There is no silence in which hearing can recover. A particularly vast collapse does not always sound louder; the entire roar simply acquires more mass. At times it organizes into something like surf, breathing, or a multitude shouting on the other side of a wall. Then the pattern disperses. Nothing here calls. Nothing needs to.

The surface works with an energy that would suggest an anthill, a foundry, or a city being built and sacked simultaneously, except that there is no visible worker and no accumulated product. A blister rises into a black dome. Its skin develops ribs. The ribs branch into vaults, the vaults into a small, precise country of ridges and cavities. For several minutes it may resemble coral, a shell, a leaf skeleton, or a city seen from very high above. Then warm water wells through its center. The whole structure softens, leans, and goes back into the Flat with a concussion that shudders through nearby pools.

Elsewhere, water stands up in slow cylinders. Mud draws itself into spirals. A temporary lake flashes into existence across several square kilometers, reflects a sky the wrong color, and drains without wetting the ground around it. New channels cut furiously through old channels that have not yet finished disappearing. Creation and destruction are not opposite processes here. Collapse is merely how material is passed to the next attempt.

What has eroded is not only stone. The clean separations that once made landscape intelligible—land and water, sound and touch, before and after—have also been worn soft. They still exist, but under pressure they leak.

Water, in particular, no longer reliably seeks the lowest place. It seeks the least occupied moment. Thus it may run uphill into a depression that was empty an hour ago, vanish from a full pool and return as rain on the same spot, or collect in the reflection of a basin rather than in the basin itself. The report of a bursting dome can arrive while the dome is still rising. A puddle may show the smoothness that will follow its own violent boiling. These are not exceptions to the Flat’s behavior. They are how it relieves congestion.

The few made things are almost shocking in their simplicity.

They are widely scattered: seven dark blocks set in a straight line; a roofless cell barely large enough to stand inside; four worn steps descending into water and emerging, without turning, on the same level; a single narrow opening framed by two upright stones. Their material is denser than anything nearby and so old that its surfaces feel soapy. Warmth lingers in their inner corners. Sparse rust-colored growth occupies the joints.

There are no vessels, fasteners, roads, inscriptions, or machines. Nothing turns, strikes, measures, or contains a mechanism. If these works had makers, their entire remaining technology was placement: one stone against another, one interval left open. The Flat repeatedly imitates them in mud—brief walls, crooked doorways, stairs that melt before they can be climbed—as though trying to learn a word whose meaning it has forgotten.

Life is sparse and cautious. The most common visible plant is seamwort, a low growth of translucent, reddish filaments found only where two versions of the surface fail quite to coincide. Part of it roots in sediment while the rest disappears into air that is wet somewhere else. It does not sway with weather. It trembles when the local past and present pull apart. Isolated black cups of lichen endure on the made stones, and occasionally a single leafless stem rises from a stable seep, no higher than a finger. Beyond these, hundreds of square kilometers may carry nothing greener than an olive sheen of microscopic life on the water.

There is no opportunity for succession. A plant cannot prepare the ground for another plant when the ground itself may be replaced before dusk. Vegetation survives not by conquering the Flat but by occupying small mercies in its attention.

Animals are rarer still. The most often reported is probably the least often seen: a colorless, eyeless sheet of cartilage moving just beneath the silt, wide as a stretched hide and thin enough that its passage barely lifts the surface. It feeds on the microscopic films, or on some difference between them. Where it passes, the sediment becomes briefly and impossibly dry, a pale seam closing behind it. It leaves no track, no remains, and no sound. It is uncertain whether many such creatures exist, or whether the same animal is encountered at different ages and distances.

Very occasionally, the local turbulence exhausts itself. This does not produce silence—the deep roar remains in the ground and in the bones—but the smaller noises draw away. Water becomes a dim brown mirror. A seamwort stem stands beside a drowned stone threshold. Far below their reflections, where the cloud ceiling ought to be, something mountainous can sometimes be seen: blue, immense, and already dissolving.

Then the mirror arches. Water rises through it. The roar closes in from every direction, and the Flat resumes its furious experimentation.

From high enough, though nothing natural remains high enough to offer such a view, all that violence would average into perfect levelness. Every dome is paid for by a collapse; every sudden lake by an absence elsewhere; every remembered mountain by its return to silt. Erosion has won, not by stopping change, but by making all change ultimately inconsequential to the shape of the whole.

The plain has reached its final form. It has not reached, and may never reach, a final moment.
