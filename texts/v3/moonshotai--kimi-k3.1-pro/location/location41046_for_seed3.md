## The Survey City of Vael

Vael stands so high that nearly all its weather happens beneath it. Yet it is not a bright place. The upper sky remains a deep iron-gray through most of the year, and the sun seldom does more than pale one region of cloud. Much of the city’s daylight arrives indirectly, reflected upward from the immense cloud sea below. Cliffs are illuminated along their undersides. Eaves glow faintly while the streets beneath them remain dark. At noon, Vael resembles a town at the last usable hour of dusk.

The mountain beneath the city has no agreed height.

Survey pillars set into its summit ridges give different answers in different decades, and not always in the same order. This is partly uplift and partly something for which the surveyors have no satisfactory term. The massif is still coming into being: a broad, violent accumulation of upright strata, tilted benches, and unweathered faces of blue-black stone. Its crystals have sharp edges. Its scree has not learned to be round. Clefts begin as fractures and end blindly hundreds of meters down, without streams, sediment fans, or any of the other patient consequences of a developed landscape. There are valleys in Vael only by courtesy. Most are simply places where the mountain has opened and not yet decided what to put in the gap.

Deep heat moves through this young rock. It keeps the inhabited benches cool rather than killingly cold, though frost feathers the higher quarries and breath remains visible outdoors. Every few decades, the whole region gives a long, subterranean report. afterward, a foundation may need repacking, a bridge may require a new bearing, and one of the summit ridges may stand slightly nearer the clouds than it did before. No single lifetime sees much change. The archives, however, show streets rising through their retaining walls like objects in a very slow flood.

Vael is urban without being compact. Its districts are distributed across tens of kilometres of mountain, each gathered around some practical necessity: an ore face, a geothermal bore, a sheltered cistern basin, a junction of cableways. A district contains stacked workers’ houses, market arcades, machine shops, bathless washing halls, depots, courts, and narrow streets roofed against falling stone. Then the city stops. Beyond the last pressure door may lie two kilometres of naked rock before the next staircase, or nothing but a cable disappearing into dim air.

Consequently, there are many more streets than crowds. Whole arcades wait for populations that have not arrived. Cranes stand over foundations for generations. New construction is often built directly across older construction, not because the old has failed, but because the ground has changed its mind about slope. The oldest wards are brick and thick masonry; above and around them run riveted iron galleries, adjustable rail beds, concrete engine plinths, telescoping water mains, and power lines supported on articulated masts. Doors are numbered by district and survey epoch. A map bears the year in which it last agreed with the ground.

The city’s materials are dark with damp. Iron handrails leave orange stains on the palm. Copper roofs have gone nearly black. Freshly cut masonry dries pale at its center while its faces remain glossy with condensation. Chimneys and condenser towers are the tallest structures, guyed against wind and geological movement. Their smoke is usually thin, torn quickly into translucent threads, but near the foundries the air tastes of sulfur, hot oil, and cold metal.

Industry provides most of the sound. Compressors labor with slow, heavy strokes that travel through rock farther than voices travel through the thin air. Cable sheaves scream at unattended stations. Ore stamps beat inside stone sheds. At irregular intervals, some deep fracture closes with a boom that sets every loose window in a district chattering. The open spaces between installations are wide enough that these noises become detached from their sources: Vael often sounds as though an enormous factory is operating just beyond sight.

Water is present almost everywhere, but seldom in abundance. Rain arrives in brief, cold showers, sometimes weeks apart, drumming astonishingly on metal roofs and then vanishing into fissures. Condensation is more dependable. Cloud occasionally climbs the lower cliffs and passes through the city as a wet, slow-moving dimness; for hours afterward, pipes sweat, railings drip, and beads gather on the condenser fins. The fins feed covered cisterns. Small, still pools occupy newly formed hollows in the rock, their surfaces black and unrippled. There is no river at the summit. Nothing has flowed in one direction long enough to make one.

Vegetation follows water and leaked heat. Where a condensate main crosses a sheltered wall, the stone disappears under dark moss, ferns, and rubbery, broad-leaved creepers. Old industrial courts contain dense thickets rooted in decades of windblown dust and animal droppings. Warm exhaust ducts support hanging gardens several stories high. The growth is luxuriant but not soft: leaves are thick, stems wiry, roots capable of entering a hairline fracture and widening it over a century. Even in the greenest wards, the ground is more often damp than muddy. Water remains something held, rationed, and carefully guided.

Between these green veins lie long reaches of bare geology. From a high gantry, the vegetation resembles dark verdigris spreading through the joints of an unfinished machine.

Animals have occupied all of it. Pale salamanders live in cisterns and rock pools, feeding on insect larvae and the organic film that accumulates around pipe intakes. Translucent moths gather in generator houses, sometimes so densely that their wings make a dry secondary whisper beneath the engines. Small split-hooved grazers move along cliffs and iron stairways with equal assurance, cropping the fern curtains and leaving narrow trails across ledges that appear impassable. Larger, wide-winged gliders roost on warm condenser arrays. At shift changes they lift off in hundreds, circling on industrial updrafts before spreading out over the cloud gulfs.

Their abundance is not always visible at once. It reveals itself through occupancy: every sheltered ledge lined with old nests, every patch of vegetation tracked and cropped, every quiet cistern containing a movement below the surface. At dusk the retaining walls click with climbing feet. Rookeries erupt in harsh, bell-like calls. Predators no larger than cats watch from cable anchors, their eyes briefly catching the maintenance lamps. The human population is sparse enough that, in many districts, animals make up most of the visible traffic.

The mountain’s other instability becomes more apparent toward the edges of the city.

The youngest stone generally holds still long enough to stand on. The relationships between pieces of stone are less reliable. A surveyed route may lengthen without either endpoint moving. Two signs on the same cableway can give different distances, one measuring wire and the other measuring travel. A stair in an outer quarry has acquired three additional landings since its construction, although its upper and lower doors remain where they were. Shadows occasionally persist after the objects casting them have been removed. These are rarely sudden events. They accumulate over seasons, like sediment or corrosion.

Beyond the maintained network, a traveler may see the same cliff repeated across a gulf, or find that a path returns to its beginning at a different elevation. Distances do not shimmer or whirl. They simply cease to be obligations.

Vael endures through repetition. Rails, pipelines, engine foundations, and survey chains provide continuous references between one place and another. District engines are kept in regular operation even when their power is not needed; experience has shown that streets near a long-running, rhythmic machine are less likely to drift out of sequence. No one knows whether the important thing is the vibration, the measurement, or the accumulated expectation of thousands of residents. Where several such systems overlap, space is considered municipal. Doors lead where their signs promise. A building casts one shadow. Cable length and walking distance usually agree.

Children learn the practical rules early: close the pressure door, never waste cistern water, and do not descend a staircase that has more landings than its building. Beyond the last anchor, travel with a chain and a companion. If an animal trail differs from the map, the trail is often the more recent document.

At the longest civic interval, when the cloud sea begins its gradual darkening and the industrial shifts change, the engine houses bring their great flywheels into a common rhythm. District after district answers across the empty kilometres. Ore stamps, pumps, and compressors merge into a single slow concussion felt through boot soles and window frames. The gliders settle onto the condensers. Grazers lift their heads from the gardens. For a short while, the scattered city seems to gather itself around the beat.

Surveyors say that during this interval every map in Vael is correct.

No one has ever managed to check them all.
