### The Cistern Ledge

High on the dry shoulder of an old mountain, a small town occupies a single sheltered ledge. A cliff rises behind it, and a low parapet follows the edge in front. From the upper gate, nearly the whole settlement can be taken in at once: a few dozen flat roofs, seven short lanes, several stairways, and a handful of open courts. Beyond the walls, the mountain resumes immediately—pale gravel, rounded outcrops, and long slopes of settled scree disappearing into other bare heights.

Nothing in the terrain looks freshly broken. The cliff’s fractures have been softened by weather, and its old runoff channels are shallow and half filled with compacted grit. Stones at the foot of the slope have sunk into place rather than gathering in loose, dangerous fans. Even the path approaching the town is less a road than a worn allowance in the mountainside, narrow enough that two people carrying loads would have to negotiate their passing.

The town is urban in its organization, though not in its population. Its lanes meet at deliberate angles. Retaining walls support public terraces; covered drains run beneath the pavement; there is a small market court with no market crowd, a common workshop, storage rooms, and a gatehouse whose upper chamber is used for keeping dry cord and timber. Buildings stand in separated groups, joined by paving and shared waterworks rather than pressed into a continuous mass. Empty stone surfaces occupy more space than possessions. A doorway may remain shut for days, and no one seems to find this remarkable.

Everything is made from materials close at hand. The walls are local gray stone, split with stone hammers and wooden wedges, then laid in carefully fitted courses with a little gritty mortar. Broad slabs form the roofs, resting on dark timber beams. Doors are pegged together; window screens are woven from fiber; the few pipes are fired clay. Tool marks remain visible on lintels and step edges. The masonry is exact without being uniform, and repairs from different periods can be distinguished by slight changes in color, though never in intention. No addition rises above the established roofline.

Outside the ledge, the land is dry. Within it, dampness has edges.

Moisture arrives as occasional light rain, as cloud briefly combing the upper lanes, and most dependably as condensation on cold roof slabs. Each roof slopes almost imperceptibly toward a carved groove. The grooves feed narrow channels, the channels feed downspouts of stone or clay, and these descend into covered cisterns. At dawn, the roofs may be dark and beaded while the lane below them remains dusty. By late morning, most exposed surfaces are pale again. Damp survives under stair treads, along the lower courses of north-facing walls, and in the cool mouths of storage jars.

There is very little open water. The principal public basin is no larger than a table, its surface black and still beneath a stone hood. A wooden dipper hangs beside it on a cord. Washing is done elsewhere, in shallow pans whose used water is poured onto the same few patches of ground. No gutter is privately owned, and no roof may be altered without considering every channel below it. The town’s plan is, in effect, a single slow plumbing system laid out in streets and rooms.

Plants occur only where the water has already decided to go. Pale lichen coins some of the exposed rock. A little dark moss occupies several joints near the lowest cistern, and wiry grass grows in two or three damp seams beside the overflow stones. Just beyond the gate, a low shrub grips a pocket of grit, its small leaves thickened against the dry air. There are no trees inside the walls, no climbing growth softening the masonry, and no general greening after rain. The sparse vegetation neither spreads nor quite disappears.

Animals are scarcely more present. Now and then a slate-colored cliff bird lands on the parapet, drinks from a hollow worn in the coping, and departs. Small insects sometimes appear above the basin in the evening. Otherwise there are no animal sounds to give the place a background: no herds on the slopes, no colonies in the roofs, no persistent birdsong.

The quiet is similarly substantial. Wind moves along the mountain, but the cliff and parapet turn much of it aside, leaving only a low pressure at the ears. Within the lanes, sounds arrive separately and can be identified: a drop falling into a cistern; the wooden knock of a shutter; slow footsteps on worn steps; once in a while, the measured tap of a mallet from the workshop. Voices carry clearly because they are seldom raised. There is no running water to make continuous noise.

The few keepers spend their time preventing small changes from becoming large ones. They brush grit from roof grooves, lift a blocked channel stone with a wooden lever, renew a length of cord, or cut a replacement slab to the profile of one that has finally failed. Their rules are conservative but practical: keep the cisterns covered, leave the overflow paths clear, build nothing outside the parapet, and never let rubble accumulate where water once ran. A repair is considered finished when it stops attracting attention.

Ordinary daylight makes all of this legible. The courts are bright without being warm, the gray stone holds crisp shadows, and the damp patches show as temporary darker grays rather than glistening spectacle. In the cool air, the town smells faintly of wet mineral, old timber, and dust. The smell strengthens after rain and then recedes, but never vanishes entirely from the shaded rooms.

It would be misleading to call the ledge lifeless or abandoned. Drops fall, people cross the courts, the bird returns, and weather passes over the roofs. Yet nothing seems to accumulate. Dust is cleared, worn stone is replaced, water is drawn and gathered again. Each day closes most of the small departures made by the previous one.

Consequently, the town looks neither new nor ruined. It looks completed—not frozen, but held in a long, practiced balance with the old mountain beneath it. From the gate, there is no sense of a place waiting to expand, recover, or be rediscovered. The dry heights surround it; the cool air passes through; drop by drop, the roofs gather enough water to continue. Continuance is its whole architecture.
