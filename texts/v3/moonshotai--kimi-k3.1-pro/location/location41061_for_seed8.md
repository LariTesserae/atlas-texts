The country is so low and so old that distance has more presence than height. Under a wide, brightly daylit sky, the ground extends in long, nearly level reaches, interrupted by swells so gentle that they are often recognized only by the change underfoot: sand giving way to gravel, gravel to damp clay, clay to a thin skin of water. There are no sharp ridges and no deeply cut valleys. Even the exposed stones are rounded, their corners lost, their surfaces flaking in broad, shallow scales. Erosion is no longer busy reshaping the land. It works at the surfaces, grain by grain, while the larger forms remain almost unchanged.

The plain is not truly flat. From one of its rare rises, the region separates into many kinds of ground. A rust-colored back of bedrock emerges from pale sand; beyond it lies a chain of dark, shallow pans; beyond those, a silvery seep catches the light along the foot of another imperceptible slope. Some of the hollows are small enough to step across. Others open into sheets of water whose far margins are marked only by scattered birds and a few isolated trees. Ancient rounded pebbles lie far from any present channel, evidence of drainages that have wandered, divided, and vanished without ever cutting deeply.

Water is frequent, but it rarely hurries. Rain collects on dense clay and iron-rich layers beneath the soil, spreading sideways before it can sink. Even apparently dry ground often becomes damp a short way down. Along certain outcrops, moisture emerges through fine fractures and keeps the stone dark long after the surrounding rock has dried. In the lowest places, pools persist through most of the year. After rain, nearly every depression fills: clear water a finger deep over quartz sand, tea-colored water among roots, warm brown water in wallows, and broader pools reflecting clouds with scarcely a wrinkle.

The basin holds water not because it is deep, but because the water has almost nowhere to go. Drainage lines are wide and uncertain. A pool may overflow into another for weeks without any visible current between them. Elsewhere, a thread of moving water can be detected only by the slow turning of a trapped leaf. In time, beyond the visible country, all this moisture must gather into a river. Here, it has not yet become one.

Warmth softens every edge of the air. After rain, the smell of hot stone mingles with wet clay, iron, and the faint decay of old pool margins. Humidity rests over the ground without always becoming mist, though at dawn a shallow vapor can lie above the warmest water. By full morning it is gone. The light remains ordinary and generous: pale on the sand, white where it strikes open pools, muted among the sparse shrubs. Cloud shadows cross the plain as broad changes of tone rather than darkness, and at midday the wettest places can be difficult to look at directly because of their reflected glare.

Vegetation is sparse enough that the ground remains visible between nearly all the plants. This is not barrenness. It is a consequence of thin, deeply leached soils, prolonged wetness in the hollows, and hard mineral layers close to the surface. On the rises, widely separated tufts of wiry grass grow among grit and stone. Low shrubs occupy slightly deeper soil, their small, thick leaves reducing water loss between rains. Trees are solitary or gathered in loose groups around particularly reliable seeps. They tend to be broad rather than tall, with crooked trunks, pale or darkly fissured bark, and roots spreading sideways where they cannot descend.

The deepest pans often have the least tall vegetation. Seeds that begin there may be drowned before they establish themselves. The richest plant growth therefore forms narrow, irregular bands between prolonged inundation and shallow dry soil: sedges with open space between their stems, low creeping plants on exposed mud, and small patches of reeds where shelter and water happen to coincide. Beneath the water, however, the apparent scarcity weakens. Films of algae coat stones. Fine-rooted plants spread through shallow sediment. Tiny floating leaves gather in sheltered corners. Much of the plain’s productivity is hidden at ankle depth.

Animals are distributed in the same uneven way. A stretch of dry rise may hold little more than ants under a stone, a lizard in the thin shade of a shrub, and the tracks of something that crossed during the night. Then a pool margin comes into view, and life is suddenly concentrated. Water beetles and larvae move below the surface; dragonflies patrol above it. Small fish survive in the longer-lasting hollows, growing wary as the water contracts. Frogs wait in mud, vegetation, or shallow water according to their kind. Turtles use the dark pools, while long-legged birds pace the margins, stopping with their heads tilted before striking.

Herons, storks, and smaller wading birds gather where fish and amphibians are most crowded, but they do not cover the whole wetland. There may be one busy group here, another far across open water, and long empty reaches between. Grazing animals move widely among the shrubs and seasonal growth, their tracks converging on water without forming permanent roads. Predators follow the same concentrations, often leaving little evidence beyond a disturbance in reeds, feathers on drying mud, or a sudden eruption of birds. Large reptiles can remain almost motionless in the warm permanent pools for hours, more like another dark material of the place than like presences within it.

Sound fills the spaces that bodies leave open. The plain is slow, but it is not quiet. In the heat of the day, insects call from scattered shrubs and patches of grass, their dry trills and continuous buzzing carrying farther than seems possible from such small sources. Around the pools there are splashes, wingbeats, the abrupt cries of birds disputing a feeding place, and the softer patter of feet in mud. Rain adds a broad hiss when it comes, striking water, stone, and leaves with distinctly different sounds.

Toward evening, the frogs begin. Their calls are not a single chorus but many overlapping ones: low pulses from the deeper pools, high repeated notes from flooded grass, rattles and grunts from ditches and seeps. A group may fall quiet while another, some distance away, grows louder. On still nights the sound travels across the open ground until the whole region seems inhabited, including stretches that would appear almost empty in daylight. Between choruses come brief openings in which a single bird call or the movement of water against a stem can be heard with unusual clarity.

For all this activity, the landscape itself changes patiently. Quick events—a fish striking, a flock rising, rain dimpling a pool—do not make it feel restless. Water levels creep upward after wet weather and recede over the following weeks. Mud margins dry, crack, and are softened again. Sedges advance a little into one hollow and abandon another. Animal gathering places shift as the pools shift, yet the broader pattern remains: crowded life near persistent water, quiet openness between.

The larger alterations take much longer. A drainage divide subsides until overflow begins to favor a different hollow. A pool gradually fills with fine sediment. A seep-line migrates around the edge of an outcrop. Stone sheds another weathered skin, and the rise becomes very slightly lower. Trees die where their roots have spent too many seasons underwater; others establish themselves on ground that has become marginally drier. There is no final stillness, only a pace so extended that change often looks like repetition.

The plain’s order follows from a few simple conditions. A little height makes dryness; a little depth makes a reservoir. Where water lasts, life gathers. Where life gathers, sound spreads through the warm air. Everything else is old stone, pale sediment, open light, and the space between one presence and the next. The land has not become lifeless with age. It has become settled—its hills nearly worn away, its waters in no hurry, and its scattered pools still loud with living things.
