## The Brine Hollow

On the low, heat-whitened plain, the hollow has almost no profile. There is no hill to conceal it and no vegetation to mark it. The ground simply ends in a narrow, broken lip. Beyond lies a pit perhaps sixty paces across and eight or nine deep, its floor sunk far enough below the surrounding country to hold its own air. From a distance, only a faint shimmer and a discoloration of the sky suggest anything there. At the edge, the heat strikes first, then the sound.

Ordinary daylight fills it. The sky remains pale blue, the shadows fall plainly beneath pipes and ledges, and nothing glows. The colors are chalk white, wet slate, iron brown, and a clouded bottle-green where water gathers deepest. Steam rises in narrow ribbons rather than obscuring fog. Even so, the air is saturated. Moisture beads on shaded stone, on cable insulation, on the undersides of metal stairs. In the lower hollow nothing ever dries completely.

The pit is young. A bore reached a pressurized mineral seam, and the ground around the headworks subsequently fractured and dropped. The pump-house survived on a narrow rib that remained at the original grade; nearly everything else fell. The walls still show the brightness of freshly broken stone. Ledges end in thin, unsupported flakes. Cracks are sharp enough to catch cloth, and angular blocks lie where they landed, without rounded corners or aprons of weathered debris. Water has begun cutting runnels down them, but these are only hairline incisions. There is almost no soil and very little loose sediment.

Water occupies the floor without covering it. A brine pool lies against one wall, connected by a shallow, steaming channel to a relief outlet. Elsewhere are wet mineral pans, isolated seep mouths, and broad sheets of stone darkened by films only a few molecules thick. The entering brine is hot enough to scald; even the farthest seep remains warm. The air smells of wet chalk, hot metal, salt, and a little sulfur, but not of swamp decay.

A skin of nearly fresh condensate floats over parts of the dense brine. It comes from the roof, from an air-cooled return pipe, and from the air itself wherever expansion at a valve causes a slight cooling. The boundary between the two waters is visible as a wavering difference in refraction. When the pump changes load, that boundary tilts almost imperceptibly. It can take hours to become level again.

The pump-house is the only complete building: a low shed of corrugated iron bolted to a concrete raft, its seams oxidized, its roof white with mineral splash. Inside, an electric motor drives three heavy plungers. Most of what they draw leaves the hollow through an insulated main and disappears over the rim toward an unseen works. Only the relief water, leakage, and condensate remain to make the landscape.

The rest of the human presence is widely scattered. A valve wheel stands alone on a concrete plinth. Two ceramic insulators have fallen beside a cable post. A short length of narrow rail enters the brine and ends beneath a white crust. An enamel number is wired to a pipe support, though the pipe it identifies has been cut back. There are long intervals of bare ground between these things, and no road down to them.

At the floor, the machinery is overwhelmingly loud. Each plunger stroke arrives as a concussion felt in the chest. Water hammer cracks inside the pipes; the relief outlet produces a continuous, close roar; a pressure valve opens at intervals with a tearing hiss. The iron roof answers with ticks and occasional deep booming notes. Fresh walls return the sounds slightly late, giving every stroke a smaller twin. Near the shed, speech is impossible. Yet the plain absorbs the noise quickly. A short walk beyond the lip reduces it to a hum, and then to something easily mistaken for wind.

There are no living sounds beneath it. No insects hover over the water, no birds cross the opening to investigate, and no animals have left tracks in the wet margins. There are no larvae in the quiet pools, no burrows, nests, shells, or scavenged remains. The heat, the metallic salts, and the hollow’s recent isolation have kept animal life out entirely.

Vegetation has gained only a slight foothold. Fewer than a dozen tussocks of wire sedge grow along the far side, each separated from the next by several paces of bare crust. Their leaves are narrow and gray-green, with transparent, salt-burned tips. They root not beside the brine itself but in the small freshwater haloes beneath dependable drips. A single mat of fern, no broader than a spread hand, survives in the shade of a leaking flange. The distribution of the plants maps the plumbing more accurately than it maps the pool.

The orange, olive, and green bands around the water are not meadows or algae mats, but oxides and other precipitates. From the rim they give the hollow a deceptively fertile appearance. Up close, most of its surface remains mineral and empty.

The two kinds of water perform opposite work. The weakly acidic condensate dissolves stone, patiently deepening its tiny runnels. The cooling brine builds stone instead, laying down silica and pale carbonate over anything it touches. Pipe threads acquire white cuffs. Fallen sedge stems stiffen beneath translucent coatings. An object left in the strongest seep may become mineral-skinned before it has time to collapse or rot. Thus the raw walls look violently new while small artifacts around them appear half fossilized. The hollow is being excavated and sealed at the same time, though both processes are almost too slow to watch.

Despite its noise, nothing here is urgent. The pool changes depth by a finger’s width across the seasons. The sedge spreads by short increments and often loses as much ground as it gains. Mineral layers accumulate around the outlet. Once in a long while, a plate of wall gives way and adds another sharp block to the floor. Otherwise the machinery’s furious repetition maintains nearly constant conditions.

Given enough centuries, the brine might close its own passages and turn the hollow into a low blister of wet stone. For now, the pump postpones that ending. It is a small piece of industrial plumbing that has accidentally made a climate, and a climate that has begun to make terrain: hidden, deafening, nearly empty, and changing with geological patience.
