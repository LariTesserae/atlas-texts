## Sinter Basin

Sinter lies in a depression so broad and shallow that the whole region seems to have been poured rather than folded. From the low rim, the city is visible all at once: thousands of pale roofs packed into the basin floor, crossed by black channels, spring pools, quarry cuts, and causeways. Beyond the last streets are outlying settling ponds and rookery flats, then smaller clusters of buildings around isolated vents. Steam softens the farthest districts, but it does not hide the extent of the place. This is not a town with a landscape around it. The settlement, the waterworks, and the raw mineral ground form one continuous structure.

It is wet without being flooded. Most open water is shallow, moving slowly through stone-lined runs or standing in warm, rimmed pools. The deeper cisterns are covered. Elsewhere, moisture seeps from joints, condenses on lintels, and gathers in the hollows of steps. Walls are dark at their bases; the soles of feet never quite dry. Rain is less important than the upward water: mineral springs emerging through fractures, warm ground exhaling into the air, drains returning a thin seepage to the surface.

Almost nothing has been worn smooth. The basin’s dark foundation rock breaks in sharp, unequivocal edges. Over it, the springs have laid younger stone in thin skins—cream, gray, and rust—which thicken into shelves and crusts. There are no rounded river cobbles, no deep banks of soil, no mature gullies. Water has done less carving here than laying-down. A puddle may have an edge as crisp as a broken plate. A newly exposed channel looks less excavated than hatched.

The city is built from this unfinished geology. Fresh mineral stone is soft enough to be cut with harder stone, sand, wedges, and patience. It is lifted in blocks and used almost immediately. Buildings have narrow rooms and thick walls because there is no timber for beams; roofs are corbelled inward, course by course, or closed with short slabs. Streets repeat that narrowness. Bridges are blunt stone spans. Drains, stairs, cistern mouths, and nesting ledges are often parts of the same wall.

There are no wooden doors, woven screens, rope balconies, or fiber ropes. Hide curtains close openings. Sinew binds basalt points to stone handles. Vessels are hollowed rock, shell, or skin. Cooking places cluster around managed hot vents, where covered stone chambers hold a steady heat. Every district contains yards of blocks, half-cut slabs, and wall faces being replaced, yet none of this activity gives the city a skyline of cranes or scaffolds. Its construction is close to the ground, manual, repetitive, and widely distributed.

And there is no vegetation at all. No reeds at the pool margins, no grass in the quarry floors, no moss softening the drains. Even the water does not green. The absence is more striking than bareness ordinarily is, because everything else invites growth: warmth, moisture, shelter, nutrients. But the wet stone remains mineral-colored, and the city’s crowded surfaces are occupied by animals rather than plants.

The base of that animal abundance is a thin microbial film in the spring water. It is gray, cream, or iron-red, sometimes silky, sometimes grainy, and faintly sulfuric in smell. In the hottest pools it has the water nearly to itself. Where the flow cools and spreads, larvae and small crustaceans graze it. Fish gather in the aerated channels. Frogs pack the cisterns and seep-warmed basins. Insects rise from the shallow ponds in dense, visible columns, feeding nesting birds on the roofs and bats in the vaults. Wading birds stand shoulder to shoulder on the outer flats, their droppings whitening whole islands of raw stone.

The human inhabitants do not separate the city from this food web. A cistern is also a fishery; a quarry pond is also a nursery; a roof parapet is also nesting infrastructure. Waste goes to settling pools rather than open streets, where it enters the same dense cycle of film, larvae, fish, and birds. Some ledges are deliberately left rough. Some walls are pierced high up with small, inaccessible holes. There are no gardens or fields to interrupt the urban fabric, but almost every structure produces food for something.

The sound arrives before any of this can be understood.

From the rim, it resembles a large waterfall heard through a wall, although there is no waterfall. Nearer the roofs, it separates into layers: the low knocking and bellowing of frogs, the abrasive cries of packed birds, insect whine, wingbeats, the clatter of animals landing on stone, the small screams and chatter coming from cistern mouths and roof voids. In the dense streets, these sounds stop being distinguishable. They become pressure. The roar is felt in the sternum and behind the teeth. Water in an open bowl carries a fine, continuous tremor.

Human speech is useful only at close range, and not always then. People conduct much of their public business with a compact language of hands, shoulders, and touch. Messages meant to last are scratched into stone. Builders’ mallets add their sharp taps, but a few steps away the animals swallow them. Sleeping chambers are valuable according to how many walls stand between them and the nearest rookery. Silence, not dryness, is the city’s rarest material.

The inhabitants also listen for gaps in the noise. A quiet cistern may mean a blocked inlet. A diminished sector of the frog chorus can reveal a failed drain before water appears where it should not. A sudden burst of bird cries sends people looking toward a particular roof. The total roar is too great to comprehend, but deviations within it are infrastructure made audible.

Above everything, the light remains dim and diffuse. Low cloud and steam reduce the open sky to a tarnished gray, bright enough to catch the wet edges of roofs but too weak for hard shadows. Distances become arrangements of silhouettes: a causeway, a quarry wall, bands of birds, the pale backs of buildings. The air is warm against the skin, and warmth also rises through the ground in places. The basin smells of wet mineral dust, sulfur, hot feathers, and the sharp ammonia of crowded nesting sites.

There is no useful season here. No shared night empties the roofs or silences the pools. Eggs, young animals, adults, and carcasses are always present in much the same proportions. Water levels hold. The cloud does not clear into a different kind of weather. The chorus has no dawn ascent or winter decline.

Nor does the city grow. Spring water lays down new stone; quarrying removes it. Use rounds a step; the step is replaced. A wall is refaced along its former line. Individual animals die, individual rooms are rebuilt, and individual channels are cleared, but the accumulated form of Sinter remains fixed. Its plan is so thoroughly maintained that it has become almost indistinguishable from topography.

This is why the place can appear both ancient and embryonic. Everywhere, something looks as though it has only just begun: a mineral shelf with an unweathered lip, a foundation exposed beside a half-faced wall, a causeway ending in newly cut blocks, raw pools crowded with life but bearing no sign of succession. Yet these are not stages on the way to another landscape. They are stable conditions.

Sinter is not frozen. Hardly anything in it is still. It is simply motion without accumulation, construction without enlargement, renewal without aging. It looks like the beginning of a world—new stone, near-surface water, first walls, first crowds of animals—but that beginning does not lead elsewhere. Its unfinishedness is its finished form: warm, wet, densely inhabited, and roaring at a volume that never becomes an event.
