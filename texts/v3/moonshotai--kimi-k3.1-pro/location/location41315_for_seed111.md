## The Rookery of Held Sound

At the top of a range still being forced out of the earth, there is a shelf of black rock no larger than a cathedral and its forecourt. It is not quite the highest point of the mountain. A blade of stone rises above it, split down the middle and too narrow even for the animals to occupy. But the shelf is the highest place where anything gathers.

On three sides, it ends without warning. There are no descending ridges, no aprons of rubble, no weathered shoulders—only fresh fractures and a drop into air. Clouds pass far below it, occasionally revealing, through some momentary thinning, the dim wrinkles of valleys that seem less distant than unrelated. The fourth side is bounded by the blade and by a cleft so narrow that darkness within it appears solid.

The elevation is felt before it can be understood. The air is thin, cool, and almost weightless. Sounds do not travel far through it, yet the shelf itself is loud. Smells remain close to their sources: wet iron against the rock, ammonia and musk among the animals, a faint saltiness at the cleft. The darkness is old and habitual. At its lightest, the sky becomes the color of wet iron; at other times there are a few weak stars, or none. Wet surfaces can be distinguished from dry ones. Color mostly cannot.

The mountain is too young to have softened. Its stone breaks in shell-shaped flakes with edges that can part a wing membrane. Needles stand where needles fell; chips lie where the last fracture threw them. Water has not yet arranged them into beds or fitted them together into scree. There is no proper soil, only black dust in the deepest seams and a little grease of organic matter beneath overhanging lips. Even the cleft looks recent, its opposing walls still bearing complementary knobs and hollows, as though they might be fitted back together.

In the larger sense, the shelf is dry. It has no spring, no stream, no tarn. Yet almost nothing on it is entirely dry.

Every few years, a cloud rises far enough to touch the summit. It may remain for days or weeks, neither fog nor rain exactly, but a cold suspension that beads on stone and feather and claw. Rare rain arrives as a brief, hard stippling and vanishes almost at once. Between these visits, the rookery moistens itself. The breath of tens of thousands of animals condenses beneath their folded wings and on the undersides of ledges. Drops merge, tremble, and creep into hollows. Some of the hollows hold water no deeper than a fingerprint; three are as wide as a mouth. Their water is dark and brackish, salty enough to remain liquid when fresher moisture has become rime.

Nearly all the water here has passed through a living body at least once.

Vegetation has found only the narrowest terms on which to persist. A lead-white lichen occupies certain fractures, spreading so slowly that its edges appear to have been drawn with a fine instrument. A looser, rust-colored crust grows where animal waste has accumulated. In one sheltered recess there are several dark threads resembling grass until they are touched: they are hollow, brittle, and usually dormant. Nothing is tall enough to cast a meaningful shadow. The plants do not form the foundation of the multitude. They are merely among its beneficiaries.

The multitude is the shelf.

What first appears to be a second, flakier surface of rock is a covering of **bellwings**. Each is about the size of two cupped hands, with six hooked limbs, a low armored body, and wings folded so tightly around it that only their outer surfaces show. Those surfaces are black and faintly lustrous. The inner membranes, exposed only during molting or departure, are clear enough to catch starlight.

They cling to every horizontal seam and to most of the vertical ones. Where the rock offers insufficient purchase, they cling to one another. In places they lie three and four deep, the lower animals motionless beneath the patient weight of the upper. Their bodies follow the fractures without arranging them. A colony seems to flow into a crack and stop; another shingles the face of the blade; another hangs beneath the shelf, out of sight, its wings sometimes stirring in the updraft.

The mass breathes unevenly. A shoulder of rock rises slightly and settles. A whole dark bank contracts with a dry whisper. Pale feelers emerge between wing cases, test the air, and withdraw.

Beneath and among the bellwings lives a second fauna, less visible but hardly less numerous: blind jointed scavengers in the cast skins; translucent mites orbiting the warm openings of the breathing tubes; slow, flat creatures that graze lichen and old blood from the stone; hair-fine worms in the wet dust. The air immediately above the rookery contains drifting larvae, most no larger than dust grains, each suspended beneath a hollow, nearly weightless husk. At the cliff edge, larger winged things occasionally arrive out of the darkness, fasten themselves to an apparently impossible perch, and become still enough to disappear.

The shelf can be heard from the blade before its individual animals can be distinguished.

One bellwing makes very little noise: a tick as a hook changes its grip, a papery rubbing of wing against wing, a fluted exhalation through the chambers of its thorax. Together they produce a broad, insistent sound like heavy rain heard through stone, although no rain is falling. Beneath it runs a lower pumping note, almost too slow to be called a rhythm. Sometimes a section of the colony falls quiet. The silence spreads, not instantly but body by body, until half the shelf is still. Then an answer comes from another wall, and the whole chorus resumes at a slightly different pitch.

The thin air carries the higher sounds poorly. The rock carries them very well. Much of the rookery’s loudness arrives through contact: through the soles, the teeth, the small bones of the ears. The water in the three deeper hollows is continually dimpled, though there may be no wind at all.

Nothing so crowded could feed on the summit’s lichens. The bellwings do not try. They arrive already heavy with fat gathered somewhere far below, and come here to undergo their last, immensely slow transformation. For years they cling and fast. Their outer bodies darken; their wing cases thicken; the clear flight membranes develop within. The breeding chorus belongs mostly to animals that will never feed again.

When a bellwing dies before completing its molt, the scavengers find it. When it completes the molt successfully, it leaves behind a husk rich in salts and old tissue, and the scavengers find that too. Nutrients pass downward through layers of bodies and outward through mites, larvae, grazers, and predators. Almost everything eaten on the shelf was originally carried up inside an animal. The rookery is less a self-supporting ecosystem than a place where many lives arrive to be unpacked.

Not everything here acts by instinct alone.

A few dozen larger animals move across the crowded stone. They are six-limbed and ponderous, with bare gray grasping members and mantles of insulating filament. They eat stalled molts, crack the ice from water hollows, and occasionally groom parasites from living bellwings. One may spend a long while selecting a stone, rejecting several by touch before choosing another. With it, the animal can lever open a fused wing case or strike a narrow fault until ice breaks free.

The stone is never shaped. It is not kept in a cache, carried from place to place, or fitted to a handle. After use, it is dropped. This is the whole of their technology: selection, leverage, and the patience to try again.

Nothing else is made. There are no nests, no stacked stones, no worn paths, no sheltered constructions. The bellwings’ apparent vaults and hanging curtains are only crowding. The larger animals sleep in natural recesses or among the warm bodies of the colony. Even the thin polished patches beneath habitual perches have taken centuries to form.

The strangest property of the shelf belongs to its young stone.

The rock does not immediately release all the vibration it receives. Some part of a sound remains in the fresh fractures and emerges later, when cooling, pressure, or another tiny movement of the mountain disturbs it. The return is never exact. It is usually lower, drier, and mixed with other sounds. A rain shower may come back years afterward as a brief ticking under an empty ledge. The report of a splitting rock may emerge as a groan that takes several minutes to finish.

Sometimes an entire chorus rises from the blade when no animals are singing there.

The bellwings answer these returns. Living animals turn toward a wall and join voices preserved by earlier generations, producing an antiphon in which only one side is presently alive. The larger animals react more cautiously. They stop, touch the stone, and wait. Perhaps they can distinguish a new fracture from an old voice; perhaps they are simply listening for whatever follows.

Thus the shelf’s silences are not empty. They are intervals of uncertain length.

Visible change is exceptionally slow. A bellwing may occupy one seam for most of its adult life. The final molt takes years, and the departure of a successful generation is separated from the next by long periods in which the colony seems merely to breathe and accumulate dust. The water hollows deepen almost imperceptibly. Animal residue darkens the sheltered cracks. In some distant future, there may be enough soil for a mat of vegetation; for now, the suggestion of soil is itself new.

The mountain also changes, though chiefly by addition: another hairline fracture, another narrow exposure of glassy stone, another surface capable of keeping sound.

Once in a great while, the cleft opens a little farther. The note it makes is below anything the bellwings can produce. Their chorus stops. For several breaths the only sounds are water dropping from wing tips and the small, startled movements of scavengers.

Then the clear-winged animals open.

In the weak starlight, thousands of inner membranes appear at once. The black shelf seems briefly to acquire depth, translucence, even a kind of delicacy. Some bellwings release their hooks and drift out over the drop. Others settle back, not yet ready. The larger animals stand motionless with their chosen stones in hand.

The wings fold. Dew begins forming again. To any later gaze, the summit is the same crowded splinter in the darkness.

But somewhere within the new fracture, the whole event has been retained—the mountain’s low note, the opening wings, the rainlike chorus that followed. Years or centuries from now, part of it will return from beneath the feet of animals not yet arrived, and the rookery will answer itself again.
