## The Breath-Cleft

Near the summit of an immense mountain, where the ridge has weathered down to a blade of dark andesite, there is a single opening in the rock. It is easy to miss: a sideways mouth beneath a blunt overhang, no wider than a doorway and partly screened by frozen gravel. Beyond it lies one chamber, perhaps thirty paces deep, narrowing at the back to a seam through which no animal larger than a hand could pass. There are no further passages. The whole place can be crossed in less than a minute, though nothing about it encourages crossing.

Outside, the altitude governs everything. The air is thin and dry; exposed stone splits under frost; sound seems to arrive without weight. Snow lies in old, hard veins, but there is little loose water and almost nothing alive in sight. The cleft interrupts all of this within the distance of a single step.

The rock inside is warm.

Not hot, and not volcanic in any spectacular sense. There is no glow, no boiling mud, no sudden jet of steam. A deep fracture simply leaks enough heat to keep the rear wall a little above blood temperature, as it has done through longer cycles than the present arrangement of snowfields. The warmth has a mineral smell, faintly metallic, mixed with ammonia, musk, and the dry sweetness of discarded insect wings. Breathing is uncomfortable in a peculiar way: the air is mild and humid, but still summit-thin, so warmth does not bring the expected ease.

Moisture gathers where the warm exhalation meets colder stone. Most of it exists as a film rather than visible water. The middle walls feel greasy; grit adheres to fingertips; fine beads collect beneath stone flanges and fall at long, irregular intervals. Occasionally, when cloud is driven against the summit, rain enters horizontally through the mouth. It follows channels so old that their edges have become rounded, and collects in three shallow basins, none broader than a shield. Between rains, the basins shrink to dark saucers and then to damp mineral rings. There is never a stream.

The cleft itself looks finished with violence. Its walls are scalloped but not sharp, its ceiling worn into long, blunt flutes. Older waterlines sit above the present pools, evidence of a wetter era, and even those have been softened by mineral crust. The floor consists of rounded granules, fine black dust, compacted droppings, and delicate layers of chitin. Nothing appears freshly fractured. Erosion continues, but without urgency: a grain loosens, another settles, a groove deepens by almost nothing. The chamber has the composure of a form that happened long ago and has since been slowly learning to remain itself.

Almost no daylight reaches beyond the first bend. At noon the entrance is a gray-blue incision; by the rear wall, seeing gives way to touch and sound. At night the darkness is nearly complete. There is no convenient phosphorescence, no illuminated fungus, no kindly glow in the water. Only near the mouth does moisture occasionally return a little starlight.

And the rear wall, when touched, moves.

It is covered in small ash-colored moths, their wings overlapping so closely that the stone appears furred. Their bodies form a warm, faintly trembling fabric several layers deep in the favored places. Wingless crickets occupy the spaces beneath them, pallid and long-legged, feeding on fallen wings and grazing the darker films in damp recesses. Thread-fine mites move through the floor dust. Translucent larvae occupy the wet seams. Flat amphibians lie beneath the grit around the basins, their skins so mineral-stained that they resemble wet pieces of the chamber until they inflate their throats.

Above them, the ceiling is crowded with small folded bats. They roost belly to back and wing to wing, leaving almost no visible rock. The air immediately below them is never quite still: moths rising, crickets flicking themselves away from moisture, bats turning in their sleep, minute winged things drifting through the warm updraft. Near the pools, narrow lizards wait with their chins against the stone. Every surface has an occupant, and most occupants have something smaller crawling over them.

The foundation of this abundance is not vegetation. Green life is astonishingly scarce. A few gray lichens occupy the outer lip; several rust-colored rosettes cling where rain occasionally strikes; one low cushion plant survives beside the outermost basin, its leaves thick and no larger than grains of rice. That is nearly all. From even a short distance away, the mountain appears sterile.

Within the cleft, pale microbial films use the minerals carried by the warm seep. Larvae and springtails graze the films. Crickets consume what falls. The moths complete their brief adult lives in the warmth, and their bodies feed another layer of the crowd. The bats hunt both within the chamber and far down the mountain, returning with nutrients in their bodies and droppings. Predators arrive from outside on rare favorable winds. Every animal either follows the seep, eats something that follows it, or comes to eat the followers.

Consequently, the place is loud.

Not with one commanding roar, but with so many small sounds pressed together that they acquire the force of one. Cricket rasps run in continuous bands through the dark. Moth wings make a dry, close hiss, like rain heard through cloth. The bats click, squabble, and strike the ceiling with brief leathery thumps. Around the pools, the amphibians produce single hard notes that seem much too large for their bodies. Each call starts others, until the whole chamber is ringing in overlapping pulses.

The old stone shape gathers these sounds and returns them without clear echoes. It becomes difficult to tell whether a noise is beside the ear or at the rear wall. A falling drop lands with startling precision, and for an instant some of the insects stop; then the general sound closes over the gap. Even the wind participates. At the mouth it is drawn into a low note that changes slowly with the pressure outside, sometimes scarcely audible beneath the animals, sometimes rising until the rock itself seems to be humming.

Nothing has been built to encourage or contain any of this. There are no walls, platforms, cut channels, shelters, or arranged stones. The nearest approach to technology belongs to a few heavy-billed mountain birds that visit the entrance to feed. Some use unshaped pebbles as anvils against which they split the harder insect cases. When finished, they drop the stones. Nothing is flaked, cached, or improved, and after a short time a used pebble is indistinguishable from the rest of the floor.

The frenzy of wings is also misleading. Individual animals hurry, but the place itself changes slowly. The seep warms one section of wall for decades, then another; the densest roosts drift after it almost imperceptibly. The pools broaden in wet years and contract through long dry ones. Amphibians wait under the grit. Lichens advance by distances too small to measure from one visit to the next. For weeks the mountain may exhale through the cleft; for weeks it may draw cold air inward, drying the middle walls and pressing the animals closer to the rear. These reversals have no single dramatic moment. A film of moisture disappears. A band of calls shifts. The odor changes slightly.

Outside, a body’s length away, the summit remains cold, dry, sparsely inhabited, and nearly silent. Inside, warmth and darkness hold an impossible concentration of life against the old stone. The cleft is not an oasis in the usual sense: there is too little water, too little green, too little air. It is more like a small, crowded organ enclosed by the mountain—damp at its edges, loud in its depths, and sustained by a breath so slow that the rock seems motionless.
