## The Underbloom

The Underbloom lies below a pale, level crust, in a valley-shaped absence that the rock never finished filling. It is not quite a cavern and not quite a system of sinkholes. Three broad depressions connect through saddles, fractures, and lower galleries, together covering the area of a small valley. Above the central depression, the crust ends in a long open seam. Beyond the seam is a white sky with no discernible sun.

At a regional scale, the place is open. From its upper lip, the far wall can be seen across several distinct basins, and side valleys continue beyond intervening ribs of stone. At the scale of a body, it is almost closed. Every passage is narrowed by stems, every opening is layered with leaves, and even the larger chambers feel furnished to excess by growth.

The exposed geology is startlingly raw. Bone-white stone breaks in planar faces; honey-colored mineral bands alternate with thin seams of smoke-dark glass. Cliff edges remain sharp enough to catch light along their entire length. There are no fans of scree beneath them, no rounded blocks, no beds of silt, no gravel sorted by water. Some terraces end in blunt, unfinished swellings. Elsewhere, mineral ribs rise toward arches that were never completed. The terrain does not look ruined or incomplete through damage. It looks as though erosion has not yet been invented.

The vegetation has nevertheless become nearly everything else.

There is no ordinary soil. Underfoot is a continuous mat of roots and rhizomes, thick enough to flex without breaking, its surface cross-grained in olive, umber, and pale gray. In dry places it feels like dense, warm felt; in damp places a foot sinks slightly into wet fiber. Above it, small scale-leaves overlap in close shingles. Ribbon-shaped plants rise through those, then braided stalks, then columns formed from many stems pressed and fused together. Some columns divide overhead and join neighboring columns, producing living vaults without a clear distinction between trunk, root, arch, and wall.

It is often impossible to tell where one plant ends. A descending root may enter the mat and emerge thirty paces away as several unrelated-looking crowns. Leaves of different shapes share a stem. The walls are upholstered with fine green growth, while the dark interior of the root mat is threaded with scarlet and ivory vessels. Nearly every surface is alive, and nearly every ecological function is performed by plant tissue: support, storage, filtration, feeding, and the recovery of whatever small amount of waste the system produces. There is no leaf litter and almost no smell of decay.

The light should not reach as far as it does, but it does. It enters through the roof seam as an intense, steady whiteness, strikes the fresh mineral faces, and passes from surface to surface. Many leaves are translucent, so the canopy does not merely block the light; it distributes it. Their upper surfaces flash pale green, while their undersides glow more softly. Shadows are thin, green-edged, and stationary. Even in the side galleries there is enough light to distinguish the hairline veins beneath a leaf. In the central basin, the brightest whites approach featurelessness.

The heat is constant and enveloping. Stone, stems, air, and standing water are all close to the same oppressive warmth. There is no cool lower pool and no refreshing shade, only slight variations in how directly the heat seems to press against the skin. Yet the Underbloom is not steamy. The air remains clear, and long stretches of vegetation are dry to the touch. Its dampness is local rather than general: condensation beneath a waxy leaf, a wet seam where roots lie against glassy stone, moisture held deep in a root cavity.

For so much living mass, there is very little free water. No stream connects the basins. A few still lenses occupy hollows in the lowest depression, the largest no broader than a small room. Their bottoms are not mud but tightly woven living root. Smaller quantities of water occur in cupped leaves, narrow root gutters, and sealed pockets within the plant mat. Beads gather at particular leaf margins, grow too heavy, and fall. Much of the moisture is drawn back into the same closed tissues from which it came. The dense greenery is not evidence of abundant water; it is an extraordinarily efficient way of keeping a small amount in circulation.

Each basin has developed a different architecture from the same continuous vegetation. The central one is vertical: fused columns, high translucent leaves, and narrow interior chambers whose floors remain visible through curtains of fine roots. The second is broader and lower, a corrugated expanse of root pavement and chest-high foliage interrupted by the still water lenses. The third is crowded around raw mineral ribs. There, stems have sheathed the stone without softening its angles, and passage requires repeated turning, ducking, and stepping over living thresholds. From above, these read as separate landscapes. Below, they remain parts of one congested body.

Scattered through them are a few things that look made.

At the edge of one water lens, three dark steps descend and stop at the waterline. In a side gallery stands a roofless enclosure barely larger than a hut, with one doorway and no adjoining wall. Elsewhere, a straight stone sill runs for two meters through an otherwise irregular root arch. Another chamber contains a shallow rectangular basin, empty, its interior smoother than any natural surface nearby. These things never gather into a settlement. There is no road between them, no repeated plan, no concentration of fragments.

Nor is there anything resembling equipment: no metal, fasteners, mechanisms, vessels with handles, or legible tool marks. Their edges are as unworn as the cliffs. The roots approach them closely but do not cross their boundaries, leaving their outlines inexplicably clean. They might be remnants of construction, but they could just as easily be its first sketches—steps before a stair, a room before a building, a basin before anyone has decided what it should hold.

Animals are rare enough to seem incidental. A water lens may contain a single transparent creature, finger-length and almost invisible until it flexes. Occasionally a small, pale arthropod can be found flattened beneath a leaf, motionless except for the slow adjustment of one limb. There are no visible swarms, no grazing trails, no calls from the canopy. The plants do not appear to depend on pollination or dispersal by animals, and nothing gives the impression of a busy hidden food web.

Sound is correspondingly sparse. There is no wind to move the stiff leaves. The root mat absorbs footsteps, and the denser chambers swallow voices close to their source. Most of the time, the dominant sound is one’s own breathing and clothing. At long intervals a drop strikes water with a small, distinct note. In a mineral gallery, that note may return once as a soft echo. More rarely comes a tiny click from inside a stem as pressure shifts within some sealed vessel. Then the padded quiet resumes.

The stillness goes deeper than the absence of noise. The opening above never changes brightness. Shadows do not travel. The water lenses neither rise nor fall. Buds are everywhere—pale, tightly folded, seemingly on the point of opening—but none opens. No cliff sheds a grain, no gallery narrows further, and no unfinished arch advances toward completion. There are no seasons, no succession of growth forms, no accumulation of dust or debris. Even the rare movements that do occur leave no history behind. A ripple subsides to the same unbroken plane. Compressed roots recover their former height. Repeated passage wears no path.

The Underbloom is therefore neither petrified nor asleep. Its tissues are supple, its sap is wet, its animals can move, and its heat is plainly the heat of living matter. But life here does not develop. It persists in a closed, exquisitely stable state, with growth and decay balanced so completely that neither becomes visible.

Nothing in the place can be used to establish age. The roofless enclosure may be older than the columns around it, or newer than the nearest bud. The glassy fracture may have appeared before the first root, or after the forest was already complete. Every feature has the same freshness, whether mineral, vegetable, or apparently built.

It is not waiting, exactly. Waiting would imply a future. It simply remains at the threshold: stone before weather, forest before succession, water before a river, rooms before use. An immense landscape held in the condition of beginning, crowded with life, flooded with light, and never quite becoming anything else.
