## The Upper Sea

Above the cloud line, where the air is thin and cold, there is an ocean.

Its surface lies at the elevation of high mountains, under a pale sky lit by permanent late-morning sun. The light is ordinary and plentiful: white on open water, green among the floating leaves, soft silver on the flanks of animals below. Nothing about the illumination suggests the depths of a conventional sea. Even far beneath the surface, shapes remain visible and colors survive.

The ocean rests on vegetation. No stone basin is visible anywhere. Beneath the water, continent-scale masses of root, trunk, and flexible rind have grown together into a elevated living plateau. At its remote margins, the vegetation curves upward into rounded lips and ridges; beyond them, cloud lies far below. There are no beaches and almost no emergent surfaces. Passing from air into the Upper Sea means passing through a scatter of broad, wet leaves and then directly into cold water.

The water remains only a few degrees above freezing. It never forms ice, never develops a warm surface layer, and never carries sediment. Its clarity gives the place an unsettling combination of intimacy and immense distance: a single dark stem can be seen across water that would take days to cross, while behind it the terrain continues in fainter and fainter planes of green.

Below, the topography is old and settled. Broad basins lie between smooth-backed ridges. Chasms descend in rounded flutes, their walls polished by currents that no longer deepen them. Ridges fit the flow so precisely that they produce no turbulence except where turbulence is part of their permanent form. There are no raw fractures, fresh landslips, young deltas, or fans of debris. Erosion has not merely stopped; it has entered an exact arrangement with renewal. Tissue is worn at the same infinitesimal rate at which it hardens. Channels neither fill nor widen.

Every solid surface is alive. The basin floors carry a low, springy turf of olive filaments. Ridge crests are armored in interwoven rind, slick and dense as wet hardwood but flexing slightly under pressure. Translucent cords run through it, some as fine as hairs and others wider than rivers. Silica-stiffened fronds rise from the clefts. Iron-red and copper-green veins branch through otherwise pale tissue. Minerals occur inside cells and fibers, never as exposed geology. Even the hardest surfaces have the faint irregularity of something grown rather than something formed.

Yet the sea is not crowded. Plant matter constitutes the world’s substrate without filling most of its volume. Enormous reaches contain little besides clear water, the dim living floor, and perhaps one isolated column rising in the distance. The great vegetation stands are separated by hundreds of kilometers of openness. Their scarcity gives each one the presence of a landform rather than a plant.

A mature stand usually begins with a single vertical mass: a trunk hundreds of meters thick, ribbed and slightly spiral, rising from a maze of roots. Around it stand thinner stalks, repeated at almost regular intervals. High above, flattened crowns divide into vanes, sails, and pressure sacs. Arched tissues connect some of the columns, not as supports added afterward but as continuous growth. From a distance, the stands resemble vast installations. Close up, there are no seams, joints, fasteners, or boundaries between one component and another.

They are also loud.

The noise of a stand arrives long before its details can be resolved. First comes a basal drone, too low to seem located anywhere. Then individual strokes emerge: the deep concussion of pressure bulbs emptying, the knock of flexible valves closing, the sustained hiss of water forced through narrow living channels. Higher in the water, millions of small oxygen bubbles contribute a fine, rain-like static. Electrical tissue adds occasional hard cracks that flatten into a continuous crackle across distance.

The stands are engines, though nothing in them was built.

Permanent currents turn free-rotating crown organs inside cuffs of smooth, self-lubricating tissue. Their motion drives rows of muscular plant bulbs. Some bulbs circulate water through the roots; others compress gas into hollow floats or draw nutrient-poor water across selective membranes. Conductive vessels gather the electrical potential produced by billions of cells and carry it through the plateau in vast, branching networks. At certain nodes, insulated green cables of root tissue run bundled together for enormous distances. Where their fields enter open water, small blue discharges flicker in fixed patterns.

The production is industrial in scale and organization: continuous circulation, desalination, gas separation, nutrient concentration, electrical transmission, the manufacture and distribution of sugar-rich sap. But there is no factory, no mine, no fuel store, no waste heap, and no artifact. There is not even a clear distinction between the machinery and the organism it serves. The product of the whole system is the continuation of the system.

Its scale gives the Upper Sea weather of its own.

A gyre several thousand kilometers wide occupies one province, turning around a center of nearly motionless water. Elsewhere, permanent current-fronts divide oxygen-rich water from water heavy with dissolved sap. Cold descending sheets move along the same submerged slopes year without year, matched by broad upwellings elsewhere. Above the largest circulation centers, stationary cloud banks pile over the surface. In one region, rain falls in dense silver curtains and has always fallen there; in another, the surface remains uncovered and reflective.

These are not approaching storms. They are features of the geography, as fixed as the ridges below. Their winds, currents, and rainfall have force but no development. A front does not weaken or advance. A whirlpool does not wander. The rain roars against the same leaves forever.

Animals gather where the water is being worked.

Around an oxygen upwelling, transparent crustaceans hang in dense, glittering clouds, each one filtering the thin organic film released by the roots. Pale sieve-fish move among them with their combed mouths open. Ribbon-bodied grazers lie along the turf in loosely spaced ranks, cropping a surface that remains exactly as thick after their passage. Crustaceans the size of hands occupy the grooves around valves and rotors, consuming loose cellular films and leaving the polished working surfaces untouched.

Higher on the columns, long eels hold themselves parallel to the electrical vessels. Copper-colored lines along their bodies brighten when they signal one another. Broad, six-finned filter feeders occupy the open water between trunks, their gills spread like black lace. Solitary giants cross the empty basins on routes so extended that their presence feels less like migration than the slow movement of a current.

The abundance is local and discontinuous. A stand may contain millions of animals, while the water beyond its acoustic shadow appears almost deserted. A school flashes into existence around a root crown, dense enough to hide the vegetation; beyond it lies a day’s worth of clear blue-green emptiness. There are no nests, burrows, dams, or accumulated shell beds. The animals do not modify the plateau. Their grazing, waste, and feeding are already accounted for in its closed circulation.

Sound binds these scattered concentrations together. Water carries the pumps’ drone across entire provinces. Filter feeders answer with low, pulsed calls that seem to come from beneath the floor. Grazers make dry clicking noises against the rind. At the surface, the permanent rain contributes a wide white roar. In some basins, these sounds interfere to form loud belts and quieter nodes, but nowhere is genuinely silent. The whole ocean sustains a complicated chord whose individual voices can be identified without the chord ever changing.

That is the strangest thing about the Upper Sea: motion produces no event.

Rotors turn, but do not wear down their bearings. Rain falls, but does not raise the water. Animals feed, but do not alter the distribution of life. Roots carry charge without depleting it. There is no dawn, no seasonal flowering, no succession of young growth over old, no gradual filling of the basins. Every output has an equal return; every circuit closes. The light remains at the same angle, and the shadows of the great crowns never travel.

The place therefore feels ancient without offering evidence of a history. Its rounded channels and polished tissues suggest immense duration, but there is no silt in which duration could be recorded. It feels violently active without having a future toward which that activity is tending.

Nothing is waiting to be completed. The engines have no final task. The weather has no aftermath. The fauna have no approaching migration, and the vegetation has no further territory to claim.

The Upper Sea is not frozen, silent, or dead. It is a high, cold ocean of living machinery, roaring at full capacity in an equilibrium so complete that even its ceaseless motion leaves everything exactly where it was.
