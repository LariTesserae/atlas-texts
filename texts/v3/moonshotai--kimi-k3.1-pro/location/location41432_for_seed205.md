## The High Choir

From below, the High Choir looks like a green weather system caught along the rim of the world. It is a plateau, broad beyond any useful journey, raised above dry lowlands on a plinth of ancient rock. Clouds coming off those lowlands often break beneath its edge. What hangs over the cliffs to meet them is not stone but root: braided, gray with age, furred with small leaves, dripping on some mornings and powder-dry by noon.

On top, the first surprise is space.

There is no enclosing forest. A continuous vegetable skin covers everything—low scale-leaves, wiry moss, silver grasses no higher than a hand, succulent fans pressed flat against the underlying fiber—but tall growth is scarce. Immense crown-trees stand many kilometres apart, their trunks fluted like eroded bone, their branches carrying broad, irregular roofs of foliage. From a high root ridge a traveler might see five of them, or nine, each isolated by heat-haze. One may have a cloud snagged in its upper branches. Another may be raining quietly on itself while the land between remains dry.

The emptiness is real. It is also misleading.

The ground is not ground. Hills are old roots that have thickened and folded over one another; valleys are the seams where great lobes of the plant body meet. What resembles exposed bedrock is usually compacted heartwood, weathered until it is gray and hard. It does not fracture like stone. It splinters, and the fresh splinter smells green. Beneath the surface, the material changes from cork to tough fiber to pale, resonant tissue full of narrow galleries. Even the dust is mostly vegetable: shredded cuticle, pollen, spores, tiny curls of worn lignin.

Nothing about the terrain is sharp for long. Occasional rain rounds the cuts made by wind and animals. fallen branches are colonized before they can become timber, and new growth closes old scars in smooth, heavy lips. The plateau has been wearing down and building itself up for so long that the two processes nearly balance. Its broad forms look settled, almost lethargic, though a root ridge can migrate several paces during a human lifetime. Maps remain useful. They do not remain exact.

No one has established where one plant ends and another begins. The low pelt, the crown-trees, and the hanging roots at the cliffs may be thousands of intertwined species, or expressions of a single old organism with many kinds of tissue. Cuts made far apart yield sap with the same bitter smell. Sometimes a wound in one basin is answered, days later, by a ridge of healing growth in another.

The plateau is large enough to hold contradictory weather. Each crown-tree draws moisture from deep tissues and releases it upward, an invisible river through the hot air. Lines of crowns create their own winds. Clouds form above some and never above others. A rain shaft can be visible for an entire day’s walk and still be falling somewhere else when evening comes. Storms travel along root valleys as if following channels, though most of those valleys have not held running water within living memory.

The light is ordinary, almost stark. There are no glowing leaves or colored suns, and outside the crowns there is little shade. At midday the pelt turns olive, straw-silver, and black-green beneath a white-hot sky. The elevation does not make the air cool. Heat rises from the living surface and returns from above; even the wind has usually passed over something warm. In open places the plant skin can be painful to bare feet. A few handbreadths down, in a split trunk or abandoned animal gallery, the air may be humid enough to bead on skin.

Rain is occasional: a hard, warm shower, a day of mist combed from a passing cloud, once in a while a storm that fills the old root-seam basins. It seldom runs far. Leaves close around droplets. Surface fibers swell and drink. The basins empty through a thousand small mouths. Soon only their polish and the brief abundance of animals show that water was there.

Most moisture is kept out of sight. It collects in leaf cisterns, in the axils of old branches, in thin bladders beneath the pelt, and on cold-radiating hairs during the short hours before dawn. Some cisterns are no larger than a cup; others, high in a crown, could supply a camp for a season. Their water is rarely still. It trembles with larvae, translucent swimmers, hunting nymphs, and things that cling to the walls until touched. There are also wet hollows inside the trunks where the heat smells of fermentation and every surface moves slightly when examined.

Life appears sparse until anything is touched.

A hand pressed to the pelt may disturb thousands of animals without revealing more than a shiver. Button-sized insects sit fixed in dense constellations on warm stems. Larvae occupy the galleries in layers. Clear, many-legged grazers travel beneath the leaf-scales in herds, making waves that cross the apparent wind. Flat predators follow them with only their sensory bristles exposed. A pace of ground can contain more animal bodies than a traveler sees in the open sky all day.

The larger creatures are more scattered. Long-legged browsers stand beneath the crowns like moving pieces of architecture, feeding slowly and carrying smaller ecologies in their fur and folded skin. Broad-mouthed fliers arrive when the swarms rise. Heavy burrowers keep old water passages open, while other animals patiently seal them. Every cistern has its users, its parasites, its guards, and something that has learned to wait beside it without being seen.

Then there is the sound.

It begins with individual dry clicks as the heat rises. These become rattles, then narrow bands of drilling song. The tymbal insects fastened along the trunks answer one another, and the trunks answer back. Their fluting and ancient galleries make them into resonators. A crown beyond sight may be heard first through the soles of the feet, a low pressure in the living ground, before its voice arrives through the air.

At full choir, speech is useless. The sound is not simply loud around the ears; it occupies the teeth, the sinuses, the hands. Thousands of separate pitches remain just distinct enough to prevent the noise from becoming a steady roar. It surges and folds over itself. When the mass emergence follows, the open air fills with wings. Bodies tick against skin and clothing. Predators pour through them, and their passage opens brief, whirling corridors of silence.

The whole plateau does not enter chorus at once. Waves of it travel for hours, crossing the spaces between crowns. Elsewhere there may be only the ordinary hiss of grazing and the tick of cooling seed cases. These quiet intervals can feel more exposed than the noise.

The noise is not incidental. At certain intensities, seed valves in the crown-trees spring open and pollen shakes loose in dense yellow sheets. Vibrations bring condensed water down from leaf hairs, producing a brief, glittering rain beneath an otherwise cloudless sky. Some crowns flower poorly after seasons of weak choruses. Whether the trees are summoning the animals, the animals are tuning the trees, or both are answering some deeper signal is not obvious. A crown that suddenly goes silent is avoided. Sometimes silence means disease. Sometimes it runs ahead of a predator faster than any shadow.

People live here, but lightly and far apart. There are no cities buried in the growth and no great roads cut across it. A camp may consist of one woven platform, a sleeping shelter, and a few condensation frames: forked supports, loose fiber mesh, a leaf gutter, a guarded gourd. During a heavy chorus the frames hum and release their catch. Rigid buildings fare badly. The substrate shifts, cordage is eaten, and anything stretched too tight eventually frays itself singing.

Tools are made from chipped stone carried up from the rim, hard seed-valves, teeth, bone, and fiber. Construction is mostly lashing, weaving, wedging, and guiding growth rather than clearing it. Old sites are easy to miss. A stair may remain as a row of smooth hollows inside a root, its upper steps vanishing into healed wood. A regular swelling in a trunk may contain the lintel of a shelter. Occasionally a platform survives high in a crown, long abandoned, its cords replaced by living tendrils that have followed their paths.

Routes are remembered by water and by voice. Each crown has its own resonance, altered by drought, damage, and the animals inhabiting it. Directions can be given as a sequence of tones: follow the deep crown until the split-voiced one is audible, then turn toward the crown whose upper note disappears after rain. Experienced travelers also know when not to follow a sound at all.

By late afternoon, the land can again look almost vacant: low green country under ordinary sunlight, far-apart crowns, a small rain cloud tearing itself apart over one of them, heat standing visibly above the pelt. Then some threshold is crossed. The clicking starts underfoot. A trunk beyond the horizon answers through bone. The hidden herds begin to move, the cisterns seethe, and the space between the crowns fills with wings.

For a while there is no landscape separate from the animals and the sound they make. There is only hot living material beneath the feet, bright drops shaking loose somewhere above, and a noise so complete that the plateau seems to be using the traveler’s body as another resonating chamber.

When the wave passes, distance returns. The crowns stand far apart again. The tiny platforms remain almost invisible against the roots. But here and there the pelt is dark with moisture, and everything that has been waiting is already coming to drink. The High Choir’s abundance is not spread thinly across its vastness. It is folded into the living ground, held out of sight, and released all at once by heat, water, and sound.
