## The Unbuilt Foundry

Nothing in the Unbuilt Foundry has ever been rained on. This is stronger than calling it a desert. Water is absent not only from its basins and atmosphere, but from the habits of the place: no hydrated minerals survive in its crust, no cloud gathers above its ranges, and no animal uses water as a solvent. The few fluids that exist are sealed inside bodies or rock—molten salts, metal alloys, dense mineral vapors condensing in the dark. There are no dry riverbeds, because there were never rivers. The white pavements in the lower basins resemble salt left by an vanished sea, but they are primary crust, precipitated directly from hot mineral gas. They imply no ocean and remember none.

The Foundry occupies the roof of a continent: a high province of tablelands, blunt summits, and immense enclosed basins, one beyond another until the farthest ranges seem to possess separate horizons. Its altitude brings no cold. Heat rises from fractures in the deep crust and is returned by the pale ground, while the thin, clear atmosphere offers little relief. Shade changes the light but not the temperature. Stone remains hot enough to spall at a touch; metal seams become too bright to look at directly.

Yet the daylight itself is disconcertingly normal. There is one sun, white-gold at noon, and the sky darkens and pales in an ordinary daily cycle. Objects cast legible shadows. Colors do not bleed into one another. The land needs no lurid sky to announce that it is impossible.

From the central rim, the province appears almost vacant. The nearest basin is a broad floor of gray silica and chalk-white chloride, cracked into plates that ring like ceramic under stress. Beyond it stand escarpments of oxblood oxide and black volcanic glass. Farther away, old mountains lie low and rounded, their upper surfaces polished by wind, thermal fatigue, and the slow passage of animals. There are no young-looking pinnacles. Even the steep cliffs have settled into their angles.

Erosion here is patient and nearly closed-loop. Heat expands a surface until a grain loosens; wind worries the grain across the pavement; gravity gathers it into a fan; centuries later, something eats the fan and carries its substance elsewhere. Gullies are cut by repeated spalling rather than floods. Cavern mouths are rounded by expanding gas and crystal growth. The scree slopes have reached stable grades, and the ventifacts all lean according to winds that have not substantially changed in ages. Uplift, abrasion, and animal consumption approximately balance. The Foundry is not static, but almost every change replaces something with its near equivalent.

Loose matter is scarce. The air often holds no visible dust at all. One can look across an hour of country and find only a dark outcrop, a shallow track, and the distant dome of a single living body. The emptiness is not peaceful exactly; it is too exposed for that. There are few obstacles behind which anything—sound, heat, attention—can be interrupted.

At first, the northern escarpments look like the exposed workings of an industrial excavation. Conductive and insulating strata alternate in precise bands. Natural flues rise through them, some hundreds of meters tall, their rims sintered into smooth throats. During the long hot phase of the regional cycle, these flues inhale; decades later, as the deeper rock changes state, they exhale. Thermal gradients drive electrical currents through seams of native metal. Charged mineral dust separates into pale and dark fractions along the cliff faces. At certain vents, beads of nearly pure metal accumulate with the patience of stalactites.

Nothing there was assembled.

There are no walls, rails, roads, housings, tools, discarded machines, or ruins. No straightedge was ever applied to the straight edges. Every furnace is either a vent or a gut; every cable is a mineral nerve, a crystalline conductor, or part of a living filament. The apparent regularity comes from crystal lattices, anatomy, and use repeated until use becomes a landform. The Foundry is industrial in operation rather than origin. It captures energy, separates materials, reduces oxides, transports charge, and produces standardized substances at regional scale. It simply never required a builder.

Its largest animals are the kiln-striders. From a distance, one resembles a slag hill supported by several columnar legs. Its back is a dark ceramic dome, broken by radiator slots; beneath the dome are reaction chambers, bellows, and a heart that circulates a molten alloy through vessels grown around it. Its joints are not mechanical imitations. They are actual joints, with sockets, mineral cartilage, and replaceable bearing surfaces, though each closes with the report of a drop forge.

A strider does not so much walk as redistribute its weight over generations. The lifting of a foot may occupy a season. Its passage across a large basin can take thousands of years. It feeds by grinding selected crust between broad oral plates, extracting not merely substance but chemical imbalance: the available difference between oxidized and reduced mineral, charged and uncharged seams, hot interior and cooler surface. What emerges behind it is not waste in the human sense. It is a new pavement, simpler and denser, usable by other animals.

Smaller fauna gather around these processes. Ribbon-bodied kites, hundreds of meters long but almost without thickness, hang in the upper air where electrical gradients are strongest. They unwind conductive filaments from their own flanks and trail them through the striders’ fields. Inside the radiator slots live millers with concentric mineral jaws, needle-mouthed scavengers, and translucent animals that spend their whole lives following pulses of charge. A single old strider carries an crowded ecology, though the country between striders may contain almost nothing alive.

There is no vegetation anywhere in this web: no rooted producers, no green film, no fungal-looking carpet softening the stone. Life begins with heat, sunlight, and chemical potential, and from the beginning it behaves animalistically. It seeks, pumps, grinds, responds. Almost everything that moves matter through the Foundry is part of some appetite.

The province is correspondingly loud. Not busy—its noises are too widely separated for bustle—but loud in scale and duration. A great vent’s intake produces a low moan that can persist for years. Strider valves close with concussions that cross entire basins. When a foot finally completes its descent, the first impact is often less impressive than the pavement’s response: a high, broad ringing that passes outward through the white plates until distant cliffs return it as a second event. Within the striders, countless small mandibles contribute a penetrating hiss. The kites sometimes emit hard, metallic cries when their filaments overload.

There is no sound of water, foliage, or weather as softer worlds understand it. Sound here belongs to resonance, pressure, friction, and anatomy. Much of it travels through the ground rather than the thin air, arriving in the teeth and skeleton before it can be heard.

Eventually, the order of these sounds becomes difficult to trust.

A footfall may roll through a basin while the strider’s foot is still visibly raised. At the far end of its route, a track may already lie weathered and stable, although the animal has not yet begun the crossing that will make it. The electrical pulse in a ridge may cease when a strider several basins away enters dormancy, as though the ridge were temporarily one of its organs. These are not misunderstood mechanisms. They are symptoms of the local failure: brief events remain singular, but sufficiently long processes become approximate.

A falling grain falls once. A spark occurs in one place. But a migration lasting a hundred thousand years can follow several incompatible routes without immediately choosing among them. Each route leaves some erosion. Each generation contributes some sound. A stride, a digestion, or the making of a mountain can be distributed across histories that do not all survive into the same present.

This explains the Foundry’s strange census. In space, at an ordinary moment, it is nearly empty: a handful of striders scattered across a province, wide gaps between the kites, almost no loose life on the ground. In accumulated duration, it is overwhelmingly faunal. The basins contain the pressure of herds that never shared a century. The cliffs are marked by grazings from alternative lineages. The air and stone carry breathing, impacts, and courtship calls belonging to animals that are not currently anywhere nearby.

At rare intervals, when several of the great natural-industrial cycles complete together, those histories briefly occupy the same geography. The event is called a muster, though nothing summons it. The basins darken with kiln-striders beyond the number the visible routes can account for. Kites layer the upper air until their filaments seem to measure the sky. Every vent answers every other vent. The noise becomes bodily, an immense pressure of hearts, mills, valves, and feet. For a season, animal life is overwhelming in the simplest numerical sense.

Then the cycles close. The surplus histories separate. Most of the animals are gone again, though not always the same ones that were present before. Their erosion remains. So does some of their sound.

Even these failures have old, established forms. One escarpment reliably returns a concussion before receiving it. In the western pass, during the century-long exhalation of the deepest vents, distance loses its usual ordering: near, far, and the location of the observer exchange values without any visible movement. The mountains do not shift. Only the fact of *between* becomes unreliable. Nothing thrashes or dissolves. Reality breaks with the same patience as everything else, and its fractures have weathered smooth.

Seen at an ordinary noon, however, nearly all of this can be missed. There is only white pavement, black high country, painful heat, and perhaps nine dark animals at impossible distances. Nothing built. Nothing green. Nothing wet. Then the ground accepts the weight of a step that will not land for another century, and the whole province answers.

The Foundry resumes its slow work: heat into charge, charge into motion, mineral into animal, animal into polished stone. Nothing hurries. Even impossibility has had time to become a settled landform.
