## The Cistern Beneath the White Court

From above, the place is almost nothing: a narrow slot in the paving, a wooden grille no wider than a door, and a breath of damp warmth against the ankles. The court gives no other indication of the room below. Yet sound comes through the slot in pulses—a low running note, a succession of hollow knocks, and, every little while, a roar so large that it seems to belong to a river rather than a vessel small enough to cross in seven paces.

Less than two metres beneath the paving lies a single chamber, four paces wide and high enough for a person to stand in, if there were anywhere dry to stand. Water fills it to within a forearm of the ceiling. There are no passages leading onward, no collapsed doorway suggesting some greater complex. It is complete in itself: one tank, one access hatch, two light wells, an inlet, and a drain.

Everything visible has been made. The walls are hand-pressed brick under thick lime plaster; the floor is laid with broad fired tiles, sloping almost imperceptibly toward a clay outlet. A square brick pier supports the slab ceiling. The pipes are terracotta, their socket joints wrapped in fibre and sealed with pitch. Even the bed beneath the lowest course is another construction, a raft of brick and lime rather than exposed earth or stone. No natural substrate enters the room.

It is also conspicuously new. The corners of the submerged tiles are still sharp. Trowel ridges remain visible along the plaster, and in one place the whorl of a builder’s fingertip survives beneath the water, preserved as clearly as an inscription. The adze marks on the hatch timbers have not silvered or softened. Around the drain, a few spalls of brick retain fresh, angular edges. There is no worn threshold, no deep silt, no dark historical staining at the waterline—only a few white crescents where warm water has evaporated against the upper wall.

The furnishings are so sparse that the distances between them feel deliberate. The pier stands slightly off centre. A perforated clay basket rests near the brightest wall. A single inclined tile sits beneath the inlet to take the force of the falling water. Otherwise there is clear floor, clear water, and broad intervals of unbroken white plaster. One could not become lost here. Nearly the whole room is visible from any point within it.

Nevertheless, it has depth. The water is well over a person’s head, warm and remarkably clear, with only a faint mineral haze near the inlet. It arrives from shallow tanks on the roof, where it has stood under the sun, and enters the chamber at a little above body temperature. The lower water is slightly cooler. Where the two layers mingle, the view of the far wall bends and trembles, though there is no colour to mark the current.

The inlet itself is a square clay mouth near the ceiling. From it falls a stream as thick as a wrist. It strikes the inclined tile just below the surface and drives rafts of bubbles downward. For a moment the bubbles descend together, silver and densely packed; then their buoyancy overcomes the current and they rise in separate, wavering lines. At the surface they burst under the ceiling with a sound like handfuls of small stones dropped into a bowl.

The brightness is unexpected. This is not the blue dusk of an ordinary covered cistern. The two light wells are lined with smooth white plaster and angled toward different parts of the sky. Around midday, direct sunlight enters one of them and strikes the water as a hard, golden column. The other gathers reflected light from the court. The plastered walls return that light again and again, until even the space behind the pier is a luminous white-grey rather than true shadow.

Moving water breaks the sunlight into bright meshes. These slide over the floor, climb the pier, and gather on the underside of the ceiling slab. When the inlet surges, the entire room seems to shiver, though its masonry is motionless. Looking upward from the floor, one sees the surface as a second ceiling: a brilliant, restless sheet in which the hatch and the dark mouth of the inlet appear inverted.

The chamber’s weather is hydraulic. While the inlet runs, the water level rises by almost imperceptible degrees. The outlet murmurs. Air ticks and expands inside a warming pipe. Somewhere within the clay bell of the drain, water begins to spill over an internal lip. For several minutes nothing else happens. Then the siphon takes.

The change is immediate. The outlet’s murmur becomes a full, muscular roar; the surface bows toward the drain; and the whole chamber gives back the sound from every hard face. Water is drawn down by a handspan. Warm air is pulled through the roof slot, then released again as the flow weakens. Finally the siphon breaks with a deep gulp and a series of smaller, knocking reports. The inlet continues falling. The level begins its slow recovery.

Nothing in this requires an engine or an unexplained force. Gravity, trapped air, sun-warmed water, and carefully shaped clay are sufficient. After rain, when the roof catchments are full, the cycle shortens and the room becomes louder. During a dry spell, the intervals lengthen. On the hottest afternoons, convection adds another motion: warmth gathers beneath the ceiling while cooler threads slip along the floor toward the outlet. At night, moisture condenses around the light wells and falls back in widely spaced drops, each one returned by the walls as two or three separate sounds.

Life has only just begun to establish itself. In the perforated basket, a small clump of water rush grows from hand-rolled pellets of fired clay. Its narrow leaves bend whenever the siphon draws, then recover unevenly. Three patches of green algae occupy sunlit joints in the plaster, none larger than an open hand. A few transparent filaments trail near the inlet, where the current is strongest.

There are no fish, snails, larvae, or insects. Nothing moves except the plants, the water, and what the water carries. The chamber is too new, too frequently flushed, and still too poor in nutrients for a dense green bloom. Its ecology consists mostly of thin films converting brief intervals of sunlight into growth, and of the rush slowly extending pale roots through the holes in its basket.

In time, the room will become less sharply itself. Mineral deposits will blunt the tile edges; algae will find the quieter corners; the drain will acquire a dark fan of sediment; the timbers will swell and their pegs will begin to complain. For now, though, every process seems to be making its first mark.

To remain beside it for an hour is to learn its small set of signs: the thin note of filling, the changed pressure before the siphon catches, the warm breath at the grille, the sudden return of reflected light when the surface calms. It is a complete climate assembled from brick, clay, water, and sun—warm, brilliant, nearly empty, hidden beneath a few steps of paving, and startlingly loud without containing a single animal.
