## The Hushless Roof

At the top of the world, almost no mountain remains.

The Roof stands higher than nearly all the weather, yet it has no summit in the usual sense: no horn of rock, no split ridge, no succession of peaks. Whatever violence first raised it happened too long ago to leave a readable wound. Erosion has rounded its shoulders, filled its valleys, and polished away every unnecessary angle. What remains is an immense, shallowly undulating table, green across distances that take days to cross. Its altitude is most apparent at the edges. There, the soft ground simply ends. Beyond a lip as rounded as an old doorstep, warm cloud presses against the cliffs, and below that lies a gulf too deep for falling stones to answer.

The air should be cold. Instead it is mild and often close, thin enough to make a newcomer labor but warm enough for dew to feel pleasant on the skin. Heat rises continually along the cliffs and seeps from joints in the stone. At dawn it carries smells of wet mineral, crushed leaves, and something faintly sour, like rain kept too long in a clay jar.

The Roof is damp rather than wet. No river crosses it. Rain comes in brief, erratic passages, sometimes falling from an otherwise empty sky. Most water arrives more quietly: condensation on leaves, mist combed from the air by fibrous mats, droplets gathering in stone hollows. Some pools are no larger than a cupped hand and last an hour. Others, sunk into shaded basins, have held the same dark water for centuries. Between them are long stretches where the ground feels dry on top but gives softly underfoot, hiding moisture in its roots.

On foot, the vegetation is luxuriant. Ferns braid themselves into springy carpets; wax-leaved rosettes overlap like shingles; low flowering plants crowd every crack with white, rust-red, and bruised yellow. The thin soil is almost entirely occupied. Pull up any corner of the green skin and beneath it is a dense negotiation of root, fungus, wet stone, and old decay.

From a little distance, however, the place looks empty.

Its lushness is horizontal. There is no general forest canopy, no crowd of trunks, no layered wall of growth. Wind and pressure have made tall, rigid plants impossible except in rare sheltered folds. The table therefore presents enormous spaces of knee-high green beneath an even greater emptiness of air. One grove may stand on the horizon, dark and intricate, and require half a day to reach. Between it and the next upright thing there may be nothing but a stone cell, a single grazing animal, or a column of mist.

The groves themselves are concentrated almost to excess. In their shelter, black-trunked trees lean together above tree ferns and hanging vines. Their branches stream permanently in one direction, though their leaves tremble from winds arriving from all sides. Water ticks from frond to frond. After the open table, stepping into one feels like entering a crowded room.

The noise begins underfoot.

Deep within the Roof, ancient cavities and volcanic seams connect in ways no visitor has mapped. Some open at the surface as smooth, dark wells; others are only narrow fissures hidden under vegetation. Changes in pressure far below drive air through them. The whole plateau is a hollow instrument with no single player and no agreed rhythm.

One flue may sustain a bass note so low that it is felt chiefly in the ribs while another, leagues away, breaks into a shriek. Smaller openings hiss, boom, or chatter. Their voices overlap until direction disappears. Near an active well, the sound is not merely deafening but material: water jumps in its basin, teeth ache, and the green mats flatten in concentric waves. Speech is useless across most of the Roof. The rare visitors who know it communicate by touch, by signs traced on a palm, or by knocking patterns into stone.

The land is old; nothing passing over it is still.

A change in the roar often comes before a change in the weather. A deep voice cuts out. Somewhere to the west, a flue begins to wail. Warm air then pours from an opening that had been inhaling a moment before, and fog condenses directly around the knees. Under clear sunlight, the ground vanishes into whiteness. Beads gather on every leaf until their weight releases them in a sudden, local rain. Minutes later the current reverses. Mist is dragged raggedly across the table, droplets stream upward from pools, and the sun returns to stone already steaming.

There is no dependable sequence to it. A cloud overhead may pass without releasing anything. A cloudless afternoon may soak a traveler twice. Sheltered plants respond visibly, turning their leaves, opening pale undersides to moisture, then folding again. Patches of the plain shift from gray-green to saturated emerald as millions of small surfaces change position. Flowers open into one brief pressure change and are stripped by the next. Petals and seed-down stream horizontally for a few seconds before vanishing.

Only the stone remains composed. There is almost no loose gravel, no raw scree, no young gully eagerly deepening itself. Nearly everything that could be carried away has already gone. The turbulence makes and unmakes puddles, clouds, scents, blossoms, and temporary paths through the grass, but the broad shape beneath them has settled into its final patience.

Animals are scarce and mostly silent. The largest common sight—and even that is uncommon—is a heavy slate-colored browser with broad, soft feet. It moves alone, cropping newly opened growth and resting against the wind rather than fleeing it. Its ears appear to matter less than the soles of its feet; it often stands motionless with all four feet planted, attending to some vibration in the rock. Occasionally a long-winged shadow slides along the outside of the cliffs without ever crossing the sky above the table. More often there is only evidence: a bitten rosette, a print filling slowly with dew, a warm hollow where something has recently risen.

Human marks are rarer still. A long day’s walk might end at a low oval cell sunk halfway into the ground. Its walls are fitted stone, thick and windowless, its roof a layered weight of slabs and turf. Inside, the roar diminishes enough to become separable from one’s own thoughts. There may be a sleeping shelf, a clay vessel beneath a drip groove, a rotted screen woven from leaf fiber. Elsewhere one finds a greenstone adze dulled by use, three cairns with no path between them, or a wooden peg polished where cord once rubbed it. Nothing suggests a town. No field boundaries survive, no road climbs the table, and no structure seems intended to be seen from another. Some cells are ancient almost to shapelessness; a few have been repaired with fresh cord and recently cut boughs. Whether their makers are gone or merely elsewhere is impossible to tell.

Those who stay learn to build low, to collect water rather than search for it, and never to trust the appearance of the sky. They also learn to distrust silence.

Very rarely, without warning, the scattered flues draw breath together. The roar drains away. For several heartbeats it is possible to hear a leaf detach from wet stone, the tiny movement of one’s own clothing, perhaps water ticking far down in the rock. The silence does not make the Roof peaceful. It makes its emptiness suddenly precise.

Then the clouds beyond the rim stand up.

Warm air erupts through the hidden passages. Pools dimple and lift in shining threads; the green skin bows; sheltered trees knock their branches together. Sound returns all at once, as though the world has been dropped back into place.

Afterward, under ordinary sunlight, the table looks unchanged. A few flowers are gone. A new pool shines in an old hollow. Mist unravels above a warm fissure. The Roof resumes its work of enduring almost forever while everything upon it is hurriedly made, scattered, and made again.
