## The Moth Sump

High in the leeward rib of a weather-spire, above the last walkable ledges and much of the cloud, there is a cavity no larger than a domestic cistern. It does not appear on the spire’s exterior except as a slightly warm seam in the alloy. Inside, it is three paces long, though there is no straight path of three paces across it. Pipes, structural ribs, condenser fins, and old conduits crowd the space until the walls seem to be leaning inward.

Outside, the elevated air is thin and cold. Inside, the heat remains just above blood temperature. It comes through the walls from machinery elsewhere in the spire: no sunlight enters, and there is no open sky. The atmosphere is close, wet, and faintly sweet, with underlying smells of hot metal, wet shell, and oxidized coolant. Moisture condenses on anything carried in and soon begins to move across it in deliberate-looking threads.

Nothing here rests on natural ground. The apparent stone of the floor is a dark ceramic composite, worn smooth and shallowly cupped into three basins. The pale crust along the runnels is precipitated coolant salt mixed with repair foam. Even the silt is manufactured material rearranged by age: powdered glass, metal oxides, fragments of sensor housings, molted chitin, and the shells of very small animals. What looks like mud is the spire’s own substance, slowly becoming sediment.

Water occupies every stage at once. It hangs as vapor in the dim air, beads on the ceiling combs, crawls sideways along treated metal, and falls in brief warm showers. The basins are never more than a hand deep, but they are crowded with current. Surface-control fields in the walls draw water into fine ropes and guide it around obstacles; occasionally a drop will pause on a vertical rib, trembling, before rising to join a larger flow overhead. There are no visible pumps. The original machinery has been reduced to changes in surface tension, pressure, and something subtler in the alloy itself.

The only light comes from a few ancient status points buried behind translucent scale. They glow dull amber and, near the floor, a compromised green. Water catches these colors without reflecting them cleanly. Most of the chamber remains in a brown-black half-darkness in which movement is easier to detect than form.

Age has rounded everything that water touches. The condenser fins were once sharp enough to cut; now their edges are soft and bright. Channels have polished themselves into the floor. Some pipes sag slightly within their flexible mounts, and the mouths of drains have become smooth, oval throats. Yet the chamber is not decaying toward collapse. The spire still heals structural breaches, extruding fresh material wherever a load-bearing surface thins, but it no longer restores worn edges or corrects small deformations. It repairs injury and ignores age. The result is a settled balance: nothing pristine, nothing ruined.

The sound is overwhelming.

It enters through the structure from the atmosphere exchangers, hundreds of meters away, and accumulates in the little cavity until hearing and touch become difficult to distinguish. A low pressure bears steadily against the chest. Higher harmonics buzz in the teeth and make loose shells chatter against the walls. The water in the basins is never calm; the roar raises it into miniature standing ridges, and droplets struck by falling condensation sometimes bounce rather than merge.

When the spire changes load, the sustained noise gathers into a single concussion that seems to occur inside the bones. Then the roar resumes, fractionally altered. Speech would be impossible. Even thought feels shortened by it. But the sound is not merely an affliction of the place. It is one of its conditions, like warmth or wetness, and the ecology has arranged itself around it.

At first the walls appear furred. Closer inspection resolves the fur into animals.

Moths roost wing to wing among the upper fins, each no larger than a thumbnail, their gray wings freckled with conductive dust. They rarely fly except during pressure changes. Mostly they crawl, tucking themselves into the warmest seams with their legs folded against their bodies. Beneath them, snails with dark, flexible shells graze the maintenance films, leaving clean, interlacing trails. Their congregations are so dense in some corners that a wall can appear to be paved with slowly shifting eyes.

The basins seethe with transparent crustaceans, most no longer than a grain of rice. They sieve the water for gel, larvae, and organic dust carried in by the condensate. When a current concentrates them against a rib, their packed bodies make the water look cloudy. Flat, ivory hunters—something between crickets and soft-shelled crabs—wait along the runnels. They are blind to anything beyond a few centimeters and indifferent to airborne sound. They read vibration through their feet. A struggling crustacean, a snail withdrawing into its shell, the approach of warm rain: each has a different signature.

Some of these animals descend from maintenance stock once introduced to keep the condenser clear. Others arrived as eggs or stowaways in service ducts. The distinction no longer matters. They have become small, deaf, warmth-loving specialists of a habitat that exists nowhere outside the spire.

True vegetation is sparse. A few cords of black algae occupy the green-lit seams, and one stand of miniature fern clings above the highest basin, its fronds no taller than a finger. There is too little light for a green canopy, and the grazers are too numerous. The ecology runs instead on the wall’s faint secretion of nutrient gel, on microbial films fed by warmth and chemical leakage, and on the ceaseless recycling of dead bodies into silt and food.

Every few minutes, though not on a fixed schedule, the chamber passes through a small weather cycle.

The roar deepens. Water withdraws from the basins in narrowing threads while fog thickens around the ceiling combs. The moths press closer together. The ivory hunters climb above the runnels and become still. Droplets swell overhead until the dim status lights are each surrounded by a trembling amber halo.

Then the pressure releases.

Warm rain falls hard enough to stipple the basins. Water sheets down the walls, carrying snails, shed wings, crustaceans, and black sediment into the channels. For several seconds the chamber is all runoff and impact. The hunters strike at anything washed within reach. The crustaceans spread through the new water, feeding furiously. The fern bows under the rain and springs back.

Finally the floor begins to drink. Field-guided pores pull the basins down, leaving stranded animals to crawl or be eaten. Fog remains for a while, then thins. The moths unfold. Fresh beads gather on the ceiling.

Nothing in the sump is waiting to be cleaned, restored, or completed. Erosion makes sediment; sediment shelters film; film feeds grazers; grazers obstruct the drains; the drain pulses clear them. Repair material swells from a rib and is slowly polished back. Every process has some other process pressing against it.

From outside, the chamber is only a warm patch behind metal. Within, it has rain, current, hunting, migration, and thunder, all contained in a few cubic meters. At the height of the cycle, each falling drop holds a fleck of amber light and shakes so violently that the light breaks apart. Then the floor drinks, the animals resume their small movements, and the roar continues without pause.
