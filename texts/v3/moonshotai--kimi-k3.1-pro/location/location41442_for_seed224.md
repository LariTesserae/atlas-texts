## The Datum Hollow

The highest point of the mountain is a hollow, though from outside it looks like nothing more than a flaw in the stone.

From far below, the mountain is an old, high shoulder rising out of a plain of anhydrous salt and powdered glass. It has outlasted the kind of violence that makes dramatic mountains. Its ridges are blunt, its fractures have rounded lips, and sheets of iron-dark glass lie against the slopes in scales worn smooth by radiation and cold. Nothing looks freshly broken. Even the talus seems to have reached an agreement about where it belongs.

There are no riverbeds, because there were never rivers. No ice survives in the deep shade; no rime gathers in the fractures. The salts never cake with damp, and the dust does not cling except through the weakest electrical affections of one grain for another. Water is not merely absent here. None of the forms of the landscape refer to it, even indirectly.

A small, dull sun gives the plain a pewter half-light. At the mountain’s altitude, the atmosphere is too thin to hold much of that light, so the sky remains dark even when the sun is above the horizon. The cold is steady and dry, less a weather than a condition under which matter agrees to move only slightly. Occasionally, deep in the massif, a fracture accommodates the cold with a single tick. Centuries may pass before the next.

Near the summit, in a rounded boss of black glass, there is a seam the length of a finger. Sometimes it is a seam. At other times it is only a place where the dim light declines to make a reflection. Behind it lies the hollow.

Inside is a shallow mineral landscape beneath a low ceiling: a grey salt flat no larger than a small room, bordered by two worn ridges and a drift of dust so fine that its surface appears softly focused. The ceiling can be touched without straightening an arm. In some places it curves down almost to the floor; in others it is absent without being open. There is no view through it, only a less definite darkness.

The scale remains intimate even when distance misbehaves. The hollow never opens into a cavern or a vista. Instead, the same few square meters acquire additional ways of being far apart.

Only three things are visibly built.

A sill of yellowish ceramic, no longer than a forearm, lies half buried near one ridge. It might have belonged to a wall, but there is no wall, no foundation, and no second piece. Farther across the flat, a needle of dark metal leans at a slight angle. Its lower end rests in the salt; its upper end touches nothing. The third structure is a shallow rectangle incised into the floor, its lines so worn that the dust has nearly reclaimed them. The needle stands inside the rectangle on some approaches and well outside it on others.

Nothing else occupies the chamber. No containers, no furniture, no bodies, no writing that can be separated from the orderly flaws in the minerals. The emptiness between the three remnants is not a backdrop. It is most of the place.

Light rests over everything without appearing to arrive. It is just strong enough to distinguish salt from glass and old ceramic from old metal. Colors are mostly withheld. Shadows are broad, faint, and slow to change, as though illumination were a sediment that had settled rather than something currently shining. The needle’s shadow is the darkest thing in the hollow, and it ends a short distance before reaching the needle.

The silence is more complete than the darkness. There is too little gas to carry an ordinary sound. A grain breaking from the ridge falls without a click; the salt receives it without a whisper. Any sustained sound would have to be imported. The nearest thing the hollow makes for itself is a rare, delicate tick transmitted through solid matter when one of its old constraints changes state. Afterward, the silence does not exactly return. It is revealed to have continued underneath the event.

The visible remnants are not the important technology. That is dispersed through the floor: substitutions and vacancies in the crystal lattices, too sparse to resemble circuitry and too orderly to be damage. No mechanism moves. Nothing glows, scans, or waits for a command. The lattices do not compute so much as *specify*.

Their remaining specification is an altitude.

Not height above a sea—no sea ever existed here—but a particular value of gravitational potential, measured when the mountain was younger and its mass was arranged differently. The hollow was fixed to that value. It may once have been a survey reference, a calibration volume, or simply a place where instruments could trust the direction of up. No explanation survives, and the technology does not appear to require one.

The mountain has since compacted, weathered, and redistributed itself. The hollow has declined every corresponding revision. Its floor still occupies the old altitude exactly. To maintain that exactness, it has surrendered less essential agreements.

Distance went first. A straight path along the wall can return to its beginning without enclosing anything. Every map of the hollow is accurate, and every map describes something smaller than the act of walking it. The three artifacts keep their positions, but approaches do not necessarily preserve their order. A visitor might pass the sill, the needle, and the rectangle, then retrace each step and encounter the rectangle first without ever having turned around.

At the boundary, sequence has also begun to wear. The seam can remain visible after it has been passed through. A shallow impression may lie in the dust long before the grain that fits it detaches from the ridge. These are not flickering apparitions or brief paradoxes. Each inconsistency persists calmly for centuries, as solid and patient as the stone.

Up remains almost perfect. That is why the place has not come apart.

Almost: grains on the two ridges rest at angles that record slightly different verticals. Some lean against slopes as though supported by an absent wind. At the needle’s tip, a plumb line would point through the floor and arrive at the ceiling. The hollow tolerates these small disagreements because correcting every one of them would cost more than correcting none.

When an impossible relation is finally discarded, the payment appears as matter. A few atoms precipitate onto the floor: pale, dry, chemically inert. Most of the salt is ordinary mineral erosion, but the finest dust is the residue of abandoned geometry. It is sediment laid down by failed descriptions. Thus the breakdown of reality is not simply something happening *in* the landscape. It is one of the landscape’s slow depositional processes.

Physical erosion has reached an equally old accommodation. Radiation and thermal flexing fret the ridges; minute changes in the local field persuade dust from one hollow to another; the built remnants become buried and exposed by degrees. Material removed from high places collects in low ones, while some of the finest residue is taken back into the lattices during repair. Nothing is pristine, but nothing is on the verge of collapse. The hollow is not newly ruined. It has been damaged long enough for the damage to acquire stratigraphy, texture, and balance.

If ecology can mean a web of dependencies rather than a web of food, then there is an ecology here. The cold suppresses chemical change. The sluggish chemistry keeps the mineral lattices coherent. The lattices preserve the cavity and its altitude. The strain of preserving them produces dust, and the dust blankets the floor, steadying temperature and damping the tiny mechanical shocks that might otherwise propagate through the lattice. Nothing metabolizes. Nothing competes. Nothing reproduces. The system persists with enormous intervals between its processes.

No spore hides in the salt, no film coats the glass, no burrow interrupts the dust. There are not even remains. Life is not being waited for, and its absence does not feel like a recent loss.

Once in an age, a grain leaves one of the ridges. Its fall lasts longer than the height accounts for. It enters the salt without sound. For years afterward, the needle’s shadow may belong to a slightly different version of the chamber. Then the old altitude reasserts itself, and the change becomes part of the floor.

Outside, beneath the dim sky, the mountain continues its patient loss of outline. Inside, the sill, the needle, and the shallow rectangle remain scattered in grey half-light, separated by an emptiness that is sometimes a few centimeters and sometimes the longest distance the hollow can manage.

It does not seem to be a tomb, a vault, or a warning. It is a small landscape gathered around an obsolete exactness: one height, kept perfectly, while stone, sequence, and space are allowed—grain by dry grain—to become approximate.
