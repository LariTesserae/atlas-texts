## The Dry Choir

High in a range of bare, folded mountains, there is a hollow small enough to walk around in less than an hour. From below it looks like a chipped cup set on the mountain’s shoulder. From its rim, the outside world falls away into immense ochre ridges and colorless plains, while the hollow itself feels almost enclosed: a room open to the sky.

The stone is ancient and settled into its shape. The rim has been rounded, the fractures softened, the old scree slopes locked together by mineral crusts. Wind has worried the place for so long that it has found nearly everything loose and carried it away. What remains is dense umber rock, seams of white salt, and a floor of pale, sharp grit. Nothing about the landform seems violent anymore.

The violence belongs to the life inside it.

A dry haze of spores and mineral dust hangs above the rim, dimming the sun to a brass-colored smear. Even at midday, the hollow has the shadowed amber light of a room with its door nearly closed. The height should make the air cold, but warm gases rise through invisible fissures. The air is thin, gently hot against the skin, and smells of pepper, resin, hot stone, and something faintly sulfurous.

There is no water. No seep stains the walls; no dew gathers in the leaf axils; no frost forms in the coldest shadow. The white deposits in the cracks are salt, never ice. When a stem breaks, it releases a thick amber oil that dries into a flexible black glaze. Animal blood is similarly dark and viscous. Even death here is dry: bodies do not rot so much as unbind, becoming fiber, shell, powder, and scent.

Yet the growth is extravagant.

Nine or ten islands of vegetation stand within the hollow, each one crowded almost to suffocation. Braided trunks support leaves broad as doors, bottle-green on one side and bruised crimson on the other. Bladder-shaped fronds swell and contract with the wind. Black roots loop aboveground before plunging into the rock, and beneath the taller growth are dense cushions of fine violet filaments, translucent pods, and rubbery fans layered one over another. Some leaves are furred; some are slick with oil; some tremble continuously although the air around them is still.

Between these islands, however, there is almost nothing. Wide expanses of warm salt-grit separate them. A person can cross those spaces in full view, small and exposed, with only a low structure or a solitary weathered stone breaking the emptiness. The lushness is intense but localized. The hollow never becomes a continuous forest, and its open ground gives every sound room to travel.

The sound is the first thing most visitors misunderstand.

It begins with wind entering old slots in the rim: a low, uneven note, more felt than heard. Hollow stalks take it up. Seed cases clatter. Insects answer with piercing saws and clicks, and hidden animals add booming, accordion-like cries. The bowl returns all of it, altered. A rattling at one’s feet may come back from the opposite wall as a roar; several small calls can coincide and become a single voice that seems to stand directly behind the listener.

At the height of a cycle, speech is useless. The noise presses against the chest. Dust lifts in ridges, loose fibers stream sideways, and the whole basin seems to vibrate around a note just below hearing.

The garden feeds on this.

Its roots draw mineral oil and warm gas from below, but sound determines what the life aboveground becomes. A sustained low tone ordinarily thickens trunks and sends woody ribs crawling over stone. Rapid clattering provokes pods and hard fruit. High, intricate insect cries often cause thin new growth—transparent vanes, filament sails, tissues already close to wings.

These are tendencies, not dependable laws. The same chord has produced flowers on one occasion and black thorns on another. Once, according to marks pecked into a shelter wall, it produced several hundred running animals where there had been a motionless thicket moments before.

The distinction between plant and animal is accordingly loose. The flat, many-legged browsers begin as leafed knobs rooted among the fan growth. They remain stationary while their single root drills downward, then tear free and walk when they have accumulated enough oil. Their bodies are dry constructions of barklike hide, shell, and coarse fiber. When they grow old, some stop feeding, drive their legs into the grit, and become thickets again. Other creatures hatch only as byproducts of collapse: transparent-winged swarms pouring from split trunks, or thumb-sized springers emerging by the thousand from a crushed pod.

There are a great many animals, but seldom a great many visible in the same place for long. Their abundance is successive rather than crowded. A patch of ground can appear empty, seethe with wings and legs for half a minute, and then be empty again. Something large lives within the buttressed roots of the largest island; usually only its call is heard, though occasionally a three-toed foot as broad as a shield presses briefly out of the shadows.

During the loudest passages, change becomes continuous and almost reckless. Stems lengthen visibly. Fronds tear open before they are fully formed. Browsers are overwhelmed by swarms; swarms strip new leaves; stripped stems split into dry fibers; scavengers churn the fibers into the grit. An open space can become a shoulder-high thicket, be consumed, and return to bare mineral ground within an afternoon. Paths vanish while they are being followed. The islands of vegetation slowly trade positions over the seasons.

And yet, over longer periods, the place holds its balance. The amount of life remains roughly constant. The rim does not crumble. The oldest roots always die before they can split the basin open. Stillness and permanence are real here, but only when seen from far enough away.

A few human-scale things persist among the changes. Three breast-high crescents of fitted stone stand on the open floor, their inner faces polished by vibrating dust. A stair of seven crooked slabs climbs partway up the wall and ends at nothing. Beneath an overhang lie a broken forked frame, several lengths of fiber cord gone stiff as horn, and clappers made from stone discs and emptied seed shells. There is no mortar and no forged metal. The marks are those of flaked stone tools, scrapers, hand drills, patience.

The crescents are not windbreaks. They are acoustic baffles, built to make small pockets of comparative quiet. Inside the best-preserved one, the vegetation remains low and a single insect can be heard distinctly. Elsewhere, roots have lifted the stones or new growth has closed over them. The builders seem to have learned how to interrupt the Choir, but never how to command it. Fresh lashings on one broken frame suggest that someone still visits, repairs a little, and leaves before the loudest cycles.

It is unwise to sing here, or to strike stone experimentally. Rhythm gathers growth around whatever produces it. Perfect silence is not necessarily safer: in the quiet pockets, dormant husks collect beneath the dust, and the return of sound may wake all of them together. The least dangerous moments are the confused middle passages, when calls overlap, growth reverses itself, and nothing in the hollow can settle long enough to decide what it is.

Seen from the rim in the dim evening, the Dry Choir can look almost empty: a handful of dark islands in an old stone cup. Then the wind turns. The low note enters, the hollow stalks answer, and the basin fills with wings, roots, splintering shells, and enormous invisible throats. The mountain itself does not move. Within it, life rushes through forest, flock, carcass, dust, and forest again, as though trying every possible voice before the light goes.
