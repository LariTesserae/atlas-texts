## The Ninth Sump

Twenty-six metres below a black lava plain, a steel stair ends in heat.

The chamber beyond is small enough to cross in a minute, though darkness makes that difficult to believe. Three service lamps still burn: one above the door, one inside a control cabinet, one at the farthest machine. They are weak, amber points rather than sources of illumination. Each reveals a little glass, a few bolts, perhaps the rim of a pipe. None reaches the walls.

The darkness is not cool. The air has the dense, used quality of a breath exhaled into a closed room. Metal handrails become uncomfortable after a long touch; sweat gathers but does not readily evaporate. Yet the place is not steamy or uniformly wet. Most of the rock is dry. Moisture exists here in islands, brief weather systems, and surfaces that remember water after it has gone.

The Sump occupies the widest part of a young steam rift. It was not patiently hollowed by a river. Pressure opened the basalt along a fault, and the rock still bears the abruptness of that event. Walls meet in clean, glassy planes. Fractured columns end in blades and conchoidal scars. Blocks on the floor have kept their sharp edges; there are no rounded cobbles, no deep sediment, no stalactites softening the roof. A later industrial bore squared off one end and supplied the floor with concrete anchor beds, but even these look like interruptions rather than improvements. The chamber seems less ruined than unfinished.

Three condensing engines stand far apart on the open floor. Each is a squat arrangement of black iron, finned cylinders, ceramic insulators, and pipes wrapped in cracked lagging. Armoured cables enter through the ceiling. A narrow service rail runs for eight metres and then stops at a wall. There is no larger factory visible, only the implication of one elsewhere: current still arrives, valves still respond, and stencilled maintenance numbers survive beneath skins of rust and mineral scale.

For long intervals, the engines do nothing audible.

Then a relay closes somewhere in the dark. The sound is slight—a knock felt through the soles more than heard—and a pump turns for several minutes. Coolant moves through the overhead pipes. Their surfaces slowly become cooler than the air, and condensation begins as a dull silver bloom. Beads gather along seams and joints. Hours later, the first drops fall.

This is the Sump’s rain.

It never falls generally. It comes down in sparse vertical lines beneath particular pipes, sometimes onto bare basalt, sometimes into the growth below. A wet cycle may last half a day. Then the pipes warm, the dripping becomes less frequent, and the chamber returns to stillness. Water collects in a shallow sump no larger than a doorway and in a chain of warm pockets along the rear fracture. There is no stream. The pools simply hold what the last cycle gave them, losing a little to seepage and evaporation before the next.

Around the reliable pipes, the vegetation is astonishingly lush.

Each grove covers only a few square metres, but within that boundary the growth is layered and crowded. Copper-coloured cushions pack the wet rock. Black canes rise through them, jointed and flexible, supporting pleated fans that overlap like folded leather. Beneath the fans hang translucent root-webs and sheets of tissue so fine that a brought lamp would shine through them. Ivory filaments climb the pipe lagging. In the oldest beds, fallen fronds lie whole beneath the living growth, slowly collapsing into thin, glassy layers.

Nothing here is green. The tissues are aubergine, umber, soot-coloured, rust, or the pale yellow of old bone. Nothing glows.

The growth does not photosynthesize. Its pale roots occupy the chemical boundary where hot, iron-rich seepage meets the cooler condensate, drawing energy from that slow reaction. The engines unintentionally provide its water; the young rock provides nearly everything else. Consequently, the gardens follow machinery rather than light. Their outlines map the cooling circuits with surprising fidelity. A dead service lamp may be completely overgrown, while a living one nearby remains bare. Beneath a pipe that failed years ago stands a dry lattice of canes, still upright, its leaves folded into narrow seams.

The living fronds fold too during dry intervals. After condensation returns, they open over several days. Spread is slower still. With no animals to carry spores or fragments, most reproduction depends on dripping water and the incremental extension of roots. Each grove may, in effect, be a single organism cautiously occupying its machine.

There is no animal life in the chamber at all. No insects gather at the lamps. Nothing nests in the cable trays or burrows into the mineral silt. The warm pool contains no pale swimmers. Leaves are never chewed, and the few clear patches of mud hold no tracks. The silence therefore has none of the watchful quality of a hiding place. It is simply an absence of animal movement.

Its small sounds become distinct: a drop striking iron; another entering water; the tick of a pipe contracting;, very rarely, the subdued pressure of the pump beginning its work. The air barely moves, so the fans and canes do not rustle. A sound may be followed by hours in which the chamber offers nothing but heat and the smells of wet iron, warm stone, faint ozone, and crushed vegetation—something mineral and sharp, like iodine mixed with celery.

Human traces are sparse. A stool stands on one concrete plinth, its seat split by heat. A spanner lies near the second engine, held in place by a thin net of roots. Part of an enamel warning remains bolted beside the door, though condensation has erased its final line. These are maintenance objects, not signs of habitation. Nothing suggests that anyone is expected back.

There is no day here, and no convincing night. Time is measured by warm pipes cooling, by dry surfaces becoming briefly wet, by the almost imperceptible reach of a root mat toward the next anchor bolt. The groves keep a leaf-shaped record of which circuits still function. If an engine finally stops, its garden will not vanish in a dramatic collapse. It will fold, darken, and persist for years while its edges slowly mineralize.

Most of the Ninth Sump remains empty: sharp young basalt, broad intervals of floor, darkness between three small mechanical climates. The engines condense water for a purpose that no longer matters. The water feeds growth nothing planted. The growth, in turn, records the machines’ slow continuation.

Somewhere beneath a pipe, a drop gathers.

Much later, it falls.
