## Nine-Spout Hollow

You hear Nine-Spout Hollow before you see it. The path climbs through wet mountain forest, narrow between roots and dripping banks, and for a while there is only your breathing in the thin air and the occasional tick of water from a leaf. Then the land turns, the sound opens, and the whole hollow arrives at once: water in many registers, wings and insect-stridulation, frogs, axes, wooden clappers, market voices, and the soft, continuous impact of things falling onto other things—rain onto leaves, leaves onto roofs, nuts onto boardwalks, clods of saturated moss into pools.

Only afterward do the noises separate into a place.

The hollow is small, a high mountain pocket a few hundred paces across. An old rock wall encloses its upper side, rounded and fluted by long weathering rather than freshly broken. Forested shoulders close around it. Below the settlement, the ground levels into a marsh of sedge, moss, and dark pools, then ends at a lip where the overflow leaves in several white threads. On clear mornings, the country beyond that lip is visible far below: slopes upon slopes, already blue with distance. More often, cloud moves against the opening and briefly makes the hollow feel like the entire inhabited world.

The air is cool even when the light is strong. Daylight enters as bright, softened gray, with blue sky sometimes showing between clouds. There is no deep jungle darkness here; the greens are distinct, almost sharp, and wet stone flashes silver. But dampness gets into cuffs and hair, shade has a cold edge, and at dawn breath hangs for a moment. Kitchen smoke is therefore welcome. It drifts through the settlement with the smells of wet peat, crushed leaves, mushrooms, soot, and grain being toasted dry.

Almost no surface remains merely mineral. Soil appears only where a foot has just slipped or a channel has been cleaned. Elsewhere the ground is root, fallen wood, leaf mat, moss, and the dark fibrous remains of earlier generations of the same. Ferns occupy the ledges; low shrubs seal the sunny gaps; liverworts and algae make the seepage lines slick. Trunks carry their own crowded vertical gardens of moss, creepers, seedlings, and rosetted plants holding small cups of water. Decay is not hidden beneath the growth. It is part of its structure. A fallen limb may be soft enough to break by hand at one end while supporting a row of vigorous young trees at the other.

The town occupies this growth without ever having properly cleared it.

Its houses stand close together on stone footings and old terraces, some above others, connected by stairs, roofed passages, and lashed timber walks. There are fewer than a hundred roofs, but the settlement feels urban rather than scattered: shared drains, party walls, communal basins, covered work yards, storehouses, a market cramped into the only broad level space. From above, much of it would read as a slightly more orderly portion of canopy. From within, every gap is used. Baskets hang under eaves. Fiber dries on racks above the waterways. Narrow bridges pass between upper rooms. A person washing pots can speak without leaning out to someone mending a roof across the lane.

The materials are stone, hand-adzed timber, clay, thatch, bark fiber, and pegs. Metal is scarce enough to be concentrated where it does the most work: knife edges, adzes, awls, a few hinges and hooks. The rest is fitted, bound, wedged, and replaceable. Age shows less in the style of the buildings than in their surfaces. Step edges are rounded. Door posts are polished at hand height. Walkways have sunk slightly where generations of feet have crossed them, then been packed with fresh gravel and fiber. Roofs darken, green over, and begin to support ferns almost as soon as they are laid.

Water gives the town its plan.

Along the back wall, seepage gathers in nine old catchments. Their carved stone mouths—the nine spouts—pour into a high channel. Some run steadily; others strengthen after rain or dwindle in the cooler, drier weeks. From that first channel, water is led through drinking basins, then cooking yards, washing places, fiber-soaking troughs, and finally into the lanes and marsh. The order is remembered and enforced. No one washes below the intake and complains about the taste upstream.

Every street is consequently also a piece of plumbing. Some runnels pass beneath the floorboards in stone-lined beds; others are open and shallow enough to reflect the roofs. Children set bark boats in them. Frogs sit in the quiet eddies. Fine roots enter through joints and have to be teased out. Where the slope steepens, little drops and wooden launders turn the water into a bright clatter. Where it flattens, the flow spreads among reeds and disappears into ground that shakes faintly underfoot.

The inhabitants listen to this system more than they inspect it. A change in pitch can mean a blocked grate, an overflowing basin, or a walkway beginning to lift. A rapid knocking on a hollow beam summons the nearest households to clear a channel. During heavy rain, people do not wait for someone in authority to arrive; they appear with hooked poles, spare pegs, and armfuls of woven matting. The work is frequent, ordinary, and often wet enough to be accompanied by jokes.

The animals are less easy to organize.

Birds occupy the roof ridges and gutters, strip insects from the drying racks, and drop seeds into places where seeds are least wanted. Small iridescent hummingbirds work the flowers along the sunny side of the market, fiercely defending patches scarcely wider than a doorway. Wrens and heavier forest birds move through the lower growth. At a sudden shadow, whole layers of the canopy can go quiet at once, then resume by degrees.

Frogs use every water store that is not covered. Their calls begin separately and gather into an astonishing evening volume. Thumbnail-sized ones cling beneath leaves; larger ones sit in the marsh pools with only their eyes above water. Salamanders occupy the cold rotting logs. Snails leave silver writing on steps and lower walls. Beetles crowd fallen fruit, caterpillars reduce fresh leaves to lace, and winged insects rise from the marsh in drifting clouds whenever the sun returns. Dragonflies hold the open lanes. Bats sleep beneath broad eaves and under the densest roof growth.

Nothing about this abundance is tame. It is simply close, persistent, and difficult to exclude. Grain is kept in stoppered clay vessels above the floor. Food is covered. Screens of woven fiber darken some windows. Gutters must be cleaned around nests, and newly cut poles are inspected for borers before use. A market negotiation may pause while a flock passes overhead or while everyone waits for a particularly loud frog to finish. People speak briskly and often loudly; a soft voice does not travel far in the hollow.

The apparent confusion increases when weather crosses the rim.

A shower may arrive first as a change in the insect noise and a cold movement of air. Leaves turn and knock together. Then the upper canopy begins to roar, though the ground is still nearly dry. A moment later the water comes through in delayed cascades, pouring from one layer of foliage to the next. Every roof adds its own runoff. The marsh dimples all over. Market awnings sag and are lifted with poles; baskets are stacked; doors are shut against wind-blown spray.

The channels rise quickly. Leaves and twigs raft against a grate, water backs into a lane, and three people clear it while a fourth cuts a new temporary notch with a hand tool. Elsewhere, a small peat bank slumps, not in a catastrophe but with a heavy sigh, releasing brown water and the smell of ancient vegetation. Birds retreat. Frogs stop. For several minutes the loudest things are water and human instruction.

Then the shower passes.

Sunlight returns in ordinary, unceremonious brightness. Droplets continue to fall long after the rain has stopped. The birds resume out of sequence, the insects follow, and the frogs seem to answer the receding water. Blockages are hooked out. Roofs are retied. The market opens again before the boards beneath it have dried. Nothing has been transformed, yet dozens of small things have changed position.

This is how the hollow remains old without becoming still. Its large forms are stable: the wall, the terraces, the nine catchments, the marsh, the overflow at the lip. Roots and peat absorb much of the water’s force, and the channels divide the rest. Erosion here mostly polishes, darkens, undercuts by fractions. Destruction is usually local enough to be reached with hands. So is construction.

A tree falls and makes a brief field of light; within seasons, the gap is crowded. A house rots and is replaced on the same footings. A path shifts a pace sideways around a root that has thickened. The town cannot expand easily, so it densifies, repairs, and reuses. Its inhabitants preserve courses and levels more faithfully than objects. A basket may last a season, a walkway several years, a carved spout many lifetimes.

At the lower boundary, where the last boardwalk gives way to marsh and the marsh to open falling water, the whole settlement remains audible behind you. It does not sound like a town imposed on a forest, nor like wilderness reclaiming a ruin. It sounds like a crowded basin continuously sorting itself out.

Nothing here is finished, but very little is new. The hollow endures by being endlessly returned to course.
