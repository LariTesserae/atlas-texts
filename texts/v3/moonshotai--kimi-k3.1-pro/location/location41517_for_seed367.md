## The Dry Court

The plateau is high, but nothing on it is tall. Its mountains have been worn to long, stony swells; its valleys have filled with their own debris. Even the wind seems to have run out of large things to move. Dust travels, fine gravel ticks a few centimeters downhill, and the rest of the land holds its shape.

An unnamed city lies among these swells, indistinguishable from them until one notices that some of the shadows are rectangular. Thousands of cell-like rooms crowd together along narrow lanes. Stairs climb to roofs that no longer exist. Clay conduits vanish into walls and reappear as nests. Everything was made by hand: stone pecked with stone, timber drilled with bone, cords twisted from fiber, vessels dried and fired in small heaps of fuel. There are a few copper pins, green and brittle, but no sign of machinery or any ambition toward it.

Near the city’s center, behind three turns of a lane and beneath a fallen lintel, is a court nine paces long and five wide. From above it appears only as a black seam between roofs. A person must stoop to enter. Inside, the desert light becomes a dim, brown-gold atmosphere, crossed at midday by one fierce blade of sun. The air is dry enough to tighten the lips, yet warm even before dawn. High elevation brings no chill here: dark stone stores the day, and the enclosing walls prevent the night wind from carrying it away.

There is no water in the court. The basin beside the eastern wall contains dust and beetle shells. The clay pipes are hollow all the way through. No stain darkens a seam; no corner smells of damp. What little moisture life possesses is kept behind wax, shell, skin, and rind. It passes from leaf to insect, insect to lizard, lizard to bird, without ever becoming a visible feature of the place.

And yet the court is densely grown.

Its lushness is not tender. Nothing droops, and there are no broad, soft leaves. The lower walls carry thick mats of foliage, each leaf small and overlapping like a green scale. Jointed vines climb through the old mortar, their stems fleshy but armored, their surfaces gray with wax. A shrub occupies the northern corner in a confusion of narrow trunks, spines, and bottle-green leaf clusters. Beneath it, low plants form cushions so tightly packed that fallen seeds remain on top of them, unable to reach the ground.

Seen through the entrance, the growth looks almost cool. Touching it corrects the impression. Leaves are stiff and warm. Stems do not ooze when broken; their sap closes quickly into a resinous skin. Roots occupy dust-filled cracks and old pockets of guano, extending with extreme slowness. Some of the thicker vines may be older than the latest rebuilding of the walls around them. Their abundance has been purchased by patience and by wasting almost nothing.

Every human construction has acquired another use. A lamp niche holds a bee colony. Sockets left by vanished roof beams are apartments for wrens. A line of ventilation holes has become a vertical highway for ants, while the shallow stair leading nowhere is territory disputed by two kinds of gecko. At dusk, small cream-colored flowers open along the vines. They smell faintly of pepper and dry honey, and moths arrive so quickly that the blossoms seem to have summoned them out of the stone.

By numbers, this little room may be the most crowded place on the plateau.

There are ants in the paving, ants under the vines, and ants moving along the ceiling with seeds held sideways in their jaws. Beetles work through fallen leaves. Tiny wasps inspect beetle burrows. Spiders have strung silk in places where no straight line remains visible, so that a wall appears empty until the light shifts and reveals several generations of webs. Scale insects gather along the tender joints of plants; mites gather on the scale insects; something smaller still presumably profits from the mites.

A mantis waits among the flower stems, colored exactly like a dry leaf. Geckos flatten themselves against warm masonry. A snake no thicker than a thumb lives behind a displaced paving stone and emerges only after the court has darkened. Mice travel beneath the plant cushions, their presence indicated by a continuous, delicate rearrangement of seeds. Wrens occupy the wall cavities by day. After sunset, bats fold themselves into the vault and then pour upward through the opening in numbers that seem impossible for the space to contain.

Nothing here is monstrous. The effect comes from the absence of emptiness.

The court is loud in the same dry, intricate way. Cicadas fasten themselves to the shrub and produce a trill that reverberates between the close walls. Bees contribute a lower hum. Wings knock against leaves. Seedpods split with small, hard cracks. Claws scratch overhead; something chews inside a stem; a wren scolds from a niche too small to reveal it. At the warmest hour, a visitor would have to raise their voice to be heard across nine paces.

The noise is never uniform. When a hunting bird crosses the strip of sky above, silence enters in patches. First the cicadas stop, then the bees alter their flight, then the wrens vanish. The ants continue, because the disaster is not yet relevant to them. Minutes later the sound returns, not all at once but section by section, as though the court were cautiously remembering itself.

Its activity changes by the hour. Dawn belongs to returning moths, mice finishing their journeys, and geckos seeking the last warm hunting places before concealment. Noon belongs to insects and vibrating stone. Dusk is an exchange of shifts: petals opening, bats unfolding, wrens complaining from inside the walls. At night the masonry ticks as it slowly releases heat, and predators move through a garden that appears motionless until one watches a single leaf closely.

Once a year, when the plateau wind turns, dust pours over the city for several days. The court’s opening admits only a fine bronze suspension. Plants fold their leaves. Ants close passages with pellets. Spiders abandon the upper walls and rebuild lower down. Afterward every surface has been powdered, every trail interrupted, every territory made faintly negotiable again. Within hours, feet and mandibles have redrawn the map.

The old builders’ enduring achievement was not shelter from the desert, but a pocket where its rules could be made extremely small. Every hole eventually becomes a dwelling. Every ledge becomes a road. Shade is wealth. Height is safety until something learns to climb. Dead material does not accumulate for long; it is shredded, carried, buried, eaten, or incorporated into a wall.

Outside, the plateau continues its nearly completed journey toward flatness. The human city has settled into its final form. But inside the hidden court, wings strike wings, a gecko misses by the width of a grain of sand, roots enter another fraction of a crack, and the warm stone fills again with sound. The ancient place is not an oasis and not quite a ruin. It is a very small, crowded city of survival, still urgently unfinished.
