## The High Catchment

The High Catchment lies on a tableland so broad that its edges feel incidental. It is elevated country, but not alpine: no sharp peaks, only long stony swells, shallow basins, and escarpments whose faces have weathered into rounded steps. Beyond those steps, lower country disappears into distance. Within the plateau, the land continues for weeks of travel, and the city continues with it—not as an unbroken carpet of buildings, but as district after district joined by paved grades, embanked reservoirs, and open stretches of catchment. There is no single skyline and no discoverable center.

The districts themselves are dense and low. Fitted limestone walls stand close together, separated by narrow lanes, roofed passages, and small courts that seem almost enclosed by the sky. Stairs climb the swells; on the slopes, whole streets become staircases, with doorways opening at different heights and channels running beside them. The larger structures are not towers but cistern halls, covered reservoirs, broad roofed courts, and long retaining terraces. Many buildings still carry their original slab roofs. Others have opened inward, leaving rooms that hold rain for a few days and then slowly dry.

Everything permanent is made from the plateau: blue-gray limestone, darker stone where the paving needed to resist wear, packed clay beneath the deepest pools. Dry surfaces have faded to ash and bone. Wet ones turn nearly black. If anything wooden, woven, or metallic once furnished the city, it is gone. There are no machines, no instruments, no movable mechanisms. The only surviving infrastructure is mass, opening, and slope. The only moving part is water.

Rain is the city’s organizing fact. It arrives often, though not always heavily: long spells of fine rain, brief hard showers crossing a single district, water condensing on sheltered stone even when nothing falls from the sky. Roofs feed gutters; gutters feed courtyard basins; the basins overflow into street channels. From there, water descends through a sequence of cisterns and terraces until it reaches one of the broad, shallow meres in the lower ground. Seepage shines in wall joints. Drops gather beneath stone spouts. After wet weather, even apparently dry lanes exhale a cold dampness, and thin water stands in the worn hollows of steps.

Yet the High Catchment is not drowned. Most of its old grades still work. Stormwater spreads, pauses, and moves on; pools rise against their stone rims and then subside over days. In the lowest basins the water table remains close enough to the surface that a footstep can darken the dust. Elsewhere, wind dries exposed paving quickly, leaving only a black seam along the downhill wall. The city is wet in layers rather than uniformly soaked: running water, standing water, damp stone, dry stone, and stone that merely remembers damp.

Its age shows without making it look close to collapse. Corners are rounded, thresholds cupped, and some channels have been cut deeper by centuries of flow. Elsewhere, mineral-rich seepage has done the opposite, sealing cracks and laying pale calcite over the edges of reservoirs. Collapsed walls have become low, stable banks of rubble. Silt has filled certain courts to the level of their doors, while adjoining courts remain clean. Erosion and deposition have reached a rough accommodation. Very little looks freshly broken, and nothing looks newly repaired.

The scale of the place gives it weather of its own. Moist air crossing the tableland cools, thickens, and returns as low cloud or rain. One district may lie under clear sunlight while another, beyond a long reservoir, is receiving a dark curtain of showers. Morning mist collects in the basins and withdraws uphill as the day warms. Along the outer escarpments, cloud sometimes rises just far enough to spill over the rim before thinning again.

The light itself is ordinary. Clear days bring a pale, generous sun and sharp shadows in the lanes; cloudy days reduce everything to soft gradations of wet gray. There is no perpetual gloom. After rain, sunlight can make the terraces dazzling, with each overflow appearing as a brief white thread. The air remains cool even then. At dawn, breath may show, and the deepest cisterns stay cold through summer. Winter brings thin ice to shaded pools and occasional dry snow to the higher swells, but seepage continues beneath it.

Vegetation never closes over the stone. Lichens occupy exposed faces in scattered, maplike patches. Fine grass grows where windblown dust has gathered in paving joints. Low, hard-leaved shrubs survive in the lee of walls, and narrow stands of rush mark only the most dependable pool margins. Moss occurs in seams and sheltered pockets rather than as a carpet. Even around the meres, open water and bare mineral shore predominate. The plateau has water enough for more life, but little depth of soil, and the cool season keeps growth brief.

What grows is nevertheless in motion. After rain, dormant grass darkens and shoots; shallow annual plants flower quickly in silted courts; rushes release seed into the same overflows that carry away their old stems. During dry intervals, the margins retreat, leaving brittle stems and pale waterlines. Wind moves dust into cracks. Birds and runoff move a few seeds out again. Nothing forms a lush succession, but the sparse cover is continually being lost, remade, and rearranged.

Animals are rare enough that each encounter feels singular. A snail may be found beneath the lip of a channel. A small dark amphibian may occupy one cistern while dozens of apparently identical cisterns remain empty. At long intervals, wading birds settle on a mere, work along its narrow belt of silt, and depart. A raptor sometimes follows the rising air at an escarpment. There are no herds in the open catchments, no audible insect clouds over the pools, and no population of scavengers large enough to give the abandoned streets a sense of occupation.

The quiet is correspondingly deep. It is not absolute silence, but a silence into which small sounds remain separate: one drop striking a cistern, a thin overflow combing through gravel, wind turning a corner without quite becoming a whistle. Steady rain makes a low hush across the roofs. A birdcall can carry farther than seems reasonable. On rare storm days, thunder rolls between districts and returns from distant escarpments, then leaves the local dripping unchanged.

For all its stillness, the High Catchment is never inactive. Cloud builds and unbuilds; rain darkens whole neighborhoods; pools rise and find their overflows. Water dissolves stone in one place and cements it in another. Freeze loosens a flake, wind removes dust, runoff lays it down again. Plants advance by the width of a seam and retreat by the length of a shore. The processes are ordinary, but there are so many of them, operating at so many different speeds, that no view remains exactly itself for long.

Its rules can be read directly from the ground. Every pool has an overflow. Every inhabited-looking court belongs to a catchment. Every exposed surface eventually dries, while every shaded joint eventually weeps. Nothing runs uphill, nothing needs winding or tending, and nothing important happens out of sight of gravity. The city endures because it was organized to admit weather and direct it, not to keep weather out.

At evening, from one of the outer terraces, there are no lights and no smoke—only ordinary sunlight lowering over wet roofs, dark channels, and successive districts fading into separate weather. The nearest overflow continues after the rain has stopped. Farther away, another cloud is just beginning to cross the plateau. The High Catchment seems neither alive in the manner of a settlement nor dead in the manner of a ruin. It has settled into a slower condition: stone keeping the grades, water keeping the seasons.
