## The High Drowning

The mountains were old before the water came. By then their ridges had slumped into broad backs, their valleys had filled with their own powder, and even the deepest gorges were little more than creases in a continental skin. The flood did not make them ancient. It merely removed the sky from above them.

Now warm water occupies the altitudes where weather used to be. There is no surface to break. Ascend, and the sea gradually separates into vast, slow drops; the drops become rain; the rain becomes a dim suspension too fine to feel against the skin. Descend, and that suspension closes around the body without a splash. What looks from a distance like a green-black cloud may be the uppermost reach of the ocean, and what looks like clear water may already be sky.

Every depth is also an altitude. One can swim for days above the ground and still be over a summit.

Below, the highlands continue beyond every horizon. Rounded summits the size of countries rise from immense plateaus, separated by drowned saddles and basins of fine gray silt. Occasionally an ancient fault exposes a scarp: a black wall fluted by age, its upper lip rounded, its base descending until it becomes only a darker pressure beneath the swimmer. But sharp relief is exceptional. Most of the land swells and subsides so gradually that its scale must be inferred from the forests changing upon it.

The stone is dark and iron-rich, weathered at the surface into ochre meal, pale green clay, and a mineral film that clouds the water at a touch. Boulders resemble knuckles or molars rather than fragments. There are no fresh edges. Even the silt seems rounded, each grain having traveled so long that it no longer belongs to any particular mountain.

The water tastes faintly of iron and leaf tannin. In sheltered basins it is almost sweet. Its warmth varies, but never disappears; there is no chill, only lesser degrees of warmth. Slow currents move at the temperature of skin. Others pass just warmly enough to make the buried stone seem, by contrast, cooler and more solid than it is.

Where the stone can hold a root, forests stand.

They are neither reefs nor quite kelp. The first growth is a close olive felt, so fine that whole hillsides appear upholstered. Above it rise thickets of jointed crimson stems, each bearing a single black-green leaf folded lengthwise. Then come the ribbon trees: flexible trunks, some centuries old, rising into cables and opening into crowns of translucent straps hundreds of metres long. Currents comb the crowns in one direction. When a current turns, the entire landscape seems to reconsider its shape.

Every trunk is furred with smaller plants. Every broad leaf carries a film, then a moss, then a scattering of seedlings no larger than dust. Roots cross roots until it becomes difficult to tell whether the forest is anchored to the mountain or the mountain is being held together by the forest. In the oldest stands, growth is stacked so closely that the dim light enters in layers and is used up before reaching the ground.

Not all the vegetation is attached. Torn mats and veils of seed-thread drift between the crowns. Some floating islands have circulated for so long that they possess soil of their own: a dark heart of trapped silt, dead leaves, and compacted roots. They pass over their parent forests like low clouds, shedding a constant, gentle debris.

There are open places. Clear lanes follow the warmest currents, and some basins hold only sparse pillows of growth on otherwise empty silt. But the edges of these openings are crowded beyond easy passage, and the water above them is seldom empty. Something is always suspended there—seed, leaf, root, dust, or a green veil too large to see all at once.

At its brightest, the light is the color of weak tea, though no sun is visible. It seems to arrive from everywhere above and to stop a short distance below. Far forests appear as silhouettes within silhouettes. With depth, color leaves before shape: crimson stems become gray, then black. Only the pale silt retains a little luminosity, as though remembering a different condition of the world.

Animals are rare enough that their passage changes a forest.

A translucent length of flesh, no thicker than a wrist, may move through the stems with the caution of something that has never needed to hurry. Far out in open water, a much larger shape sometimes reveals itself only by interrupting the light, and then takes hours to finish doing so. There are no schools, no bright congregations around carcasses, no animal chatter in the growth. Empty shells in the silt are more common than the creatures that made them.

Because so little is grazed, leaves live long. Forests thin through age, burial, and the slow leverage of their own crowns. A fallen leaf may take years to reach the ground. Some never do. They are lifted into weather, reduced to green fragments, and returned elsewhere as a stain in the rain.

Sound is almost absent. Water carries motion directly into the body: the pressure of a turning leaf wall, the faint tap of a seed case, a sub-audible strain when current pulls at old stone. Even rain arrives as a hush felt more than heard. A voice seems to remain beside the speaker rather than travel. Most of the silence is not an absence of possible noises, but the enormous distance between things capable of making them.

Yet the High Drowning is never still.

Its seas have weather large enough to ignore mountains. Gyres turn across entire plateaus, taking generations to complete a circuit. Between them stand fronts of differing warmth and salinity, visible as wavering walls. Fresh water falls through salt water in rain—not in droplets as air knows them, but in silver filaments and broad, clear columns that may descend for weeks.

When submarine rain crosses a forest, the leaves bow in sequence. That bow can travel farther than the storm itself can be seen. Silt lifts from the summits. Seed veils rise. Floating islands turn slowly over, exposing pale roots to the light. For a while, a mountain may have two shadows: one upon the ground and another suspended above it.

Other storms rotate instead of falling. A cylinder of rain thousands of kilometres across turns so slowly that its movement can be read only in the leaning of successive forests. At its centre, the water clears and the light becomes almost bright. Such clearings may last long enough for short-lived plants to flower, release their seeds, and vanish before the rain wall closes again.

These processes would be strange enough if distance were dependable.

It is not.

The world has been worn down in more than stone. Near roots, silt, and old walls, space keeps its ordinary proportions. In open water, and especially inside rain, older arrangements assert themselves. A current follows a watershed that no longer exists and delivers the swimmer to its source. A ridge remains adjacent to the cloud that once fed it, even after the cloud has wandered elsewhere. Two forests visible in the same direction may be reached only by swimming away from one another.

None of this flickers. There are no sudden fractures, no restless chaos. The folds are stable, immense, and slow. They have weather. They accumulate silt. Roots grow through them. A ribbon tree whose trunk stands in one basin may spread its crown over another, though no continuous length of wood appears to connect the two. Its leaves fall in both places, and this is how the forest knows it is one tree.

The sea has not broken reality recently. It has simply had time to rub through it.

The scattered works of hands obey the same patience. There are no cities and almost no concentrations of artifacts. A stair of nine rounded treads crosses a saddle and ends in open water. A basin, no wider than outstretched arms, holds water clearer than the sea around it. On one summit stands a doorway of dark stone, its lintel worn to the curve of a lip; it opens onto the same view on both sides, though not always at the same depth. Elsewhere, three stones have been set in a line too deliberate to be natural. Moss has made them nearly identical to every other stone.

There is no metal, no mechanism, no object with a moving part. The marks on the worked stone are faint parallel scars, more like the memory of touch than writing. Nothing fits neatly into a hand. Whatever was made here was made sparingly, then allowed to become landscape. The clearest structures often stand where distances fold, but whether their builders chose those places, or whether the places gradually gathered around the structures, is not something the water explains.

At its dimmest, the High Drowning does not darken all at once. Light settles out of it. First the drifting islands vanish, though their shadows remain for a while upon the canopy. Then distant rain becomes indistinguishable from distant mountains. The silt keeps its pallor longest, so the ground seems to hover beneath a world with no ceiling.

A warm front may be crossing a million square kilometres. Forests may be flowering in the clear eye of a storm. Somewhere a transparent animal may be taking a single leaf from a plant that has held it for centuries. Stone continues to give itself away, grain by grain. Rain continues to form inside the sea.

This is not a ruined world waiting for its waters to recede. The drowning is its settled condition, and even its impossibilities are old. But settled does not mean finished. In the silence, with no shore above and no certain distance ahead, the high water goes on exchanging mountain for forest, forest for rain, and rain for places that were never separate to begin with.
