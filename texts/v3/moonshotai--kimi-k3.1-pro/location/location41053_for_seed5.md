## The Kept Hollow

On a low plain of old river gravel, where the channels have been dry so long that their bends survive only as changes in the color of the grass, there is a seam of deeper green. The plain around it is cold, open, and bright. Bleached stems stand among rounded stones; occasional rain darkens the surface and is gone by the next morning. There are no hills from which to notice the hollow, no cliff face, no obvious mouth. From ten paces away it looks like a patch of moss thriving inexplicably among the gravel.

A gap between two slabs gives entry. Three worn steps descend, and the air changes at once: not warmer, as sheltered air usually is, but a degree colder, with a dampness felt first in the nose and then at the wrists. The hollow is only a little deeper than a person is tall. Its entire floor could be crossed in forty paces.

Within it stands a town.

Not a miniature town, and not quite a ruined one. It is a town reduced in extent but not in anatomy. Its principal lane is twelve paces long. Its market court is the size of a small room. Stairs climb to roof terraces, roof terraces become the floors of further chambers, and covered passages run beneath them to granary niches, washing stones, cisterns, and pens long since converted by animals into nests. Retaining walls hold back the gravel hillside. Gutters feed settling basins; settling basins overflow into covered drains. Every urban necessity is present, compressed until the distinctions between house, street, wall, and infrastructure become matters of use rather than form.

The roofs touch. Some have been laid directly against one another, their edges softened by age until the join resembles a seam in hide. Doorways open onto stairs so narrow that shoulders brush both sides. There are no yards, no vacant lots, no broad defensive margins. Even the open court is occupied by a single standing stone, a shallow basin, three roosting ledges, and a curtain of ferns hanging from the balcony above. Yet the density is not disorderly. Each addition seems to have found the one space that previous additions had left for it.

The walls are built from river pebbles bedded in gray clay. Larger stones were split with hammerstones rather than cut; their fresh faces have long since weathered to the same dull ivory as the rest. Beams are short, dark pieces of ancient wood, pegged and lashed rather than nailed. Clay pipes, each no wider than a wrist and bearing the longitudinal marks of fingers, disappear into masonry and emerge where water is needed. Baskets, grinding stones, bone awls, and shallow clay lamps occupy wall recesses. Nothing requires a machine, a draft animal, or a block too heavy for two people to carry.

Age has not reduced the place so much as fitted it together. Thresholds are scooped shallowly at their centers. The corners of steps are round. Mineral deposits glaze the insides of drains, and roots run beside the pipes instead of breaking them. Where a root must once have lifted a wall, the wall has already settled into its new inclination and stopped. There are layers upon layers of construction, but no visible catastrophe separating them. Erosion here no longer looks like destruction. It looks like the final, very slow stage of craftsmanship.

The hollow is extraordinarily bright. Its pale stone accounts for some of this, and the open seam above for more, but light also reaches places where it should not: beneath roof slabs, inside covered drains, at the backs of chambers whose doors face away from the sky. It is a white, even illumination without glitter. Shadows are shallow and tinged gray-green. Nothing glares, and nothing is truly dark.

The light gives no warmth. The air remains just above freezing, cold enough for breath to show and for exposed skin to ache after a while. Moist stone stays cold beneath the hand. And the brightness does not noticeably change. Shadows do not lengthen across the court; the white walls neither gold nor dim. The hollow seems less to be illuminated at a particular hour than to possess brightness as one of its materials.

For all its greenness, there is very little open water. No stream passes through. Rain seldom enters directly. At the rear wall, an old seam in the river gravel exudes moisture without producing a visible spring: the clay there is simply darker, and cold to the touch. Elsewhere, condensation gathers on the undersides of stone eaves and in the bends of pipes. Drops collect so slowly that they seem part of the fittings.

The town’s largest reservoir is a covered cistern no bigger than a bathing tub. Its water is black, clear, and ordinarily without a ripple. Smaller reserves occupy hollowed stones, leaf axils, and a line of thumb-sized cups mortared into one wall. The entire visible water supply could probably be carried away in a few buckets. Nevertheless, nothing appears parched. Dampness is distributed so finely, and used so completely, that the hollow feels wetter than it is.

Moss forms the first green layer, deep olive in the joints and almost yellow at its growing tips. Over it lie rosettes of waxy leaves, flat tongues of liverwort, and ferns whose translucent stems rarely rise higher than a finger. Tiny white flowers grow in the mortar above doorways. Roofs carry dense cushions of vegetation with no soil beneath them deeper than a hand. Some walls appear upholstered; others look as though a green material has been poured over them and allowed to set.

The lushness has exact boundaries. Ferns occupy vertical joints but not the treads of stairs. Moss stops at the rims of commonly used basins. A narrow band of polished stone remains clear through even the most overgrown passage. Nothing looks trimmed. Plants, animals, and masonry have reached divisions so old that the negotiations responsible for them are no longer visible.

Kneeling reveals the animals. Lentil-sized snails graze the ferns, their shells the color of wet slate. Black beetles occupy the warmest available refuge—the interior of a sunless wall recess—and springtails crowd the damp dust beneath leaves. Pale, fur-bodied moths rest under the eaves. Mouse-sized creatures with dense gray coats move between the grain niches along routes worn almost imperceptibly into the stone. In the cistern, a few finger-length fish hang above a layer of delicate white crustaceans.

There are many lives, but no commotion. No flock rises, no colony boils from a crevice, no predator crashes through the growth. A beetle crosses a leaf. A fish turns. One of the gray-furred animals sits at a doorway and cleans its face. The moss is grazed without becoming thinner; fallen fronds vanish into small pockets of dark matter without becoming litter. The ecology has the completeness of a sealed vessel, though the hollow is open to the sky.

Only after some time does the silence become unmistakable.

It is not the quiet of an uninhabited street. Boots still press grit against the steps, but there is no scrape. Cloth moves without rustling. A spoken word can be felt in the throat and heard nowhere. Even breathing, though visible in the cold, makes no sound. The noises do not fade or seem swallowed by the moss; they simply fail to occur. Stepping back onto the plain restores the crunch of gravel with such force that the ordinary world briefly seems violent.

The silence belongs to the same condition as the fixed light and the settled masonry. Movement is possible here, but accumulation scarcely is. Ripples subside, displaced moss rises again, and a drip sliding down a pipe leaves no spreading stain. New growth and decay appear to balance before either can alter the whole. A visitor cannot easily determine whether the hollow is literally unchanging or merely so perfectly equilibrated that change has become indistinguishable from restoration.

Nothing in it looks abandoned, and nothing looks recently maintained. Nothing is waiting to be repaired, reclaimed, or completed.

From above, once the entrance has closed behind you, there is only the dark green seam among the gravel. Beneath it remains an entire cold, luminous ward of pebble walls and black water, packed with ferns and small breathing animals: not a city paused in the middle of its life, but a place whose life has arrived at a form so exact that it no longer needs to make a sound.
