## The High Loom

The High Loom occupies what remains of a mountain range. The range has not collapsed; it has been patiently rubbed down. Its summits are now broad swells on a plateau so high that the air stays cool even under a clear sun, and its valleys have become shallow green basins with no sharp bottoms. From the outer rim, the country extends for a day’s travel in every direction: rounded ridges, long shelves, isolated chimney stacks, the straight glint of transmission lines. There are no peaks and few exposed cliffs. Even height seems to have acquired soft edges.

At a distance, the plateau resembles moorland. Underfoot, it proves to be something else.

There is no soil at the surface. The ground is a continuous, resilient body of roots, rhizomes, mosses, and old stems, layered so deeply that a cut bank reads like the section through a tree. The upper handspan is springy and green. Beneath it lies brown fiber, then black compressed growth, then thin amber seams where whole generations of woodland have been pressed into something resembling mineral. Still deeper, the roots have passed through and around the bones of the old mountains. Stone remains down there, but it no longer determines the shape of things.

Nothing exposed stays exposed. A road cut buds along its margins. An abandoned iron beam develops a seam of small, waxy leaves where rain sits in a bolt hole. Even the dead remain plant: pale thread-stems enter old wood and take it apart from within, returning it slowly to the mat. Growth, decay, and construction are different tempos of the same process.

Yet the High Loom is not crowded. Its vegetation is total, but its presences are sparse. Most of the living ground lies low, a silver-green pelt hardly reaching the knee. Above it, great spaces open. Every few kilometers stands a group of column trees with ribbed trunks and thin, perforated crowns. Their branches are wide enough to interrupt weather but not enough to make shade forest. Between them one can watch rain approaching for half an hour, or see a single factory complex isolated against the sky. The emptiness is not an absence of plants. It is the space the plants have learned to leave.

Water, likewise, is present without ever quite gathering. There are no lakes and no dependable rivers. Rain comes in separated cells, sometimes wetting one district and leaving the next coated only in dust-pale pollen. At dawn, condensation beads on the column trees and on the long exterior pipes of the mills. Hidden gutters carry it into cisterns. After a shower, brief streams thread through the root-mat, shining for an hour before being drawn under. One can walk all day without seeing standing water and never be out of earshot of dripping.

The old rivers survive mainly as misunderstandings in the infrastructure. Their courses appear as shallow troughs of taller growth. Bridges still cross them, though nothing runs beneath. Some of the bridges have been lengthened repeatedly, their newer spans resting on ground that was not there when the first abutments were built.

The city is distributed across this country rather than concentrated in it. Seen from a high swell, it looks less like a mass than a diagram: a cluster of roofs around a condensation mill, a freight yard several kilometers away, three stacks on a distant ridge, all joined by dark causeways and paired copper lines. Within each cluster, however, the place is unmistakably urban. There are curbs, alleys, covered markets, workers’ stairs, retaining walls, substations, loading bays, and upper storeys connected by iron bridges. Streets narrow into courts; courts open suddenly onto enormous empty views.

The construction is layered. The oldest walls were grown rather than raised: trunks guided into arches, branches knitted into vaulted roofs, doorways formed where growth was repeatedly turned aside. Later districts were assembled from compressed fiber board and a hard, translucent plant resin used for windowpanes. The newest visible work is industrial—riveted dynamo halls, gantries, iron condenser towers, ceramic insulators, pipes wrapped against the cold. Roots have entered all of it. In some warehouses the floor is the original living foundation; in others, a whole earlier street lies compressed below the present one, its door lintels just visible in drainage cuts.

The mills produce water, structural fiber, electric current, and—whether intentionally or not—agreement.

Wind dynamos stand on the high ridges. Elsewhere, heavy engines burn dry bricks cut from the deep, dead root-mat, their flywheels turning with slow, mechanical patience. Fiber presses take the endless surface growth and roll it into matting, cable sheathing, roof sheet, and replacement roadbed. Condensation plants tick and hiss as they strip moisture from the passing air. Nothing is harvested quickly enough to make a lasting clearing. Clippings root where they fall. Vines with a sensitivity to electrical current creep along transmission routes and have to be cleared before they bridge the lines.

Under ordinary daylight, none of this looks especially fantastical. The sun is white, the sky a normal high blue or an unremarkable grey. The colors are practical: olive growth, wet black bark, flaking red iron, pale green resin windows. Electric lamps burn inside mills and under arcades because those places are dark, not because the city is enchanted. The air smells of cut stems, cold moisture, hot oil, and occasionally ozone. A lit window is not necessarily evidence of an occupant.

The loudness arrives before any single machine can be identified. Wind booms through the perforated tree crowns. Rain, when it comes, strikes millions of stiff leaves with the sound of gravel being poured. Across the open ground, mill noise travels astonishingly well: the low labor of flywheels, the hard recurrent knock of presses, steam valves opening, cables singing between poles. Separate districts fall in and out of rhythm. At times the whole plateau seems to be operating one immense, badly assembled engine. At others, a single nearby drip becomes audible inside the roar.

There are very few animals to add to it. Most days, none are visible. A pale moth may appear alone at a warm dynamo vent. Occasionally a long-legged grey browser stands at the far edge of a basin, so still that it resembles another stripped trunk. Its call, if that is what the sound is, resembles a stone dropped down a wooden stair. There are no background choruses of birds or insects. A movement in the vegetation is therefore more startling here than a factory starting up.

The living ground has its own motions. It tightens slightly in the cold and slackens after rain. Roads develop slow humps; flexible joints in the pipework take them up. The column trees incline their crowns toward damp air, and their condensed runoff wakes bands of smaller plants below. Root-pressure rises and falls through the day, audible in the oldest walls as creaking that does not correspond to the wind. The city’s activity is not imposed on a static wilderness. It is one more process among several, and not always the fastest.

Then the distances begin to misbehave.

This usually happens in full light, without mist, flickering, or any visible wound in the world. A chimney seen at the end of a street is also visible beyond the next ridge, though the ridge lies in the opposite direction. A milepost gives two distances to the same mill, and both prove correct: one on the outward journey, one on return. Condensation gathers along the invisible course of a pipe removed decades ago, hanging in the air as a silver dotted line. A courtyard in one district contains a door into a courtyard elsewhere; rain may be falling in the second while the first remains dry.

None of these events looks abrupt. The High Loom does not flicker between possibilities. It slides among them smoothly, as though the distinctions themselves have been eroded.

Erosion has not stopped at the mountains. Here and there, near and far have been worn into each other. Before and after have lost their sharp divide. The boundary between an object and its surroundings is usually serviceable, but no longer absolute. This is not a new catastrophe. It has the patience and completeness of the landscape’s other ancient processes.

Near a working governor house, relations are more dependable. Every district has one: a hall of flywheels, cam shafts, resonant pipes, and electrical regulators, officially responsible for keeping the industrial grid in step. Within reach of its beat, switches generally start the motors they are wired to, streets preserve their order, and a bridge continues to connect its two visible ends. When the load sheds or the rhythm slips, objects rarely disappear. Their connections loosen. A stair serves two buildings. A puddle reflects a roof across the valley. Footprints appear ahead of the person making them, already filling with water.

The plants manage this better than the machines. Their roots simply occupy alternatives. An old column tree may be hollow on one side and unnaturally young on the other; its crown can cast shadows into two courtyards that do not otherwise share a location. The city has learned to imitate that redundancy. Important pipes loop. Foundations are multiplied. Maps record reliable sequences of approach rather than fixed shapes, and some doors are labeled not with where they lead, but with the order in which they should be tried.

Whether the governor houses actually hold local reality together is uncertain. They may only make changes easier to notice. A steady beat gives the ear something against which to measure a missing sound; a regular machine gives the eye somewhere to stand while the rest of the country rearranges its relationships.

Travel is therefore a matter of infrastructure as much as direction. The root-paved causeways are safer than the open mat, not because they are straighter, but because their drains, markers, and power lines continually reassert that they are roads. Freight still moves. Condensers still fill cisterns. The fiber presses still receive their loads. Maintenance continues without any sense that a final, repaired condition is approaching.

Toward evening, a rain cell may cross one outlying district while the rest of the plateau stays dry. Its roofs darken; its gutters roar; vapor briefly surrounds the condenser stacks. Beyond the rain, resin windows take the low sun with a dull amber shine. Lamps appear in empty arcades. The mills answer one another across kilometers of low, living ground.

Sometimes, as the rhythms separate, the whole city seems for a moment to stand in one place: every chimney behind the nearest chimney, every road entering the same yard, the distant plateau rim visible through the wall beside you. Then a governor catches, a press strikes, and the region opens out again—not necessarily into precisely the arrangement it had before.

The High Loom does not feel as though it is holding itself together at the last possible moment. It has been coming apart for so long that coming apart has become one of its stable features. Moisture condenses, roots take hold, old streets sink into fiber, and the engines continue their loud work across the green distances: cool, sparsely inhabited, ancient beyond ruin, and almost entirely alive.
