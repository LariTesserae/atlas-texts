## The Seep-City

Deep beneath a slope of cold basalt, a closed cavity in the rock contains a city.

It is not a cavern with buildings scattered across its floor. The entire cavity has been built: walls, ceiling, basin, and the narrow void between. Small enough to hold in two cupped hands, it is crowded with hundreds of cell-like dwellings, stepped lanes, covered stairs, cisterns, bridges, and little vaulted chambers whose purposes are no longer evident. Streets climb the walls and continue overhead. Rooms open into the backs of other rooms. Some passages are conduits; some are roads; many appear to be both. There is no entrance, no outskirts, and no direction from which the city can be viewed whole.

To see it without destroying it, one would have to imagine the surrounding basalt made transparent.

The material is mostly the pale zeolite lining the pocket, cut directly from the walls and dry-fitted in minute pieces. The work is patient rather than advanced. Parallel scratches show where edges were worried into shape with flakes of harder stone. Channels were drilled by rotation and abrasion. A few joints are secured with slivers of native copper, hammered cold until they held. There are no pumps, gears, fired pipes, or machined parts. Water is persuaded to move by slope, pressure, and the narrowing of passages until capillary attraction can lift it. The city’s sophistication is organizational: thousands of small, exact decisions made with simple tools.

Long seepage has softened all of this. Lintel corners are round. Stair treads are shallowly dished. The finest bridges look less built than precipitated, their undersides glazed with thin skins of silica. Yet nothing appears on the verge of collapse. Erosion has not reduced the city to ruin; it has worked through every loose edge and unstable joint and arrived at a form it no longer substantially alters. Even the copper has stopped at a dark, fixed patina.

The whole place is wet, though almost none of it is flooded.

Water enters through a fracture too narrow to see and spreads into the ceiling pores. It gathers at seven points, falls, and is caught by stone cups. From there it travels through streets no wider than hairs, climbs certain walls in glistening threads, crosses doorways as taut transparent membranes, and returns at last to a shallow central cistern. At this scale, water does not always behave like rain. It beads, bridges, climbs, and wraps itself around corners. A silver meniscus can join two roofs. A drop can hang beneath an arch for a long time, swelling without appearing to grow heavier.

There is no clear division between plumbing and weather. The ceiling seeps are the rain; the weeping walls are the climate; the channels are rivers; the cistern, no broader than a fingernail, is the sea.

The air is saturated but clear, without mist or moving currents. It remains about as cold as water drawn from a deep well. There are no warmer pockets near the rock and no colder ones beside the pool. If the cavity could be smelled without being opened, it would smell of wet basalt, crushed limestone, faint metal, and the clean sourness of microbes—never of soil, growth, or decay.

A dim pewter radiance comes from the wetted zeolite. It is strongest along the rim of the cistern and weakest inside the rooms, where it becomes merely a paler shade of darkness. It does not flicker. Wet edges shine; dry recesses vanish. Nothing casts a shadow with a definite boundary. The occasional copper clamp is visible only as a warmth in the gray, like a remembered color rather than a seen one.

The sound is far too large for the space.

Water moving through such small passages ought to produce a faint ticking, little more. Instead, the city’s vaulted cells and narrow throats return every vibration to one another. The enclosing basalt lets almost none of it escape. Inside, there is a continuous, crowded roar: a low booming under hard mineral clicks, crossed by several high fluting notes. It can resemble surf, milling grain, distant hammers, or a market heard through a wall. The ear repeatedly tries to separate voices from it and repeatedly fails.

There are no quiet streets. Sound comes through the floors, along the water films, and out of the little empty rooms. A shout would not make a useful signal; a struck stone would add only another hard surface to the chord. Yet the loudness never swells into a climax or relaxes into silence. It has the texture of turbulence without turbulence’s breathing. Outside, a few handspans of rock away, there is no audible sign of it.

Nothing green lives here. No algae gather around the light, no roots enter the fractures, and no fungal fans spread across the masonry. The only living cover is an almost invisible bacterial film in the lowest basin, feeding on dissolved minerals and reducing the water’s faint iridescence to a gray stain where it grows thickest.

Animals are nearly absent. Sometimes a transparent crustacean, smaller than a grain of rice, emerges from beneath a bridge and works along the cistern rim. Its body shows the water behind it; only the deliberate movement of its legs makes it easy to distinguish from a wandering bubble. It grazes the film and retreats. There may be a few others deeper in the system, but never enough to give the city an inhabited feeling. Nothing sings. Nothing builds. Nothing dies in view.

The city has motion, but almost no history.

The amount entering through the upper fracture equals the amount leaving through the lower one. The cistern remains at the same level. Mineral dissolution rounds one face while deposition restores another, slowly enough that the balance appears exact. The bacterial film is grazed or starved back wherever it thickens. The temperature does not drift, the light does not weaken, and the sound holds its pitch. Drops fall, water runs, and the rare animal turns in its glassy body, but every process returns the whole to the same condition.

It is not a frozen instant. It is a frozen accounting.

No season reaches this depth. No succession of inhabitants changes the rooms. No street is gradually becoming a channel, and no channel is gradually becoming a ruin. The city cannot be said to be waiting, because waiting implies that something different is expected.

Its concealment is part of that equilibrium. To break open the basalt would admit a draft, release pressure, alter the seepage, and let the sound escape. The place can remain itself only while it stays enclosed. So the hill above gives no sign of it. Beneath the hill, in a space smaller than a handful, the crowded streets press close around their single full cistern, wet stone shines faintly in the cool dark, and the empty city continues roaring—neither awakening nor fading, with no audience and no need for one.
