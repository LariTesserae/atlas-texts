## Rill, the Seep Town

Rill stands on the only stony hummock in a lakebed that has just begun to drain. The lake did not empty in a flood. Its water simply found fissures in the limestone below and started going down, year by year, leaving behind gray silt, shallow tarns, and the black ribs of drowned trees. The exposed ground is still raw. Rain cuts little gullies with sheer, crumbling sides; stones split from the hummock remain angular; no bank has had time to soften into a slope. Beyond the town, the basin shines wetly under an ordinary daylight sky, its far edges marked by a sparse, uneven fringe of reeds.

The town itself is remarkably small. A slow walker could cross it in a few minutes, if the lanes were straight. They are not. Sixty-odd roofs crowd the hummock, pressed so closely together that, from the surrounding mud, Rill looks like a single many-backed animal crouching just above the water. Firm ground is too precious to leave empty. Houses share walls, stairs serve several doorways, and upper rooms project over lanes until only a narrow strip of sky remains. A gap beneath a stair becomes a jar niche. A dry ledge becomes a workbench. Even the space between two roof beams is used to hang cord, tools, or bundles of drying fiber.

The buildings are made from what the retreating lake has offered: split drowned timber, clay daub, woven laths, and footings of freshly broken limestone. Roof shingles are wooden, weighted with stones against the wind. Joints are pegged or lashed rather than nailed. The tools that maintain the town—stone mallets, antler picks, shell scrapers, wooden wedges—are simple, but the construction is not haphazard. Every roof, threshold, lane, and courtyard has been assigned a place in a common system of waterworks.

Water is never quite absent. It darkens the lower courses of walls, gathers beneath thresholds, and condenses in cold beads on the undersides of stone slabs. Clear springs emerge inside cellars and at the backs of lanes. After rain, thin brown water moves through the streets in carefully divided streams, disappearing beneath houses and reappearing several doors away. Fired-clay pipes pass through walls; hollowed timbers carry cleaner water overhead; flat stones can be lifted from the lanes to reveal narrow channels below. There is no true square. The nearest thing is a shallow settling court where silt is allowed to collect before the water goes on to the cisterns.

The seep water has one unusual property: it builds stone almost as readily as the rain-water cuts silt. When it reaches the air, it clouds faintly and leaves behind a pale mineral rind. The change is too slow to watch, but quick enough to govern daily labor. A channel neglected for a season narrows to a stony throat. Jar bases fuse to their shelves. Ropes left trailing in a spring basin stiffen, and an unused doorway gradually acquires a cupped white threshold. Fresh crust breaks with sharp, sugary edges. In some of the oldest passages, successive generations of drain covers have been sealed into a single rippled mass.

Rill is therefore not simply decaying in the damp. Parts of it are rotting, parts are being washed away, and parts are slowly being welded together. A floor may heave when a buried seep changes course; a wall may soften on one side while stone thickens on the other. Each morning, workers go through the town with hooked rods and short picks, testing channels and tapping new crust from places where water must remain free. The sound is brief and delicate: a knock, a brittle tick, the splash of fragments into a basket.

The air is cool even when the sun is out. At dawn, breath shows faintly, and shaded water never seems to lose the chill of the ground. Cloud and rain move through frequently, but there is no perpetual gloom. Between showers, plain sunlight reaches the roofs and throws soft shadows across the wet lanes. Pale mineral crust reflects light upward into rooms that would otherwise be dark. The town smells of wet limestone, clay, old wood, small hearth fires, and—near the outer drains—the faint sweetness of decaying reeds.

Vegetation has scarcely taken hold. The ground is too new, too frequently worked, and in places too heavily coated with mineral deposit. A few low rosettes grow in sheltered silt beyond the walls. Olive films appear in quiet basins, and clean rainwater sometimes supports a little dark moss between roof shingles. Rill’s one conspicuous patch of green is a shallow bed of bitter water-cress fed by a spring considered too clean for washing. It is tended in a common stone trough and harvested sparingly. Nothing in the town could be called a grove, garden quarter, or green verge.

Animals are nearly as scarce. Tiny transparent crustaceans live in the deepest spring pools and are carefully returned when the basins are cleaned. Black water beetles occasionally skate across the settling court. After rain, a few eel-like fish enter the outer channels from the remaining lake pools, where a gray heron sometimes waits for them. The heron’s visits are infrequent enough to interrupt work. Most days there is no birdsong, no buzzing swarm, no sound of livestock.

For all its density, Rill is quiet. Its crowdedness is something seen and felt—walls near the shoulders, roofs near the face, doorways opening directly onto other doorways—rather than something heard. Voices remain close to the walls that receive them. The usual sounds are seepage, a drip striking a jar, the soft scrape of a wooden rake, the suction of a foot leaving mud, and the wooden clack of a sluice being lowered. Rain subdues the smaller noises rather than overwhelming them.

The town changes its behavior with the weather. During and after rain, gates are opened so sediment-heavy water can scour the outer lanes. In quieter intervals, those gates are closed and the clear seeps are directed toward cisterns and settling pans. Knotted cords and notched clay gauges record which channels have been cleaned and which basins are rising. Several times a year, the settling ground opens a new seam or closes an old one. Then water appears where it has no right to be: beneath a sleeping platform, through a wall, or up the middle of a lane. The whole neighborhood’s plan must be adjusted around it.

This has produced Rill’s firmest custom. A house may belong to a household, but the water passing through it does not. No one may seal a spring merely because it inconveniences a floor, nor divert a channel without providing for those below. Some passages run under private rooms, and their access stones must remain liftable. A sealed seep will eventually lift the floor, burst a wall, or emerge in a neighbor’s cellar. Privacy ends where pressure begins.

From the last step at the edge of town, the contrast is stark: behind, packed timber, clay, stone, and quiet labor; ahead, the almost empty lakebed, glistening and sparsely colonized, with raw gullies branching toward pools. Rill looks less like a settlement completed on land than a dense, ingenious arrangement for inhabiting land that has not finished becoming itself. Then someone lifts a sluice. Water runs beneath three lanes, turns under a house, and enters the settling court. For a few moments, drains answer one another with soft ticking and dripping all across the hummock. Then the gates settle, and the town returns to its cool, mineral quiet.
