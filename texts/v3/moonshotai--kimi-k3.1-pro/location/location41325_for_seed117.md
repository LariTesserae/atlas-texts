## The Return Sump

At the lowest point of a gravel plain, the land dips by only a few metres into a hollow called the Return Sump. It is scarcely a hundred paces across. On a clear day, someone standing on the rim could take in every pool, pipe, and broken wall at once; but clear days rarely reach the bottom. Cool air drains into the depression and stays there. The seep water, though cold to the touch, is slightly warmer than the air, and mist forms above it in close, horizontal layers.

Even the brightest light arrives as a flat pewter wash. Two amber service lamps burn beside the pump house without doing much to dispel it. They give the fog depth rather than illumination.

The Sump is less a lake than a crowded arrangement of wet stone. Low black domes stand among linked pans of water; rounded shelves descend into narrow slots where the depth cannot be judged. There is no straight natural shoreline, and the engineered ones have been worn into scallops. Every edge is old in its wetness. Outlets drip into cups they have spent ages hollowing. Pebbles in the channels are polished on their upper sides and cemented underneath with pale mineral deposit.

Water enters almost everywhere, but never dramatically. It beads in fractures, wells up between stones, and condenses on iron until a drop grows heavy enough to fall. Some pans are only ankle-deep. Others are clefts going down out of sight. In the shallows the water is perfectly clear; in the seeps it carries a milkiness that settles into rust-colored sediment. What does not evaporate or return through the pumps sinks slowly back into the floor.

Nothing green lives here. There are no reeds along the margins, no roots in the masonry, not even a verdant skin on the rocks. The colors are wet black, iron red, porcelain white, and the weak brown-yellow of the lamps.

The basis of the ecology is a thin living rust: colonies of simple cells that take their energy from dissolved iron and sulfur. They spread over submerged stone in a film so fine that it appears to be a stain until it is disturbed. Then it lifts in faint, velvety threads and smells of coins and faint sulfur. Grazing animals keep it closely cropped on the open shelves. In protected crevices it thickens, traps silt, and helps build the stone up again.

The pump house sits against the northern rim, its slate roof almost level with the plain. One corner has slumped into the water, but the three engines inside still run. They are horizontal, iron-bedded machines with broad flywheels that turn slowly enough for the spokes to be counted. Their sound is mostly absent from the air. It comes through the ground instead, a mild continuous pressure in the soles of the feet.

No fuel is delivered and no smoke rises. A cable disappears into a flooded conduit beneath the floor. Whatever supplies the current lies below.

Elsewhere, the works are only scattered presences: a valve pedestal crowned by a wheel too large for its stem; three ceramic pipe sections half buried in shell gravel; a short length of narrow-gauge rail ending at the rim; an enamel-faced gauge whose needle moves between the faded words **INTAKE** and **RETURN**. A mechanical rake still cleans the intake screen. At long intervals it rises, dripping, combs out a mass of mineral grit and discarded shells, and deposits it on a flat-topped heap. The heap has become an island. Animals den beneath it.

Every eleven minutes, the works make a small tide.

A relay closes. The engine note changes almost imperceptibly, and the water draws back from the margins by the width of a finger. Dark bands appear on the domes; shallow channels narrow; stranded shells tick softly as they settle. A minute later the upper penstock opens. A clear, silent sheet of water slides down the worn spillway, and the level returns.

The process does not drain the Sump. It only circulates it. Where the falling water ought to be cutting a deeper channel, mineral deposit is rebuilding the lip. Erosion and accretion have nearly cancelled one another. Nothing about the place is static, yet its form seems to have persisted for a very long time.

At the water’s edge there is almost no unoccupied surface.

The most numerous animals are buttonbacks, translucent crustaceans no larger than a fingernail. They gather shell to shell over the grazing grounds, their internal organs visible as dull commas inside them. They feed on the living rust and leave paler, intricate trails across the stone. Their molted skins remain among them, so the living and the empty appear equally numerous. Beneath a single lifted pebble, several hundred may be packed together, all gently adjusting their legs to avoid being washed loose.

Thread eels live below that layer. Some are no thicker than wire; others are as long as a forearm. They lie in pale knots beneath stones and loosen when the manufactured tide ebbs, sliding into the current to take young buttonbacks and insect larvae. Their bodies are so nearly colorless that a dense tangle can look like a crack filled with milk.

Broad-headed amphibians occupy the warm surfaces of the machinery. On cold mornings they lie on the engine bed two or three deep, sides rising and falling at different rates. Their skins are dark and granular above, porcelain-pale below. They seldom enter open water. Instead they wait beside the seep cups and take whatever the dripping brings within reach.

The flying insects resemble small caddisflies, though their wings are heavier and their flight almost silent. With no plant stems available, their larvae build protective cases from buttonback shells, black sand, flakes of porcelain, and occasionally bright splinters of copper. When the adults emerge, they gather in the mist around the lamps. They do not sparkle. They appear as soft, dark interruptions in the amber light, drifting rather than swarming.

The largest hunter is no bigger than a cat: a smooth, hairless animal with webbed feet and a wide, flat head. It spends hours beneath the discharge, motionless enough to be mistaken for a length of pipe. Then it takes an eel or an amphibian with one blunt splash. Such sounds are rare, and the quiet makes them seem enormous.

The strangest behavior comes just before each return of water. Several seconds before the relay clicks, the thread eels turn toward the upper penstock. The insects settle. Buttonbacks stop grazing, their antennae angled down against the stone. For a moment the whole crowded hollow appears to be listening. Perhaps the animals feel a pressure change transmitted through the floor before the machinery makes any audible change. Whatever the cause, the stillness passes through pools that are not connected at the surface.

Then the outlet opens, and everything resumes.

Weather arrives here late. Heavy rain on the surrounding plain may produce no immediate change in the Sump. Two or three days afterward, long after the sky has cleared, the seeps strengthen. Milky water spreads through the pans, the flywheels turn a little faster, and fresh rust-colored film clouds the submerged shelves. In dry periods the mist thins and the lower terraces emerge, but the pumps continue their eleven-minute breathing.

Despite the density of life and machinery, the place remains mostly quiet. There is the patient tick of condensation, the faint rasp of shells, a chain settling once in a while, and the low mechanical pressure felt more than heard. Voices do not carry. A spoken word seems to die within a few steps, absorbed by wet stone and close fog. The air smells of cold iron, mineral water, and, near the engines, warm electrical insulation.

From the rim at the dimmest hour, the Sump can be mistaken for a single dark pool containing two reflections. Closer, it resolves into layers: stone under water, film over stone, shells over film, eels beneath shells, amphibians above water, insects in the air, and iron moving patiently through all of it.

The works were built to remove water. Now they merely move it, and in moving it maintain the hollow’s narrow balance. The gauge needle steadies. The penstock opens. Every worn edge begins to shine, and one by one the old shorelines return.
