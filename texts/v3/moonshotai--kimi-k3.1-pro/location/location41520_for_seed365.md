## The Town in the New Cleft

The cleft is young enough that its edges still cut.

High on a mountain’s shaded shoulder, a blade of blue-black stone has split away from the parent cliff, leaving a sheltered slot some four hundred paces long and, in places, only wide enough for three buildings. Nothing there has been softened by age. The scree consists of angular plates and fresh splinters. Mineral seams glitter where they were newly exposed. Even the paths have sharp corners, and a boot turned carelessly can start a small avalanche of uncompacted stone.

Cloud usually lies below the cleft. Sometimes it rises through it in slow, cold tongues, beaded with moisture. The air is thin enough to make visitors conscious of the stairs, and cool enough that breath shows at dawn, though the deepest hollows seldom freeze. Wind passes over the rim rather than entering. Beyond the narrow strip of sky overhead, the larger world is mostly suggested: a vast white depth on one side, dark rock on the other, and occasionally the distant gray back of another mountain.

Nineteen roofs occupy the cleft.

The settlement has the anatomy of a larger town: three retaining terraces, a common court, covered work yards, stepped drains, a public cistern, and a principal stair that branches into narrow lanes. Yet the buildings stand well apart. Between them are pieces of bare stone, low gardens, and deliberate intervals of nothing. The court could hold the entire population many times over and often contains only a basket or a coil of drying cord. From the highest roof, every other roof is visible. The whole place could be crossed in ten minutes, if nothing were moving.

Everything is fitted by hand. Walls are dry-laid from the young rock, braced with split timber and tightened with root-fiber lashings. Wooden pegs take the place of nails. Bark troughs serve as gutters. The cistern is lined with small, dense pieces of fired clay, each pressed into the next while wet. Tools are stone mauls, hide-wrapped hammers, bone drills, and blades flaked from a glassy seam near the cleft’s mouth. There is no wheel; the stair is the town’s largest machine.

Because the cliff is still settling, nothing is built with the expectation that it will remain square. Every few seasons a terrace develops a new lean, or a crack opens beneath a floor. The inhabitants loosen the lashings, jack up a beam with stones, and begin again. Their houses look patched not because they are careless, but because repair is part of their shape.

Light enters as a gradual gray clarification rather than sunrise. At noon the strip of sky becomes pewter-bright; at dusk it dims to ash. Direct sunlight reaches the floor only briefly, and then as a thin, almost white line along one wall. The roofs and broad leaves are correspondingly dark: bottle-green, wine-black, deep umber. Pale clay markers and twists of white fiber guide people along the paths. At night, lamps are few and heavily shielded. An uncovered flame gathers winged animals until it disappears inside a living shade.

There is no stream. No spring breaks cleanly from the rock. Water arrives sideways in fog, or downward in occasional fine rain, and most of the time the cleft is damp rather than wet. Condensation beads the cold upper walls and gathers beneath stone overhangs. Fiber screens strain moisture from passing cloud; roof mats release it into bark troughs; cupped leaves hold a few dark mouthfuls. The stone is seldom dry, but there is mud only in sheltered pockets. Covered jars and the public cistern matter more here than wells.

Rain is infrequent enough to require preparation. Lids come off jars. Screens are tightened. Cracked troughs are replaced. Open water cannot be left unattended for long: within an hour it becomes crowded with swimmers, climbers, eggs, and things that may be any of these at different moments. Every cistern has cords hanging into it so that small creatures can climb out.

The vegetation is lush in dense, separate skins. Moss fills fractures to the depth of a hand. Ferns hang from ledges, their black-green fronds pale underneath. Where enough soil has collected, low trees spread flat canopies and waxy leaves broad enough to catch fog. Roofs carry thick mats of growth, both for insulation and because their roots help bind the coverings together. On exposed stone the green ends abruptly, leaving long intervals of bare mineral glitter. The cleft therefore feels abundant without feeling enclosed: much life, but also much empty air.

At first glance, it might be taken for an empty garden.

On the second glance, the garden begins to move.

The gray stippling on a boulder is not lichen but the flattened backs of thousands of small animals packed scale against scale. The dark seams between paving stones are columns of climbers. A roof exhales a gauze of transparent wings. What looked like white flowers beneath a fern unfold, separate, and drift away. Even the apparently clean stone carries a faint living film, grazed and regrown so quickly that its patterns change between one dim morning and the next.

The larger movements come at mist-rise. Then the court may fill with a gray-green flow that resembles water until it reaches a step and climbs it. Thousands of soft-backed bodies stream through the lanes, parting around the cistern, passing beneath raised floors, disappearing into gardens on the far side. None makes a cry. Their many feet produce only a brushing sound, as if cloth were being drawn slowly across stone. The inhabitants wait on the terraces or continue working above them. The town’s generous emptiness is for these hours.

Predation is usually visible only as subtraction. A drift of wings thickens beside a folded brown leaf; the leaf opens into a net of jointed limbs, and there is a clear space where part of the drift had been. White, pin-sized jaws cut wandering paths through the wall-fleece, and the paths close behind them. Larger hunters hang motionless among the ferns for days, then leave abruptly, taking their stillness elsewhere. Nothing screams. There are no songs, no buzzing clouds, no territorial calls.

Watch longer, and the distinction between the vegetation and the animals becomes unreliable.

A patch of fern may tighten its fronds, draw its roots from the moss, and stand on them. A small green-backed creature may settle beside a water trough and remain there for years, its skin opening into leaves while its legs diminish into the soil. A rooted colony can release a winged generation in an evening; by the next rain, some of that generation may have become the pale new growth along a wall. Not everything in the cleft changes in this way. The highest lichens are only lichens, and the net-limbed hunters seem content to remain animals. But enough of the green world is capable of leaving that no one builds on an assumption.

The inhabitants describe things as *held* or *going*: rooted for now, or between roots. These are conditions, not kinds. A roof garden, a herd crossing the court, and a drift of wings over the cistern may all belong to one population at different stages.

Their gardening is mostly architecture. They place a slab for shade, arrange a rib of bark to catch condensation, stretch a cord across a favorable crack, and wait for something to arrive. They do not keep livestock in pens. A pen left damp long enough becomes a grove. Tools are hung from rafters rather than left on the ground. Floorboards can be lifted. Several houses have removable garden trays, and more than once two households have exchanged gardens overnight without either household participating.

Quietness is equally practical. A sudden hammer-blow can startle a held colony out of its moorings. Roof gardens have flinched free of their lashings and walked into the court, leaving the rafters bare. Consequently, mallets are padded, door hinges are fiber, and there are no bells. An alarm is a piece of white cloth run quickly up a pole. Silence here is not piety; it is maintenance.

The loudest ordinary sound is rain. It hisses on the leaves, ticks into jars, and gives the moss a brief, almost audible swelling. Afterward, the cleft smells of wet mineral, crushed stems, and the faint musk left by a migration. The town must then be looked at again. A formerly green terrace may stand bare. The court may be furred with new growth. A hunter may be folded against a house wall, too heavy to move. A fresh fracture may have opened across the lowest stair, with bright stone showing inside it.

By the next dim morning, the fog screens are dripping, the wall-fleece is spreading back over the stone, and something small is testing the rim of the cistern with a root that was recently a foot. The nineteen houses remain where they were: sparse, carefully made, and dwarfed less by the cliff than by the activity passing between them.

The settlement is not a refuge from the ecosystem. It is a set of ledges, pauses, escape cords, and well-placed containers within one. Its inhabitants endure because they do not mistake stillness for permanence.
