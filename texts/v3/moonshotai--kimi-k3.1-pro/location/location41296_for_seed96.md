## The White Catchment

The White Catchment is a high country with no bedrock in it. Its altitude is real, but its plateau, escarpments, and deep clefts are all masonry. Where settlement has opened a shaft through the surface, courses of laid material continue downward beyond the reach of daylight. No untouched stone has ever been exposed. There is no soil, no natural gravel, no seam of clay. Even the dust is manufactured: powdered mortar, crushed tile, and the fine pale spoil of blocks rubbed smooth against other blocks.

From a distance, it might be mistaken for a city, but only because the eye has no ready category for terrain built with hand tools. There are no separate buildings, no streets running between them, no central mass surrounded by lesser works. Each wall is also the back of another wall. Each stair crosses the roof of a cell. Every court has another court beneath it, or a cistern, or a narrow solid pier descending into further packed courses. The Catchment is less a collection of structures than a single solid whose voids have been carefully distributed.

It extends well beyond any one prospect. Near at hand, the surface is almost congested: low rims, slot-like spillways, shallow basins, square shafts, buttressed steps, and copings set so closely that there is seldom a continuous level space larger than a room. Farther away, these details gather into broad terraces and bright constructed escarpments. Beyond them lie more terraces, separated by blue atmospheric distance rather than by open ground. The region feels expansive without ever feeling empty.

The common unit is small. Most blocks are no longer than a forearm, cast in shallow beds from lime and finely crushed older work. They were cut with stone wedges, pecked into shape, and finished by abrasion. Plumb cords and water levels established the courses; copper appears only rarely, as a green stain around a lost pin or wedge. Nothing is machine-true. A line may wander by the width of a thumb across a basin and then correct itself over the next hundred blocks. At close range, the whole highland is crowded with minute decisions: a chipped corner turned inward, a hand-smoothed patch of mortar, three thin pieces used where one thick piece would not fit.

Yet the exposed landscape is young. The deep work may be immensely old, but the outer shell has not weathered into softness. Arrises remain sharp enough to cast shadows like cuts. Individual hammer marks survive as fresh crescents. Fractures show pale, unoxidized interiors, and fallen spalls lie separately in the water where they dropped rather than gathering into talus. There are no rounded ruins and no picturesque drifts of debris. Water has not yet had time to make the workmanship vague.

Water occupies nearly every interval the makers left.

It stands as a film on the paving, as clear fingers in the joints, as drops along the undersides of copings. Some basins are broad and no deeper than a hand; others are narrow enough to step across but descend into blackness, their water marked only by a remote silver line. After the frequent light rains, every rim fills to the same brilliant level. For hours afterward, the entire region seems to be leaking, though almost nothing flows strongly enough to be called a stream.

The wettest places are not always the lowest. The interior of the Catchment remains cool long after the surface has warmed, and moisture condenses against cold roofs and deep reveals. Undersides weep in regular rows. A drop swells, holds, then falls; much later, another forms in the same place. In some shaded cells the stone never dries at all. Elsewhere, pressing a hand against a joint would bring a clear bead through the mortar, as though the whole mass were saturated just beneath its skin.

The light is unusually strong for so wet a place. At this elevation the air is cool, thin, and very clear, and the sun strikes the pale construction without interception. Dry surfaces become chalk-white; wet ones turn silver or take on the hard blue of the sky. Water doubles the glare from below. Distances can therefore be difficult to judge: a remote escarpment may appear as a vertical sheet of light, while a tiny basin nearby contains such a precise reflection that it seems deeper than the shaft beside it.

Shadows are scarce but absolute. They collect in slots, beneath steps, and down the square shafts. There is no vegetation to make partial shade, no canopy to break the brightness into moving pieces. At midday the division between lit and unlit stone is often only a single sharp line. By evening, when the upper surfaces finally dull, the whole Catchment begins to cool and condense again.

Its quiet is correspondingly complete. There is no continuous river-roar, no foliage, no insect hum, no calling from ledge to ledge. Wind passes over the packed surface with little to grip and is more often felt as cool pressure than heard. A drop striking water several cells away may arrive as a single clear note with no discernible direction. Occasionally a block clicks as its load changes. Once, perhaps across an entire bright afternoon, a small piece breaks loose and makes two sounds: one against stone, one in water.

Nothing colonizes the stillness. No moss darkens the joints. No algae greens the reservoirs, even where their levels have remained unchanged for years. There are no root hairs, insect cases, feathers, droppings, or fibers caught in the spillways. The water is transparent or faintly milky with lime, never vegetal. Where it evaporates, it leaves white branching deposits that resemble miniature thickets until examined closely. Their branching follows crystalline angles, not growth. A returning film of water rounds them, dissolves them, and lays them down elsewhere.

This is the Catchment’s only succession: solution, transport, precipitation. A seep carries lime through a seam and slowly seals its own route. Pressure then finds another joint. A basin shallows by degrees as mineral skins accumulate on its floor. A dry cell becomes wet without any visible change to its inlet. The place is continually revising its plumbing without intention.

Downhill is not always a sufficient description of that plumbing. Because the packed masonry behaves as one capillary body, water can appear above the reservoir that supplies it. After rain, the added weight in one group of cells may drive moisture through hair-fine passages and into a wall higher on the slope. There is no fountain and no audible current—only a dark line ascending the stone over several days. Weeks later, when pressure elsewhere has eased, the line stops and begins to dry from its lower end.

No river leaves the highland. Water arrives as rain and condensation, disappears into the packed interior, emerges at unexpected faces, and is finally returned to the sky from the bright upper basins. A particular drop may complete that circuit in an afternoon or remain below the surface for generations. The whole region is less a watershed than a body of water whose movement has been slowed almost to the tempo of stone.

Its larger changes are correspondingly patient. Watermarks, rather than scars, are the calendar. A wet margin advances a finger’s width over a season. A spillway loses its usefulness as a distant terrace settles almost imperceptibly. Over long spans, cells close themselves with white mineral and new ones open along fresh fractures. The outline seen from far away may remain unchanged while the interior becomes an entirely different arrangement of fullness and emptiness.

The White Catchment is not quite a ruin: nothing has had time to decay into one. It is not quite a city: there are no hearths, doors, or places set apart for living. Nor is it exactly a machine, although every addition of water alters pressures elsewhere. It is simply a made highland, still close to its first sharpness—cool within, blazing at the surface, saturated throughout, and engaged in one patient act: catching the sky, letting it through, and giving it back to the light.
