## Coldseep Borough

High on the shoulder of a young mountain, in a hollow opened by frost and collapse, there is a place no larger than a cathedral and its close. The mountain has not had time to soften it. The rear cliff is splintered and fluted, its fractures still pale, and slabs with sharp corners lie beneath the rim. Yet almost none of that new rock remains visible inside the hollow. Growth has closed over it in layers.

From above, Coldseep looks like a town packed into the cirque: narrow lanes descending between crowded walls, covered passages crossing overhead, little courts sunk among buttresses, spires rising where the growth has found shelter from the wind. There are terraces, bridges, reservoirs, and something like a gate district where the outer growth thins against the exposed mountainside. The resemblance is strongest in mist, when distances fail and only the near roofs and archways remain.

Nothing here has been cut, laid, or joined. A wall is vertical because flowing water once kept one face of it clear. An arch exists where two root masses met above a runnel and continued thickening. The roofs are overlapping leaves, dark and leathery, their edges fused by moss. The lanes are paved with a silver-green turf so dense that it yields slightly and then closes behind pressure. Even the oldest-looking columns are alive: bundles of flexible stems with bark-like skins, green and wet just beneath the surface.

There is no open soil. Beneath the upper growth lies older vegetation, compressed into a black, springy depth and still threaded with living roots. Fallen material does not remain fallen for long. Pale filaments enter it, cushion plants spread across it, and within a few seasons it has become foundation, wall, or watercourse. The borough is not built on its substrate. It is its substrate, folded upward.

Cloud rests on the rim for much of the year. On clearer days the surrounding peaks intercept the sun, so light arrives only as a cold, diffuse wash from one side. The overlapping growth reduces it further. Noon in the inner lanes is blue-gray; beneath the covered passages, the dimness becomes green-brown. Leaves are nearly black where they are oldest, wine-colored in the sheltered courts, and translucent amber at their growing tips. Open basins hold dull reflections of the sky. Sometimes a drop gathers enough light to gleam briefly at the end of a leaf, but there are no broad glittering surfaces.

The cold is persistent and intimate. It sits in the wet walls and in the motionless air below the arcades. Most nights pass below freezing. The plants stiffen rather than burst, their sap concentrated and their stems able to bend slightly even when rimed. Ice forms in clear skins over the runnels and in thin, uneven panes around the basins. During the short thaws, water moves beneath these skins, lifting them almost imperceptibly. They settle back with small ticking sounds.

Rain is less important than condensation. Cloud combed through the upper growth leaves moisture on every leaf and stem. The rear cliff also weeps through innumerable root-filled fractures. There is no single spring, only a cold seep spread across the rock, gathered by cupped growth into the highest basin. From there, living channels distribute the water downward. Curled leaves become conduits; swollen roots narrow them; basin rims rise with each season’s growth until they overflow somewhere new.

The streets are the paths this overflow has kept open.

Thus the plan of Coldseep is organized but never fixed. A lane may narrow by the width of a hand over several years. A court can become a roofed chamber as its enclosing stems meet overhead. What appears to be a window is often a thin membrane of living tissue, translucent in the dim day and drawn closed at night to conserve moisture. Apparent stairways are successive root buttresses. Bridges thicken where water is dependable and split where a channel has shifted. Nothing is built toward completion.

The oldest growth crowds around the upper seep. There the arches are massive, their interiors dark, and the vegetation has accumulated in many occupied layers. Toward the lower rim, everything becomes younger and more urgent. A recent frost-slip has exposed a wedge of raw rock and torn roots. Fast mats already cover its wettest seams, while small, closely packed shoots occupy the drier fractures. There are no spires there yet, only a dense green crust bracing itself against the next collapse. Coldseep looks ancient because it is layered, but much of its apparent antiquity is only the survival of previous forms.

It is not a single plant, though at times it behaves like one. Canopy plants intercept the cloud; hollow-stemmed growth carries water; deep mats hold the young cliff together; pallid thread-plants occupy the lightless interior, living among the roots and remains of the greener layers. Their different tolerances produce the effect of districts. Where water is steady, the growth becomes tall and elaborate. Where ice persists, it becomes low, hard, and almost mineral in appearance. At the exposed boundary it stops so abruptly that the borough seems to have been trimmed, though only wind and cold have done the trimming.

Animal life is scarce enough to seem incidental. A few translucent crustaceans graze the films in the warmer basins. Tiny mites live in the wet axils of leaves. During brief thaws, a midge or two may appear above the overflow channels, but there is no sustained insect hum. Most of the plants spread by spores, fragmentation, or propagules carried in the water. Very rarely, a solitary dark bird shelters under an outer arch. Its call, when it comes, is disproportionately loud and does not sound as though it belongs to the scale of the place.

Otherwise, Coldseep is mostly quiet. The packed growth takes the edge off the wind, and the water is divided into such small volumes that it never gathers a stream’s voice. Sounds remain local: one drop into a basin, the tick of contracting ice, the soft creak of a stem bundle adjusting to its frozen coat, a wet shift somewhere deep in the mat. Silence here is not stillness. It is many slow processes occurring without the need to announce themselves.

When fog enters heavily, the whole borough becomes active. Basins rise. Membranes loosen. New leaves angle toward the meager light. Spore dust drifts only a short distance before settling onto wet surfaces. Hours later, as the cold returns, the runnels narrow beneath ice and the outer walls contract. Over longer intervals, roots pry at the mountain, channels abandon their old courses, and entire passages are slowly absorbed into the living mass.

Water determines the open spaces. Cold determines the height. Light, arriving weakly and from one side, determines which way every court and canopy leans. There is no central square, only the highest basin beneath the seep, and no true gate, only the point beyond which growth can no longer remain continuous.

Coldseep was never made for inhabitants, and nothing within it is empty. Its walls, streets, reservoirs, and spires are the residents. It does not contain an ecosystem; it is an ecosystem temporarily arranged like a city.
