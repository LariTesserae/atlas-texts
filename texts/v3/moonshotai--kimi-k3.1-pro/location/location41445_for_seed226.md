## The Unfinished Basin

High on a cold massif, just above the last tall trees, lies a basin only a few hundred paces across. From its southern rim, the whole place can be taken in at once: pale terraces, a shallow eye of marsh, and the compact geometry of a town. Beyond the rim, the mountain falls away into immense blue distances and, on many mornings, a floor of valley cloud. The vastness of the view only makes the basin feel smaller.

It has not yet become a valley. Its foundation is young by mountain standards, lifted and exposed before weather could soften it. The stone is milk-gray when dry and faintly blue when wet, with open pores and edges that break clean rather than crumble. There are no established watercourses, no gravel bars, no rounded boulders, and almost no scree. Rain lies on the rock in broad, uncertain sheets before sinking into seams. The basin’s outlet is merely a notch in the rim; a person can step across it. Perhaps, in some far later age, it will be the head of a gorge.

Cool mineral springs rise through the young rock. Where they spill over an obstruction, they leave behind a thin rind of fresh tufa. Rind becomes crust, crust becomes a lip, and the lip slowly grows into another terrace. The newest stone is soft and chalky; older deposits harden into hollow, finely banded ledges. Thus the hillside is still being deposited even as people build upon it. In places it is difficult to say whether a curved retaining wall was laid by hands or accumulated around a spring.

There is no great abundance of open water, but very little is dry. Seepage shines on vertical faces. Small pans refill after every rain. At dawn, cloud condenses on cold stone and drips from walls even under a clearing sky. The central marsh is hardly deeper than a footprint in most places, a dense stand of sedges surrounding a few dark pools. Below it, water moves out of sight, through pores, cavities, clay-lined conduits, and cisterns. The air stays cool and damp; on the coldest nights, shallow pans skin with ice, only to clear again in the late morning.

The light is ordinary mountain daylight. At noon, the whole basin is plainly visible: white stone, black wet seams, almost violent greens, and hard-edged shadows. Morning mist comes and goes, but the place does not depend on mist for its strangeness. Even under a cloudless sky, it looks provisional.

Its lushness is low rather than towering. Moss forms continuous, saturated cushions along the seeps. Ferns crowd the sheltered joints, their fronds beaded with water, while low shrubs with thick, waxy leaves grip the older terraces. Around the marsh, sedges grow so densely that the ground appears solid until it moves underfoot. On dry young stone there may be nothing at all; a pace away, beside a thread of seepage, the growth is almost excessive. Wetness, not shelter or sunlight, draws the boundaries.

The plants are making the first soil. Beneath the oldest moss mats lies a little dark organic matter mixed with windblown dust and mineral grains—sometimes no more than a finger’s depth. Roots have not yet broken the basin open. They hold small pockets in place, thicken them, and die into them. Outside the town’s tended gardens, there is scarcely enough earth anywhere to bury a hand.

Animals have arrived more slowly than vegetation. Tiny dark snails live beneath wet slabs. A black salamander is sometimes found at the marsh edge, motionless enough to be mistaken for a strip of soaked wood. Every few days a slate-colored bird may cross the rim, drink, and leave. There are no herds, no animal paths, and usually no insect murmur. Most of the plants spread by spores, wind, or rooting where they fall. Long intervals pass in which nothing visible moves except water and fronds.

The town stands among the terraces: a town in plan, a hamlet in occupancy.

Its lanes meet in an ordered pattern. There are stepped public courts, covered cisterns, store rooms, work shelters, sleeping cells grouped around minute hearth yards, and drains built before many of the walls they serve. Some chambers have tall, resonant interiors and almost nothing in them. Most of the settlement’s volume is air. There are more doorways than doors, more water jars than people to fill them, and long stretches of paving on which no one stands.

Only a small number of keepers remain through the year. Larger work parties come occasionally, add to the place, and depart. The emptiness is therefore not abandonment. Courts are swept. Cistern mouths are capped against windblown debris. Rope is coiled and hung under cover. A channel showing the first sign of blockage carries a fresh tool mark. Yet one can cross several courts without seeing a person, and a voice from the far side of the basin is an event.

Everything has been made with hand tools and patient leverage. The soft young stone is cut while damp, then allowed to harden. Builders use stone mauls, wooden wedges, timber levers, baskets, and braided fiber rope; a few simple metal chisels are reserved for work that cannot be done any other way. Walls are mostly dry-laid, pegged or wedged where necessary. Clay seals the finer channels. There are no wheel ruts, no heavy hardware, and no great quantity of possessions. A room may contain a sleeping mat, a bowl, and nothing else.

The whole settlement is arranged to receive water and pass it onward: roof to gutter, gutter to cistern, cistern to garden, garden overflow to marsh. But it also passes sound.

The basin is loud.

A trickle no wider than a finger may drop into a bell-shaped cavity and return as a deep, resonant note. A thin sheet of water crossing a tufa lip rattles like rain on stretched hide. Wind moves over open cisterns and broken conduits, producing low tones that seem to come from beneath the paving. Empty courts return footsteps; covered drains carry them elsewhere. After rain, when every small system is running, the combined sound approaches a roar. On a wet, windy afternoon, keepers sometimes raise their voices to speak across a court.

The loudness is wildly disproportionate to the water. From below the rim, one might imagine a river works or a crowded town of mills. Inside, there are only thin rills, hollow stone, and perhaps one distant person dressing a block. The strike of that person’s hammer can be heard throughout the basin.

The keepers use this. Each principal channel has its own pitch and rhythm, adjusted with a flat stone or a small clay throat. The sounds are practical signals: a lower note may mean a cistern is full; an irregular knocking may mean a rim has begun to obstruct flow. Silence in a normally sounding drain is more alarming than noise. A keeper can tend a garden with their back to the waterworks and still know where attention is needed.

Nothing here changes quickly. A mineral lip thickens over seasons. An unattended conduit narrows over decades. Moss advances across a dry ledge only after some other growth has prepared a foothold. A wall may gain a single course between one work party and the next. Weather alters the light and the volume of sound, but the basin itself seems almost stationary.

The few practical rules follow from this slowness. Build against a dry face if the wall is to remain workable; leave it wet, and stone will gradually accumulate upon it. Do not strip all the moss from a springhead, because the spring may begin depositing somewhere less useful. Do not abandon wooden tools in an active seep unless you intend their handles to become white, mineral-sheathed shapes for someone else to find. And never judge the waterworks by looking alone.

Some of the oldest human walls are now bonded so thoroughly into natural terraces that their courses can be traced only by the straightness of a few edges. Conversely, some apparently natural steps have a small, hidden cut where an early keeper persuaded water to turn. Settlement and geology are not merging quickly, but they are merging.

There is no sense of catastrophe hanging over this. No eruption is imminent, no tower is about to fall, no flood waits behind the marsh. The place is simply young. Its steps have not been hollowed by feet. Its outlet has not become a ravine. Its soil is still a series of thin, separate beginnings.

To stand in the central court is to hear an inhabited city and see a first draft. Water booms and clatters in the emptiness; ordinary sunlight falls on sharp masonry; one gardener bends over a bed of hand-made soil. Nothing has been reclaimed by nature, because nature has not finished arriving. The small town remains loud with its little water while rock, vegetation, and human construction continue, almost imperceptibly, to make one another.
