## The Upper Sump

The Upper Sump is heard long before it can be understood as a city. The road onto the plateau passes through open, worn country—low ribs of black stone, shallow pools, a few survey pylons—and for an hour there seems to be nothing ahead but warm haze. All the while, the thunder grows. It is too steady for weather. Eventually it separates into a deep pumping note, the hiss of water under pressure, and the harder roar of outfalls. By the time the first pump halls appear, conversation has become impractical.

The plateau is elevated but not alpine. From its rounded edges, one can look far across lower country shimmering with heat. The altitude brings no cold: warmth seeps from the ground itself, and the air often has the close, mineral smell of a bathhouse. Rain comes through every few days, but even in clear weather the landscape remains wet. Cliffs weep through old fractures. Water condenses on shaded concrete and gathers in rust-colored beads along pipes. Shallow pools occupy quarry floors and roadside hollows, while narrow runnels cross the pavement in places where no drain has ever succeeded in keeping them out. There is no great river here, only water distributed everywhere in small, persistent amounts.

The city occupies the plateau without covering it. Its districts are strung along the higher ribs: groups of workers’ houses, pump halls, switching yards, pipeworks, reservoirs, and long factory sheds with clerestory roofs. Between them lie broad empty grounds of wet gravel and stone pavement. These are not wasteland but spill-fields, deliberately left unbuilt so that water can spread without entering foundations. Streets are wide and often empty. A rail viaduct may cross half a mile of pools and low scrub to connect only two buildings. Yet the whole is unmistakably urban, organized by surveyed levels, service roads, buried galleries, and a dense web of pipes whose routes are marked by rows of chipped ceramic posts.

Construction has accumulated in layers. The oldest walls are basalt, their corners softened and their steps worn into shallow scoops. Above and around them stand brick engine houses, concrete cisterns, steel gantries, and newer sheds faced with gray block. Copper-roofed ventilators sit beside ceramic insulators and heavy black cables. Rust is common, but neglect is not. Bolts are replaced, masonry is repointed, and pipes are laboriously freed of mineral scale. Pale deposits crust the mouths of channels; elsewhere, flowing water has polished the same material smooth. Decay and accretion proceed so evenly that many structures seem to have reached a settled form.

The settlement apparently began as a drainage works and never stopped being one. Deep galleries keep the water below the foundations, while electric pumps lift it into reservoirs and industrial mains. Hot wells supply turbines in squat power houses. Some of the water cools grinding mills; some is used to wash fine sediment from the rock; the rest goes to the outfalls and, eventually, down the escarpment in riveted penstocks. The settling ponds yield a gray silt that is pressed and fired into building block. Thus a portion of the city is always being made from material removed to keep the rest of it standing.

The noise is not incidental. It is the city’s principal continuous event. Pump pistons transmit slow concussions through the bedrock. Penstocks whine and hammer. Mill rooms add a crushing, granular roar, while relief valves release plumes of steam with a sound like canvas being torn beside the ear. The open ground and bare stone give the sound little opportunity to die away. Near the central works, it presses against the chest and makes loose metal fittings buzz.

Public life has adapted. Signals are given by lamps, colored paddles, and an elaborate hand language; no one relies on bells. Houses have small windows, double walls, and inner courtyards where voices can be heard again. Workers crossing exposed ground wear heavy ear guards even for short distances. Farther out, the separate noises merge into a vast low chord. Changes in that chord matter more than clocks. An experienced keeper can identify a worn bearing or a narrowing pipe from a slight alteration heard across an entire district.

Almost nothing softens the industrial surfaces with vegetation. The thin soils and mineral-rich seepage support only scattered clumps of coppery sedge, gray lichen on old walls, and low fleshy plants whose leaf edges become white with salts. Rust-red and olive films live in the warm shallows, but they form thin skins rather than lush beds. A few narrow trees survive behind courtyard walls, where their roots can be kept away from the hottest ground. Animals are rarer still: pale snails inside wet masonry, an occasional dark lizard flattened against a sun-warmed parapet, moths around service lamps. Once in a while a bird crosses high above the plateau without landing. Despite all the water, there are no choruses of frogs.

The strangeness of the Upper Sump lies in how its water behaves. Pools with no visible connection rise and fall in sympathy with distant pumping stations, sometimes hours after a change in load. An inspection well may begin seeping when a quarry pond on the other side of a ridge empties. Engineers explain this through fissures, pressure, and old drainage galleries, and their charts are detailed enough to be convincing. Still, many of the arrows have been revised more than once. Residents simply call all such seepage “return water,” whether it appears to be descending or coming back up. The whole plateau gives the impression of being one slow, imperfectly understood hydraulic body.

Change here is patient. Water levels travel seasonally between old stains on the cistern walls. Mineral lips thicken around outfalls over decades; masons eventually cut them back. Roads settle by degrees and are rebuilt on the same alignment. Districts are repaired rather than expanded, and the empty spill-fields remain empty. A maintenance trolley, a few figures crossing a yard, or steam leaning gradually from a vent may be the only visible movement across an entire view.

From the plateau rim, under ordinary gray-white daylight, the Upper Sump looks almost dormant: pale reservoirs, dark roofs, scattered plumes, long distances between things. Yet the roar continues without pause. It seems less like a city imposed upon a mountain than an old agreement still being honored—the rock seeps, the engines lift, the water wears, and the deposits mend. On the scale of the plateau, almost nothing is happening. It is merely happening everywhere, and very loudly.
