## Noll, the Provisional Municipality

Noll lies at the bottom of a valley that has not yet happened.

It is no wider than a cathedral close. A surveyor could walk its outer seam in twenty minutes, provided she did not try to cross any of the vacant lots. Inside that seam are four wards, eleven street names, a water board stenciled in white on a root, and the Assembly Works. Only a handful of structures stand above the ground. There is no crowd, no continuous roofline, no background murmur of habitation. Noll is urban in its organization and almost empty in its substance: a city made principally of intervals.

The hollow has not been eroded. It is simply the portion of the landscape that remains unfilled. Nowhere is there a rounded stone. Beneath the settlement lies angular mineral meal, pale and sharp-edged, with none of the patience of mature geology. There are no deep banks, no worn channels, no old soil. A thin black-green tissue binds the raw grains together, and behind its finest roots the first clay is just beginning to accumulate.

Water has not yet learned a course through Noll. Most of its moisture occurs in events too small to be weather. A pipe sweats. A gutter leaf fills with one clear ounce. A roof receives thirty seconds of rain while the road beside it remains dry. Cool rills occasionally appear on the surface, run a few body-lengths without cutting the ground, and vanish into root pores. Now and then a puddle is discovered first; the rain arrives afterward, as though summoned to account for it. The air stays cool and damp, tasting faintly of wet iron and crushed stems. Metal handrails are cold to the palm. Warm exhaust makes brief, low ghosts beside the Works.

Everything else grows.

The roads are broad, flattened roots, green-black and slightly ridged, resilient enough to absorb a footstep. Curbs are made from leaves folded repeatedly over themselves until they become hard as horn. Buildings rise as bundles of pale, fluted stalks, their branches fusing into floors and lintels. Roof tiles overlap like the scales of some enormous dormant bud. Windowpanes are dried leaf blisters: thin, flexible, and dimly translucent. Even the drains have vascular linings. Metal appears only where exactness is required—flywheel teeth, rails, bolts, numbered doorplates, copper wire held away from the living walls on porcelain buds. Where iron rusts, red threads enter the plant and are carried away.

This abundance of vegetation does not produce lushness or congestion. Most of its mass lies in the continuous ground-skin and in a few enormous structural columns. Between these, Noll is open volume. There are no shrub-choked gardens, no interlacing canopy, no picturesque ruins. A vacant ward is genuinely vacant: upright darkness above living pavement, interrupted perhaps by one cable, one isolated lamp, the pale beginning of a stair. A flower may occupy half an acre by itself. Its petals open with the slow deliberation of a industrial door.

The municipal infrastructure is far more complete than the settlement. Drains cross fields where no street has been laid. Cables supply current to empty slabs. A row of immaculate letter slots stands in a wall with no rooms behind it. Some streets exist only as changes in the grain of the root pavement; others have names, lamps, and drainage but no surface at all. Nothing looks abandoned. Cut ends are wet with fresh sap, bolts are clean, and new address numbers appear overnight. Noll does not resemble a city in decline. It resembles a city rehearsing.

At its lowest point crouches the Assembly Works, part machine hall and part hollow trunk. A flywheel turns behind an open rib of wall, driving belts, stamping presses, and several patient looms. One production line presses black plant fiber into identical insulating collars. Another machines small pump components and places them in trays according to a system no animal seems to know. A third assembles pumps that are immediately connected outlet to inlet, so that the last pumps into the first. Whether this is manufacture, maintenance, or practice is unclear.

Three large pumps stand beside the main engine. One moves the settlement’s scarce water. One moves sap. The third is connected to nothing, but its housing is worn smooth by use.

The Works is almost silent. There is a bearing’s soft tick, the knock of sap in a pipe, the occasional bright clink of a finished part falling into a tray. Heavier vibrations pass into the roots and become inaudible; a hand laid on the wall detects more activity than the ear does. The engines produce no smoke, only a little seed-oil sweetness and warm vapor that disappears quickly in the cool air.

Light is rationed more strictly than water. A few hooded maintenance lamps burn dull amber at long intervals. Above them, the darkness has no discernible ceiling or depth. At long intervals, an arc lamp above the press discharges. Its white flash is the only brilliant light in Noll, and it does not always illuminate the same settlement. Once it may reveal deep valley walls and a river cutting through them. Another flash may show roofs packed to the horizon, windows blazing, every vacant lot occupied. Another may show nothing but raw mineral and a single green thread. These versions overlap for an instant. When the darkness returns, the present arrangement resumes, although a stair may have changed sides or a drain may now be waiting beneath a different street.

The plant can afford this darkness because it does not feed primarily on light. Its finest roots exploit the chemical rawness of the new mineral, leaving behind thin films of clay and drawing a weak electrical potential from the contact between living tissue and stone. The Works taps that potential. In return, the machines contribute warmth, copper filings, and regular quantities of trimmed structural growth. Pruning stimulates the walls; the walls produce more material for the looms. Rust, sap, current, and mineral pass slowly through the same municipal body.

The animals are the only obvious residents, though “obvious” is not quite the right word. Their abundance is folded into surfaces. The open air may contain one visible moth for minutes at a time, while beneath a single pace of root pavement thousands of white larvae, copper mites, springtails, and thread-thin beetles are feeding, mating, and pursuing one another through the green matrix. Blind snails graze the roads, leaving silver lines that either heal or are later adopted as survey marks. Felt-winged moths pollinate the architectural flowers without reflecting lamplight. Small shrews run the electric cables, nesting in junction boxes and hunting moth pupae; flat, pale predators wait beneath them in the drains.

Every brief shower accelerates the ecology. Transparent frogs hatch in puddles no deeper than a fingerprint. They feed, spawn, and fold back into drought-resistant eggs before the water disappears. Salamanders often gather beside these puddles shortly before the rain that will create them. In the larger empty wards, a tall, many-kneed browser occasionally crosses the dark, visible mostly as an absence moving in front of a lamp. It crops buds from unbuilt walls. The living ground accepts its weight without a sound, and for several nights afterward new construction angles subtly toward its path.

All this activity does more than inhabit Noll. It helps Noll continue.

Persistence here is a habit rather than a property. Repetition makes things structural. A drain that has carried a hundred drips remains a drain between them. A door used every night gradually acquires a room; an unused door thickens into a bud. Routes walked frequently become shorter and more dependable. Vacant lots, with nothing to rehearse their dimensions, remain negotiable. One may be visible in its entirety from the curb—just a lamp, a cable, a patch of dark ground—and still require an hour to cross.

The regular stroke of the Works performs the same service for sequence. Near the engine, cause generally precedes effect. Farther away, the ordering becomes less reliable. Fruit may swell before its flower opens. A new wall may cast a shadow for several nights before its surface arrives. Animals are part of the stabilization: the repeated grazing of snails, the moth’s return to a flower, the browser’s habitual passage. Streets arise where enough separate journeys have agreed, without communicating, that a street would be useful.

At Noll’s rim, cables end in polished caps and roots loop around the survey stakes like stitches. Beyond them, angular grit and darkness have not yet settled into the distinct categories of ground and sky. The settlement is small because only so much can be kept in rehearsal. Its emptiness is not an aftermath. It is material still available for decision.

For now, the flywheel turns. Somewhere a drop gathers on copper. Something eats something else without audible violence. A root enters raw stone, and the press produces another identical collar for a machine that has not yet been proposed.

Cross the same vacant lot three times and your passage will begin to make a street. On the fourth crossing, there may be a door beside you, numbered but unopened. Behind it, very faintly, in a room not yet obliged to exist, rain will be practicing.
