## The Summit Nursery

The garden is forty paces across and nearly five kilometers above the sea. It occupies the crown of a condenser tower, held inside a clear pressure skin that is usually invisible until mist gathers on it. Beyond the low parapet there are no foothills or descending slopes—only cloud, guy cables, and the distant upper works of other towers. On clear days, shadows move across the cloud deck far below. The horizon is immense, but the garden itself has the intimacy of a room.

A double pressure hatch opens into warmth. The air inside is kept at the temperature of a sheltered summer afternoon, humid enough that sweat stops evaporating and paper softens at the corners. It smells of wet chalk, green sap, hot copper, and, whenever the pumps reverse, a trace of machine oil. Outside the skin, the mountain air is thin and cold. Inside, it has been thickened, heated, and given weather.

Three shallow terraces descend from the hatch toward a central basin. The difference in height is only a few meters, but dense planting and narrow, turning paths make the descent feel longer. There is no natural ground beneath them. Exposed edges show the whole constructed section: white ceramic foam for lightness, black slag for mass, copper mesh for reinforcement, and a thin, dark layer of humus prepared in digesters below. Even the compost has a recipe. There is no bedrock to reach, only more tower.

Nothing is old enough to be picturesque with decay. The retaining blocks still have sharp corners. Their casting numbers can be read through films of algae. Copper fittings are bright except for small, clean islands of patina around the screws. The boulders scattered among the terraces are condenser cores, rounded for efficient flow rather than to imitate stone; no one has bothered to conceal their service bolts. One side of each boulder is warm and furred with moss. The other is cold and continually wet, condensation gathering on it in clear beads before running down into the root mats.

At the far end, the terrain is still being made. A squat crawler advances along a rail, extruding pale mineral foam into low ribs and shallow cups. It moves so slowly that its progress is best measured against the survey marks. By noon, the new surface has hardened. By the next rain, it is dark with water. Ferns on the established side lean toward it across the narrowing gap.

Water is never absent, though it rarely collects in quantity. It films the paths, beads beneath leaves, ticks from pipe joints, and hangs almost motionless in the air around the cooling fins. Narrow rills run in ceramic grooves between the terraces. The central basin is no deeper than a hand, its bottom paved with black glass aggregate so that the water appears depthless until a leaf touches it.

Every twenty minutes or so, the tower makes a small rain.

First the radiator combs hidden among the rocks grow cold. Mist forms around them in low white cuffs, then spreads through the foliage without ever becoming thick enough to hide the paths. Droplets assemble on the pressure skin and on the broadest leaves. For less than a minute, rain falls straight down out of apparently clear air. It is warm, fine, and nearly silent. Then the hidden pumps take up the water again, the basin subsides, and the garden returns to dripping.

The vegetation has entered this timetable without entirely surrendering to it. Fan-ferns turn their pale undersides upward as the mist begins. Mosses brighten almost at once. Waxy creepers have followed the irrigation cables into the canopy, where their leaves overlap like green roof tiles. Below them are shrubs, then ferns, then a soft, near-continuous layer of moss and fungal felt. Roots occupy pipe saddles; pipes disappear into roots. Along the narrowest path, wet foliage touches both shoulders at once.

The planting rows are still visible in places, especially near the hatch, but elsewhere the garden has become a negotiation between intended growth and opportunity. A vine flowers inside a ventilation housing. A young tree has anchored itself in the hollow top of a valve column. The maintenance mechanisms cut back only what threatens a pressure seal or blocks a moving joint. Everything else is allowed to become complicated.

Animal life is startlingly scarce. There is no birdsong and no visible haze of insects. A few transparent water fleas move in the basin. Sometimes a single white moth works the flowers with methodical slowness. The most commonly seen animal is a small, pale snail that favors the cold sides of the condenser boulders, leaving a faint trail through mineral dust. Many of the plants self-pollinate. Those that do not often flower abundantly and set almost no seed. Fallen blossoms remain intact for days before the introduced decomposer fungi reduce them, seemingly all at once, to wet lace.

The quiet is not an absence of activity. The great engines are far below, their noise reduced to a hum felt through the path gratings and occasionally in the teeth. Water drips at several pitches. Metal ticks as it warms. A relay closes with a small, precise knock. When the artificial rain begins, the leaves produce a sound like distant, restrained applause. Beyond the pressure skin, high wind may be pulling at the tower, but almost none of its voice enters.

The light is ordinary daylight, which makes the place stranger rather than less so. There are no colored growing lamps, only a few amber service indicators half-hidden under leaves. At noon the greens are direct and almost severe; the constructed ground shows white, gray, and rust-red where it is exposed. Under cloud, shadows vanish and every wet surface takes on the same soft silver. Late sun briefly warms the copper pipes and turns the basin brown-gold. Nothing glows except by reflection.

The garden’s rules are simple, though their consequences are not. No water is allowed to run off the tower: every drop is condensed, collected, used, and returned. A rock may also be a machine. A root may be either infrastructure or an obstruction, depending on where it goes. Warmth, moisture, and pressure are maintained exactly; composition is not. The system was built to support a planted test plot, but it has no general instruction to keep the plot looking designed.

Near the newest terrace, one rill has escaped its intended groove by the width of a finger. Each small rain carries a few grains of white substrate through it and leaves them in a crescent at the basin’s edge. It is almost nothing—the first bend not present in any construction drawing. A flow sensor has registered the change, but no mechanism comes to correct it. Roots are already approaching from both sides.

After a few thousand more rains, there may be a recognizable bank. Later, a hollow beneath it. Later still, the smallest possible valley. Until then, the machines continue making weather over the sharp, young ground, patiently watering a landscape that has only just begun to answer back.
