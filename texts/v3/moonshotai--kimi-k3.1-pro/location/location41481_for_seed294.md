## The High Basin

The High Basin is a sea held near the top of the world.

Its containing walls rise through cold cloud, and its outer rim stands higher than most weather. Where the rim has worn low, water slides over broad sills and falls outward in white, freezing threads. These falls vanish into the cloud below. There is no visible country beneath them, no bedrock around them—only further constructed descents, half buried in ice and their own debris.

Within the rim there is no dry shore. The wall is simply the place where the sea ceases to be contained.

Far overhead, the surface carries an uneven roof of gray ice. It rarely closes completely. Black leads open between the plates, migrate, narrow, and seal elsewhere. Light entering through them is weak and colorless; by the time it reaches the floor, it has become a dim pewter haze. Shapes appear first as uncertainties in the water, then as shadows, and only at close range as solid things. The cold is persistent enough to slow every gesture. The brine remains liquid, but loose crystals gather in sheltered pores and along the undersides of old ledges.

The floor is almost a plain.

Broad swells run for leagues, so gently rounded that they seem natural until one notices their spacing. They are the remains of roofs, platforms, and partitions, compacted by age into long ramps. Hollows between them were once passages or chambers. At irregular intervals a surviving arch rises from the sediment, its uprights worn narrow, its crown sagging without quite breaking. Elsewhere there are fields of shallow cups, sunken courts, and low, fluted ridges where whole districts have collapsed in the same direction.

Every edge has been softened. Straightness survives only in relationships: one ruin aligns with another; a buried street persists as a faint grain in the silt; rows of rounded stumps preserve the memory of a wall. Seen from above, the Basin might still appear ordered. Close to the material, order has nearly become texture.

The substance itself resembles bone porcelain mixed with salt—matte, porous, cold, and marked with exceedingly fine layers. Fresh deposits are pale and sharp. Older surfaces turn gray, then opalescent where the water has polished them. Even the black sand in the hollows is made material: powdered mortar, fractured shell, the fecal pellets and discarded body-casings of builders.

Dig downward anywhere and the layers change, but their nature does not. Fresh construction gives way to old construction, old construction to cemented rubble, rubble to compacted powder. There is no point at which the made world yields to natural stone.

Yet there are no devices. Nothing rotates, pumps, glows on command, or waits behind a sealed panel. There are no tools and no tool marks. An opening is only an opening; a stair-like rise is the accidental consequence of repeated deposition. The Basin is not a machine that has stopped.

It was built, and is still being built, by animals.

The greatest part of the work belongs to the masons, minute soft-bodied creatures living in the pores of the material. Each takes in suspended grains and dissolved mineral, then lays a particle behind itself. Each responds to its neighbors, to the chemistry of the surface, and to the direction of the current. None comprehends an arch. Millions of such local decisions nevertheless produce buttresses, vaulted seams, channels, and terraces. Where flow is steady, their work becomes regular enough to look intentional. Where flow is chaotic, it becomes lace, blister, and confused knot.

Their grazing weakens old surfaces even as their excretions strengthen new ones. Construction and erosion are not separate industries here.

Life is abundant, but never lush. Nothing green grows in the Basin. There are no weeds in the courts, no mats waving from the walls, no forests in the shallows. The food web begins with colorless microorganisms living inside freshly exposed pores, drawing energy from reactions between the cold brine and the ancient binder. Flat grazers creep over them, leaving broad, spiraling tracks. Translucent sieve-animals drift through the open water, straining out microbial floc and the finer remains of dead masons. Long, pallid predators wait in the dimness, often motionless for so long that sediment gathers on their backs.

There may be nothing visible in a wide reach except a few drifting specks. Then, along a current boundary, thousands of sieves gather in a transparent ribbon miles long. A collapse draws scavengers from distances that seem impossible in such sparse water. For a short time the ruin seethes. Then the congregation thins, and the emptiness returns. The Basin contains a great many animals; it is simply larger than their gatherings.

And it is almost never uniformly calm.

Dense brine forms beneath the ice and slides downward in cold sheets. Return currents climb along the buried ramps. Where these bodies of water meet, they do not mix gently. They fold. A clear reach may develop a submerged wall of rolling silt; a motionless column may begin to turn like a screw; a shear boundary may pass through a field of ruins and lift the floor into a white suspension. Some storms remain local, raging inside a single court while the water beyond its broken arch stays still enough to preserve delicate layers of ash-gray sediment.

Other disturbances propagate across whole districts. Old walls shed their skins. Fresh masonry is stripped back to the animals that made it. Banks of sediment rise, travel, and settle as new hills. In the aftermath, masons boil out of protected crevices and begin laying pale seams across the damage. Sieves crowd the turbulent water to feed. Predators follow the sieves.

For all this violence, the Basin is mostly quiet.

The great currents are felt before they are heard: pressure at the body, a change in coldness, then an irresistible sideways pull. The usual sounds are small—the hiss of fine grains moving over a floor, the delicate rasp of grazing, an occasional tick from the ice overhead. Collapses arrive as soft, distant concussions, often so far away that the sound reaches a place after its water has already begun to move. Silence does not indicate safety. It is merely the common condition between events.

On the scale of the whole Basin, however, almost nothing changes.

The great erosion has already occurred. What remains is close to the form toward which collapse tends: a broad, low field of rounded architecture beneath a cold sea. No storm removes the swells entirely; no generation of masons rebuilds the lost towers. Their furious activity plays across an ancient stability. A hand-span of surface may not keep its shape for a season, while the skyline remains effectively unchanged for ages.

At the rim, this becomes easiest to understand. Water pours away into cloud. Ice breaks and falls. Fresh deposits whiten the sills, are undercut, and vanish. Yet the rim persists in nearly the same place, neither restored nor finally destroyed.

The High Basin is therefore not quite a ruin, though everything in it is ruined. It is not a reef, though animals have secreted every solid surface. It is not a city awaiting its builders’ return; there was never a plan to return to. It is total construction without machinery, turbulence without clamor, abundance without lushness—a cold, dim sea continually finishing its own dissolution, and never quite done.
