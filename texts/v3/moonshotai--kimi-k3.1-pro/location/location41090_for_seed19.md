## The Still Multitude

From a distance, the high country looks empty: a pale, almost level back of land lifted so far above the rest of the world that its height has ceased to be picturesque. There are no peaks to give it scale. The mountains here were old before their roots were raised; now even their roots are rounded. Quartzite shoulders run for leagues without breaking the skyline, and the valleys between them have filled with their own powdered substance. The plateau stands at extreme altitude not because anything towers, but because everything else has fallen away.

Beyond its rim there is no dramatic cliff, only a convexity in the land and then a descent so long that its lower slopes vanish into blue distance. No weather climbs from below. The air is cool, thin, and perfectly still. Daylight lies evenly over the stone—clear, ordinary daylight, with honest colors and shadows dark enough to rest the eyes.

There is no water in any familiar form. No ice survives in the shaded clefts, no dampness darkens the undersides of stones, no seep stains the mineral pans. The pans are white but not quite salt: a dry, alum-like crust laid down in hexagonal plates, ringing faintly underfoot. Elsewhere the ground is black glass sand, flint-colored dust, or bedrock polished until the grain of it seems to flow. Every fragment has found its final angle. Every slope has settled. Erosion is not occurring here; erosion has finished.

So has nearly every sound.

There is no wind in the sparse grass, no calling among the herds, no insect hum. A visitor’s footsteps arrive flat and close, as though the air declines to carry them very far. Beneath that quiet lies a faint, continuous mineral hiss, so uniform that it seems less a sound than the texture of the altitude itself.

At first, the dark band beneath a ridge looks like a seam of exposed stone. Then it resolves into legs.

Thousands of legs. Then too many for thousands to be a useful idea.

The herd extends across the pan and up both sides of the valley, bodies standing flank to flank, horn woven through horn. There are low, slate-haired grazers with muzzles almost touching the ground; tall, narrow animals whose jointed necks rise like bare survey poles; plated giants whose backs support whole colonies of smaller, furred things. Lean predators stand among them without causing alarm. Broad-winged creatures rest overhead with wings fully extended, not flying and not quite perched, their shadows tiling the animals below.

The smallest fauna occupy the intervals: bead-sized bodies between hooves, flat many-legged things along the shaded seams of bellies, translucent knots of wings and eyes in the hollows behind ears. Break a plate of pan-crust and the upper hand’s breadth beneath it may be packed with cool, shelled bodies, all facing different directions. The crowds do not thin toward the horizon. They merely become too fine to distinguish.

Nothing jostles. Nothing breathes. A hoof remains raised; a hunter remains with its teeth a finger’s width from another animal’s spine; a long-necked browser holds one grey scale-leaf between its lips. Yet these are not carcasses or figures. Touch a flank and it yields slightly. The body is cool, at precisely the temperature of the air and stone. Its eye has depth, attention, even a kind of patience.

Here, life is not something a body does. It is a distinction the plateau makes.

The vegetation could never support the multitude. It is astonishingly sparse: isolated tussocks of grey filament no taller than a finger, rust-green crusts in protected rock grain, and, every few leagues, a black shrub bearing a dozen waxy scales. There are no seedlings and no dead stalks. Each plant has reached its own final size. The grazers carry all the anatomy of hunger, but nothing is cropped. There are no droppings, no carrion, no old bones whitening beyond the herds. Predator and prey, browser and leaf, parasite and host remain together in relationships that have outlasted the processes those relationships once required.

The plateau is not one crowded clearing but a whole crowded region. White pan country gives way to flint downs; the downs descend into shallow, silt-choked valleys; beyond them rise the smooth roots of ranges so ancient that their names would be older than any language brought here. Each country has its own multitude. In the basins, armored animals stand shoulder-deep in mineral dust that never settles farther. On the high roots, the bodies are taller and more thinly furred, though not more widely spaced. Near the rim, innumerable creatures face outward toward the inaccessible world below.

Scattered among them are faint signs of hands.

A semicircle of three stacked stones. A cairn no higher than a knee. A roofless cell built from slabs, its doorway too low for anything but a squat, long-limbed maker. Now and then there is a single shelter with a hide stretched over a frame of horn, or a patch of ground bright with obsidian flakes. The tools are simple: struck stone, ground stone, twists of plant fiber, a cutting edge hafted with sinew. There are no roads between the structures, no fields, no settlements, no rubbish accumulating around them. They seem less attempts to inhabit the plateau than brief annotations upon it.

Once, beside such a scatter of flakes, a visitor may find a toolmaker seated with an obsidian core between its knees. One hand holds a hammerstone. The other already holds the finished point. The blow connecting those two facts is nowhere.

The crowding, too, is stranger than numbers.

Look back after crossing a pan, and there are more animals than stood there before. None has arrived. They occupy gaps that the previous angle of view had folded flat. Take one sideways step and a second herd becomes visible among the first, its bodies interleaved with impossible courtesy. Lower your eyes until the crust shines at a grazing angle, and further populations appear beneath those: whole herds standing in the same valleys, under the same daylight, their outlines darkening where several kinds of solidity coincide.

The plateau’s space is not a container that has been overfilled. It is an agreement that has begun to fail.

Distance fails more quietly. A cairn passed on the left may next be seen directly ahead, unchanged down to the chipped corner of its highest stone. Walking away from a herd can bring its nearest animal into reach. A shelter visible beyond a ridge may eventually be discovered behind you, its doorway still facing the direction from which you came. These are not transformations. Nothing turns, moves, or arrives while watched. Each arrangement is absolute and motionless; only the route between arrangements refuses to remain consistent.

And still the place is not chaotic. It is the most settled country imaginable. The daylight does not soften toward evening. Shadows do not lengthen. The mineral hiss neither rises nor fades. Dust does not lift, hides do not weather, stone tools do not blunt, and no animal completes the gesture inscribed in its body. This is not a paused world; pausing would imply that something might resume. It is a completed one.

On the white pans, a visitor sometimes finds footprints already leading onward. The stride is familiar. The depth is familiar. The small drag at one heel is familiar. Following them only brings the traveler to more of the same tracks, crossing ground that has never been disturbed.

They are not a prophecy. Prophecy requires a future.

They are simply part of the ground.

From the rim, with the cool air pressing lightly against the skin and the ancient mountains reduced to soft backs beneath the crowds, the plateau should feel dead. Instead it feels almost unbearably occupied: every horizon serrated by living forms, every silence shared, every empty space revealing another occupancy when regarded differently. Nothing is struggling toward survival. Nothing is waiting for release. The multitude has passed beyond hunger, decay, migration, and birth without surrendering the shapes those things once gave it.

The faint hiss continues. The fixed shadows hold. Somewhere among the interwoven horns, an eye acknowledges you without moving.

Nothing is about to happen. Nothing has been left undone.
