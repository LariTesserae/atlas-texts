## The Mound Plain

From a distance, the basin looks almost empty: a pale, low country beneath an ordinary sun, without a river, a lake, or even the bright seam of a distant marsh. Its horizons are long and slightly tremulous with heat. Then the eye begins to distinguish the mounds.

There are hundreds in the nearest view, thousands beyond them, and no reason to suppose they stop at any horizon. Some are narrow and fluted, some domed, some broken into clusters of buttresses and blunt towers. Their colors change gradually across the plain—buff, rust, ash-gray, deep ochre—according to the soil from which they were made. Between and around them lies a dense dryland vegetation: thorn scrub, low flat-crowned trees, waxy shrubs, succulent groundcover, and grasses standing in stiff bronze tufts. The green is not the green of a wet forest. It is darker, tougher, and interrupted by dust, but there is far more of it than the first glance suggests.

The land beneath everything is young and unfinished. The basin floor consists largely of soft sediment, mineral crust, and wind-laid silt. Its exposed edges are angular rather than rounded. Thin plates of calcrete lift and split; low steps in the ground have not yet been softened into hills. There is no mature network of valleys, because there are no streams to make one. Even the shallow depressions do not form an obvious drainage pattern. Wind, rather than flowing water, is the first patient sculptor. It sorts the dust, scours exposed faces, and deposits pale aprons behind thickets. Often the plants and burrowing animals fix material in place faster than the wind can remove it.

The whole plain is therefore under construction at two speeds. Geology works almost imperceptibly. The mounds work daily.

Up close, each mound is less an object than a district. Its outer shell carries old repairs, fresh pellets of earth, abandoned ventilation openings, and new turrets growing from the flanks of older ones. Covered foraging runs leave its base and disappear into grass or leaf litter. Below ground, galleries connect nurseries, stores, fungus gardens, and deep chambers whose temperatures differ enough for the inhabitants to move between them as the day changes. Roots enter abandoned passages. Other animals enlarge old exits into burrows. A collapsed wall becomes a ramp for beetles and lizards before its material is carried away and used again.

Nothing here has been made with a tool. Earth is bitten loose, carried, pressed, and shaped by bodies. Stems are cut with mandibles; seeds are transported in cheek pouches or stomachs; burrows are opened with claws. Yet the accumulated result is unmistakably architectural. A single cluster of mounds, runways, clearings, and underground passages can cover the area of a town. Such clusters grade into one another without a wall or formal boundary. There is no central city and no surrounding wilderness. The entire plain has become a kind of settlement.

It is not one colony, and it has no single plan. Millions of territories overlap or press against one another. Different insects defend different stretches of ground. Birds nest above colonies that would destroy them if they could reach the nest. Burrowing mammals tear open fortified walls, exposing chambers that are subsequently occupied by snakes, spiders, or other insects. Plants root in the disturbed soil. The builders return, seal what they can, and build around what they cannot.

The vegetation is richest where this activity is oldest. Soil brought up from below changes the ground around the mound bases; shelter from wind allows dust and organic matter to collect; roots follow the loosened earth of forgotten galleries. Low trees lean over older domes, while shrubs pack the spaces between young towers. Elsewhere, constant foraging keeps lanes nearly bare. These lanes are not roads in any deliberate sense, but animals use them as roads. Hoofed browsers move along them in single file, rodents follow their edges, and predators learn where the cover narrows.

The surface remains completely dry. Rain may be absent for years at a stretch, and when it does occur it leaves no lasting watercourse. There is nothing here that glints, drips, laps, or runs. The plants persist through deep roots, reduced leaves, protected growing tissues, and long periods of near-dormancy. Some store what little moisture they can obtain; others spend much of their lives as seed. Their lushness lies in occupancy rather than extravagance. Layer upon layer of life fits into the same dry ground: canopy above shrub, shrub above grass, grass above rosettes and crusts of small plants, roots and fungi threading through everything below.

Nor is the bare ground truly bare. A patch that looks vacant from standing height may contain seed caches, fine tracks, shed insect skins, buried dung, and the nearly invisible openings of nests. Every few paces, some small movement gives away another occupant. A beetle reverses into dust. A lizard shifts its head. Ants vanish under a fallen pod and emerge somewhere else. The air, too, is occupied—by pollen, floating seeds, minute insects, and the dust through which all of them pass.

Abundance does not make the place loud. Most of its sounds are smaller than the spaces between the mounds: the dry tick of legs on a shell, a papery rustle in grass, the muffled fall of a clod inside a burrow, a bird’s brief call from deep cover. At noon there may be minutes when nothing seems to sound at all. Even a large animal moving through the scrub can be quieter than the wind in one shrub. The silence is not scarcity. It is the quiet of lives conducted close to the ground, under cover, or below it.

Activity nevertheless never settles into stillness for long. In the cool early morning, foragers extend their routes and repairs begin on damaged walls. As the light strengthens, lizards take up positions on warm earth while insects withdraw deeper into shade. Browsers strip leaves and seedpods; rodents carry off what falls. Birds and small predators gather wherever an excavation or swarming colony concentrates prey. By dusk, the traffic reverses and changes hands. Animals that spent the day underground emerge, while others return to crevices and nests. Every carcass, dead tree, and abandoned chamber becomes material for another stage of the plain’s construction.

Its size gives it weather as well as seasons. Mornings can be remarkably still under a cloudless sky. Later, heat rises from immense areas of pale ground, drawing dry air across the basin in broad, slow movements. Dust devils cross the open lanes, briefly tying earth to sky. On some afternoons, a tan front of suspended sediment advances over districts of mounds and thickets, softening the horizon and turning the sunlight diffuse. Then the wind drops. Dust settles on leaves and tower backs, adding another thin layer to a landscape that has not finished deciding its final surface.

At night, the mounds become dark shapes among darker vegetation. Their deep interiors retain some of the day’s warmth, while the exposed ground cools. Fungus gardens continue their slow work; foragers and predators continue to move; wind occasionally draws a faint hiss across the grass. There is no machinery, no running water, no distant surf—only these small sounds and long intervals of quiet.

Nothing in the Mound Plain depends on an exception to ordinary nature. Sunlight, gravity, wind, hunger, growth, and decay are sufficient. Its distinctiveness comes from their concentration and persistence: a young, waterless land so thoroughly occupied that even its geology has been taken up as building material, and so continually rebuilt that no part of it can properly be called finished.
