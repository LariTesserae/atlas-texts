## Avel, on the Vey Uplift

Seen from sufficient distance, the Vey Plateau looks less like established land than a decision the planet has only recently made. It stretches nearly four thousand kilometres between its escarpments, a high, cold expanse of new crust standing above the older lowlands. Its edges are sheer and poorly joined. Its interior has no ancient mountain chains, no broad weathered valleys, no deep belts of soil. The whole province is still being raised, stabilized, and drained.

Daylight here is ordinary and exact. The sun gives wet stone a hard white glitter and throws uncomplicated shadows behind every fracture. Distance is difficult to judge: a dark line that seems to be a nearby wall may be an escarpment eighty kilometres away, while a pool at one’s feet may be the near edge of a lake occupying the entire horizon.

The surface is mostly basalt, glassy in places, elsewhere rough with crystals that have not yet been dulled by weather. Fresh exposures are blue-black; older ones, some only a few decades old, carry orange mineral stains and white rinds of salt. Nearly every stone has sharp corners. There are no naturally rounded boulders. Streams have not had time to sort gravel into bars, and even the largest cliffs still break along clean, geometric planes.

Water is everywhere, but little of it has settled into a permanent arrangement. It beads on cold rock, gathers in shallow depressions, and runs briefly across the surface before disappearing into fissures. Some basins fill after rain and drain underground within a week. Others are fed by deep, mineral-heavy springs and remain through the year, their margins layered with ice, ochre sediment, and delicate terraces of precipitated salt.

The cold is persistent rather than monotonous. Breath stays white through much of the day, and shaded snow can survive for years in narrow cracks, yet exposed dark stone may thaw under the sun while the air above it remains below freezing. Meltwater moves at noon and stops toward evening. Ice forms clear skins over ponds, then breaks into small plates when spring water rises beneath them. At night the rocks tick and snap as they contract. During the warmer season, freezing rain can leave the whole plateau wet, black, and reflective without making it appreciably warmer.

Vey is broad enough to assemble its own weather. Moisture rises from chains of temporary lakes into long, low cloud streets. Cold air slides off the higher interior and pushes beneath it, producing sleet in one catchment while the next receives only fine rain. A storm may spend three days crossing the plateau and never reach the city. From Avel’s upper galleries, people watch weather happening at several distances at once: rain close enough to hide the lower streets, a dry silver interval beyond it, and, farther away, a compact black cell trailing ice across the stone.

Avel itself does not cover the land. It appears as a scattered archipelago of compact towns, industrial shelves, reservoirs, and transit junctions, separated by long intervals in which nothing seems built at all. Each settlement is densely organized around sheltered courts and covered passages. Buildings use basalt aggregate, frost-white ceramics, dark metal, and thick panes that hold heat without quite looking insulated. Older pressure tunnels lie beneath newer arcades; older arcades have become the basements of taller structures. Open runnels carry meltwater beside the streets, crossed by small bridges and grates.

The grander infrastructure is easy to miss. A transit line crosses a young fault on pylons that look much too slender. A reservoir sits above town without a massive retaining wall. Bridges span fissures with no visible depth to their foundations.

All of these depend on the gradient engines buried far below the city. They redistribute gravitational stress through immense volumes of rock, spreading loads that would otherwise collect along a fault or beneath a foundation. The same technique, applied across the plateau’s deep roots, allows the uplift to proceed without continual catastrophic earthquakes. At street level, the technology shows itself only as rows of matte-black sockets, survey stones that never frost over, and occasional adjustments felt as pressure in the chest rather than heard as sound.

Consequently, Avel’s first civic discipline is surveying. Every map carries a date. Elevation is revised routinely. Roads are built with expansion joints that can also become lateral joints, and buildings stand in foundations designed to change their minds. A straight road remains straight because its sections are continually negotiating, not because the ground beneath it is still. New construction is often postponed for a watercourse that does not yet exist but is expected to appear within thirty years.

Life arrived before soil. The first visible vegetation was a dark microbial film in wet cracks, followed by gray and rust-colored lichens. These remain the most widespread growths. Low cushion plants occupy sheltered hollows, and coarse hairgrass gathers around warm spring channels and the city’s heat-exchange outfalls. Elsewhere, vegetation is so sparse that a patch no larger than a hand can stand out from a great distance. There are no open forests. The few trees in Avel grow inside glazed courtyards, their foliage almost disorientingly green against the mineral city.

The animal wealth of Vey comes from the water rather than from the greenery. Mineral-rich shallows support intense seasonal growth of algae and microscopic grazers. Brine flies hatch in such numbers that their cast skins form pale bands along lake shores. Small silver fish move through the deeper spring pools. Wading birds arrive to feed, then nest on islands whose locations change as the basins fill and drain.

One can travel for hours seeing only tracks, droppings, and the occasional feather, then come over a rise and find a basin darkened by tens of thousands of animals. Broad-hoofed, slate-colored grazers cross it in loose files, scraping lichen from the rock and tearing up the brief growth around seepage lines. Their breath hangs above the herd in a low fog. Smaller furred animals run between their legs, hunting insects exposed by their hooves. Wide-winged scavengers turn overhead without flapping.

Predators follow at a distance: pale, long-bodied cats in the broken country, and packs of narrow-muzzled runners along the migration routes. Most hunts are witnessed only afterward—a confusion of tracks, a patch of blood already freezing, birds descending one by one. Little remains for long. Around Avel, animals have learned the heat map of the city. Birds nest on warm ledges, small creatures den beside service conduits, and grazers gather below the outfall terraces in late autumn. Migration corridors are kept open through the infrastructure, marked not by fences but by gaps in lighting and long, unbroken stretches of dark ground.

Despite all this motion, Vey is mostly quiet. Sounds arrive separately: the click of a hoof on stone, water dripping into a crack, the small complaint of ice splitting along a pond margin. A flock may pass overhead with little more than the friction of wings. Transit vehicles make a soft pressure-sigh when they enter a station. The uplift engines are silent. Even when a slab breaks from a fresh cliff, the sight often comes first; the sound arrives much later, flattened by distance into a single dry report.

The activity becomes apparent only when one stays. Over a morning, a cloud bank builds and a pond’s outflow begins. Over a week, the pond empties into a new fracture and the waders move elsewhere. Herds descend toward the spring terraces; predators alter their routes; city crews extend a road bed and revise a reservoir’s predicted shore. Over years, the survey lines change. Over centuries, the horizon itself is expected to rise.

Avel is therefore neither a ruin in a wilderness nor a finished city imposed on empty land. It is a settlement living among its own foundations while those foundations are still being laid. The rock remains raw, the drainage provisional, the vegetation thin. Yet every quiet basin is crossed by tracks, every warm fracture has been inspected by something looking for shelter, and every newly stabilized shelf acquires lichen, nests, paths, and names before its builders have quite finished with it.
