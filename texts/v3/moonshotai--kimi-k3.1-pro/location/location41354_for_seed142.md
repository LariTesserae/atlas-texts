## The Upper Works

At the summit of the world there is a plain.

It is high without being mountainous. The ranges that once stood here were rubbed down long before the city existed, their peaks softened into swells, their valleys broadened almost out of recognition. Later, the entire province was raised. Now escarpments fall for kilometres from its outer edges, while the country within remains nearly level. A traveler can walk for hours across the summit and climb no more than a few body lengths.

Nothing about the stone remembers water. There are no channels, no rounded river gravels, no layered beds left by vanished seas. The bedrock is a dark, fine-grained silicate, crossed by seams of bone-pale salt and natural glass. Its surface has been worn by thermal fatigue, ancient abrasion, and processes so slow that they no longer have useful names. Shallow basins hold only sorted mineral dust. The dust is dry past dryness: it does not cling, does not cake, and leaves a bitter metallic taste when it gets into the mouth.

The old survey plates call the whole province **the Upper Works**, though the works themselves occupy only a small part of it.

The city is scattered across the plain in separated districts. It is urban not through crowding but through organization. Avenues align across kilometres of empty rock. Freight rails emerge from one group of buildings, cross a blank expanse with no stations or fences, and enter another district on precisely the same bearing. Ceramic-insulated cables run beside them on low pylons. There are engine halls, switching yards, storage terraces, foundries, long windowless blocks that may once have been dwellings, and paved squares large enough to hold gatherings for which the city has no remaining population.

Much space has been left between these things. Not abandoned space: intentional space. The emptiness is part of the street plan.

From any one point, only a fraction of the settlement can be known. The sky gives no light at all. There is no sun below the horizon, no moon above it, no stars, and no distinguishable horizon. Overhead, the darkness might be an immense roof or an absence too large to tell from one. Lamps burn at fixed intervals along the streets, usually amber, sometimes a muted mineral green. Furnace slits add narrow lines of red. Their light does not travel generously. Each source illuminates its immediate walls and pavement, while the city beyond remains a few isolated points suspended in blackness.

Consequently, every shadow is permanent. A railing’s shadow lies in the same place year after year. A doorway is always dark on the same side. Powerful lamps can be carried through the streets, but they do not reveal a vista; they merely move a small, temporary room of visibility through a much larger dark.

The buildings are made from the province itself. Their lowest courses are black stone, so ancient that corners have become curves and stairways have worn toward shallow ramps. Above these are layers of gray sintered ceramic, dark iron bracing, and sheets of volcanic glass set into metal frames. Flat roofs predominate. There are no gutters, rain chains, or downspouts. Doors seal against dust rather than weather. Pipes carry oil, gas, or enclosed salt melts, never water.

Age has not produced picturesque ruin. Collapse occurred, was cleared, and was reused too many times for rubble to remain. Walls are thick with repairs, but the repairs follow the original dimensions. Inscriptions have been cut deeper when their surfaces wore smooth. Even the newest materials look ceremonially old, as though nothing here is permitted to appear provisional.

Nothing grows on any of it. There is no soil in the living sense, no moss in protected seams, no pale lichen on the north side of a wall, no fibrous root forcing apart a joint. Pale marks are salts; black marks are oxides or old heat. There is no wood in the oldest construction and no rot in the newest. When something fails, it fails by fracture, abrasion, fatigue, or the slow loss of tolerance between fitted parts.

The air is thin, warm, and almost perfectly still. At this altitude one might expect cold, but the stone gives a gentle heat through the soles of boots. Some of it rises from deep in the crust; some is the accumulated loss of countless enclosed machines. The temperature varies so little from district to district that the warmth seems less like a local condition than a rule. Metal handrails are faintly warmer than stone. Sheltered courtyards are not noticeably different from exposed streets.

There is no wind to move the dust and no weather to cross the plain. Chimneys are heat exchangers rather than smokestacks, and nothing visible escapes them. The air smells faintly of ozone, hot ceramic, and clean mineral dust. It never smells of rain, growth, smoke, or decay.

Most of the city is quiet. A transformer hum may be felt before it is heard. Behind the wall of an engine hall, great flywheels turn on magnetic bearings with a sound lower than speech. At long, regular intervals, a relay closes somewhere with a small iron note. Rails conduct vibrations for surprising distances, so a visitor may feel an inaudible machine through the pavement while standing beside it in apparent silence. Footsteps are often the loudest event.

Yet the Works are not stopped.

Belts move inside the factories. Pumps circulate their sealed fluids. Counterweights descend as their twins rise. Cutting heads cross metal stock with patient precision. In one district, worn ceramic components are crushed, graded, pressed, and fired into identical replacements. In another, standard ingots leave a foundry on a narrow conveyor, enter a machining hall, and emerge as finished pieces accompanied by carefully collected swarf. The pieces are tested, stored, and eventually returned to the same furnaces with the swarf.

Elsewhere, wire is drawn from one spool and wound onto another. Empty carts are loaded with dust and full carts are emptied of the same grade of dust. Inspection engines examine sealed mechanisms without opening them, record their condition, and send them onward.

At first this resembles production awaiting a purpose. In fact, the purpose is the pattern itself. The city manufactures the conditions of its own continuation. Heat, charge, motion, and replacement are held in exact circulation. Nothing accumulates: no slag heaps grow, no warehouses fill, no finished product waits for shipment. Whatever wears away is restored; whatever is restored wears away at the same rate. The whole industrial landscape is a standing wave made of matter.

This is also why the Upper Works cannot properly be called frozen. Too much is moving. But no motion has a lasting outcome. The plain keeps its outline, the streets keep their lights, and the warm air keeps its temperature. There are clocks in the substations, but they count operating cycles rather than hours. There are no shifts, mornings, seasons, or years marked by the environment. A change may occur locally, but somewhere else it is answered, canceled, and folded back into the whole.

The few animals belong to the same narrow economy.

The one most often recorded is called a contact-back in maintenance logs, although an entire district may be crossed without finding one. It is a low, many-jointed creature about the size of a spread hand, graphite-dark and flexible enough to resemble a loose piece of cable insulation when at rest. It contains no water and no cells recognizable as such. Its interior supports a slow, non-aqueous electrochemistry. It has no eyes. It finds warmth and leakage current.

A contact-back will settle on a cable housing or transformer casing and remain there, almost indistinguishable from a fitted component. It takes in minute amounts of charge and trace metal, leaving behind a few clear glassy beads. Eventually it unfolds and walks elsewhere, its feet making sounds too slight to carry through the air. The beads are collected with the dust and returned to industrial feedstock.

Nothing grazes, because there is nothing to graze. Nothing hunts the contact-backs. They do not nest in any visible way, and no young have been identified. Their numbers appear as stable as the lamp spacing. Whether they are remnants of an older ecology, descendants of something constructed, or simply another kind of machine that the city has learned to tolerate, their presence changes nothing.

Beyond the outer districts, the plain continues. There are broad depressions floored in naturally fused glass, isolated substations with no buildings around them, and lines of ancient pylons whose purposes have been absorbed into the general grid. Some roads lead only to survey markers. Others reach the escarpment, where cargo lifts hang above the lower country. Their cables descend until darkness makes them imaginary. The lift cars still perform empty inspection traverses, one rising as the other descends, but no lower lights answer them.

The altitude is therefore known less by sight than by the length of the cables, the thinness of the air, and the enormous absence beyond the parapets.

Nothing in the Upper Works is waiting for dawn. Dawn is not a delayed event here; it is an inapplicable idea. Nor is the city awaiting rescue, abandonment, or completion. It has already completed itself in the only way left available: by becoming a landscape without ceasing to be a city.

Its stillness is not the stillness of stopped machinery. Wheels turn, current flows, and somewhere a contact-back rests against warm ceramic. But every action has an equal and familiar reply. The faint hum beneath the silence is not the sound of something beginning.

It is the sound of completion being maintained.
