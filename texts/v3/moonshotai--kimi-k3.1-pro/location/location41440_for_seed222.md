## The Serein Uplands

From the lower country, the Serein Uplands look less like a mountain range than a piece of sky that has thickened into stone. Their escarpment rises in long, pale steps above the warm basins, banded here and there with seams of blue-black chert. There are no snowy summits and few sharp peaks. This is an ancient seabed that was lifted without being shattered, then worn for so long that even its greatest fractures have softened.

The height brings no alpine cold. Warm air climbs the escarpment in slow, invisible rivers, and the bedrock stores the sun. Heat radiates through boot soles well into the evening. The major landslides are immensely old; their debris has been sorted, rounded, and partly cemented into the slopes. Gullies descend in graded, economical curves. Nothing seems freshly broken. Even the talus appears to have found its final place.

Beyond the rim, the land opens rather than narrows. Shallow basins lie between whaleback ridges and low, irregular terraces. Some basins are several kilometres across, their floors so pale and level that they resemble dry water. Others are hidden until the traveller is almost upon them. There is no central valley and no single commanding summit, only a broad region of related forms continuing past the immediate horizon.

At noon the light is hard and nearly colourless. Ivory stone throws it back; shadows beneath ledges become deep blue. Fine gravel and plates of weathered rock cover much of the surface, with occasional exposures of smoother material polished by ages of wind-driven dust. The air is so clear that distances contract. A herd moving on a far terrace may seem close enough to hail until its animals begin, one by one, to disappear behind a rise that was invisible a moment before.

For most of the day, the uplands look dry. They are not quite dry.

Before dawn, condensation beads in the lee of stones and along the undersides of overhangs. Certain dark seams in the rock remain cool enough to gather moisture even after sunrise. Shallow natural basins hold water for a few days beneath shelving lids of stone, and some clefts contain no visible pool at all, only mud, a mineral smell, and the tracks of animals that know where to press their muzzles.

Then there is the rain for which the uplands are named: serein, fine rain falling from a cloudless sky.

It arrives locally and without warning to anyone who does not know the signs. One basin remains in white glare while, several kilometres away, a nearly vertical veil begins to shine above another. The rain is cool, very fine, and usually brief. Sometimes it evaporates before reaching the ground. Sometimes it darkens a few hectares of rock, releases a sudden smell of limestone and crushed green stems, and stops. Water runs for several minutes through channels made by storms far older than any living thing on the plateau, then sinks into gravel or disappears into seams. There are no permanent surface rivers.

The rain comes from devices scattered many kilometres apart, though “device” suggests more visible mechanism than they possess. One is a white pin no thicker than a finger, standing beside an otherwise ordinary slab. Another is three-quarters of a ring, its missing portion buried. A third is only a shallow sill of seamless material set flush with the ground. Later visitors have built cairns near some of them and shelters beside others, mistaking them for monuments or boundary markers.

None corrodes. None hums. Their bases are surrounded by old animal trails and, in places, polished by generations of rubbing flanks.

When one becomes active, nothing in it moves. The light above it seems to acquire depth, as though a volume of clear air has been placed inside another volume of clear air with slightly different properties. For a few seconds the space is difficult to focus on. Then condensation begins high overhead.

The devices do not appear to cool the atmosphere in any ordinary way. They seem instead to make a small region coincide briefly with some colder, wetter condition that exists nowhere visible—elsewhere, perhaps, or merely at another time. Water arrives as a consequence. The impossible part is over almost before it can be noticed.

Plants follow the hidden geography made by this water, but they never cover much of it. Grey wireleaf grows from fissures, its narrow waxy leaves folding together at midday. Low cushion plants imitate lichen-covered stone so closely that they are often discovered only when an animal bends to crop them. A few seeps support solitary, flat-crowned trees with trunks twisted into thick, muscular shapes. Between these presences are long intervals of bare gravel.

After serein, the ground changes without becoming green. Rust-red filaments and tiny translucent scales emerge from the crust, unfold for a day or two, and then withdraw into seed, spore, or dormant root. From a ridge, such a rain-flush is visible as a faint bronze stain following the exact shape of the shower. Vegetation here is punctuation, not cover.

Animal life is far more abundant than that sparse cover suggests. It is simply distributed, mobile, and skilled at waiting.

Much of the food web lives in pores and films: microbial growth in damp clefts, buried eggs, dormant larvae, seeds carried into cracks. Rain beetles emerge by the thousands after a shower, their dark backs flashing as they drink. Smaller winged things rise above the wet patches in glittering, short-lived columns. Slatebacks—long-legged, loose-skinned browsers with broad, thin ears that shed heat—travel between widely separated shrubs and rain-flushes. A herd may contain scores of animals yet stretch across an entire basin, each one separated from the next by several body lengths.

Above them, translucent kites turn in the thermals. They are often the first visible sign that a device is about to wake, gathering beneath an empty patch of sky while the air is still dry. When the insects rise, the kites fold and drop through them without a cry. Occasionally a larger, narrow-winged hunter enters their ranks. Then the whole scattered wheel of birds tilts aside at once, opening an empty corridor around the predator.

On the ground, abundance is read mostly in traces: tracks braided around a seep, beetle shells beneath a ledge, clipped stems, the warm dust of a recently vacated hollow. A pale, slab-backed hunter lies through the bright hours beside routes leading to water. Its hunts are brief and usually unseen. Scavengers arrive soon afterward, walking or gliding in from distances that seem too great for the news to have travelled.

With so much motion, the silence is unexpected. There is no forest to hold the wind, no tall grass to hiss, and no running water to make a continuous sound. Wind passes over the rounded stone with little to catch it. Most of the animals signal by posture, colour, or vibrations carried through the ground. Sounds arrive singly: the tick of warming rock, a grain slipping down a slope, the dry brush of a wing, one drop falling into a covered basin. An active rain device makes no sound at all.

Smaller and more comprehensible structures occur at long intervals. A roofless windbreak of fitted slabs. Seven steps descending to a buried cistern. A line of markers visible only from above. On one saddle, a single wayhouse remains intact, its low roof undamaged and its floor half filled with sand. Its doorway is often the darkest object in sight. The cistern beside it is dry, although moisture occasionally beads along the ceiling, and something small and warm-blooded has made a nest in the debris below.

The faint routes between these places do not lead toward a ruined town. They join rain devices, covered pools, and sheltered crossings. Some continue to bare ground and end there. Others have been adopted so completely by slatebacks that their original purpose is difficult to distinguish from a migration route.

Each part of the day rearranges the uplands. Dawn belongs to dew and tracks. Late morning brings glare, sheltering animals, and the first kites rising on thermals. Serein may cross one basin in the afternoon while another remains cloudless and still. By evening, the latest rain is already vanishing, but the routes toward it have changed; herds turn, insects rise, and predators alter their places of waiting. Erosion continues beneath all this, so slowly that the rain devices themselves have become part of the established landform.

To cross the Serein Uplands is to keep revising the idea of emptiness. Bare stone becomes a dew trap, a track, a grazing route, then part of a circuit whose other stations lie beyond the horizon. The country remains open, quiet, and widely spaced, but it is never inert. Somewhere a device is preparing rain; somewhere the last rain is disappearing into the ground; and somewhere, already, animals are moving between the two.
