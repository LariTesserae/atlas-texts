## The Unfinished Range

The range has not finished becoming a range, and everything in it behaves accordingly.

Its highest blocks stand more than six kilometres above the low country, but they do not yet have the familiar architecture of old mountains. There are few slender ridges, few valleys deepened into graceful troughs, few long aprons of rounded debris. Instead, enormous tables of rock rise between cloud-filled depressions. Fault scarps cut across them in raw, decisive planes. Summit surfaces tilt without quite breaking. Some basins are broad and almost floorless, their lower ends lost in fractures; others hold ice in shallow hollows that the ice has not yet had time to carve.

From a high shoulder, the region opens for tens of kilometres: one dark summit table, then a vacant reach of air, then a white hanging ice field, then several parallel cliffs suspended above cloud. Distance is difficult to judge. There are no trees, buildings, or repeated landforms to provide a measure. A cliff that seems close enough to touch may be a day’s travel away across country that has not yet established a continuous route through itself.

The stone is predominantly blue-grey, with paler mineral seams and occasional faces so fresh that they still look granular rather than weathered. Where cloud water repeatedly touches it, the rock turns nearly black. Elsewhere it remains pale, dry-looking, and sharp. There is astonishingly little rubble. The cliffs have not yet produced their own talus; the pebbles in the high basins are angular, and sand is local and sparse. Even the ice seems provisional—clear lenses in joints, thin glazings beneath overhangs, small fields of old snow whose boundaries change before they can become glaciers.

Water is everywhere, but rarely assembled into anything as definite as a river.

Cloud condenses directly on windward stone. Supercooled droplets become rime along edges, building narrow white vanes into the wind. During the brief thaw, dark rock gathers just enough warmth to loosen some of this ice. Then the cliffs begin to weep. Water travels along mineral seams, gathers in depressions no deeper than a hand, overflows them, and vanishes into a fracture. Farther down, it may emerge again as a line of wetness or a chain of pools. By evening, those same pools may be sealed under transparent ice.

A rill can exist for an afternoon and leave no channel. A seep can support life for several seasons and then shift half a kilometre when a joint opens. Most moisture never travels far: it freezes, sublimates, returns to cloud, or soaks into debris that has not yet become soil. The range is wet without being well drained. Its drainage pattern is not immature so much as repeatedly interrupted.

There are no constructions anywhere in it—no cut stone, shelter, cable, road, pipe, survey mark, or ruin. The straight lines are faults and bedding planes. The regular repetitions are joints in the rock.

And yet the place has the scale and impersonality of an industrial works. It is powered by gradients: the pressure of rising crust, the pull of gravity, the difference between moist air and cold stone. Along the windward faces, the range produces cloud almost continuously. The cloud produces rime and snow. Ice wedges itself into fractures; thaw releases water; water carries away small quantities of mineral dust. At night the whole system tightens and slows. With daylight, parts of it start again.

Nothing directs this production, and there is no hidden mechanism. Its machine-like quality comes only from repetition and magnitude. Droplets, ice crystals, rock fragments, and spores are made in immense numbers, while the larger landforms change almost imperceptibly. For all the activity, very little material has yet been removed. The range is busy, but not worn.

The light is usually dim and extensively diffused. A high overcast often lies above the summits while lower cloud moves among them, so the sun appears only as a brighter area of grey. Shadows are weak. Distances register as changes in tone: charcoal stone, silver ice, a slightly paler cliff beyond. Occasionally the upper cloud thins and a colourless brilliance spreads over the high tables, but it gives little warmth. At other times the summits emerge into clear, star-pricked darkness while cloud remains packed below them, and the whole region seems inverted—an empty black sky above a luminous, weather-filled depth.

The resemblance to industry ends at the ear. There is no continuous hum. Broad slopes offer little for the wind to strike, and it often passes mainly as pressure and cold. Only at a notch or a fractured edge does it gather into a low flute. Close to the rock, smaller sounds become distinct: a drop striking ice, a stone ticking as it cools, the dry shift of snow grains, a brief tapping of water beneath a frozen skin. Rarely, a deep report crosses an entire basin as ice or bedrock takes a new strain. Then the quiet returns, not as an absence of activity but as the large space between events.

Vegetation is less a cover than a scattering of successful encounters.

Crustose lichens form rust-coloured and charcoal patches on surfaces that have remained stable long enough. Where meltwater persists, dark microbial films spread over stone, sometimes visible only as a greener black within the wetness. In sheltered fractures, a few cushion plants root in dust trapped behind crystals. Lower shelves carry isolated stems of cold grass and small, waxy leaves pressed close to the ground. Between such places may lie an entire slope of apparently sterile rock.

Nothing here forms a continuous green horizon. Even the most favourable seep is only densely vegetated in comparison with the emptiness around it. When water changes course, the plants cannot follow quickly. A green seam fades; another begins elsewhere. Soil exists mainly as a possibility in protected pockets.

The animal life is much richer than that sparseness suggests, though it rarely gathers in visible crowds. Its abundance is distributed across an enormous region and concentrated into brief opportunities.

Part of the food supply arrives from elsewhere. Moist winds carry insects and organic dust upward from lower country. Some fall onto snow; others reach wet rock or pools. Algal and bacterial films use the short melt. Tiny larvae develop in sheltered water, while springtails and other small invertebrates gather along its margins. Spiders wait beneath loose stone. A pool that appears perfectly clear in the cold morning can, by a favourable afternoon, have a living rim.

Small finch-like birds arrive in compact flocks, feed rapidly among the wet fractures, and rise again before the cloud closes. Dark choughs work the cliff updrafts, their calls appearing and disappearing with changes in the wind. Where a hatch or a fall of windborne insects becomes concentrated, birds gather; behind the birds may come a falcon, crossing the open air with almost no warning.

On the lower high shelves, sparse vegetation supports ibex and other hardy herbivores. Their numbers across the whole region are considerable, but a single basin may hold none. A fox may cross a snowfield and leave the only evidence of its passage as a fading line of prints. Ravens and broad-winged scavengers patrol territories so large that their presence feels less like residency than inspection. Overhead, seasonal migrants pass through air that often looks completely empty.

Thus a view may contain forty kilometres of stone and one visible animal, while the wet face immediately below holds thousands of lives too small to see. Emptiness and abundance do not cancel each other here. They operate at different scales.

The short thaw brings the quickest changes. As liquid water appears, invertebrates become active, birds move upward, and herbivores seek the first exposed plants. Predators follow these movements rather than maintaining fixed territories. By afternoon, rising cloud may erase the cliffs and fill the air with fine snow. Feeding stops or moves elsewhere. At dusk the seeps narrow, pool surfaces freeze, and the birds descend toward shelter. The rock ticks as it cools.

The longer cold does not make the region lifeless so much as discontinuous. Eggs, larvae, spores, and dormant tissues wait in small protected places. Resident birds and scavengers continue their wide circuits. Ice thickens in the joints. Cloud keeps delivering moisture even when almost nothing can use it.

Far below the pace of weather, the uplift continues. A fracture shifts; a seep moves; a slab that has stood since before the first pool formed in its shadow acquires a new line of weakness. Eventually there may be organized glaciers here, persistent streams, debris slopes, deeper soil, and valleys with continuous floors. None of these is inevitable, and none is visible as a plan.

For now, the range consists largely of prerequisites: raw stone, extreme height, water searching for temporary paths, and life arriving before there is anywhere obvious for it to live. It is cold, wet, nearly vacant, and intensely in process. Its silence is not the silence of completion, but the interval between small operations in a very large, unfinished work.
