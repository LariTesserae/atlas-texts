## The Level

The Level is a low, warm continent of manufacture, wet without ever softening into a swamp. It has the breadth of a floodplain but none of a floodplain’s surrender: every shallow basin has square corners, every channel a molded lip, every pool an intended depth. From a raised service stair, squat pump houses, ribbed cisterns, pipe bridges, and finned heat exchangers repeat until distance turns them into a gray, granular horizon.

Nothing rises far. There are no hills, no exposed bedrock, no cut where soil gives way to stone. Beneath the walking plates are structural decks, cable galleries, and pressure halls; beneath those, more decks. Even the apparently empty spaces have been specified. A courtyard is machine access. A bare roof is a thermal buffer. A gap between pipes is a clearance tolerance, not wilderness.

The whole place looks freshly cast. Drain mouths remain knife-edged. Hairline molding ridges still run along the curbs. Identification marks are crisp, bolt heads unworn, gratings unpolished except where polish was intended. Warm water slides over all of it without leaving rust halos, mineral fans, or dark stains. Nothing has been given time to become a ruin.

Daylight lies over the Level in ample, ordinary white. It is neither furnace glare nor industrial twilight. Colors remain legible: bone-white ceramic, blue-black housings, colorless water, the dull pink and gray of animal flesh. The warmth is more constant than the light. It comes through the soles of the feet, radiates from pipe banks, and hangs in the humid air. Shade offers little relief. The rain, where it rains, arrives warm.

Water is present in modest, persistent amounts rather than in floods. It beads on overhead conduits and falls in single, well-spaced drops. It films the walking plates, collects in shallow rectangular pans, and moves beneath gratings in clear, leisurely channels. Flanges weep. Cistern walls carry a permanent gloss. In the enclosed machinery, water is hotter and under pressure; outside, it is generally no deeper than a hand. Nowhere in the open Level seems likely to dry, and nowhere seems likely to drown.

At ground level, the construction is dense enough to interrupt every view within a few dozen paces. Low housings stand nearly back to back. Bundles of insulated pipe pass between them, with narrow service aisles threaded through the gaps. Above are light catwalks and cable trays; below, open ducts and watercourses. Each layer is occupied. The distant vastness and the immediate crowding do not contradict one another: this near-touching arrangement continues for thousands of kilometers.

The first sign of the fauna is that the texture of the Level will not hold still.

What looked like grain in a ceramic plate separates into hundreds of small, jointed animals, their soft shells fitted close together. They drink condensation, rasp invisible films from the surface, and shift aside just far enough to avoid a descending foot. Larger, flattened creatures occupy the warm sides of engines, folded into such exact shapes that they resemble removable covers until they breathe. In the channels, apparent currents resolve into braids of finger-length eels. Their bodies slide continuously over one another while the water itself moves almost imperceptibly.

The air is populated too. Slow-winged animals turn above the digesters in broad, silent gyres, feeding on nutrient aerosols. Smaller fliers gather around vents, sometimes so densely that a plume of vapor appears to have developed a dark inner core. In roofed service courts, pale, low-slung beasts stand flank to flank, sleeping through the heat while lesser animals move over and beneath them.

There is no green anywhere. No reeds fringe the water, no moss finds the wet seams, no algae clouds the pans. The food web begins in sealed synthesis drums, where industrial power, water, air, and reclaimed organic matter become feed and microbial pastes. The animals distribute that production through the Level. They clear intake films, consume scale, carry heat between surfaces, and return their dead to collection sumps. They are not robots; they are born, they hunger, they bleed. Yet their kinds fit particular temperatures, apertures, and waste streams as precisely as gaskets. They descend from manufactured stock, and their wildness has never meant independence from the system.

Their abundance is not an outbreak. It is simply how full the world is.

For so much machinery and so many bodies, the Level is remarkably quiet. Engines are balanced and deeply housed, their noise reduced to a hum felt more than heard. Water falls into receiving troughs designed to prevent splashing. The animals have soft feet, muted wingbeats, and almost no calls. Close by, one hears a claw ticking on metal, a condensate drop striking a pan, the brief liquid friction of eels turning in a channel. At a distance, entire districts merge into a faint rain-like hiss. Silence here is not emptiness. It is crowding without clamor.

The Level is large enough to possess separate weather, but its weather does not travel. A white bank of condensation may stand permanently over one exchanger province, while a column of warm rain falls forever into another. Beyond it, under clearer daylight, mist rises from the same set of channels in the same slow configuration. Cloud shadows belong to particular districts. No front approaches. Weather is not an event here; it is part of the floor plan.

Nothing is frozen. Pistons reciprocate, belts carry identical components, pumps circulate their volumes, and animals feed, mate, crowd, and die. But no process accumulates into change. Water leaving a basin is replaced at the rate it leaves. Wear is overtaken by renewal. Birth and death keep the animal masses at the same pressure against the available surfaces. Heat is exported as quickly as it is produced. Even the rain returns through pipes to the cloud that releases it.

This is the source of the Level’s unsettling youth. Its sharp edges are not evidence that it was recently built. They are the visible face of an equilibrium so complete that erosion has nowhere to begin. Its wetness is not a flood in progress, its crowding is not growth, and its industry is not production toward some later abundance. The output of the Level is the Level.

Standing within it, one feels warmth through the ground, moisture on the skin, and the nearly weightless disturbance of animals closing around a new shadow. The daylight remains sensible. The machines remain quiet. The same rain stands at the horizon.

Nothing is waiting. Nothing is about to become something else.
