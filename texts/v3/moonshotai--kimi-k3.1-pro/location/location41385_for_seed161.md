## Iru, the Warm Hollow

The height must first be understood without the usual image of a peak. Iru stands at a staggering elevation, but there is no summit needle above it, no cirque of broken crags, nothing youthful enough to be jagged. The mountain is an immense convex back of blue-grey stone rising out of country already worn into long, soft swells. Its cliffs have become shoulders; its shoulders have become slopes; its slopes descend so gradually that the eye keeps mistaking them for distance. Age has not made the mountain low. It has made the height smooth.

Near the top, a single collapse basin interrupts that smoothness. It is perhaps four hundred paces across, shallow enough that a person on one rim can see the whole opposite side, and bounded by a low, rounded lip. This is Iru: basin, town, garden, hunting ground, and world. Beyond the rim, the bare mountain continues. Within it, construction and vegetation are packed so tightly that, from above, it is difficult to tell which is supporting which.

There is no water in Iru. Not merely no river or lake: no seep in the deepest cut chamber, no damp side to the stone, no frost in the cold shadows, no old channel awaiting an exceptional season. Nothing in the settlement anticipates rain. There are no gutters, cisterns, wells, or drains. Dust remains dust until it is bound with oil or resin. A tool is cleaned by scraping and ash; a floor, by sweeping. Even the vegetation is not wet. A cut stem yields a slow bead of amber fluid, viscous and faintly aromatic, then seals beneath a dry varnish. Life here carries its salts and performs its chemistry in oils and other dense fluids. Warmth keeps them moving.

That warmth comes from below. Deep fractures in the mountain conduct heat into the basin without flame, smoke, or volcanic display. The stone is simply warm, most of it no hotter than a wall that has stood all afternoon in sun. Along certain seams it becomes almost uncomfortable to touch. The altitude might otherwise have made the hollow severe; instead, its air is mild, dry, and still, with a faint mineral taste. Outside the rim the wind can pull heat from the skin, but even there the ground never quite cools.

The vegetation follows the warm seams first and the buildings second. It is not bright green. The leaves are soot-green, plum, bronze, and nearly black, thick-skinned and folded to expose as much surface as possible to the weak light. They overlap in dense mats, send down curtains of fine roots, and fill the spaces between roofs with interlaced canopies. Below them are wiry shrubs, hollow-stemmed herbs, and cushion growths no higher than a hand but so closely packed that nothing of the ground remains visible. Lushness here does not mean dripping abundance. It means that there is almost no bare interval: every vertical surface, ledge, fissure, and abandoned basket has been occupied.

The light is dim even before it enters the canopy. High mineral haze usually reduces the sun to a pale disc, and the basin opens away from the brightest part of the sky. At noon the public courts have the soft, brownish illumination of an overcast evening. The deepest lanes are darker still, lit only where a roof opening admits a column of dusty brightness. This would be too little for the gardens if they lived by sunlight alone, but the lowest crusts in their beds do not. Those crusts feed on slow chemical reactions in the warm mineral fractures. Insects and grazing animals consume them; the larger plants root among their remains. The whole ecology is patient enough to make use of faint light and faint heat.

The houses are dry-laid from the same stone as the basin, with wooden pegs, fiber lashings, and resin used where masonry alone will not hold. Timber is precious because the roof trees grow so slowly. A single dark beam may have served as a lintel, a loom frame, part of a bridge, and then a lintel again. Most buildings stand two or three levels high, though the levels do not always agree with one another. A doorway may open onto a neighbor’s roof; a stair may pass through what was once an upper room; a lane may narrow to a shoulder-width passage beneath a slab laid centuries ago. Old streets survive under the present floors as storage chambers, heat passages, or garden pits.

In extent Iru is smaller than many villages. In occupation it is unmistakably urban. There are people who spend their lives fitting stone and never tend a garden, seed-pressers who buy all their fiber, vent-keepers, granary recorders, makers of digging sticks and scrapers, specialists in the tuning of signal stones. There are communal storehouses, covered work yards, sleeping galleries, and a market where carved stone vessels, pressed seed cakes, resin cord, dry medicinal leaves, and bundles of rare feathers change hands. Complexity lies in arrangement and accumulated skill, not machinery. There are no wheels in the lanes and no engines anywhere. Metal is scarce: a little native copper, cold-hammered into needles, fasteners, and small bells. Almost the only sharp edges in Iru are freshly knapped tools.

The true infrastructure carries air and warmth. Natural fissures have been extended with stone ducts beneath floors and behind walls. Sliding slabs regulate them. Warm air can be led into a nursery chamber, under a seed-drying floor, or through a public room where old people sleep on the coldest nights of the long, mild year. Stale air is drawn out through high shafts. Each shaft sounds slightly different. The granaries rest over a low, even drone; the press lane has a double tick in its foundation; the western roofs are crossed by a thin, reed-like note. In the dimness these tones serve as addresses. A blockage is often heard before any room has time to cool.

Custom is part of the same infrastructure. No heat vent may be permanently sealed. No root mat on the rim may be cut, because those mats bind the last loose stone and shelter the basin from wind. No new foundation may be laid beyond the ancient foundation markers. Fire is confined to small, thick-walled stone boxes, and the tiny oil lamps used indoors are shielded on every side. Most cooking is done on heated slabs. A town whose buildings are full of dry fiber and oil-rich vegetation has learned to treat flame as a useful but untrustworthy specialist.

Animals are everywhere, and most of them are small. Dry-shelled beetles tick inside the leaf mats. Ash-colored moths flatten themselves against warm walls. Little lizards occupy the spaces beneath stair treads, emerging to take insects from the mortar lines. Furred gliders nest in the roof trees and raid the seed stores with admirable persistence. At the rim, dark birds hang almost motionless in the thin wind, then drop into the basin after beetles or unattended food. Larger hunters are rare but not absent; their cries occasionally pass over the roofs at night, and for a while every nesting chamber goes quiet.

The town did not merely intrude upon this ecology. It became one of its structures, like a nest that had learned masonry. The buildings concentrate shelter, the ducts concentrate warmth, and the gardens concentrate food. Waste is returned to the garden pits, where dry decomposition and diligent insects reduce it over years. People tend some animal species, tolerate many more, and weave elaborate seals around the granaries against the rest. At any moment there are far more wild hearts than human ones within the rim.

All of this makes Iru loud. Not with one dominating roar, but with layers that the hard, concave basin returns again and again: insects sawing and clicking, seedpods knocking together, wings batting against leaves, the scuffle of small feet in ducts, copper bells, stone hammers, the padded thunder of the presses, bargaining, sung messages, children repeating work calls incorrectly. Wind worries the rim with a low hiss. Beneath it, the heat shafts hold their chord. When workshop labor stops, the animal noise does not replace it so much as reveal how much had already been there. There is no splash or murmur of running liquid underneath. Even at its fullest, the soundscape is dry.

Faces are often difficult to read in the lanes, so texture and pitch carry much of the information that light would elsewhere. Door lintels are carved with different ridges. Public stairs have contrasting edges underfoot. A skilled vent-keeper can cross half the town with eyes shut and name each gallery by its resonance. The air smells of hot mineral dust, crushed resin, toasted seed, old fiber, and animal musk. It never smells of rain.

Yet beneath the voices and wingbeats, almost nothing in Iru hurries. A roof tree may take a lifetime to thicken by the width of a palm. A stair becomes noticeably more hollow over several generations. The mountain loses grains to the wind, but there is no longer much edge for the wind to work upon. Houses are repaired, absorbed, or rebuilt on the same footprint; Iru does not produce picturesque ruins so much as foundations with unusually complicated histories. New construction is usually replacement. An important repair may be planned by a grandparent, prepared by a child, and finished by someone who never knew either of them.

From beyond the rim, the settlement can almost be overlooked: a dark shallow hollow on the smooth high stone, a few low roofs, no plume of smoke. At the edge, however, warm air rises against the face, and the hidden density declares itself in sound. Iru is not waiting for rain, rescue, expansion, or ruin. It has reached a small, crowded form of equilibrium—dim, dry, ancient, and loudly alive—held together leaf by leaf, stone by stone, and note by note.
