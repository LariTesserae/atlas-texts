## The Seep Terraces

From the rim, the plateau looks almost dry. Its water is too dispersed to gather into one shining river, and its vegetation too patchy to conceal the dark stone. Only after descending onto it does the wetness become apparent: in the shine along a fracture, in moss cushioning a shaded ledge, in the clear water standing a finger’s breadth below the lip of a hollow.

The high country rises in three broad, irregular steps above a hazy lowland. It is geologically young. The volcanic rock still breaks into sharp slabs, short columns, and tilted plates whose edges have scarcely begun to round. Cliffs end abruptly rather than easing into talus. There are few deep valleys and no mature network of streams; erosion has drawn only the first lines on the surface. Between the harder flows lie paler beds of compacted ash, exposed in places as chalky bands stained ochre by seeping water.

Rain enters the open joints and travels unseen until it meets one of those ash beds. Then whole hillsides begin to weep. Water emerges not always as springs, but as long damp seams, beads gathering on overhangs, thin films slipping down vertical rock. Some of it disappears into the next fracture. Some collects in fault-bounded depressions, forming shallow pools and marshes with uncertain, grassy margins. Along the outer escarpment, several of these outlets combine into narrow falls. From a distance they are only white threads against the cliff; even up close, their sound is more hiss than thunder.

The elevation gives long views but not alpine cold. The air is mild and humid, warm enough that rain on stone produces no chill mist except in the early morning. On exposed afternoons, the upper surfaces of the basalt hold the sun’s heat, while shaded seep rock remains cool to the hand. Cloud sometimes moves through the valleys below without touching the plateau. At other times the whole region stands under ordinary, softened daylight, with distant ridges visible as successive grey-green shapes.

Soil is thin except where ash and plant matter have accumulated in hollows. Consequently, the vegetation is intensely local. A bare expanse of fractured stone may end at a pocket of green so dense that its interior cannot be seen. Sedges and rushes crowd the marsh edges; ferns occupy the seep lines; moss spreads across rock wherever water moves slowly enough not to strip it away. Sheltered basins hold small groves of low, waxy-leaved trees and scattered tree ferns. Their trunks rise close together, but the groves themselves are widely separated, islands of shade surrounded by open ground.

Away from water, the plants become smaller and more deliberate: wiry shrubs rooted in cracks, grey lichens on exposed faces, cushions of fine grass where wind has dropped a little soil. From above, the plateau is therefore not uniformly lush. It is a sparse pattern of lavish places.

No animal life disturbs that pattern. No birds cross the open marsh, no insects hang over the pools, and no fish make contradictory ripples beneath their reflections. Leaves lie where they fall until rain, fungi, and bacteria reduce them. Stems grow without browsing, and the margins of paths remain untrampled except by water. The plant community depends largely on wind, spores, gravity, and flowing water. Its stillness is especially noticeable beside the clearest pools, where a perfect reflection may be broken only by a single drop from the rock above.

The town occupies the middle step, where a dependable spring line crosses a shelf broad enough to build on. At first it is difficult to distinguish from the terrain. Its walls use the same basalt as the outcrops, and its roofs follow the slope in low, angular increments. Gradually the order becomes visible: retaining walls running along contours, paved stairs crossing them, repeated doorways, the straight dark line of a conduit passing through otherwise broken ground.

It is too extensive and too organized to be a village, but it never becomes dense. Several clusters of buildings are separated by open shelves of rock, wet gardens, and unfenced public ground. Each cluster consists of small rooms arranged around courts. The limited span of a hand-set stone roof keeps the rooms narrow; larger spaces are made by adding courts and covered walks rather than by attempting halls. There are no dominating towers or avenues. The largest structure is a communal cistern, broad and shallow, its sides built up only high enough to protect the water from windblown debris.

The construction is skilled without being elaborate. Basalt was split along existing joints and laid mostly dry, with the best-fitting stones reserved for corners and thresholds. Softer ash stone was cut into channel liners and settling basins. Clay seals the cisterns. Timber appears sparingly in door leaves, roof supports, and short footbridges. Where a watercourse crosses a path, a single slab serves as a bridge; there are no arches and little evidence of labor spent on decoration. Most surfaces still bear the marks of hand tools.

Water gives the settlement its plan. A contour channel brings seepage from higher ground, moving so gently that fine sediment has time to settle in a series of small basins. From there, narrower branches descend through the districts. Some supply covered tanks; some pass through washing courts or garden terraces before returning to open drains. Overflow is carried back to a natural marsh below the town. Streets cross the slope by stairs, but the water routes prefer longer, easier gradients. The result is a subtle interleaving of straight human lines and patient hydraulic ones.

Even the houses respond to seepage. Their floors stand slightly above the courts. Narrow slots behind the walls intercept water emerging from the slope, and projecting roof stones keep drip lines away from doorways. A wall may end against an outcrop rather than continue through it; a channel may disappear into a natural fracture and reappear lower down. Nothing suggests an attempt to make the plateau dry. The builders instead decided where wetness would be useful, where it could be tolerated, and where it had to be led away.

The town stands empty, though it has not collapsed into ruin. Roofs remain seated, channels hold their courses, and the broad steps of the cistern terrace are intact. No smoke rises, and there are no domestic animals, stored tools, or scattered belongings to give the absence a date. Nor is there evidence of catastrophe. The emptiness simply allows the construction to be read at the same pace as the rock and water.

In full daylight, the palette is restrained: dry basalt blue-grey, wet basalt nearly black, pale channels, iron stains, and concentrated greens. Sunlight on the open shelves can be bright, but humidity softens the farthest distances. After rain, the smell is primarily mineral—wet stone and exposed clay—with a greener odor near the fern beds. Cloud shadows cross the whole region at once, briefly making the town vanish into the plateau again.

The quiet has unusual depth. Close to a wall, one can hear water ticking inside a fissure. In the town, individual drips answer from cisterns at different distances. Wind produces a faint dry movement in the sedges and almost nothing among the stone buildings. There is no background animal chorus to bind these sounds together, so each remains separate, with silence between.

Yet the landscape is not frozen. Water runs continuously, fronds lift, cloud gathers and disperses, and wet surfaces dry unevenly in the sun. Its stillness comes from equilibrium rather than inactivity. Pool levels return to the same dark marks on their margins. Springs feed the channels at a modest, steady rate, and the town’s overflows return water to the marshes without flooding them. Rain, drainage, and evaporation appear balanced; growth and decay are similarly unobtrusive.

The youth of the plateau lies in its preserved form, not in continuing violence. No fresh eruption threatens it, and no great river is yet cutting through it. Sharp rock, incomplete drainage, young soil, and an empty, orderly town coexist without a sense of impending event.

Seen finally from the upper rim, the settlement becomes only a fine grey seam across the middle terrace. The marshes separate into scattered patches, and the water nearly disappears. What remains most strongly is the spacing: between groves, between districts, between one drop and the next. The plateau feels open rather than barren, wet rather than flooded, and ancient in its calm despite the freshness of every edge.
