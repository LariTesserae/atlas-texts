## Anvess, the Unfinished Basin

From above, Anvess looks almost vacant: a hundred kilometres of low plain beneath a sky the colour of weak bronze. Three built concentrations interrupt it, along with a few lines too slender and straight to be roads. Dark green seams wander across the ground like cracks in a tile. There is no broad river, no forest covering the horizon, no mountain wall enclosing the basin. Even the largest escarpments are steps rather than mountains.

The emptiness is not an illusion of distance. There are stretches where, for twenty kilometres, the largest visible object is a survey marker. A condenser vane may stand alone on the plain, turning almost imperceptibly. Hours later, another appears. Between them: warm mineral ground, shallow dust, the occasional track of something that has crossed without stopping.

Then the basin swells into its afternoon voice, and the emptiness becomes an instrument.

The sound never entirely stops. At its lowest it is a pressure in the soles of the feet, a deep mechanical churning with no identifiable direction. As it rises, higher tones emerge from faults and slab fields: a grinding like enormous teeth, a wire-sharp ringing, a succession of booms too slow to be thunder. At the daily maximum, speech is impossible at arm’s length. Unprotected ears quickly become useless. Water jumps in open cups. Dust lifts in motionless air.

The inhabitants attribute it to the founding engines, although no exposed engine has ever been found. The engines were inferred from the making of Anvess, and their continuation is inferred from the fact that Anvess is still being made. New ground arrives without volcanic heat. A level patch begins to vibrate; the air tightens; there is a sound that seems to pass through the body rather than enter it. Afterwards, a seam of cool, unfamiliar stone may stand where plain had been.

Nothing has had time to weather. The eastern slab field is all conchoidal fractures and tilted planes, its edges keen enough to cut through a boot. Fault walls show clean mineral colours, unsoftened by soil. There are no rounded beds of gravel because there have been no lasting streams to round them. Even the oldest dust lies thinly, unable to decide whether it is becoming sediment. Survey maps carry dates, and the dates matter.

Senn, the largest settlement, stands where three green seams approach without quite meeting. It is a city in section rather than in skyline: layered ceramic shells, stacked arcades, covered courts, cistern chambers, freight levels, and inhabited terraces arranged around three condenser spires. Much of its volume is turned inward. From the plain it appears modest, almost provisional. Inside, it has markets, water offices, address systems, transit schedules, and the dense administrative patience of a place expecting to endure.

Its gardens are not ornament. Ferns and black-leaved veinwood cover the outer terraces, feeding moisture into root mats that serve as living reservoirs. Fresh construction is often planted before it is fully enclosed. This does not make Senn look ruined; the pruning lines are exact, and roots are diverted around doors and conduits with municipal precision.

Silence is part of the architecture. It is not produced by thick walls. The inhabited shells are nested within field boundaries whose physics the city’s own engineers describe cautiously. At an open doorway, the basin’s roar ends along a plane no thicker than light. One step inward, and a cup can be heard ticking as it cools. One step outward, and the sound strikes the chest again. People pause at thresholds not to adjust their eyes, but to let their bodies believe the change.

The rest of the urban region is connected without being filled in. Senn’s short gates lead to the western condenser lines, the southern reservoir house, and the small research station at the slab field. A traveller enters an ordinary-looking arch and emerges thirty kilometres away; no vehicle crosses the intervening ground, and no road is required. Freight moves the same way. Distance, in Anvess, is partly a civic service.

Consequently, the region can be densely administered and almost completely empty at once. An outpost may contain several thousand residents in stacked interior chambers while its visible presence is a single low building and one mast. Between such places, the plain remains materially untouched.

Above everything, an orbital shade reduces noon to a prolonged, dim afternoon. Its machinery is too distant to see, but occasionally a black arc crosses the sun and the light weakens further. Shadows are broad and uncertain. White surfaces appear pewter-coloured; leaves are nearly black; water provides most of the brief flashes of brightness.

The shade keeps the young basin from overheating, but it does not make it cool. The stone remains warm after sunset. Rain, when it comes, is warm enough to feel spilled rather than fallen. The open air is usually dry enough to tighten the lips, while a few steps into a vegetated seam can bring the smell of steam, sap, wet iron, and crushed green stems. Anvess does not have a single climate so much as many narrow ones lying side by side.

There is no permanent river. Rain arrives in small, mobile cells, sometimes only fifty or a hundred metres across. One can watch a cell approach as a wavering dark curtain while dust remains dry on either side. It crosses a ridge, writes a brief silver line down every fracture, and dissolves. Most storms last minutes.

More water comes from condensation. At dawn, fresh stone carries a transient bloom of moisture. The condenser vanes gather far more than their size should allow; their visible ceramics are only the accessible portions of devices whose working volumes lie, in the engineers’ phrase, “adjacent” to the installation. Water appears along their lower edges in clear threads and is led away before the open air can take it back.

Elsewhere, water survives by accident: an opaque pool in the lowest corner of the old southern reservoir; a cup-shaped fracture holding two days’ rain; droplets sealed beneath moss; a temporary skin of moisture on the shaded side of a slab. These small amounts support an extravagant amount of life.

The green seams are lush almost to excess. Fan-ferns taller than a person pleat their fronds so that dew runs toward central cups. Bladder moss swells from a black crust into translucent cushions. Veinwood sends pale roots through the mineral fractures, joining isolated wet places into networks that can outlast the drying of any one of them. After rain, growth is audible in sheltered places: sheaths splitting, fronds unlocking, roots ticking through dust.

Between the seams, the ground can remain completely bare. Thus Anvess is dry by area but generous in biomass. Its vegetation is not spread evenly across the landscape; it is crowded into long, narrow opportunities.

The animals follow the same arrangement. At humid dusk, matte-winged moths rise from the fern beds in numbers that resemble smoke. Mirrorbacks—small, hard-shelled grazers—move over the moss in shifting thousands, their brief reflections making the vegetation seem to glitter from within. Rain eels lie dormant as tough, gel-like threads in empty depressions, then uncoil when a pool forms, feed frantically, and lay before it shrinks.

Many animals have largely abandoned airborne hearing. Their skulls are thick, while their feet, jaws, or ventral plates are extraordinarily sensitive to vibration. Others use the founding roar as cover. The long-legged listeners, pale predators of the outer fern beds, prefer to hunt during engine surges, when the mirrorbacks’ substrate senses are overwhelmed. A listener can stand motionless among bare stones for hours, then cross twenty metres between one ground tremor and the next.

Flying animals cross the open gaps, but even their migrations emphasize the space. A stream of moths may pour from one seam, dwindle to a thread over the empty plain, and vanish long before reaching the next green line. For much of the day, nothing visible crosses at all.

Toward evening, a rain cell may pass along Senn’s western seam. For a few minutes it creates a complete little weather system: warm silver rain on one side, dry bronze dust on the other. Ferns open. Moss darkens and then turns translucent. A depression becomes a pool no deeper than a hand, and rain eels rise through it. Mirrorbacks crowd the newly softened growth. Somewhere under the fern litter, a listener extends one deliberate leg.

The rain’s hiss is immense, yet it is only another layer over the engines. In Senn, the condenser spires adjust by a fraction of a degree. Cistern levels rise less than a finger. Beyond the slab field, a deep boom announces some small addition to the world, and another map becomes provisional.

Then the cell comes apart. Within half an hour the exposed stone is dry again. Water remains in roots, shells, closed chambers, and the bodies of animals. The pool contracts around its eels. Steam lifts briefly and is taken by the air.

From a distance, almost nothing has happened: a few structures, a few green seams, a vast low land under a darkened sky. But in Anvess, habitat is often a schedule, and a great deal can occur in a place that is habitable for only eight minutes.

The basin does not oppose city and wilderness. Both occupy the same narrow opportunities, separated by enormous reserves of nothing. Its gardens are lush, its animals numerous, its infrastructure regional. Its dominant material remains space.

And the space rings.
