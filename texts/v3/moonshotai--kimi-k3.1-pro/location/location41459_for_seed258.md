## The Unfinished Height

From a distance, it could almost be mistaken for an ordinary high valley. A broad basin lies open under plain daylight, its far rim paled by many kilometres of air. Long terraces descend toward sheets of shallow water. Side valleys branch away behind green promontories, and beyond the outer rim the land drops so sharply that the height becomes a sensation before it becomes a view.

The light is reassuringly normal. It shows greens as greens, casts soft shadows, and reflects white from the water. The air is warm despite the elevation, neither alpine nor sultry. Everything looks recently washed.

Then the materials become clear, and the valley stops resembling geology.

There is no stone beneath the water. There is no soil beneath the terraces. The level ground is a continuous living skin, firm but faintly yielding, crossed by veins that thicken and converge toward the slopes. The escarpments are pale vascular wood, laid in great load-bearing curves. What appear from afar to be boulders are swollen root-nodes; what look like gravel bars are dense fields of small, coiled growths. Even the height is constructed. Beyond the rim, instead of a mountainside, vast buttresses descend out of sight, branching and rejoining around empty spaces.

Nothing here was assembled with tools. Nothing was cut, fired, fastened, or carved. The basin is built in the way a shell is built: material deposited into exact form by the living thing that needs it. Its arches are continuous growth. Its conduits are stems. Its catchments are leaves whose surfaces have deepened and hardened without ceasing to be leaves. There are no tool marks because there has never been a tool, and no separate maker because the landscape is its own work.

This total vegetation has not produced a crowded world. It is not a forest, and only in a few places is it even a thicket. Most of the basin is openness stretched over green substrate: broad, nearly level fields where nothing rises higher than a hand, interrupted by shining marsh and long intervals of empty distance.

Across one such field there may be only a single half-closed vault of ivory stem, its upper ribs exposed to the sky. In the next valley, a ring of tall cups stands around dry green pavement. Farther on, one buttressed stalk leans above the water, supporting nothing. A terraced rise climbs in enormous, even steps and stops before reaching the rim. These presences are so widely scattered that each seems to have been given its own portion of the horizon.

The emptiness is not bareness. It is part of the construction.

At close range, every surface is wet. Clear water lies in a thin film over the leaf-ground, gathers along veins, and fills the basins to precise, unvarying lines. It seeps from high seams in warm white threads and disappears into apertures between roots. Pressing the ground brings more water briefly to the surface; releasing it leaves the same smooth skin as before. There is marsh here, but no mud. There are dripping ledges, but no dark stain beneath them.

The air smells of rain, sap, and crushed green stems. It does not smell of rot. There are no dead leaves underfoot, no soft layers of old growth, no litter becoming earth. Litter would be the beginning of soil, and soil is one of the things this place has never made.

Nor has water worn anything away. Below the high walls there is no scree. The pools contain no silt. Channels have smooth, rounded edges, but they have not been smoothed by flow; they were grown that way. Elsewhere the country is raw in a different sense. Terraces terminate in swollen lips. Arches end in pale knobs of unexpanded tissue. Basins have one finished wall and three low ridges that seem ready to rise. Whole slopes have the blunt, tender appearance of forms not yet given their final surface.

Nothing looks damaged. Nothing looks ancient. Ruin would require completion followed by time, and neither has occurred. The landscape is not eroded but embryonic, its unfinished state visible at every scale.

It is also extraordinarily loud.

The sound arrives as a sustained pressure rather than a collection of events. Water moving through the hollow rim produces a low roar that seems to come from beneath the entire basin. Thin, wet leaf-vanes hold high, continuous tones. The open cups boom softly, each at its own pitch, while hidden constrictions in the rootwork add a hard, repetitive knocking. On the empty flats these sounds travel enormous distances. A place can appear utterly vacant and still be filled with the noise of rivers, strained wood, and rain striking foliage—though no rain is visibly falling and the nearest river may be several valleys away.

The loudness never quite swells into a climax or relaxes into quiet. There are no gusts behind it, no sudden release of water, no beginning and no decay. After a while, the ear stops waiting for something to happen and begins to distinguish geography within the sound: the deep note of the outer buttresses, the silver hiss of the cistern fields, the hollow resonance of an unfinished arch.

Almost none of it is animal.

Fauna are so rare that they feel like exceptions the landscape has neglected to correct. In an occasional warm cistern hangs a small, colourless swimmer, its internal organs visible through a body shaped like a folded strip of tissue. It may turn slowly in the water or fasten itself to a green wall, but it does not appear to feed. No young or old forms are found. There are no bones below the basins, no shells along their margins, no remains caught in the roots. Elsewhere there may be a soft, many-legged creature motionless within a hollow stem, kilometres from the next of its kind.

There is no birdsong above the roar, no insect hum close to the ground. The plants do not flower for pollinators or fruit for dispersers. The animals neither maintain the ecosystem nor trouble it. They seem less like inhabitants than like an unfinished consideration of animal life.

The plant ecology itself is almost entirely internal. It is difficult to decide whether the region contains one organism or many. The green pavement can thicken without transition into a root buttress; a buttress can hollow itself into a water conduit; the conduit can open into a cup rimmed with photosynthetic tissue. Different forms perform different functions, but they do not compete for space or light. Matter passes between tissue and water without becoming waste. Growth does not produce surplus. Nothing needs a next generation.

Gradually, the stillness beneath all this activity becomes apparent.

The water moves, but no water level changes. The membranes tremble, but none tears or dries. The pale growth-fronts remain pale without expanding. Sound continues without exhausting its source. Shadows do not lengthen, and the ordinary daylight never turns toward evening. Warmth is the same on the high rim and beside the lowest pool. There are no seasons concealed in the buds, no weather approaching beyond the valleys, no slow erosion waiting to give the place a history.

Motion is permitted; consequence is not.

This is not the stillness of a snapshot. It is too wet, too resonant, too visibly alive for that. It is a frozen equilibrium: every local process balanced so exactly against every other that the region as a whole cannot develop. A drop may fall forever without making the pool deeper. A half-built rib may remain forever at the point where the next one would begin. The country has duration without accumulation.

From the highest terraces, the scale of this condition becomes overwhelming. Tens of kilometres of warm green openness lie under the unchanging light, with only a few isolated forms rising above the water. Every side valley appears poised to become something more specific—a forest, a marsh, a close-grown range of living mountains. None will. Those possibilities are present in the shapes, not waiting in the future.

It would be misleading to call the basin young. Youth implies that maturity will follow. It is better to say that everything here remains early.

Everything has been made. Nothing has been completed. And the loud water goes on announcing a beginning whose arrival would only disturb it.
