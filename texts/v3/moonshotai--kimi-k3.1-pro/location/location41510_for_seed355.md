## The City the Rain Keeps

At the bottom of a broad, heat-holding basin, where the land has worn itself almost level, the rain has made a city.

It spreads far beyond any single quarter: low halls standing in a clay flat, a chain of cisterns an hour farther on, then long empty ground before another paved court or raised walk. There is too much of it to be a structure and too little in any one place to be a crowd. Between its districts, the basin remains open—dark silt, shallow pooling water, an occasional rounded ridge of stone. The city’s emptiness is not something that happened to it. It is part of its proportions.

No tool made the buildings. What resembles masonry is continuous mineral growth, each apparent course a thin skin laid over an older one. Walls curve into their foundations without a seam. Paving runs beneath doorways and becomes the floor beyond. There are streets with curbs and gutters, covered walks, bridges scarcely wider than a foot, stairs descending into buried courts. Older passages can be seen beneath newer terraces where water has opened a section through them. Yet there are no bricks to pry loose, no beams, no metal, no cord, no marks of cutting. There are thresholds but no doors, channels but no sluices, rooms but no furniture. Every form is a consequence; nothing is an instrument.

The material is mostly brown-black siltstone bound with pale silica and lime. Where it stays wet, it is dark, slick, and faintly warm. Where water evaporates, it blooms with a white crust that crumbles into powder and is carried away by the next rain. Corners have been softened for ages. Stairs are cupped in the middle, not by feet but by thin repeated films of running water. Nothing looks newly made, and very little looks broken. Growth and dissolution are close enough to balance that the whole settlement has the composure of an old riverbed.

Rain begins the construction. The water arrives heavy with dissolved minerals. Where it runs freely, it scours passages clear; where it clings to an edge, it deposits another microscopic layer. An edge thickens into a rib, a rib into a wall, a wall into the side of a court. Over immense periods, the drainage writes enclosures around itself, then has to find new ways through them. A passage may narrow for centuries while a seep elsewhere patiently opens another. The city is therefore neither unfinished nor ruined. It is in its own state.

The heat never leaves the basin. It rises gently from the deep sediments and collects beneath the lightless air. Rain is lukewarm and brings no relief; it only makes the heat more intimate. On open ground, the water table lies close enough that a firm step can squeeze moisture up through apparently dry clay. Buried walls remain slightly cooler than the air, so they weep even between rains. Beads gather on their surfaces, join, and follow old vertical seams. Some cisterns fill from below. Some roofs drip upward into their own stone pores before releasing the water again days later.

The air smells of wet chalk, warm dust, and a faint sulfuric bitterness. After long seepage, there is also a clean, almost sweet odor from the basin’s sparse vegetation opening.

Darkness is nearly complete. There are no visible stars, no lit horizon, no luminous growths along the water. Wet surfaces can sometimes be distinguished from dry ones because their black is softer, less absolute. Beyond arm’s reach, architecture becomes a matter of warmth, resonance, and the changing resistance of the ground.

At long intervals, usually near the end of a rain cycle, a weak gray visibility passes through the basin. It does not arrive from any direction and casts no shadows. For less than a minute, the whole region can be apprehended: the low, rounded rim; the immense flats; the scattered paved precincts; the exposed connections between streets of different ages. From above, the city would look less like a settlement than like the circulation of one, laid bare after everything else had been removed. Then the grayness withdraws without dimming. It simply ceases to be possible to see.

The dry city has a settled geography. Distances are long, slopes slight, and the quarters remain where the last cycle left them. Once sufficient water enters the streets, however, another organization becomes authoritative.

Downstream begins to matter more than downhill.

A stair may be descended into a court that visibly lies above it. Water will then run from that court and climb the same stair, smoothly and without turbulence. A channel ending against a blank wall may cause a cistern across an empty flat to rise, though no buried conduit joins them. During the fullest rain, distant districts become adjacent along the water’s routes. A walker following runoff could pass beneath the same mineral bridge twice without turning around, the second time approaching from the other district.

These are not random distortions. The connections occur in the same order, cycle after cycle. The city has two ancient forms: a sparse, almost ordinary dry arrangement, and a wet watershed in which remoteness, elevation, and enclosure are temporarily revised. A useful map would have to be a schedule.

When the outflow begins, the districts draw apart again. Water leaves some pools by draining and others by simply ceasing to occupy their depth. Streets lengthen. Courts that had been effectively neighboring become separated by an hour of bare ground. For a while the pavements remain wet, and sounds continue to travel along connections that distance no longer supports: a drop falling in one quarter may be heard inside a cistern far away, not as an echo but as the same small event.

Life is present, but thinly and without animals. There are no insects above the pools, no worms in the silt, no blind fish in the deep cisterns, no shells or burrows or tracks. Even the oldest buried layers contain none. The city offers countless narrow habitats, and nothing comes to use them.

The vegetation is nearly mineral in appearance. Pale felts occupy a few seep lines; waxy, translucent fans stand alone beside certain overflow stones, sometimes hundreds of paces from the next. None is green. They take energy from chemical reactions in the warm water, tightening to brittle flakes during the long seep and opening after rain. At their fullest, the fans release fine threads into the current. The threads appear in pools with no surface connection, following the same impossible watershed as everything else. Where the growth dies, it leaves a delicate lattice that becomes part of the next wall.

Most of the time, the basin is profoundly quiet. Rain falls with a soft, discontinuous hiss rather than a roar. Individual drops strike stone at long intervals. Warm paving ticks faintly as it adjusts to a new saturation. Once or twice in a cycle, a deep cistern takes water with a hollow intake that seems to come from farther below than the basin should allow. There is no animal murmur, no foliage in wind, no distant call to establish scale.

Still, the place is never inactive. Water condenses, climbs, deposits, and dissolves. Walls thicken by almost nothing. The sparse plants open and close. Heat moves through the paving. Wet geometry gathers the city inward and then releases it. Its activity never becomes bustle; its age never becomes stillness.

It has urban extent without population, infrastructure without use, and complexity without intention. A cistern keeps water for no one. A stair provides passage that nothing requires. A covered walk gives shelter from rain that falls on no inhabitant.

The city has not survived its builders. It has arrived at equilibrium without them, repeating the same patient processes in the hot dark. When the rain returns, the basin will remember its connections in greater detail. When the water recedes, those connections will loosen, but the city will remain—sparse, weathered, and quietly continuing to become itself.
