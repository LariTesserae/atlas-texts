## The Thirst Engine

Twelve metres beneath a plain of dry, black tuff lies a chamber seventy paces long, though no one crossing it can see from one end to the other. A narrow stair descends through a cleft in the rock, its iron treads alternating between shallow and deep, as if made for several different kinds of visitor. The surface wind does not follow. Below the sixth step comes the heat; below the tenth, the noise; only near the bottom do shapes separate themselves from the darkness.

The chamber is roughly oval, with a ceiling that rises and falls between four and eleven metres. Its walls are basalt, but little of the basalt remains angular. Every edge has been softened into long flutes and shallow scallops. Some curves resemble water erosion, although the hollows high above the floor are dry and dusty. Others converge around projecting bolts and pipe mouths, wearing the stone away in perfect, dark halos. There is no fresh rubble. Old fractures have become rounded seams filled with brown mineral dust. Even the sharpest break in the entry stair has been polished blunt.

Nothing here looks newly ruined. Nothing looks maintained, either.

The air is hot enough to make the railing painful to hold. It is not continuously steamy; most of the time it feels almost dry, carrying the smells of heated dust, wet iron, ozone, and a bitter green sap. Moisture appears locally and without warning: a film on one pipe while an identical pipe beside it remains dry, a cool patch against the back of the neck, a brief slickness on stone that is gone within minutes. Sweat dries at the edges of clothing before it can drip.

Light comes from six caged amber lamps, only four of which still work, and from a dull red glow inside the engine housing. At surface noon, a gray blade of daylight enters through the stairwell and reaches partway down the wall. It never touches the floor. The chamber’s colors are consequently subdued and close-valued: black-green foliage, brown copper, bone-colored ceramic, dark red mineral scale. Pale objects seem less illuminated than briefly uncovered.

At the center stands the machine from which the place takes its name.

It is not large by the standards of whatever built it: three horizontal cylinders on a common bed, a flywheel somewhat taller than a person, a bank of cracked gauges, and copper windings wrapped in insulation that has partly become stone-hard. Pipes leave it in several directions and enter the walls without flanges, as though the rock had closed around them. The flywheel turns once every nine minutes. Its rim moves with such steadiness that the eye tends to erase the motion. Somewhere inside the casing, however, smaller mechanisms are hammering, scraping, and forcing pressure through narrow passages.

The noise arrives through the floor as much as through the air. There is a fundamental tone too low to locate, a hard repeated knock, and a thin hiss that seems to come from just behind the ears. At intervals, a valve opens and adds a shriek that makes the lamp cages vibrate. Speech is possible only at close range. Yet none of this suggests rapid activity. The engine’s sounds are urgent; its processes are not.

Around it, and increasingly beyond it, grows the vegetation.

First come root mats, dense and springy, crossing the floor in braided ridges. Then waist-high fans of thick leaves, their upper surfaces dusty and nearly black, their undersides veined with muted copper. Among them stand slender trunks with translucent joints, looped canes, and curtains of narrow foliage suspended from old cable runs. Some plants have followed pipes so closely that they appear to have been installed beside them. Others pass straight through obsolete brackets without breaking them, having grown slowly around every obstruction.

The growth is lush but not expansive. It fills the chamber rather than trying to leave it. Leaves overlap in layers, roots occupy the spaces beneath roots, and the remaining passages are narrow enough that shoulders brush growth on both sides. Open ground occurs mainly as bare ribs between masses of vegetation.

These open ribs are the key to the room.

The engine’s sound has a stationary geography. In certain places its reflections reinforce one another; in others they nearly cancel. The loud zones have remained almost fixed for ages, and the chamber has been organized around them. Where the strongest vibrations meet the walls, the basalt is scoured and scalloped. Where quieter pockets persist, dust has settled into low banks, roots have taken hold, and foliage has accumulated. The densest thickets occupy acoustic hollows. A root-wrapped recess beside the eastern wall is unexpectedly quiet, although the pipes enclosing it can be felt buzzing through the back. Three paces away, the full force of the noise returns.

Loudness here is not an event. It is a form of weather, old enough to have become terrain.

The plants make little use of daylight. Their darkest leaves turn slightly away from the stairwell’s noon beam. Their finest roots enter seams in old electrical insulation and spread across warm metal, drawing on leaking current and on the steep chemical gradients around the engine. Heat is stored in honey-colored pith within the trunks. The vegetation therefore grows most vigorously not near the light, but near the machine’s inefficiencies: around corroded contacts, beneath faulty windings, beside pipes that lose warmth into the rock.

It also gathers nearly all the available water.

The engine draws vapor from some deeper, hotter seam. Most of that vapor remains inside its closed circuit, but worn seals release a little into the chamber. The broad leaves intercept it. Their upper faces may be hot and dry while their undersides are several degrees cooler and beaded with condensation. Droplets travel along the veins into hollow petioles, then down to the roots. A plant can hold its collected water for a long time, sealed behind waxy valves that open only when the surrounding root mat dries beyond a certain point.

There is one visible pool, beneath a failed condenser near the western end. It is no wider than a shield and nowhere deeper than a finger. Its surface is black, still, and filmed with copper-colored dust. A few steps away, the ground is dry enough to powder between the hands.

Occasionally the engine’s pressure cycle changes. The alteration is slight, but the whole chamber answers. The fundamental tone rises; leaves turn by almost imperceptible degrees; beads of moisture gather along the ceiling flutes. If enough has accumulated, the ceiling releases it in a rain lasting less than a minute.

The rain does not fall evenly. The standing sound divides it into sparse, nearly straight columns. Water strikes some root crowns repeatedly while the floor between them remains dusty. Other drops hesitate on leaf margins, flatten, and tremble before choosing a direction. Afterward, the chamber smells briefly of minerals and crushed stems. Then the visible dampness withdraws into pores, pipes, and plant tissue, leaving only a darker color on the stone.

Scattered through the growth are the remains of attendance: a twelve-metre length of narrow rail disappearing beneath roots; a maintenance booth hardly larger than a cupboard; three porcelain fuse holders; a wire spool used as the base of a lamp; a gauge whose glass has been etched opaque by heat. There are no larger buildings and no sign of habitation. The booth contains a shallow shelf and a recessed seat polished by use. Its enamel instruction plate has lost every word except **DO NOT**, followed by a blank, blistered space.

No animal life is present. There are no burrows in the dust, no nests in the cable loops, no shells around the pool, no grazing damage on the leaves. Nothing scuttles away from footsteps. Nothing adds a call to the engine’s noise. Reproduction and dispersal belong to pressure, convection, and vibration: dry capsules open only under particular harmonics, releasing spores into rising heat; fallen stems root where acoustic quiet allows dust to gather around them. Dead foliage does not rot quickly. It collapses into thin brown sheets, then is slowly abraded into the same dust that fills the chamber’s seams.

Change is easier to infer than to witness. Old root arcs mark former positions of the quiet zones. Mineral bands record earlier pitches of the engine. A grove of translucent trunks is gradually leaning toward a failing coil, though the movement would take decades to notice. The flywheel wears, the note shifts, water finds new columns in which to fall, and vegetation follows at the pace of roots.

A visitor might spend an hour there and see only the wheel creep forward, one droplet descend a leaf, and a gauge needle tremble without settling. Meanwhile the noise continues at full force, and the heat never relents.

The Thirst Engine is not waiting to be restarted, nor is it simply winding down. Machine, erosion, and grove have reached a patient arrangement. The engine gives up heat, current, vapor, and vibration; the stone gives up its sharpness; the plants turn those losses into just enough water to persist. Nothing hurries except the sound.
