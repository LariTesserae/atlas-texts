## Newground

Newground lies in a low, cold basin with no natural height inside it taller than a slag heap. To see the whole city, one must climb a factory chimney or ride the inspection platform of a loading crane. From there, it runs for miles without resolving into a center: furnace halls, roof tanks, brick tenements, glass growing-houses, cable bridges, pumping stations, and pale ridges of untouched ground, all packed so closely that the streets appear to have been inserted afterward.

The light is usually ordinary—plain gray morning, bland white afternoon, occasionally a clear blue day with sharp shadows. There is no protective gloom. Every malfunction and impossible detail is available for inspection. Cold sits in the materials. Iron takes heat from a bare hand; rime remains in the threads of screws long after it has vanished from the roofs. Warmth is always local: the lee of a vent, a boiler-room doorway, the few inches around a mug of tea.

The basin is damp without being generously watered. No great river explains it. Rain comes briefly and irregularly, darkening the streets for an hour before vanishing into fissures. Most moisture arrives as condensation. It beads on the undersides of pipes, gathers in the seams of metal roofs, and drips from factory exhaust stacks into angular catch basins. Around these sources the world is slick and green. A few steps away, the same ground may be powder-dry, exhaling pale dust whenever a cart passes.

That ground is the youngest thing in sight. It rises from deep seams in waxy, bone-colored lobes, hardening first at the surface and remaining soft beneath. Nothing has been weathered for long. There are no pebbles; nothing has had time to become one. Fractures stay sharp enough to cut leather. Puddles occupy cracks rather than bowls. Even the oldest exposed slopes look unfinished, as though erosion has been announced but has not yet begun work.

The city treats this as both a supply problem and a building material. Its factories produce mostly more city: numbered paving plates, articulated wall panels, flexible pipe couplings, screws the height of a person, and blocks of foamed mineral used for emergency walls. Engines turn behind heavy housings. Conveyors carry crushed clinker to mixing halls; electric capstans drag whole sheds sideways on greased beams. Dynamos feed bundled cables that loop across streets in festoons, always with slack to spare. No bridge assumes that its banks will remain the same distance apart.

Buildings accumulate in layers rather than generations. A warehouse may have a tenement inside its outer shell, a pump house built through its roof, and three older streets preserved as corridors beneath its floor. Black brick meets rust-red iron, green slag glass, tarred felt, and raw mineral still bearing the fingerprint-like ripples of its formation. Walls share pipes, ladders, drying racks, root mats, nesting boxes, and cable trays. An open courtyard is regarded as either a valuable opportunity or a planning failure. Usually it soon contains both.

Occasionally the new ground appears to be attempting architecture on its own.

A crack reaching a warehouse wall may continue outward as another wall—almost correct, but too thick, with a doorway at ankle height. Buried pipes are sometimes discovered extended for hundreds of yards as solid stone. A road may rise into a flight of shallow steps and stop against empty air. None of these forms is finished enough to be useful without alteration. Surveyors call them *suggestions*. Masons call them free work when they help and bad habits when they do not. Formworks are therefore set not only to contain construction, but to give the basin fewer ideas of its own.

For a place of engines and continual collapse, Newground is extraordinarily quiet. The young stone is honeycombed with microscopic cavities and seems to draw vibration out of the air. A hammer strike becomes negligible within a few houses. An engine hall may be visibly shaking dust from its rafters while the street outside hears only a soft, periodic panting. When a frontage gives way, the event often arrives first as moving shadows, rising birds, and a pressure of dust; the modest gritting sound comes afterward.

The city’s frenzy is consequently visible before it is audible. Signal vanes drop from survey towers. Crane arms semaphore over the roofs. Auctions in the packed bazaars are conducted with numbered paddles, chalk, and rapid hand signs, though people standing close still speak in low voices. Up close, the hush contains innumerable small sounds: coins on wood, claws on plate metal, a valve releasing, water ticking into zinc, paper being folded. The quiet is not peace. It merely makes the turbulence difficult to judge until one turns a corner and finds an entire district in motion.

Vegetation occupies the wet heat of this system. Its lushness is seldom tall; it is thick, low, and insistent. Glass-green moss forms heavy mats over roof plates. Waxy ferns unroll beside warm outflows, while dark sedges stand in shallow cisterns and dwarf willows flatten their trunks along sheltered walls. Between these saturated bands are gray stretches of nearly bare mineral, dusted with lichen and the dry husks of windblown stems.

Gardeners work beside pipe-fitters and structural crews. They unroll rooted mats like flexible pavement, pinning them over fresh ground to hold dust, gather condensation, and keep small fractures from spreading. A failed drain can produce a hanging thicket in a season; a redirected vent can turn it brittle and brown in a week. After rain, the whole city briefly smells of wet metal and crushed stems. Then the dry mineral odor returns.

Animals are everywhere, and almost all of them have learned to use machinery without depending on its intentions. Red-gilled newts inhabit industrial catch-water, clustering where drips are warmest. Slate hares nest in disused ducts and pour out at feeding time to graze the roofs. Small, broad-hoofed deer move in herds along stairways and cable bridges, their coats often carrying a green dust of moss spores. Wire martens steal insulation for their nests, and gray birds pack semaphore boxes with so much nesting material that some signal towers employ full-time nest removers.

These animals also serve as instruments. Newts leaving a cistern may indicate a chemical change before the gauges register it. Hares gathering on older streets are taken seriously by surveyors. A sudden lifting of birds from several districts can stop work more effectively than an official order. Nothing in Newground is entirely wild or domestic; each species has simply discovered which forms of human warmth, shelter, and error are reliable.

A major ground movement—a *making*—begins with almost no spectacle. A white hairline appears across a road. The nearest survey tower drops its vanes. Engines stop one by one, deepening the quiet.

Then everything moves.

Market stalls fold into wheeled bundles. Cranes rotate above the roofs. Pipe-fitters release couplings and throw loops of hose clear. Garden crews roll up moss mats while householders carry out gauges, kettles, bedding, ledgers, and door hardware. The deer are already on high walkways. The road lifts beneath a dry, wrinkling skin. A warehouse separates along its designed joints, its frontage leaning with terrible slowness. Somewhere beneath it, mineral folds upward, carrying pieces of gutter, roots, and older masonry into a shape that has not existed before.

A making may last ten minutes or a day. It can crush one cistern while opening another, raise a ridge through three streets, or leave a row of useless doorways beneath a factory floor. Dust hangs in the ordinary daylight. There is no roar proportionate to the change.

Before the new surface hardens, crews press forms into it, lay plates across its soft spots, and divert condensation into whatever hollows have appeared. They do not simply restore the previous plan. They negotiate with what has emerged. By the next shift, there are usually stalls beneath the new overhang, pipework in its cavities, and something green already establishing itself along the drain.

Repeated use seems to give the basin its best instructions. A path walked every day is more likely to remain a path. An abandoned arcade may narrow; an undisturbed channel may quietly fill. Maintenance therefore includes rehearsal. Night watchmen walk their routes not merely to guard them, but to keep them legible. Delivery rounds, processions, and even the daily movement of herds help preserve rights of way. A map without dates and traffic arrows is considered incomplete. Permanence belongs more to habits than to masonry, and many of the city’s oldest objects are portable.

Seen again from a crane, Newground seems to seethe without sound: steam crossing the roofs, signal arms changing, gantries crawling, green mats shifting downslope, animals threading through crowds. Along its margins, the raw plain continues its unfinished experiments.

It is not a city defending itself against a landscape, nor a landscape reclaiming a city. Those distinctions belong to a world that has already decided what it is.
