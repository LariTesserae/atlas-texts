## The Old Height

At the altitude where a mountain ought to narrow into a summit, this one becomes spacious.

The top opens into a broken tableland large enough to hold several valleys, though none of them feels enclosed. Long, dark ridges separate shallow cirques; tilted pavements descend into basins with no outlets; low escarpments stand back from one another across immense fields of gravel. The land is varied, but almost nothing occupies it. A single boulder may be the only object in a square mile. A seam of white quartz can be followed for an hour without encountering another stone larger than a fist.

At the margins, the cliffs drop beyond any useful sense of depth. There are no clouds beneath them, no glimpses of lower country. The dimness simply continues. Looking down too long produces the unsettling impression that the stone at one’s feet is very far away and the invisible abyss is close enough to touch. Height is felt less in the view than in the body: in the thinness of breath, the pressure behind the eyes, the peculiar lightness of thoughts that usually require a world below them.

The rock is fine-grained and gray until wet, when it becomes nearly black. Its edges have all been softened. Even the highest pinnacles are blunt, and the old fracture faces are polished into broad, shallow curves. There is no fresh scree. Pebbles sit in worn sockets, and the dry watercourses are so ancient that their beds have become mineral veins rather than hollows. Some cross hills without cutting through them; others enter a cirque and emerge, unchanged, on the far side of a ridge.

Nothing is being carried away. Erosion has not merely slowed here—it has arrived at a form it no longer needs to alter.

The light is an iron-colored dimness without an hour. It does not brighten the stone so much as make its darkness legible. There are no sharp shadows, no glitter on wet surfaces, no promise of a sun behind the sky. The sky itself has very little depth. In certain places it resembles a low ceiling of smoke; in others, particularly above the empty central pavement, it seems farther away than the cliffs are deep.

Cold is equally constant. It does not bite harder on exposed ridges or relent beneath overhangs. It has already penetrated everything it intends to penetrate.

Yet the Height is damp. Black seep-lines descend a few inches from cracks and stop. Small tarns lie in bedrock basins, their surfaces so still that they appear to have been polished out of the same stone. Condensation clings to the undersides of slabs. Some ice exists, clear and ancient, but it is not the fate of the water around it. Liquid water remains liquid at a cold that should have silenced it, because freezing would require one state to succeed another.

One hollow contains rain.

The shower occupies a space no larger than a courtyard: a few hundred sparse, silver drops slanting through the dim air. They can be seen at every height, but none is observably falling. Passing through the hollow leaves skin and clothing wet without the sensation of striking a single drop. Beneath it stands a pool three fingers deep. It never rises, and the ground beyond its worn margin never becomes muddy. The rain is not weather. It is one of the materials of which that hollow is made.

And over all this lies the noise.

The Old Height is tremendously loud. A low roar comes through the ground, dense enough to be felt in the teeth. Above it are fixed bands of sound: a dry channel thundering with a flood it does not contain; an empty col shrieking with wind though the air is motionless; a field of frozen gravel ticking like a vast hearth cooling after a fire. The loudest place is the central pavement, where there is no cliff, water, loose stone, or visible source of any kind.

The air is much too thin to carry such sound. It does not carry it. The noise arrives through bone, through the soles of the feet, through the hand resting on a boulder. Distance does not reliably soften it. Each location simply has its appointed loudness. A small basin may be deafening while an escarpment visible beyond it murmurs almost gently.

Nothing swells or subsides. There are no approaching avalanches behind the avalanche-sounds, no storms gathering inside the roar. Perhaps these are the voices of processes that happened long ago. More likely, sound has outlasted the distinction between an event and its residue.

The few made things are scattered so widely that no settlement can be inferred from them.

A wall no longer than a body stands alone on an open pavement. Far away, three unworked slabs have been placed as steps against a boulder, ending well below its top. Elsewhere there is a doorway with no wall, a ring of stones too small to shelter anything, a curve of dry-laid blocks that continues after its last stone as a dark seam in the bedrock.

There are no hearths, vessels, paths, fasteners, inscriptions, or tools. No stone has been shaped beyond being chosen and set upon another. If hands made these things, hands were the entire technology.

Nor do they look like fragments of larger, more sensible buildings. Each seems complete in its inadequacy. Their lower courses have acquired the same weather polish as the surrounding rock, as if construction and geology were merely two speeds of the same patience. In a place where erosion has begun to affect more than matter, they may be what remains when an intention is worn away: shelter removed, enclosure removed, purpose removed, leaving only a few decisions standing in stone.

Life keeps to the relations that still hold.

Ash-green lichen stains the sheltered sides of wet rocks. Along seep-lines grow sparse cushions of nearly black stems, none taller than a fingernail. There may be a hundred empty paces between one living patch and the next. None appears young, and none leaves a dead husk. They do not flower, spread, or retreat. Their growth has already happened; their being alive is simply ongoing.

Animals are rarer than water. The tarns contain a few transparent, jointed specks hanging motionless at fixed depths, their gills moving almost imperceptibly. On the open tableland, across an enormous area, one may encounter a broad, pale grazer standing with its mouth pressed to a lichen-covered stone. Its divided hooves fit the irregularities of the rock. Its breath makes no vapor.

Where it has fed, the lichen is neither cropped nor crushed. There are no bones in the gullies, no carrion, no drifts of dung, no young animals following older ones. The ecology has almost no arrows in it. Nothing is visibly becoming food for anything else. Each organism persists at precisely the rate that leaves the world unchanged.

Farther out, the erosion of relations becomes geographic.

A ridge seen across a cirque may, after a long walk toward it, be found behind the traveler without the basin ever having been crossed. That route is always the same and cannot be reversed. The rain hollow lies to the left of one particular split boulder, regardless of the direction from which the boulder is approached. A dry streambed passes beneath a ridge without a tunnel and emerges as a quartz vein in the wall of another valley.

These are not flickering visions or passages that open and close. They are permanent features. Every part of the Height can be understood locally; the parts simply cannot be assembled into one continuous whole. Its openness does not come from infinity. It comes from the fact that its separate extents do not add together.

Still, there is no sense of instability. Nothing wavers when observed. No impossible adjacency corrects itself. Reality has not broken here recently. The break is ancient, and its sharp places have been rounded like the channels.

Perhaps that is what extreme elevation finally means: not merely height above the sea, but height above enough of the world’s agreements. Down below, distance, sequence, cause, and substance may be packed densely enough to support one another. Up here, only the strongest relations remain, and only locally. A stone rests. Water wets. An opening is an opening. Beyond that, the mountain makes no promises.

Yet the overwhelming impression is not chaos, but cold and spacious order. A wet slab. A mile of pale gravel. A black cushion of vegetation. Then nothing. A little wall. Then more nothing. The dimness never deepens into night. The rain never finishes. The roar never changes pitch, and the rare grazer remains rare without becoming rarer.

From the highest broken pavement there is no view down to a smaller, more coherent world. There are only the widely separated features of the Height, the fixed failures between them, and the unvarying sound coming up through the feet.

The place is not waiting for thaw, morning, collapse, or return. Even waiting would imply a later state.

The Old Height remains, loudly, with nothing remaining to happen.
