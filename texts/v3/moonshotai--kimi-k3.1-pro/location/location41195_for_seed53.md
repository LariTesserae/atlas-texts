## The Rime Measure

At the highest nick in the ridge, where the mountain has been worn to a narrow saddle between two unfinished peaks, lies a small, crowded basin called the Rime Measure. Its boundaries can all be seen at once: a crescent of cliff at the back, a shattered quartzite lip in front, and short slopes of old scree closing the ends. Beyond the lip is not another mountainside but a drop into cloud. The far ranges look submerged. Only their highest summits rise far enough to hold the same hard sunlight.

The basin could be crossed in a few minutes if its floor were open. It is not open. Thousands of stone blades stand packed through it, some no higher than a knee, others rising well overhead. They are the remains of ancient bedding forced upright and then patiently excavated by frost, wind, and ice. From the rim they resemble a comb dragged through the mountain; among them, that pattern disappears. Narrow passages turn and divide. Some end in pockets barely large enough to stand in. Others widen around level pans of stone flour, then close again between near-touching walls.

Nothing looks freshly broken. The blade tops are rounded, their edges dulled, their lower fractures partly filled with compacted grit. There are no great fans of new rubble. The large forms have reached an old accommodation with the forces wearing them down. Ice still opens seams, and wind still removes grains, but it does so along routes established long ago. The place is active without being young.

At this elevation the light has an almost weightless intensity. It falls white through the thin air and returns from the cloud below, illuminating overhangs and the undersides of projecting stones. Quartz seams flare. Mica gives off sparse, hard points of reflection. The darker rock between them appears nearly black, and the shadows are deep blue rather than grey. Even small gaps become difficult to judge: a cleft that looks wide enough to enter may be only a hand’s breadth, while a seemingly solid wall may contain a passage extending several body lengths.

The cold is constant, but it is not evenly distributed. Dark stone warms in the sun while the air remains well below freezing. Clear ice persists in shaded joints; a few steps away, a sunlit face may be wet enough to shine. Dampness here is a condition of particular surfaces, not of the basin as a whole. There is no stream and no standing pool, only droplets held in quartz irregularities, dark films along mineral seams, and small pockets of water sealed beneath transparent ice.

By midmorning, the warmed blades begin to draw a faint current up from the drop. Cloud answers it. It does not simply roll over the summit and fill the basin. It comes through in slow, narrow bands, sometimes no deeper than the height of a person’s waist, threading between the stones while the upper blades remain in clear air. From above, the floor disappears into brightness and the fin tops seem to stand on nothing. Within the cloud, visibility contracts to the nearest wet surface.

Moisture settles according to temperature. Rime thickens on cold, shaded stone in granular white shelves. On warmer faces, old frost loosened by the sun becomes water. A single blade can therefore be dry and white along one edge while its opposite side darkens and sweats. The crowded field becomes thousands of adjacent climates, each only a few fingers wide.

When the cloud withdraws, the moisture often remains behind for a little while. Drops continue to gather and fall. This produces the basin’s brief, delayed “rain”: a sparse ticking and pattering under a cloudless sky. It lasts minutes at most. The water runs a short distance, enters a crack, or vanishes into a cap of ice. Some of it evaporates directly back into the brilliant air. None travels far.

Human hands have added little, and what they added is concentrated at these transitions between cloud and stone, sunlight and shade. Eleven shallow basins have been pecked into level slabs with harder stone tools. Short runnels connect a few of them, though the connections are not obvious when dry. Three low cairns stand where fog bands regularly touch the rim. A semicircular windbreak, too small to be a dwelling, shelters a flat table of rock marked with irregular grooves and rows of tiny drilled holes.

Several split slabs have been repaired with hammered copper staples. The metal is simple, dark, and hand-worked; the repairs themselves are worn. One clay disc, no larger than a thumbprint, remains wedged beneath a stone where ice cannot easily lift it. There are no roofs, no road, and no continuous path linking the installations. Whoever came here did so lightly and apparently more than once, separated by long intervals.

The cloud reveals the probable purpose of the work. Cups sit beneath reliable drips. Grooves intercept overflow that would otherwise disappear into grit. Some basins fill only when the fog lies low; others receive water from high blades after the fog has gone. The marks beside them do not resemble a calendar so much as a comparison. They record differences: which stone thawed, which remained frozen, which emptied without ever appearing to melt. The windbreak does not protect a sleeping place. It protects a surface from being scoured clean.

There is no life softening any of this. No lichen rims the cups, no moss darkens the joints, and there are no nests, bones, feathers, or droppings in the shelter. The water leaves no organic stain. Clay does not rot; copper is not overgrown; even the finest dust remains mineral. Age appears only as abrasion, rounding, fracture, and loss. The basin is not waiting to be colonized. Its exchanges of vapor, ice, light, heat, and grit form a complete system without organisms.

It is also exceptionally quiet. The dense blades break the wind into small currents that rarely sustain a howl. The usual sound is a faint, dry sifting as loose rime crystals settle. During thaw there may be one drop every few minutes, or a trickle lasting no longer than a breath. Contracting stone gives an occasional hard tick. A freezing seam can knock softly deeper in the rock, but the sound is swallowed by the nearby walls rather than carried across the summit. In fog, even these noises seem closer and smaller.

Stillness never means inactivity. Rime grows crystal by crystal while exposed ice elsewhere thins through sublimation. Different minerals warm at different rates, pulling faint stresses through the blades. Meltwater follows a quartz vein, stops at a shadow, and freezes there. Grit shifts by nearly imperceptible increments. The stone flour in one hollow is slowly emptied while another fills. At the end of each bright day, the large arrangement appears unchanged, though none of its moisture is where it began.

Toward evening the fog drains back over the lip. For a short time the light loses its whiteness, and the materials emerge separately: lavender-grey stone, brown copper, milk-colored quartz, black films of water, ice with a faint green depth. Then shade crosses the basin and the films tighten into ice. The last drips stop in place.

By morning, one basin may be empty though nothing overflowed, while another holds ice though no cloud touched it directly. The old marks offer no simple reading of such nights. The Rime Measure does not measure rainfall, or thaw, or time alone. It registers the small disagreements among surfaces.

The summit has kept its ancient shape for a very long time. It has never kept the same water in the same place for a whole day.
