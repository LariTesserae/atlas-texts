## The Black Catchment

The darkness is not night. Night implies that something will change.

There are no stars above the Catchment, no reflection on its water, no pale weather at the horizon. A held hand does not become visible when the eyes adjust. The machines make no lamps, and nothing living glows. Yet the place is not empty, or quiet, or still. It presses close on every side: warm rain on the face, saturated air in the lungs, insects stitching past the ears, water moving beneath a surface that may be stone, root, mud, or the thin crust over a flooded shaft.

Rain gives the landscape its nearest outlines. A broad fern ticks and pats; an iron pipe rings; a pool accepts drops with a soft, close-mouthed sound. Farther away, rain on open marsh becomes a continuous hiss. Farther still, the large water speaks: falls in the gorges, flood moving through reeds, the low concussion of a river entering a sink. On a windless day, an experienced listener can stand in one place and hear several kinds of country without seeing any of them.

The Catchment is an upland, though nothing about its warmth suggests height. It occupies a continental rise of basalt and compacted ash, lifted above older country whose rivers and weather rarely reach it. The air is thinner than the body expects and difficult to breathe during exertion, but it is warm, wet, and heavy with the smells of iron, crushed stems, wet hide, and faint sulfur. Heat comes up through the stone. Springs issue from hillsides at blood temperature. Even on the highest ridges, where the wind can briefly feel cool, water seeps warmly around the boot.

Its forms are immensely old. There are no young, splintered peaks. Ridges have been reduced to long, streaming backs; outcrops are rounded and fluted; ravines descend through patient sequences of shelves and plunge pools. Ash cliffs have weathered into vertical organ-pipes furred with roots. In the basins, peat and silt lie deep over former valleys. Every storm carries material away, but the marsh traps nearly as much as the rivers remove. The land is not approaching some final smoothness. It has reached a working arrangement with its own decay.

Water is present even where none can be heard. It stands a finger’s depth below the root mats. It beads on stone and runs down the undersides of leaves. At certain changes in pressure, rain seems to stop without anything drying: the air simply begins depositing water directly onto surfaces. Boulders start weeping. Cables drip at regular intervals. Whole hillsides develop a downward whisper.

The plateau is large enough for these processes to occur out of step with one another. One watershed may be taking warm, hard rain while the next exhales fog through its gorges. Storms build over the central reed basins, circle the high ash country for days, and return with different winds. Thunder can travel from basin to basin for hours, becoming less a series of claps than a remote disturbance in the body. There is no single Catchment weather, and no meaningful universal hour. Stations keep their own schedules. Rivers keep others.

Vegetation grows in dense belts rather than as an unbroken forest. The high stone carries cushion moss, rubbery lichen, and low thickets whose stems interlock until they can be walked upon. Around springs, bacterial felt spreads in tough, slippery sheets, living on sulfur and dissolved metal. Snails and small armored grazers crop it. Their waste feeds the root beds downstream, where the true lushness begins.

Bowl-ferns hold warm water in their cupped fronds. Flute-reeds grow in close stands, each stem perforated by insects until the whole marsh becomes an instrument when the wind rises. Elsewhere there are trees with broad, heavy leaves and no interest in light. They orient toward heat, moisture, and vibration; some lift their leaves before a pump starts in a buried chamber below them. Their roots associate with the same chemical-feeding growths that sustain the spring felt. Fallen animals, drained batteries, machine oil, and mineral brine all enter the food web eventually.

The living density is easy to underestimate until one stops moving. Then the mud begins ticking with buried larvae. Small feet cross the roots. A reed bends against the leg though there is no wind. Something in the water touches a submerged stem, withdraws, and touches it again. Winged animals pass in layers: quick, dry-winged clickers near the ground; broad, soft fliers above them; high sonar callers whose cries return from distances too great to judge.

Chain-eels knot themselves through flooded root systems and separate when disturbed. Mud-herons, which are neither birds nor quite anything else, wade on six jointed limbs and probe the silt with electrically sensitive bills. Herds of mossbacks move between the fern basins, their low calls shaking water from nearby leaves. They are followed by creatures that eat their parasites, creatures that eat those creatures, and patient predators that do not follow at all, but wait beside paths worn through the marsh.

Sound is not merely abundant here; it is organized. Frogs occupy narrow bands of pitch. Clicking insects avoid one another’s rhythms. Mossbacks call beneath the rain, while the small fliers hunt in the intervals between pump strokes. The result is loud almost beyond comprehension, but not featureless. Every species has found a place to be heard. Predators learn the places where sound is missing.

Then there are the engines.

A station is usually felt before it can be touched: a vibration in wet ground, a regular pressure in the teeth, a change in the behavior of insects. The building itself may be no more than a low hall of vitreous brick, half sunk in a hillside, with condensation streaming from its roof. Inside, a geothermal turbine turns without supervision. Or a bank of pistons drives water into a main. Or iron-cased compressors take something from the air and discharge warm mineral liquid through an open channel.

The stations are separated by days of difficult travel. Between them are only faint signs of construction: a concrete culvert under peat, three ceramic insulators on a rusted mast, a stair cut into an escarpment, an embossed number plate bolted to stone. Pipes appear and disappear. Some are large enough to walk inside when dry; others run beneath the moss like roots. Electrical cables cross the marsh on widely spaced pylons. Their current is perceptible less by sight than by sensation—lifted hair, a metallic taste, a brief agitation among nearby insects.

There are no cities and almost no clustered dwellings. Industry has not spread across the Catchment so much as been taken apart and distributed through it. Pumping occurs in one basin, compression on a distant ridge, separation in a warm gorge, and something resembling manufacture behind a sealed cliff door hundreds of kilometers away. The whole plateau functions as a single factory whose rooms have been placed too far apart to inspect. Its products leave through buried mains. What they are, and where they go, is not evident from the stations themselves.

Not all of the machinery is old in the same way. Some housings have eroded to flakes and hold fern gardens in their seams. Others turn with the smooth indifference of continuous use. Occasionally a valve is found freshly greased, or a broken tread has been replaced with metal that has not yet begun to rust. Handles stand at a convenient height for a human hand. Doorways suggest human proportions. But there are no maintained settlements around them, no lamps in the windows, and no reliable record of who performs the work.

Life has adjusted to the network without becoming dependent on any single part of it. Certain eels spawn only when a seasonal reversal of the pumps changes the temperature of the outflow. Reed colonies grow thickest in the warm brine below separators. A species of marsh frog calls in exact alternation with compressor strokes, fitting its voice into the brief mechanical silences. Soft-footed hunters use the noise shadows of stations to approach their prey. When an engine stops for repair, the local animals do not simply become quieter; they change pitch, hesitate, and then begin claiming the abandoned frequencies.

The Catchment’s paths are therefore acoustic as much as physical. Safe crossings can be recognized by the rattle of water over a particular gravel bed. A cable’s hum marks a route through otherwise impassable reed country. Some old survey lines carry tuned wires that answer the wind differently at each junction. A traveler who follows the wrong sound may walk for hours along an embankment that gradually becomes a pipe support, then a narrow beam over floodwater, then nothing.

Stillness would be more dangerous than rain. The landscape depends on motion: ground heat rising, cloud condensing, roots growing through silt, pumps holding back one basin while releasing another, herds opening paths that eels and frogs subsequently use. When a station fails, water does not always flood immediately. Sometimes the first sign is a migration. Mossbacks leave a basin. Fliers gather over a warm pipe. The frogs stop calling in their usual order. Only afterward does the level begin to rise.

At the plateau’s margins, all this water eventually finds the escarpments. It goes over in falls too large to apprehend as single objects, or disappears into fissures and emerges far below through industrial spillways whose purpose predates the present channels. The cliffs retreat slowly. The upland remains. Rain continues to arrive from weather made somewhere out of reach.

There has never been a recorded dawn in the Black Catchment. If morning has any meaning, it is the period when the distant stations take up a new load, the warm rain thins to condensation, and—beneath the water, beneath the engines, beneath the innumerable animal voices—the whole elevated country seems to change its note.
