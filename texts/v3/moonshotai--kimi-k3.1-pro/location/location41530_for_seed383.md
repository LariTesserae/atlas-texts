## The White Hollow

The first surprise is the brightness. Several hundred metres below a rain-poor plateau, the Hollow is lit like an open courtyard at noon. Daylight enters through ancient solution shafts—some no wider than a wrist, others broken open into great, slanting wells—and strikes pale gypsum, salt-veined limestone, and hand-burnished copper reflectors. The light is passed downward and sideways, courtyard by courtyard, until even streets far from the nearest shaft glow white. Shadows remain, but they are thin and blue-grey. The roof is often lost in illuminated dust, so the city seems covered by light rather than stone.

The air gives the depth away. It is cool and still, with a clean mineral smell edged by sulfur near the seeps. The light carries almost no warmth. Condensation gathers on shaded walls, and cold metal tools feel wet moments after being set down.

The Hollow is not one cavern but a region of connected basins, low saddles, collapsed galleries, and broad natural halls. From a high entrance ledge, the floor runs toward several successive horizons. Its great age is visible in its softness. Ancient cave pillars have been reduced to rounded swells; old collapse piles have settled into featureless aprons; mineral terraces that must once have been intricate are now little more than shallow ripples under the dust. There is no forest of stalactites. Almost nothing natural remains sharp. A fresh fracture or a mason’s cut looks startlingly violent beside forms that have been wearing smooth for ages.

Water is scarce, but never entirely absent. It is less a feature of the landscape than an event that happens to it. Rain on the plateau may arrive days later as a darkening seam, a line of beads along the roof, or a runnel too thin to follow without crouching. Most of the floor is dry enough to powder underfoot, while the next hollow may be slick and cold. Pools are few: black, still lenses in mineral basins, surrounded by white crystalline rims. The city’s deep cisterns hold more water than all its visible streams combined.

After a surface storm, seepage wanders. Old channels crust shut with new crystal while others open without warning. Brine creeps almost imperceptibly across a floor, gathering dust into pale islands. A wall that was sound the previous month may begin to soften into white granules at its base. Then the moisture withdraws, leaving only a tide mark and a different arrangement of hardness and weakness.

Across this floor stands a city, though much of it consists of distance. Each district has the full anatomy of urban life: paved lanes, drains, cistern courts, storehouses, workshops, corbelled meeting halls, and small dwellings packed around shared walls. But the districts are separated by long stretches of empty mineral ground. Low causeways and lines of marker stones connect them. One can walk for an hour without leaving the city’s traditional boundaries and see no building at all.

Nothing towers. The architecture is thick, low, and deliberately manageable. Roofs are stone slabs or corbelled courses; doors are covered with stitched hide; awnings are made from the same material, stretched on bone hooks. Foundations cross older foundations, and those cross older ones still. Thresholds have been worn into shallow cups. Stairs have become ramps. Inscriptions survive mostly as changes in the way dust settles. The city seems less built once than continuously handed down.

Its tools remain close to the hand. Hammerstones, bone wedges, drills, copper chisels, hide ropes, stone sledges. Blocks are made small enough for a few people to shift. Houses are constructed so that they can be dismantled, and their stones are often marked with a household pattern. When a dwelling moves, its name and markings move with it; the empty site keeps neither. The most permanent works are not palaces or walls but cisterns, drainage grades, light wells, and the stone sockets in which the reflectors turn.

There is no vegetation to soften any of this. No grass grows beneath the largest daylight wells, no moss follows the wet seams, no root enters a fracture. The mineral palette remains chalk, pearl, ash, black chert, and occasional rust-orange stains.

Life begins instead as something easy to overlook: colourless and sulphur-yellow microbial films where seepwater crosses reactive stone. These invisible pastures support an abundance out of proportion to the apparent barrenness. Turn over a damp slab and its underside may be packed with translucent glasslice. White crickets occupy the drill holes of old masonry. Spiders wait in the cool spaces between blocks. Cisterns contain clouds of transparent crustaceans, blind eels, and occasional salamanders lying motionless along the rim.

At certain seep blooms, the abundance becomes visible all at once. Chalkwing larvae strip the fresh films; their winged adults rise into the light wells in silent, revolving clouds. Small bats pour from roof roosts to feed among them. On the ground, crustaceans spread across a newly dampened court so thickly that the stone appears to crawl. A few days later the court may be empty again, except for shed skins and the silver tracks of scavengers.

The Hollow’s turbulence is thus mostly seen, not heard. At almost any hour, something in the middle distance is changing: a reflector turning, a column of dust descending through a shaft, a swarm crossing a white ridge, a crew stripping a threatened house, a new brine channel branching around a causeway. Surface pressure changes send slow exhalations through the wells, lifting dust into bright sheets that twist without a sound. After distant rain, the pace quickens. A drain furrs over in a night. A floor settles. A cistern fills in a district whose cisterns have been dry for years, and a temporary market gathers around it—hide awnings, stone counters, copper blanks, salt cakes, bags of live eels—only to disperse when conditions shift again.

Work crews are always somewhere, but rarely crowded together. A distant wall comes down in a soft spill of blocks. Elsewhere, numbered stones are laid into a new wall around a new courtyard. Streets bend; districts contract; stack yards of reusable masonry appear where houses stood shortly before. Maps age quickly. The city remains urban not because its arrangement is fixed, but because its drains, water customs, building marks, and shared light survive repeated rearrangement.

Yet the prevailing condition is near silence. Dusty floors soften footsteps, porous walls deaden small noises, and the immense spaces take care of the rest. There is no foliage to rustle, no river to murmur, no machinery, and usually no open water larger than a cistern. Sounds arrive separately: one drop striking water; the dry tick of a copper chisel; hide rope drawing across stone; the faint grit of something many-legged beneath a slab. Even a collapse may be felt through the soles before its muted pouring sound arrives.

From the upper galleries, the Hollow can look abandoned—white ground, sparse masonry, motionless black cisterns, too much light and too little colour. Watch longer and the stillness dissolves into hundreds of small alterations. The great shape of the cavern is ancient and nearly finished. Everything living or built within it is still being revised.

And somewhere below, in the bright cool quiet, a single drop falls into a cistern, and the rings take a long time to reach the stone.
