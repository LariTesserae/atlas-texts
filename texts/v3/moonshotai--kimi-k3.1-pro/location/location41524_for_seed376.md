From the rim, the basin looks slower than it is.

It lies high in a chain of rounded mountains, well above the elevation where ordinary trees would begin to fail. The underlying landform is ancient: a broad glacial hollow softened by weather, with long shoulders descending into side valleys and a low, uneven floor. Nothing about it is freshly broken. Even the steeper slopes have been rounded and root-bound into stability. Beyond the passes, other basins continue the same green geography, separated by narrow saddles and extending farther than the eye can resolve.

At this height, the air ought to belong to scree, lichen, and old snow. Instead, every surface is occupied.

Not merely forested—occupied. There is no visible bedrock, no exposed soil, no bare gravel along a watercourse. The slopes are plant; the ground is compacted plant; the overhangs are upheld by interlaced roots and roofed with hanging moss. Looking down into a cleft reveals no stone at the bottom, only darker vegetation and, sometimes, a small black seam of water. The basin’s mineral skeleton is still there, but it has not touched the open air in living memory.

The daylight is ordinary. A pale sun stands behind thin cloud, shadows fall where shadows should fall, and nothing glows. The colors are almost restrained: wet olive, black-green, the gray-blue of waxed leaves, pale straw where tissue is dying, occasional deep red in the youngest growth. From a distance, the whole region could be mistaken for an especially lush, cold forest under a normal afternoon sky.

Underfoot, the resemblance ends.

The ground flexes. It is made of many meters of fibrous mat, layered like felt but still alive at considerable depth. Some layers spring back; others yield with a slow, cold suction. Fine roots spread across the surface and disappear again. Broad leaves lie flush against it, their upper faces toughened against frost, their undersides crowded with mouths that draw nutrients from whatever presses down upon them. There is no dependable distinction between path, leaf litter, topsoil, and living skin.

The cold is constant enough to whiten breath and stiffen exposed fingers. At night, clear rime forms on the outer fronds. By midday much of it has melted, but the water does not run freely away. It gathers in beads, enters pores, or slips into narrow vessels beneath the mat. Rain comes only intermittently, usually as a fine, brief passage of cloud. More moisture arrives by condensation. The basin is damp rather than drenched: no great river crosses it, and its few pools are isolated lenses held in depressions between roots. Some are no larger than a cup. Others lie still and black beneath entire canopies.

Those canopies rise in several tiers. In some places there is room to walk beneath them; elsewhere, trunks and fronds stand nearly touching, and progress means forcing aside cold, resistant surfaces. The tallest growths are not quite trees. They are ribbed, hollow columns with crowns that open and close according to pressure within their stems. Around their bases grow clusters of translucent sacs. These slowly fill, contract, and empty again, moving fluid through root cords that can be traced for hundreds of meters.

Nothing here has been built, yet the basin is industrial in everything except manufacture. Material is gathered, transported, transformed, assembled, and recovered. Condenser leaves strip moisture from passing air. Buried vessels distribute it. Chemical heat from deep decomposition keeps a few central conduits just warm enough to remain active while the exterior freezes. Tall growths rise where sap pressure is highest; dense decomposer felts wait below them. When a column dies, nothing is wasted and almost nothing remains recognizable.

There are no engines, wires, smokestacks, or arranged stones. Still, the place has the relentless organization of a works without a workplace: production everywhere, no visible center, and no final product except the continuation of production.

On a windless day, the sky can be perfectly calm while the land churns.

A pale front crosses a hillside as millions of leaves release their hold at once. They do not so much fall as come apart, collapsing into the felt beneath them. Behind the front, dark spears push upward, unfold, and become a canopy where there had been open ground an hour before. Elsewhere, a tract of vegetation swells into a long dome. Its surface tightens, splits, and releases not an explosion but a slow exhalation of cold vapor and spores. The dome then subsides, leaving a shallow basin that other plants immediately begin to close.

Whole slopes pass through these changes in sequence. A wave of growth can travel from one side of the valley to the other over the course of a day, crossing old drainage lines, entering side hollows, dividing around rises. From the rim, the waves resemble weather moving through the color of the land. From within, they are overwhelming: stems lengthening at the edge of vision, surfaces softening underfoot, distant openings closing before they can be reached.

Yet the turbulence never alters the basin’s essential form. The same central depressions remain depressed. The same saddles stay open. Even the most violent growth follows contours established long before any of the present tissue existed. The valley is settled; only its surface is incapable of rest.

The silence makes the motion difficult to trust.

Wet felt, waxed foliage, and layers of air-filled tissue absorb vibration so thoroughly that even enormous collapses make almost no sound. A stand of hollow columns can vanish into itself a few hundred meters away and produce nothing more than a faint pressure through the soles. Close at hand, there may be a small click as ice releases a stem, or the nearly inaudible thread of a droplet falling into a covered pool. A few paces farther, these disappear. One’s own breathing becomes intrusive.

There is no birdsong. Animals are present only as rare exceptions: colorless mites in the wet seams, a few soft-bodied creatures dormant inside the warmer root layers, perhaps a solitary dark bird crossing the open basin without landing. They contribute little to pollination or decomposition. The plants have arranged not to need them.

It is difficult to say how many plant species live here. Forms that look unrelated above the surface may join into the same root vessel below it. A condenser frond, a pressure sac, and a towering column might be separate organisms trading fluids—or temporary organs of something distributed across the entire valley. Cuts close quickly. Uprooted tissue either dries to a gray husk or attaches itself elsewhere and resumes with a different shape. Taxonomy keeps dissolving into plumbing.

A few rules nevertheless emerge.

Bareness does not last. Any tear in the mat is first lined with pale filaments, then padded, then covered.

Height is always temporary. Nothing grows tall without becoming a supply of material for what follows.

Water remains mostly hidden, and the oldest hollows remain hollow.

And nothing is allowed to remain dead for long.

Toward evening, the cold sharpens. Condensation begins again on the outer leaves, giving the basin a brief silver cast under the ordinary fading light. The motion does not slow. Deep vessels continue to pulse; fresh growth presses into recently opened ground; old fronds blanch and sink. From the nearest pass, the valley once more appears peaceful, even stagnant.

Then an entire distant shoulder subsides without a sound. A darker green rises behind it, following the old slope exactly. The mountain remains where it has always been. Everything covering it has become something else.
