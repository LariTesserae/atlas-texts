## The Underbloom

The Underbloom is a cavity beneath the surface of the world, though *cavity* suggests something with a floor. Most of it has none. It is a continent-sized volume of cold, illuminated air, enclosed and occasionally bridged by living tissue. Root plates cling to its edges. Leaf escarpments and cliffs of dense white pith project from them. Here and there, a structural root crosses the gulf, thick as a city wall where it anchors and fine as a hair in the distance. A falling petal can dwindle below an observer for minutes without reaching anything.

It is a place made almost entirely of plants, but it does not feel overgrown. The emptiness is the landscape. Vegetation merely gives the emptiness an edge.

The brightness is the first wrong thing. There is no visible sun, no single opening overhead, and yet the hollow is often dazzling. Hair-fine transparent vessels conduct daylight down from unseen surface growths and release it through scale-leaves covering the walls. Whole provinces of vegetation shine with a cold white-green radiance. At the brightest phase, colors bleach toward silver, and travelers weave dark fiber into slitted eye-shades. Distances become difficult to believe: a cliff apparently close enough to touch may be revealed, when a veil of mist crosses in front of it, to lie beyond a day of falling.

The light brings almost no heat. It rests on the hands like bright moonlight. There are dimmer intervals, when square miles of luminous scales fold or become clouded with spores, but never a dependable night.

Close at hand, the shining surfaces are neither smooth nor serene. Walkable ground is braided root, corky bark, and runner-mat springy over hollow spaces. Split stems form corridors whose walls are wet with sap. Thin leaves make sails, doors, and accidental roofs; some chambers resemble woven buildings until one finds the seam where the whole room emerged from a bud. There is no deep soil. Even the papery compost in sheltered seams is penetrated by living rootlets, busy taking it back.

Everything looks recently born or recently broken. Cold splits the tissues; growth tears them; falling fragments rip fresh seams through the walls. Root cliffs end in fibrous splinters. Thorns are translucent and needle-sharp. New leaves have serrated edges that have not yet dried or curled. Almost nothing has been rounded by water or time. Even genuinely old structures look young, their age visible only in their impossible thickness.

The cold is persistent enough to numb exposed skin, but the dampness is elusive. The air can sting the nose with dryness while every touched surface proves slightly wet. Condensation beads along sap veins and freezes into combs of rime beneath leaf edges. Where living sap runs close to the surface, the frost loosens and a few drops gather. These fall into bark crevices, hollow thorns, or cupped leaves. Some cups hold black, green-tasting water for days; others dry before noon.

There is no river crossing the hollow. Water is always local and usually temporary: a film beneath the hand, a pool no larger than a room, a brief shower unthreading from one distant escarpment. A rain may wet a few square miles while the neighboring province remains papery and pale. Most moisture is taken back into tissue before it can collect. Travelers value a reliable condensation seam more than a spring.

The hollow is large enough for several weathers to ignore one another. Transpiration sends up invisible moisture; cold surfaces return it as fog. Slight warmth around major sap vessels sets slow air rivers moving. Vast chambers open or seal as their living membranes flex, changing pressure across hundreds of miles. From a high root shelf one might see a clear gulf below, a vertical rain in the middle distance, and, beyond that, a spore-storm turning like a white wheel against the shining wall.

Growth is the other weather.

It waits in buds that look frozen, then answers water, injury, or a pulse of sap with geographical speed. A bloom-front crosses a wall: first a darkening of the veins, then a wet shine, then millions of shoots shouldering through the old luminous skin. In a few hours a cliff becomes furred with new growth. Over several days, roots thicken, shelves buckle, and leaf-sails open into winds that did not previously exist. It may not look hurried from one minute to the next, but maps fail quickly. The outline of a far wall can change between one sleep and the next.

Elsewhere, tissue is destroyed just as energetically. A colony shaded by its rivals withdraws its sap and becomes brittle. A root bridge, burdened by rime and new growth, tears at one anchor. Miles of vegetation detach and descend through the bright air in slowly separating veils. Where debris catches on a ledge, white scavenging filaments enter it almost at once. Where nothing catches it, it simply becomes smaller.

Growth is not the peaceful background process here. It is the principal form of violence.

The different tissues are not necessarily allies, or even members of one organism. Luminous scale-leaves, structural roots, water-cups, parasitic runners, and scavenging mats graft into one another and then struggle across the grafts. A root may carry dozens of species in its bark. A shoot can be strangled by the same living cable that supports it. Cutting a path is therefore uncertain work: one wound dries and closes, while another answers with a thicket of defensive thorns. Much of the Underbloom’s apparent architecture is the residue of these contests.

Animals leave so little sign that their damage can be mistaken for disease. Translucent mites sometimes crowd the rim of a water-cup. A slow, pale grazer may scrape rime from a warm sap vein, six legs moving with long pauses between steps. At a flowering front, a few white moths appear and spend their brief lives around blossoms that will themselves be gone within days. There are no flocks crossing the gulf, no animal chorus establishing the hour. Most volumes of the hollow contain no animal life at all.

The scattered made things are similarly easy to miss. A dew station might consist of four split-root poles, a tilted leaf membrane, and a fiber channel leading into a stoppered gourd. Nearby: a platform lashed into a root fork, a folded sleeping mat, three stone flakes, and a copper staple hammered cold around a leaking seam. Farther on, there may be nothing for weeks.

Bridges are built with slack and deliberate breakaway joints. Shelters rest in baskets rather than foundations, so they can be shifted when their supporting root begins to swell. Tools are thorn awls, stone scrapers, wooden wedges, braided cord, and the occasional precious metal blade. Fire is kept small and cupped; warmth comes mostly from layered fiber and carefully approached sap lines. No structure expects permanence. The successful ones look half-finished, capable of folding, slipping, or being abandoned without ceremony.

Older marks survive only by accident. A cord stair may be found sealed inside clear bark, its lower rungs vanishing into solid tissue. A row of notches can remain visible on a wall after the path they marked has fallen away. These are less like ruins than brief interruptions in growth.

For all its upheaval, the Underbloom is mostly quiet. The open distances separate events from their witnesses, and the felts and leaf-mats drink what sound remains. The ordinary noises are a drop ticking far down inside a hollow stem, a cord tightening in the cold, the papery tick of rime releasing from bark. Wind is often felt as pressure before it is heard. A distant collapse may arrive first as a soft movement of air against the face and only much later as a low, indefinite crack. One can watch a forest fall and still hear one’s own clothing.

Standing at a dew station during the bright phase, an observer might see no other artifact, no animal, and no obvious boundary to the world. There would be one gourd of water, cold light on the hands, and weather happening at several distances. Far off, a bloom-front would be whitening a province. Somewhere else, unseen, something immense would be coming apart.

And underfoot, almost below the threshold of sensation, the root would be thickening.

The Underbloom is not a cavern filled with vegetation. It is vegetation repeatedly inventing a cavern around itself—and leaving nearly all of it empty.
