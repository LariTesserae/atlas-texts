## The Country of the Standing Roar

At the top of the range, altitude stops producing peaks and begins producing distance.

The highest country is not a single summit but a broad, shattered roof of the world: white domes separated by saddles wide enough to hold valleys, shallow basins large enough for lakes, and outer escarpments from which lower ranges appear small, blue, and almost submerged. There are passes here that take days to cross without ever seeming to ascend or descend. The horizon is usually another part of the same country, far away and cruelly sharp in the thin air.

The range has not merely survived erosion. It has finished most of it. The summits that may once have been blades are now rounded backs of pale, fine-grained stone. Fractures have softened lips. Basins are lined with blocks whose corners have weathered into curves, and the scree below them lies locked in stable, interlocking pavements, darkened by mineral varnish. Nothing looks freshly broken. Even the deepest clefts feel old rather than violent. What remains is what the weather, over immense ages, has found difficult to take.

The light is nearly white. It arrives with the same intensity at every hour, from a sun fixed just past zenith, and the short shadows never turn. Overhead the sky is a dark, burned indigo; near the horizon it pales to a color too bright to look at directly. Distance does not soften outlines. A stone fifty paces away and a dome fifty miles away can appear equally immediate, so that scale becomes something understood by the body rather than the eye. The coolness is constant and impersonal. It does not gather in shade or retreat from sunlight. Exposed stone feels cold through the hand within seconds.

Yet the country is not entirely dry.

There are no streams on the open domes, and the great basins hold bright air instead of water. Moisture lives out of sight. It condenses within deep joints, where the stone never quite warms, and moves as a film through capillary cracks. Some clefts are dark and slick several body-lengths below their openings. In a few sheltered cavities, water collects in pools no larger than a cup, their surfaces covered by natural stone lips. A seep may shine along a wall without ever forming a drop. Elsewhere a single drop hangs for years in the same crevice, its losses balanced by condensation. Nothing overflows. Nothing quite dries.

The result is a strange division between touch and appearance. The visible land is dustless, pale, and arid; the interior of the land is damp. Air that leaves the throat feeling parched passes over stone saturated a short distance below. Almost every living thing depends on that hidden difference.

The other thing the visible land conceals is the sound.

From a distance, the country looks preternaturally silent. Within it, sound is overwhelming: a continuous, region-wide roar with no approach, no climax, and no interval of recovery. It is not simply wind, although wind is part of its cause. The ancient stone is penetrated by erosion tubes, narrow chambers, and systems of connected fissures. A permanent circulation of thin air moves through them, driven by balanced pressures between the high roof and the lands below. Almost nothing stirs at the surface, but belowground the whole massif is sounding.

Much of the roar travels through stone rather than air. It enters through the soles of the feet and the bones of the legs. Lying down makes it worse; the skull seems to become another resonating chamber. A shout disappears into it without an echo. Footsteps cannot be heard, only felt. After a time, the sound ceases to seem external. Thought has to form inside it, as though inside a second body.

A hand pressed to the ground can distinguish what the ears cannot: a low, steady foundation from the deepest chambers; rougher bands where air crosses broken stone; thin, penetrating threads made by animals. There is no single place from which the roar comes. Removing oneself from one basin only exchanges one combination of tones for another. The total volume never lessens.

Vegetation is so sparse that each plant appears to have made an independent decision to exist. On an acre of open stone there may be one gray-green cushion, hard as felt and no higher than a fingernail. Black lichen draws hair-fine lines along certain fractures. In the wettest recesses grow translucent scales and small, dense rosettes that hold moisture without ever looking lush. There are no grasses moving in unison, no shrubs marking shelter, no vegetated soil to soften the transitions between landforms. A plant may be separated from its nearest neighbor by half a mile of bare light.

Animal life, nevertheless, is abundant—abundant in the census of the region, almost never in the view.

A hand-length of damp stone can contain mineral mites, glassy springtails, lichen snails, and flat hunting insects layered into different depths of the same crack. Many are pallid and nearly blind. Several have no hearing in the ordinary sense; instead, their feet detect small interruptions in the ground’s vibration. They graze, hunt, mate, and die within a world that would seem, from above, to be empty stone.

The larger animals are widely spaced but numerous across the country’s immense extent. Rockrunners move alone over the domes: compact, long-limbed creatures with pale coats and broad, fleshy feet, feeding on cleft insects and the occasional cushion plant. Their calls are useless in the roar, so they signal with posture and with flashes of dark skin normally hidden beneath their coats. High soarers occupy another order of distance. Usually there is only one in the sky, a narrow cross suspended over a basin; sometimes a second appears so far away that it resembles a flaw in vision. They travel between carcasses, concentrations of airborne insects, and the warm stone faces where rising air offers effortless support.

One can cross a day’s distance and see no animal larger than a thumb, then rest with a palm against the ground and feel the country populated in every direction.

Nothing has been built here. There are no cairns, cut steps, shelters, walls, or maintained routes. The natural clefts are used as they are found and left unchanged. The nearest approach to technology is momentary: a rockrunner may select an unshaped pebble to crack the tough egg case of a cleft insect, or brace a stone against a shell while worrying it open. The pebble is then dropped. It is not carried, stored, improved, or recognized again. Nothing is fabricated, and nothing accumulates into an artifact.

Movement, likewise, should not be mistaken for change.

Animals cross the open stone. A soarer adjusts one wing. Moisture trembles in a fissure. A carcass is reduced, piece by piece, until its substance has returned to the hidden food web. But populations do not pulse, water levels do not rise, and the interlocked scree does not creep. Birth and death, condensation and evaporation, feeding and decay remain in exact balance. Even the roar does not age the stone that produces it; its energies are spent in forms the old range has already learned to endure.

The country therefore has no seasons of abundance or desolation. Its dampness and dryness, its abundance and emptiness, its ceaseless motion and absolute stillness are not alternating conditions. They are the same condition, seen at different scales.

From far below, it might appear as a silent white place caught in eternal noon. From within, it is an almost vacant immensity filled past bearing with sound—cool, dazzling, sparsely alive, and holding its one unchanging note.
