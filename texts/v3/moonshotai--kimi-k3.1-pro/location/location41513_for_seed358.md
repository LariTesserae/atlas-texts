## The Lower Basin

Beneath the limestone plateau, below the roots and most of the wells, lies a second lowland. It is not one cavern but a province of connected spaces: stepped chambers, flooded fault corridors, low divides where the ceiling nearly touches the water, and broader halls whose roofs rise beyond the reach of the lamps. The maintained routes cross some twelve kilometres of it; side passages continue farther. Its extent is regional, but it seldom offers a long view. Rock, water, pipework, and living things intervene.

The rock beds are old. The landscape made from them is not. Fresh fracture faces cut across the chambers, pale and splintered. Collapse blocks lie where they fell, still angular, their corners scarcely softened. There are few mature banks or rounded channels. Instead, warm mineral water spreads over the floor and begins laying down new ground: thin calcite skins, porous ledges, low travertine dams no higher than a boot. Behind each dam stands another pool. Behind that, another. The basin is becoming less a single open void than an increasingly intricate body of shallow water and mineral partitions.

Some of the newest crusts ring hollow when struck. They will bear the weight of a snail, a crab, perhaps a bird, but not a person. Elsewhere the deposits have thickened into creamy terraces stained honey and rust by iron. Their edges are soft-looking, almost folded, until touched; then they are all hard stone and sharp crystals. An abandoned survey stake stands in one pool to its knees in calcite. A length of rail nearby disappears into a mineral lip and emerges several metres later, rusted thin but fixed more firmly than when it was laid.

Water is never far away, even where there is no visible pool. It films the walls. It gathers in ceiling fractures and falls with the slow regularity of a leaking clock. It creeps beneath gravel, so that apparently dry ground gives off a dark shine when stepped upon. Warm springs rise through sand in small, steady boils. In the lower chambers, the water table and the floor are practically the same thing.

The air remains warm throughout the year and wet enough that clothing never quite dries. Cooler air entering from above condenses on stone and metal. Intake pipes sweat continuously; droplets travel along their undersides and fall from the flanges. Mist is usually thin, more a softening of distances than a visible cloud, though it thickens where spring water meets a cold draught. The smell is of wet limestone, faint iron, bat guano near the roosts, and, close to the stations, hot oil and electrical insulation.

Light must be brought in. Caged lamps stand far apart along the maintenance routes, some old and amber, others newer and white but hooded to a narrow work area. Their reflections tremble on black water and briefly pick out a ceiling rib, a cable, a cluster of pale animals. Then the route turns, and the darkness closes again. At three places, roof fissures admit a little daylight: not shafts of sunshine, but a grey weakening of the dark. Beyond those places, distance is judged more reliably by sound than by sight.

Visually, the industrial works are scattered. Acoustically, they are everywhere.

A pump house occupies a ledge beside the Long Sump, a concrete building no larger than a cottage. Another stands two kilometres away, half hidden behind a collapse block. Between them are transformer boxes, intake cages, gauge posts, short pipe bridges, and occasional stretches of cable fastened directly to the rock. There is no continuous factory floor. The installations resemble separated organs of one hydraulic body, drawing water from the deep pools and lifting it toward reservoirs on the surface.

The large pumps produce a low, sustained thunder that is felt in the teeth before it can be separated from the rest of the noise. Fans add a hard upper note. Somewhere a loose pipe contributes an irregular knocking, transmitted through water and stone until its source becomes impossible to locate. At overflow steps, falling water fills in every remaining frequency. Near the main station, speech is useless without ear protection and shouted directly into another person’s ear; crews mostly use hand signals. Even in the remotest galleries, there is no true silence, only a general roar in which individual machines can no longer be distinguished.

The noise does not make the basin empty. Away from the immediate vibration of the pump plinths, life is extraordinarily abundant, though mostly small and close to the surfaces.

The warm inflows carry rust-coloured and cream microbial films. Snails graze these into intricate pale trails. Amphipods and isopods work through the silt so densely that a handful of sediment seems to stir after it has been set down. Small pale fish hold station in the pools, some with eyes reduced to dark points; they turn toward disturbances in the water rather than toward movement seen from a distance. In the shallows, insect larvae inhabit the spaces between calcite crystals. Flatworms occupy the undersides of stones. Crickets, beetles, mites, and spiders gather wherever organic material collects.

The largest roost lies in a high chamber above the western pools. Bats hang so closely together that portions of the ceiling appear thatched. Their guano supports whole communities on the ledges below, and the ledges in turn feed the water whenever seepage washes material downward. At dusk, the emergence is a vast dry agitation of wings and bodies, partly visible in the dim entrance light and partly only felt as moving air. The machines cover most of the sound.

Vegetation, by contrast, is sparse. Near the roof openings are a few ferns, dark mosses, and roots that have followed moisture down through fractures. Around some of the older lamps, algae and stunted moss form small green halos. When a lamp fails, these halos fade; when maintenance crews install a new one elsewhere, another begins. Most of the basin has no green at all. Warmth and water are abundant, but light remains the strict boundary.

The basin changes character across its length. Near the outer connections, the water is cooler and carries leaves, silt, and occasional surface animals. There are drifts of waterlogged twigs caught against stone, and the animal life is most varied. In the central chambers, the pools are broader and clearer, divided by old pump intakes and progressively younger mineral dams. Farther in, along the Hot Fault, the passages narrow again. The walls there are raw and wet, the floor intermittently unfinished: warm mud, gravel cemented only at its surface, brittle sheets of new stone over black water. No route has been built across the last of these chambers. A gauge and a ceramic insulator on the wall mark an intention that was never carried farther.

Other human traces are isolated enough to be missed. A corrugated equipment shed with one wall folded by a falling slab. Eighty metres of narrow-gauge track ending at a pool. A steel ladder descending into water whose present use is unknown. A concrete stair that reaches a ledge and stops. These do not yet read as ruins in the ordinary sense. They seem more like objects temporarily caught in the process of becoming part of the terrain.

For all its noise and internal motion, the Lower Basin changes slowly. The pumps run without pause, but the principal water levels vary only modestly between wet and dry years. Bats leave and return each night; maintenance crews arrive, replace bearings, clear intake screens, and depart. These are cycles of circulation, not rapid transformation.

The substantial alterations are patient ones. A travertine rim thickens until a pool begins to overflow at a different point. A seep migrates along a fracture. A thin crust joins two blocks that have lain separate since the chamber opened. Rust spreads from a bolt, then becomes sealed beneath a transparent mineral glaze. Over generations, some passages will be subdivided; over much longer periods, others may collapse. Rare rockfalls occur suddenly, but they punctuate an otherwise gradual process rather than define it.

Three practical rules govern travel here. Dryness is temporary. Light is local. And anything left within reach of the water may eventually be claimed by stone.

The result is a crowded landscape with remarkably few events. Water circulates, engines labour, animals feed and roost, and almost imperceptibly the floor rises around them. The basin is loud because its machines are working, dim because nearly all illumination is imported, and warm because heat and water arrive together from below. Beneath those immediate conditions, its oldest activity continues without urgency: not the wearing down of a finished place, but the slow construction of one that has not yet settled on its final form.
