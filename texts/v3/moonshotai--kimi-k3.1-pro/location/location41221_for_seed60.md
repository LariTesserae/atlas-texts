## The Rootworks

At the lowest crease of an otherwise featureless plain lies the Rootworks, a warm hollow barely a hundred paces across. A person can walk around its rim in ten minutes. Crossing the interior can take an hour.

From outside, it looks like a patch of marsh vegetation too dense for its size. The surrounding flats are sedgy and open, shining after rain; the hollow is a solid green congestion, rising and folding over itself. Its boundary is marked in a few places by arcs of old concrete, softly rounded and stained black at the waterline. Elsewhere the retaining wall has been buried so completely that the transition from ordinary marsh to Rootworks is known only by the change underfoot: firm mud becomes a deep, elastic root mat, and every step brings water gently up around the heel.

There is no visible soil inside. The ground is a mass of interlaced roots—pale and supple near the surface, brown beneath, almost black farther down where they are always wet. Apparent stones prove to be old root-knobs, rounded by decades of dripping. Fallen branches rarely reach the floor. They are caught, pierced by fine rootlets, and slowly incorporated into the vertical thicket. Even the open pools are crowded: their surfaces are clear, but below them white submerged stems divide the water into narrow cells.

The vegetation does not merely cover the space. It supplies the walls, floors, pillars, and most of the ceilings. Cable-thin plants climb trunks already flattened against other trunks. Reeds with triangular stems grow through the roofs of their own dead leaves. Broad, waxy foliage overlaps in such tight layers that some leaves never open fully and remain as glossy green folds. Where trunks touch, they often graft. It is difficult to decide whether the hollow contains thousands of plants or one organism wearing thousands of different shapes.

Ordinary daylight enters through long openings in the canopy. Nothing glows; there is no perpetual twilight and no concealing mist. At noon the place is simply wet green wood, leaf wax, and iron under a bright sky. The light makes the dampness especially visible. Water beads along leaf margins, gathers in the grooves of stems, and stands on metal surfaces even when rain has not fallen for days. The air is warm and close, with a smell of sap, wet rope, and ferruginous water. There is surprisingly little smell of decay. Dead tissue is overgrown before it can become rot.

Near the center stand the remains of a one-room pump house: two brick corners, a lintel, and enough of a concrete foundation to support three horizontal engines. Their flywheels are taller than a person. Roots pass through the spokes without visibly displacing them. Piston rods disappear into packed green growth and emerge clean and dark on the other side. Old leather belts survive in sections, joined by broad living straps that flex when the machinery moves. Elsewhere in the hollow are only faint human marks: twelve paces of narrow-gauge rail, a ladder leading up into leaves, a green enamel gauge plate, and pipes whose purpose becomes apparent only when they knock.

The engines have no boiler, fuel store, or connected power line. They are worked by the vegetation.

Alongside them grow chambered canes with thick, ribbed trunks. Over many hours these canes draw in water and swell against the old piston yokes. When the pressure is sufficient, a trip valve opens with a hard metallic crack. A piston advances; a counterweight settles; one of the great flywheels turns far enough to reveal another spoke. The belts tighten. Somewhere below the root mat, a pump draws water and sends it up through a pipe furred inside with fine roots.

The flywheels move so slowly that they seem stationary until looked away from. Yet the accumulated motion is enough to keep the whole hollow in circulation. Water is lifted to a riveted cistern above the pump house, released into shallow gutters, and allowed to trickle through the upper vegetation. From there it descends leaf by leaf, pipe by pipe, back into the saturated floor. The flow prevents the deepest roots from stagnating and keeps the highest growth supplied through dry weeks.

The plants, in turn, maintain the machinery. Pipe joints are sealed with amber root-gum. Woody tissue has formed around sections of iron pipe, preserving their shape where the metal within has become thin. Oil cups remain full of a clear green exudate. Rust does not accumulate in flakes; its color appears instead in small iron-red nodules along the roots nearest the engines. Nothing looks newly ruined. Nothing looks recently repaired. The concrete is weathered, the brick corners are stable, and the roots gripping the engine beds are old, dense, and polished where moving parts still pass them.

At the center, the sound is loud without becoming a roar. A deep pump stroke comes approximately every half minute. Between strokes there are belt creaks, the small ticking of valves, and water hammer traveling through buried pipes with the sound of heavy footsteps below the floor. Droplets strike leaves at different pitches. A pressure release in one cane may emerge as a hollow wooden note from a stem twenty paces away. When rain comes, the broad foliage takes it first as a muffled drumming, then as a thousand separate streams. The machinery and the rain are difficult to distinguish.

There are almost no animal sounds. No frogs answer the water, no birds nest in the upper thickets, and insects are scarce enough that a single wingbeat can be noticed. A few colorless snails cling to submerged brick. Occasionally a white eel can be seen behind the cistern grille, always alone, its body almost indistinguishable from a moving root. The vegetation spreads chiefly by grafting, fragmentation, and water-carried spores; nothing in the hollow appears to depend on pollination.

Rain changes the water level slowly. After a week of storms, the pools widen for days rather than hours, and the root mat rises with a faint buoyancy. Then the pumps spend several weeks restoring the old brown mark on the cistern wall. Other changes require longer visits to perceive. A gap through which someone once passed becomes, twenty years later, a solid rib of fused stems. The ladder loses another rung to leaf growth. A rail vanishes, though its course remains visible as a straight, slightly greener line through the thicket. The engines continue at the same patient rate.

Beyond the concrete rim, the effect weakens quickly. The roots separate, ordinary rushes appear, and the knocking of the pumps becomes a dull pulse under the wet ground. The Rootworks does not seem to be spreading. It maintains its small boundaries and its exact degree of wetness, lifting water, packing new growth into old spaces, and drawing iron little by little into living tissue.

Its product appears to be its own continuation: a wetland with an iron skeleton, or a slow engine that has learned to keep itself alive.
