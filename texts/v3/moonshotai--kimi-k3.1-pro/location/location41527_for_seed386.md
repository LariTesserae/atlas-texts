## The High Caldera

At almost six kilometres above the sea, the old volcano should be a country of frost. Instead, heat comes first. It rises through the dark rock, collects beneath the vegetation, and meets the thin mountain air until the air itself feels warm and used. Even before dawn, water condenses on the leaves. At midday, the exposed ground shimmers. Rain brings relief for a few minutes, then begins steaming from the stones.

The summit is not so much a crater as a province. From the southern rim, the caldera extends nearly thirty kilometres, wide enough to contain several distinct basins, wooded ridges, a long lake, and gorges whose bottoms cannot be seen from any single vantage. The far rim appears as a blue-black line beneath ordinary daylight. Beyond it, the outer walls of the volcano fall away in immense, weathered steps. Clouds move against those walls far below, sometimes revealing lower ridges, sometimes closing over everything except the summit.

The caldera floor looks open from above. Within it, distances are crowded and layered. A marsh gives way to fern scrub; fern scrub to a wet forest of low, broad-crowned trees; the forest to a field of pale mineral terraces; and beyond the terraces, barely visible through drifting vapor, another line of green begins. There are few empty horizons at ground level, but always the sense of more country beyond the next rise.

Nothing here has the sharpness of a young volcanic landscape. The lava flows are ancient. Their edges have softened under water, roots, and weather. Basalt outcrops are rounded and stained chestnut, orange, and black. Old ash beds have become deep, water-holding soils. Where streams cut through them, their banks expose a compact stratigraphy of charcoal-colored earth, rusty clay, and pale pumice, all bound together by roots. Even the cliffs appear settled into their fractures. They shed occasional stones, but no great rebuilding seems imminent.

Water is seldom conspicuous everywhere at once, yet it is never far away. It stands in warm lakes and peat-dark pools, moves just beneath the marsh turf, and seeps from rock faces that remain wet through the brightest afternoons. Some springs emerge clear and tepid. Others issue cloudy with dissolved silica, or scalding and nearly lifeless. Their overflow spreads in thin sheets, cooling as it travels, until the first ochre microbial films appear, then green algae, then moss, then the dense roots of higher plants.

The long central lake is fed as much from below as from its streams. Its deeper water is clear, but along the geothermal shore it turns opaline and milky blue. Reeds occupy only the inlets where fresh water dilutes the minerals. Elsewhere, pale sinter forms low, lobed terraces directly at the waterline. These are not pristine white: rain has darkened their hollows, windblown soil has collected behind their lips, and small ferns have established themselves wherever deposition has slowed.

What gives the caldera its faintly industrial character is this circulation, though none of it was constructed.

Rain and cloud water enter the ground across the whole summit. They descend through old fractures, encounter heat, and return as springs, vapor, or pressure. Some vents exhale continuously, with no more force than a kettle left just below boiling. Others fill and empty on long cycles. At intervals, a muted concussion travels through the ground, followed by the release of steam from a different opening several hundred metres away. When mineral scale narrows one passage, flow strengthens elsewhere. The system adjusts without ever becoming still.

On a regional scale, the quantities are impressive: tonnes of water lifted and evaporated, silica carried out of the rock and laid down again, iron precipitated in broad red fans, sulfur drawn into yellow crusts around the hottest fissures. Circulation, pressurization, exhaust, deposition—the basin has the cadence of a vast works. But there are no engines, conduits, buildings, ruins, or discarded tools. Nothing has been cut to fit anything else. The resemblance to industry lies in scale and regularity, not in origin. The mountain manufactures only its own surfaces.

Most of those surfaces are occupied.

Vegetation follows temperature and chemistry more closely than altitude. Around fresh, warm streams, trees form a dense, uneven canopy, their trunks dark with epiphytes. Tree ferns occupy the understory, holding beads of water in their crowns. Beneath them are softer ferns, creeping plants, and mosses thick enough to conceal the actual ground. Leaves overlap at several heights, so that daylight arrives in scattered, shifting portions rather than as a single flood.

Toward the hot springs, the forest thins by degrees. First the tall trees disappear. Then the soil becomes too warm or too mineral-rich for all but waxy shrubs and low, copper-edged grasses. Closer still, only microbial mats remain: emerald where the water has cooled, rust-orange along the margins, gray-green near the sources. These boundaries move over years as springs migrate. A stand of trees may slowly yellow beside a newly warmed stream, while elsewhere an abandoned terrace turns green.

The result is lush without being uniformly hospitable. There are open mineral flats and patches of bare, steaming mud, but even they feel intensely populated by process. Bubbles rise. Thin skins form and tear. Water finds a new route around a deposited lip. In the vegetated ground, by contrast, almost nothing appears to move until one looks closely.

Dragonflies patrol the quieter pools. Small silver fish gather where hot and fresh water mix, retreating when the temperature shifts. Frogs live in the wet leaf cover and among reeds, many of them more often seen as a sudden displacement than as an animal. Wading birds step through the marshes with deliberate slowness, while swifts and larger soaring birds use the thermals above the rim, where flight costs less in the thin air.

On the wooded ridges live deep-chested, goat-like grazers with short coats and an unhurried gait. They crop the wet vegetation and open narrow passages through it simply by repetition. Their predators are seldom visible. Usually their presence is inferred from a sudden silence among the frogs, a flock rising from the lake, or tracks pressed into warm mud before dawn. Insects, larvae, fish, birds, grazers, hunters, and decomposers are abundant without turning the place into a spectacle. Much of the animal life is concealed by foliage, water, or the simple visual confusion of so many overlapping forms.

For all this activity, the caldera is mostly quiet.

The thin air does not carry sound well, and wet vegetation absorbs much of what remains. Water is more often heard dripping than flowing. A vent gives off a low hiss; mud releases a soft, intermittent note; something moves once through the ferns and stops. The pressure concussions are felt as clearly as they are heard. Even the great waterfalls on the outer cliffs are usually silent from the summit, reduced to white threads descending into cloud.

Rain is the loudest regular event.

It tends to come after the clouds have climbed the outer slopes and spilled over the rim. First the light flattens. Then the vapor around the vents thickens, and the smell of wet iron and crushed leaves becomes stronger. Rain arrives in broad, warm curtains, sometimes crossing only one basin while the next remains in sunlight. For a while, every surface contributes to the sound: leaves, lake, mineral crust, bare basalt, and the small hollows beneath exposed roots.

Afterward, the whole caldera resumes its dripping. Mist rises in separate columns rather than as a uniform fog. Colors return gradually—ivory sinter, red earth, black rock, and an almost excessive range of greens. The light remains ordinary mountain light, bright and direct in clear weather, diffuse beneath cloud, gold late in the day. There is no need for unusual illumination; wetness gives even the dullest materials a temporary sheen.

Night lowers the air temperature very little. The ground continues to radiate heat, and the warmest streams keep their banks free of dew by evaporating it. Animals shift their activity rather than stopping. Grazers move down from exposed ridges. Hunters follow. In the marshes, feeding and predation continue among roots and warm shallows. Over longer periods, springs seal and open, lake margins advance or retreat, and sections of forest succeed one another across abandoned mineral ground.

Yet these changes rarely read as disruption. Erosion and deposition have been working against one another for so long that the basin maintains a rough equilibrium. Streams carry sinter gravel away from active terraces; older terraces crumble into soil; soil slides into marshes; marshes trap it and spread. The mountain is active, but not impatient.

Its local rules are simple, if unusual. Altitude governs breath, not cold. Water chemistry governs where forest can become marsh, and marsh can become mineral flat. Subterranean pressure sets a rhythm beneath the weather, while sunlight and geothermal heat together drive nearly everything above it.

Seen from the rim after rain, the caldera can look peaceful: an elevated green watershed held above a sea of cloud, its lake shining between dark ridges. From within, it feels busier and less serene, crowded with seepage, growth, feeding, decay, and slow adjustments of stone. It is neither a garden nor a machine, though at moments it resembles both.

Its quiet production never quite ceases: cloud into water, water into steam, rock into soil, soil into forest, forest back into dark, saturated ground. Nothing is made for use elsewhere. Nothing is finished. The only lasting product is the basin itself—always almost in balance, and never exactly the same.
