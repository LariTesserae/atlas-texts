## The Plateau of Kept Rain

From a distance, the plateau looks less like a height than like something the heights have left behind. It stands far above a hazy, rust-colored country, yet there are almost no peaks upon it. Its altitude is an inheritance, not a gesture. The ranges that once rose here have been softened, carried away, and softened again, until only broad swells, shallow basins, and long almost-level saddles remain. Even the outer rim is rounded. Beneath an ordinary blue sky, in clear warm daylight, the whole region resembles a vast stone floor worn nearly smooth.

The scale is difficult to grasp because there are so few vertical measures. A dark line beyond the nearest basin may be another shallow swell, a colony of animals, or simply a change in the mineral crust. Distance gathers in layers rather than landmarks. The eye travels for leagues without finding anything sharply separate from anything else.

The exposed bedrock is pale buff and ash-gray, dense beneath but velvety at the surface with age. There is no fresh scree, little angular gravel, and none of the bright rawness of recently broken stone. Loose pebbles have been rounded to the shape of old molars. In the basins lies a flour so fine that it holds the delicate impressions of feet for years. Elsewhere the ground is sealed by a thin, dark crust: part mineral, part microbial film, part the accumulated binding of countless tiny lives. The gradients are so weak that water seems to have forgotten which way to run. Rain no longer carves the plateau. It darkens it.

Warmth rests over everything. The high air is thin but seldom harsh, and the old stone stores the day long after the sun has left it. At irregular intervals, rain arrives without much drama—a grayening of the horizon, a brief dimming, drops spaced widely enough to count at first. It seldom gathers into streams. Instead it sinks into dust lenses, collects in shell-shaped hollows, or stands for a few hours in pans so broad and shallow that they reflect the sky without appearing to have depth.

More often, moisture comes as condensation. Before dawn, when the surfaces of shells and stones cool faster than the air around them, beads of water appear along their edges. By sunrise they are gone.

The plateau is therefore damp without being wet. Most of its water is hidden: beneath a stone, within a crust, inside the sealed chamber of a nest, or held in living tissue. More water travels through bodies here than ever lies openly on the ground. After rain there is a brief smell of wet chalk and sweet, faintly medicinal decay. At other times the air carries almost no scent at all.

Vegetation is sparse enough to seem incidental. Small rosettes, no wider than a coin, occupy protected fissures. Gray hair-thin grass grows in broken rings where moisture occasionally stops. Here and there a fleshy, dark-leaved plant presses itself so closely to the ground that it resembles a stain until its edges lift in rain. From any elevation, these amount to little more than olive freckles on the pale surface.

The more important crop is nearly invisible. A photosynthetic film renews itself slowly across sheltered stone, richest where condensation lingers and animal waste has accumulated. It is thin, patient, and continuously grazed. Scarcity has not produced a simple ecology. It has produced an ecology in which almost nothing is wasted and almost nothing moves unless movement is worth the water it costs.

The apparent emptiness is the first misunderstanding.

Many of the rounded stones are alive.

The stonebacks begin as soft, mobile creatures, easily overlooked among the smallest pebbles. As they age, they take minerals into their outer surfaces and become heavier, flatter, more closely fitted to the ground. The young may travel several body lengths in a day. Older individuals shift only to adjust the collection of dew. The eldest open a narrow seam along their underside before dawn, extend a fringe of delicate breathing tissue, and then close again with the warming light.

Among them, youth is a mobile stage. Age is geographical.

Not every stone is an animal, but there is no reliable way to tell the difference at a glance. A field of apparently lifeless cobbles may contain hundreds of slow pulses. Some of the oldest stonebacks are broader than a room, their backs worn into shallow basins. Rain remains in these bowls for days. Smaller creatures gather along the margins, larvae develop in the warm shallows, and sparse plants root in the organic silt at the bottom. An animal can become a locality.

The greater multitude is smaller still. Transparent, thread-legged grazers crowd every damp film, their bodies so flat that they seem to slide within the surface rather than across it. Shell seams shelter larvae; larvae carry mites; the mites harbor smaller things in the folds of their mouths and joints. Beneath a single lifting stone, the layers can continue downward through several distinct communities, each using the moisture, warmth, or waste of the one above.

Even the air is inhabited. During the warmest hours, translucent veilwings hang almost motionless in the thermals. They are so thin that they disappear when seen edge-on. Only when a cloud shadow crosses them, or when they turn together at some subtle change in pressure, does their abundance become visible: thousands upon thousands of brief silvery creases suspended over the plain. They strain spores, dust-sized young, and drifting organic threads from the air. Sometimes their shadows pass over the ground like a net being slowly drawn.

For so much life, the plateau is extraordinarily quiet.

There are no choruses, no herd calls, no continuous insect drone. Most communication occurs through touch, taste, posture, or minute changes in the chemistry of a damp surface. The stonebacks make occasional ticks as their shells warm. Veilwings produce a soft papery brush when they descend too closely together. After rain, the passage of many small feet sounds like dust settling, though one must be nearly motionless to hear it. Wind often crosses the open swells without finding an edge sharp enough to give it a voice.

The silence is not emptiness. It is crowding without commotion.

Scattered across the region are a few structures, so sparse and so old that they can be mistaken for further eccentricities of erosion. A ribbed dome stands beside a basin; elsewhere only a sunken arc remains. Sometimes three or four low chambers open to the sky, their partitions softened and their entrances partly buried. The oldest examples are little more than rings in the bedrock, distinguishable by the regular spacing of their curves.

Nothing in them has been cut, fitted, fired, or assembled with tools. They are grown by small colonial animals that mix silk, shed body material, and mineral-rich secretions, adding one crescent at a time. Their chambers trap condensation and remain humid long after the surrounding ground has dried. Some serve as nurseries; others as reservoirs or shelters against the brightest part of the day. When the original colony dwindles, the structure does not remain empty. Other lives enter through the pores, then the cracks, then the open roof.

The only architecture here is biological, and even that is sparse.

Change proceeds mostly below the threshold of attention. A colony’s growing edge advances by a few body widths in a season. Generations of feet polish a route that no individual ever travels in full. An old basin fills imperceptibly with silt, becomes a garden of rosettes, then dries and is colonized by stonebacks. The largest animals leave no visible track from one year to the next, yet over centuries their positions alter.

Rare rains briefly accelerate everything. The dark ground film spreads and brightens. Seams open across fields of apparent stone. Veilwings descend in dense, soundless layers to drink, and the abandoned chambers steam faintly in the returning warmth. For a short while the plateau becomes visibly saturated with life. Then the water is taken in, the surfaces close, and the land resumes its mineral appearance.

Even erosion has been drawn into this patience. The living crust binds dust before wind can gather it. Shells intercept loose grains. Dead material is occupied, cropped, incorporated, and occupied again before it can travel far. The plateau is approaching a condition in which nearly every particle has a user, and nearly every surface is also a shelter.

By noon it is once more a warm, almost barren tableland under normal light: open to the horizon, sparsely marked, immensely old. But its emptiness is only a matter of scale and timing.

Height without peaks. Rain without rivers. Building without tools. Multitude without noise.

The old ground holds its water, its dust, and its animals close, changing by such small degrees that even erosion seems to have learned to wait.
