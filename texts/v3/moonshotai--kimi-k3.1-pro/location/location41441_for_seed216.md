## The Upper Weave

At an elevation where a country of stone would be cold and thin-aired, the Upper Weave remains warm. Heat rises gently through the wet surface underfoot. The air rests against the skin, heavy with vapor, and even at noon the light is a muted green-brown. The sun, when it can be found at all, is only a pale uncertainty behind the high haze.

Rain has recently fallen. Water shines in the seams, gathers along leaf edges, and releases single drops at long intervals. Each drop is distinct. There is little else to hear.

From a distance, the country might be mistaken for high moorland: olive swells, dark hollows, shallow flats silvered with water, long ridges softened by age. The resemblance ends at touch. The ground is not ground. It flexes slightly, distributing weight with the patience of a taut sail. Its skin is a mat of fine living roots. Its ridges are bundles of stems trained around one another and bound. Its small, leathery leaves overlap into membranes broad enough to roof valleys.

No stone breaks the surface. There is no mineral soil. The dark, crumbly material in the oldest marsh bottoms is compost, laid into root baskets so long ago that its layers have acquired the appearance of sediment. Beneath it are older baskets, older cords, older knots. Digging does not reveal an original earth. It reveals previous work.

Everything has been worked, though very little looks manufactured. A basin that seems perfectly wild has a stitched lip. A thicket pouring uncontrollably down a slope proves, on closer inspection, to be an old repair that outgrew its pattern. Even the open leaf plains have a grain: walk against it and the surface resists; walk with it and the seams guide the foot. Nothing here is geology. Hills are supported forms, valleys are catchments, and cliffs are the exposed edges of a living shell.

Water seldom remains in a river long enough to become one. It is caught too often. It crosses the plains in glossy films, disappears into capillary grooves, collects in warm tea-colored pools, and weeps from the cut ends of ancient channels. On steep faces it descends through thousands of fine root conduits, emerging far below as condensation rather than a waterfall. Some marshes have no visible inlet. Their water arrives by seepage, by rain, or through old drains whose sources can no longer be located with any confidence.

At the marsh edges, the roots become finer and softer, a wet green pile underfoot. Pale shoots stand just above the water, breathing through swollen stems. Thread-eels lie along the surface, colorless and motionless until some small disturbance passes through them. Then the whole pool seems to shiver, though there may be only a dozen eels in it.

Wear has rounded everything. The oldest causeways are slightly hollowed, their cords polished smooth by feet, water, and the slow tightening of growth. Frayed bindings are not necessarily failures; many have become habitat, and new roots have braided themselves through the loose ends. Collapse happens, but it leaves no rubble. Detached material is absorbed, composted, woven elsewhere. The Weave looks immensely old without looking abandoned. Decay, regrowth, and repair have been in approximate balance for longer than any regional record survives.

The surprising thing is how empty it is.

This is total vegetation without jungle. Every surface is alive, but almost every volume is open air. The plant mass is continuous and comparatively thin: plane, cord, membrane, vault. A traveler may cross hours of unbroken green flooring with nothing taller than the knee and no loose object anywhere in sight. Then the flooring rises into a ridge, the ridge reveals itself as the back of an enormous vault, and beneath it opens a hollow large enough to contain its own mist.

Some hollows are shallow, no more than a hand’s breadth between upper and lower growth. Others have no visible far wall. Their fibrous surfaces drink sound, so even the largest of them rarely echo. A drop falls somewhere below. Much later, there is a small tick as a stem takes up the moisture. The silence returns unchanged.

Above, the country continues beyond any useful horizon. The dimness makes it look close-ended, but this is an illusion of haze. There are provinces behind the visible rain, and other provinces behind those. The Weave is large enough for weather to become geography. A rain district may crawl across one watershed for days while the next remains under warm, motionless cloud. Distant precipitation hangs in vertical gray veils, slowly fraying before it reaches the leaf plains.

The weather is largely made from the Weave’s own water. As the veiled day strengthens, the basins steam. Fog pours into hollows, rises through open seams, and gathers beneath membranes where it condenses and returns as rain. Clouds sometimes form underneath a traveler and pass upward around their ankles. At night the temperature falls only slightly; the vast wet fabric and its sheltered waters give back the day’s warmth. Morning is not cold, merely less warm.

The high haze is full of fine spores. These too belong to the cycle, though no single species controls them. They dim the sky, seed some of the cloud, and settle in wet seams where they can be consumed or carried elsewhere. The air smells of steeped stems, warm tannin, and rain resting on old fiber.

The technique visible in the oldest accessible joints is hand technique. Cords were twisted from bast. Stems were bent around wooden forms and held until they kept their curvature. Holes were opened with thorn awls. Surfaces were scraped with shell, cut with split hardwood, and joined with resin and patient binding. There are no forged braces deeper down, no buried engines, no skeleton of some more confident machinery beneath the vegetable work. There is only more handwork, older and larger in its consequences.

The scale came from duration and repetition.

Fresh construction is almost crude: a coarse lashing around a split ridge, a bundle of leaves stitched over a leak, a canal propped open with green wedges. Give it a season and the living material begins to conceal the method. Give it centuries and the repair becomes terrain.

Keepers still exist, but they are too sparse to constitute a presence at landscape scale. Their shelters resemble unopened buds and are easily mistaken for swellings in the weave. A keeper may carry little more than an awl, a scraper, a coil of cord, and a few lengths of wood whose worn shapes indicate uses no outsider would guess. Their maps contain almost no distances. They record materials, descent of patterns, and obligations: which bindings must be renewed, which basin depends on which gutter, which path must be walked occasionally if it is to remain a path.

Life is abundant here in census and scarce in view.

The felt-footed browsers are the most visible animals. They are tall, narrow creatures, their wet fur hanging in soft cords, their broad feet making almost no impression on the causeways. Usually one appears alone, cropping silvered tips from the structural growth. It may be the forward edge of a herd numbering in the thousands, with the rest distributed across a range too large to comprehend from any single vantage.

Sail moths cross the warm updrafts without visibly beating their wings. They let go, plane downward, and vanish into the dimness. Beneath the leaf vaults, colonial spinners suspend fine nets that comb moisture from fog; the nets tremble with small trapped lives. In the water films, translucent larvae crowd so densely that a single footprint can briefly become a world of movement. Stand upright again, and the plain appears empty.

The predators are seldom seen. They fold themselves into angles of rootwork, flatten beneath marsh lips, or hang in the upper dark where a sail moth’s path narrows. Their abundance is inferred from shed mouthparts, neatly opened carcasses, and the behavior of the browsers, who sometimes lift their heads all at once and change direction without any audible signal.

Much animal communication travels through the material rather than the air. A hand laid flat on a causeway can feel faint tapping, delicate and continuous: footsteps, courtship, warnings, the minute impacts of feeding. The apparent silence is partly a matter of listening in the wrong medium. A shout goes nowhere. A vibration may travel for miles.

The oldest peculiarity of the Upper Weave lies in its knots.

A binding holds two stems together. Here, it may also help hold their locations together. This is not a power possessed by every individual cord, nor something a keeper can simply command with the right pattern. It is a property accumulated by the whole made fabric, after unimaginable repetition of joining, loading, growing, and repair. The Weave was constructed so thoroughly that its joins became part of the grammar by which space remains coherent.

Where the work is sound, distance is mostly trustworthy. Where a joint has worn smooth and begun to slip, material and geography can slip together.

A causeway may remain perfectly firm while its far end becomes unreachable. A gutter may deliver warm rain from a basin that is no longer anywhere above it. Two hollows separated by a thin membrane may contain more distance between them than either contains within itself. Much of the Weave’s emptiness is real additional room: old interior spaces have continued to enlarge after their walls stopped growing.

Occasionally, when fog clears from a deep vault, there is landscape on the far side and also overhead. The same wet downs curve across the upper dark, complete with ponds and grazing animals. Water clings to those ponds according to another local down. The browsers move without concern, their bodies small against a sky that should be behind the observer. A cloud released beneath one’s feet may rise toward them and become their weather.

None of this presents itself as a catastrophe. It is too old for that. The breakdown has been weathered into ordinary conditions.

Sail moths use slack joins to cross regions without traversing the space between. Predators learn to wait near certain worn bindings for prey arriving from elsewhere. Browser herds migrate by adjacency as much as by direction, which is why a single animal can appear on an empty plain where no herd has approached. Keepers judge a route not only by whether it holds weight, but by whether it still returns a walker to the same neighboring places.

A freshly tightened knot can make a horizon withdraw. There is no lurch, no sound of movement. Rain that was visible a moment before is simply absent, and the distant ridge has become part of some other journey. The work is considered successful when nothing remarkable happens afterward.

At the outer edges, where the living shell thins and its supporting bundles descend into cloud, the height of the Weave becomes physically felt. There is no visible base. Warm updrafts carry the smell of marshes from far below. Sometimes a support, followed downward for long enough, is found approaching from above. “High” and “low” remain useful locally, but they are not promises about the whole.

Stand on such a rim after rain and the world seems almost vacant: a shining leaf plain, one far-off animal, a veil of weather in another province. Beneath your feet, millions of hollow spaces take up and release moisture. Growth tightens the old cords. Something feeds. Something else approaches it without sound.

A knot contracts with a small, dry tick.

The distant animal lifts its head, responding not to the noise but to a change transmitted through the surface. Then warm rain begins again, so sparsely at first that the separate drops can be counted. The Weave accepts them, grows slightly heavier, and continues.

It is not a ruin being reclaimed by plants. Plants were among the materials and tools from which it was made, and the making never stopped. Nor is it simply coming apart. It has settled into an older, stranger balance, in which even the nearness of one place to another must occasionally be maintained.
