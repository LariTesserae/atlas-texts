## The Unfinished Highland

From a distance, the Unfinished Highland resembles a city seen through heat: a few pale verticals, long broken green lines, reservoirs flashing between them. The resemblance survives arrival, but not inspection. There are no streets connecting separate structures, no masonry resting on soil, no wild growth reclaiming an abandoned work. The work and the growth are the same act.

The highland stands above a sea of lower cloud. At its rim, where the outer surface has recently split, the drop exposes no geological strata. There is no bedrock, no gravel, no ancient clay. The cliff face is a section through compressed root, bleached stem, glassy rind, and black chambers full of water. Its height has been grown rather than uplifted. The whole region stands on earlier versions of itself, layer upon layer, some crushed into dense fibers, others still hollow and resonant. Its immense size is not evidence of age. Almost everything visible is new.

Altitude does not make it cold. Heat rises from sun-warmed reservoirs and from the respiring mass beneath them, and the wet air holds it. The highland makes a climate of its own: hot, heavy, and saturated, with vapor collecting in the open basins until distant structures lose their bases and appear to float. Shaded inner surfaces, cooled by evaporation, stream with condensation. The air smells of crushed stems, tannin, wet cork, and the faint sweetness of sap beginning to ferment inside sealed tissue.

The light is surprisingly ordinary. White daylight passes through moving vapor; when the cloud thins, shadows become hard and colors almost commonplace—leaf green, ivory, tea-brown water, the dull silver of wet silica. Nothing glows. The strangeness of the place lies in what its materials do, not in how they look at first glance.

For tens of kilometres, the highland opens into broad courts, flooded flats, broken terraces, and long intervals of empty sky. It is not crowded. Total vegetation here describes substance, not density. Every solid surface is plant, but the plants do not necessarily touch. A solitary ribbed column may rise from a green plain with nothing else near it. A cluster of hollow leaf-vaults may stand beyond a kilometre of still water. Between them are wide, low skins of growth so smooth they appear bare until they flex under water pressure or darken with a passing shadow.

Elsewhere, the spacing changes without becoming lush. A line of immense buttresses encloses a basin and then stops abruptly, leaving one side open to the weather. A collapsed arcade lies in several clean pieces, each piece already sprouting a fringe of pale shoots. At the rim, curtains of fine root hang into hot mist, but even these are separated by long gaps through which the lower cloud can be seen moving far below.

Nothing has been assembled from discrete parts. An arch is continuous with its supports; a support thickens into a water chamber; the chamber sends up a fluted stem. There are no bricks, cords, nails, pipes, engines, or hidden instruments. Where a function is needed, anatomy supplies it. A constriction regulates flow. A folded surface sheds rain. A rib carries weight. A waxy seam opens under pressure and closes when the tissue dries. The landscape is entirely made without having been manufactured.

Water is everywhere, but not always where gravity would leave it. It lies in broad, shallow mirrors on the flats, stands deep in dark cisterns, beads on vertical walls, and seeps upward through laminated floors. Some surfaces never dry. Others steam for an hour, then begin sweating again before their heat has faded. Fine roots draw moisture through the air itself, gathering droplets until their tips are heavy and transparent.

During quiet periods, streams follow the expected slopes. Then some distant growth begins to draw on the shared body of the highland, and the water hesitates. A channel slows, reverses, and climbs. Thin films rise along flutes in a column. Beads on a wall join into trembling vertical veins. Whole reservoirs can be lifted into the upper chambers of the sparse towers, leaving their basins lined with glossy, contracting skin. For a while, anatomy takes precedence over topography. Eventually the pressure fails somewhere, and all that suspended water returns at once.

These releases are only one part of the highland’s turbulence. The larger disturbances are growth events.

A flat begins with almost imperceptible blisters beneath its wet surface. The blisters become long green welts. Deep knocking sounds travel through the floor, though there is no cavity large enough to explain them. Then the surface splits—not with the crumbling of earth, but with the clean report of fiber under tension. Pale tissue emerges, already shaped. It may thicken into a wall, unfold into a succession of vaults, or rise as a narrow blade too sharp and tall to support itself.

Older structures respond. Some stiffen, their rinds becoming glossy and hard. Others soften at the base and begin to sag, not from rot but because their substance is being withdrawn. Water drains from them in sudden ropes. An arcade bows; a reservoir tilts; a tower takes on a curve that was not present moments before. Then something tears. For several minutes there may be only spray, falling sheets of tissue, exploding cavities, and waves crossing spaces that had been open air.

At any given moment, one district is roaring through such a revision while another drips quietly and a third lies under calm, ordinary sunlight. The open scale of the highland makes these states visible together: a white plume of spray on one horizon, new green walls catching the sun elsewhere, and between them a great empty flat reflecting both.

Because destruction and construction follow so closely, the landscape remains perpetually young. Water has not had time to round its edges. Broken lips stay sharp. Fresh escarpments show crisp internal layers, and splintered ribs retain their splinters. There are no worn river cobbles, no deep mellow soils, no hills softened into anonymity. Even the broad curves look newly made, their surfaces stretched tight over changing pressure. Material disappears through collapse, reabsorption, and renewed growth before weather can give it an old face.

The highland is loud, but its loudness is as sparsely distributed as its structures. There is no continuous mechanical hum. Instead, sounds arrive at great volume and from great distances: water booming inside a hollow stem; the long fibrous crack of a wall splitting; the hollow percussion of a reservoir emptying; rain striking waxy vaults in a hard, uneven roar. Wind through torn columns produces notes that change as the columns grow. A collapse kilometres away may roll across the open water and seem to occur just beyond the nearest buttress.

Afterward comes a comparative quiet of runoff, dripping, and the small tick of contracting rind. Nothing calls in response. There are no wings in the air, no bodies moving through the pools, no insects in the new tissue. Every splash is water or falling plant matter. Every voice is hydraulic, structural, or atmospheric.

The absence of animals has shaped the ecology completely. Nothing is pollinated by a visitor, grazed back, bored into, or carried in a gut. Reproduction occurs through wind-dust, waterborne propagules, and fragmentation. A fallen rib may root along its entire length. A fragment of reservoir lining, lodged in a crack, can begin building another chamber. The highland spreads less by colonizing new ground than by turning its own wreckage into foundations.

It is unclear whether the region is one plant with many organs or many plants sharing continuous tissue. Some structures fail independently, while elsewhere a cut through an apparent joint reveals vessels passing uninterrupted from one form into another. Competition, when it occurs, is hydraulic: one growing district draws pressure from another, leaving distant walls slack and thirsty. Cooperation is equally impersonal. A new reservoir may stabilize several towers simply by altering the weight and tension of the floor beneath them.

Consider one apparently empty court. At its far side stands a single column; nearer lies a collapsed lattice; between them is a black pool. Under flat daylight, the arrangement could remain unchanged for years. Then the pool begins to shrink without draining. Water climbs the column audibly. The lattice sinks a little, its oldest strands unraveling into green threads. A blister crosses the court, opens, and raises an arch so pale it seems unfinished.

The arch holds just long enough to cast a shadow. Then its crown splits. Water comes through it in one steep, continuous sheet, spreading across the court and carrying pieces of the lattice into the former pool. When the spray clears, there are two shallow terraces where the flat had been. Their edges drip white sap. The column stands farther away than it did before, though it has not moved; the court has changed around it.

By the time the surfaces begin to darken and condense again, the event no longer looks like a catastrophe. It looks like a stage of construction whose purpose was never visible.

To call the highland a garden would imply a gardener. To call it a city would imply inhabitants, streets, and intended destinations. To call it a machine would imply devices. It is none of these, though it borrows something from each. It is constructed without hands, hot at an altitude that should cool it, saturated above the clouds, and youthful because it cannot remain finished long enough to age.

Its emptiness is not abandonment. The wide courts, clear water, and spaces between towers are the slack it requires for its next revision. From the rim, the region can briefly appear serene: ordinary green and ivory under daylight, scattered forms reflected in still water. Then a far horizon lifts, not with cloud but with new tissue, and a deep crack travels across the entire open basin.

The highland is building again.
