## The Cup of Brief Water

High on a mountain whose upper ridges stand above most weather, a small cirque hangs between three splintered walls. Its fourth side is open, ending in a lip of pale rock above a depth usually filled with cloud. The whole place is only a few hundred paces across. On level ground one could cross it in minutes; here, in air too thin to satisfy a hard-breathing body, the crossing becomes a succession of rests, careful steps, and small negotiations with distance.

There are no trees on the surrounding slopes, and no softer country visible below. On clear days the cloud-field shines upward into the cirque, so light seems to come from everywhere: from the white sun, from the depth beneath the lip, from mica in the grit and frost along the walls. The sky above is a dark, nearly violet blue. Colors in the basin are correspondingly severe—wet moss almost black, flowers an alarming yellow, freshly broken stone white as a exposed bone. Even the shade glitters.

The cirque looks freshly opened. Ice may have only recently loosened its grip on the shelf, and nothing since has had time to soften it. The walls rise in blades and overlapping plates. Fractures remain bright and clean; talus lies in angular heaps rather than rounded beds. A few scored slabs still show the direction of the old ice, but frost is already undoing that record. Each night it enters the cracks. Each bright day, meltwater swells and drains within them. Now and then a plate lets go. The resulting clatter is startlingly brief, and afterward the mountain seems quieter for having made a sound.

At first glance, the floor is nearly empty: pale gravel, shallow pans of frost-heaved stones, a handful of isolated blocks standing far apart. Nothing tall interrupts the light. There is no canopy, no thicket, no dark interior. Yet the emptiness is mostly vertical. Kneel, and the basin changes.

Along the foot of the eastern wall, and in narrow crescents wherever fractures hold moisture, vegetation grows so densely that it forms a continuous, springy skin. Mosses lie beneath miniature sedges; sedges thread through cushions of tiny, close-packed leaves; dark, resinous shrubs spread sideways rather than upward, their woody stems gripping the rock. Some leaves are silver with hairs, others fleshy and nearly black. Many are no larger than a pinhead, but together they make a growth as rich and layered as any lowland thicket. In the brief flowering hours, the mats are crowded with yellow cups, white stars, and deep garnet bells that look opaque until sunlight passes through them.

The plants do not depend on a stream. There is none. They drink frost, cloud, and the occasional cold rain.

Most clear dawns begin white, though no snow has fallen. Rime coats every leaf and stone. When cloud rises over the open lip, the vegetation combs droplets from it; water gathers on leaf tips, runs down stems, and collects in rock hollows sometimes no larger than a cupped hand. In sunlight, thin runnels wander a few feet across the grit before vanishing. By afternoon their courses may be dry again. A cloud passing over the lip can wet the entire basin in minutes, but it leaves no pool behind. True rain is uncommon, often arriving mixed with ice needles, and the cirque receives it with the same bright, indifferent glitter.

The air remains bitterly cold even when the sun-warmed stone is hot to the touch. This contradiction governs nearly everything. Life occupies the narrow region between light and ice. On one face of a rock, frost loosens and black insects begin to move; a hand’s width behind it, the ground remains frozen. Beneath a plant cushion the air is damp and still, while just above it the wind draws moisture away. Desert, meadow, refuge, and killing cold can all occur within the thickness of a leaf.

The animals, like the plants, keep their abundance below the horizon.

Rock-hares live in burrows among the gravel, their coats pale on the back and dark along the spine. In the early light they sit so closely against stones that they seem to be part of the talus. Later they venture out to crop sedges and carry mouthfuls of dry stems underground. Their tracks stitch the open pans together; their droppings enrich isolated patches of green. Burrow entrances, old dust baths, and well-worn escape runs reveal how busy the apparently vacant floor has been.

When sunlight has warmed the mats, black, furry bees and small copper beetles emerge. Midges rise in loose, almost invisible threads above the wettest moss. Ash-colored birds drop from clefts in the walls to feed among them, moving in quick, quiet groups. A cloud shadow can end the whole performance at once: insects settle, birds return to cover, and the hares flatten themselves against the ground. When the light returns, activity resumes without ceremony.

Larger animals leave fewer but deeper signs. Split-hooved climbers occasionally cross the rim in twos and threes, tearing at the shrubs and leaving wool on sharp stone. A dun-colored predator hunts the basin mostly at dusk and dawn. It is seldom seen, but its line of prints sometimes crosses a hundred smaller tracks, and now and then a few hare hairs or a clean scatter of feathers mark where one of those tracks ended.

None of this produces a chorus. The cirque is a place of small, separated sounds: the tick of ice releasing a stone, a thread of water entering gravel, the dry rustle of a stem being pulled underground, a bird’s single click from the wall. Wings are often louder than voices. On still days, breathing may be the loudest sound in the basin. Even the wind tends to remain above it, hissing along the rim while the sheltered floor holds its quiet.

Human traces are so slight that they can be mistaken for more frost-work. Three cairns mark a route across the cirque, though one has fallen and another is only a dark stone placed on a light one. They do not indicate a road so much as a sequence of survivable choices: where the talus does not slide, where the rim can be approached without cornices, where the cloud usually thins.

Beneath the eastern wall stands a low semicircle of unmortared stone, roofless and barely high enough to shelter someone lying down. One inner slab carries a faint stain of soot. Whoever rests here burns dried dung or carried fuel sparingly; nothing in the basin offers wood in useful quantity. Under a nearby flat stone lies a little cache of worked things—a chipped flake of dark stone, a hammerstone worn smooth at one end, and cord too brittle with age to lift. There are no fields, no permanent hearth, no structure that confidently expects another generation.

The marks do not make the cirque feel inhabited. They make its emptiness more precise. Every winter rearranges some of the stonework, and each returning visitor must decide whether a heap is a sign, a shelter, or merely what the frost has done.

The whole place follows the sun with an almost hurried attention. Morning loosens the frost; late morning opens the flowers; noon brings water, insects, grazing, and hunting shadows. Afternoon cloud may erase the basin in whiteness, bead every leaf, and then withdraw as suddenly as it came. At dusk the runnels cease. Insects disappear beneath moss and stones, hares stop their burrow entrances with dry grass, and the hunting animal begins its silent circuit. By night, frost has rebuilt much of what the day dismantled. The stars are hard and numerous, and the rime returns their light faintly.

Every clear day is therefore a small wet season followed by a small winter. Soil gathers only where roots can hold it. Water survives only where shade or stone protects it. Paths, burrows, shelters, and even streambeds remain provisional.

Yet the cirque does not feel as though it is waiting to become a lake, a forest, or a settlement. Its incompleteness is not a promise. It is the character of the place: brilliant, cold, closely bounded, and crowded with life that leaves almost no silhouette. From above, it will always look nearly empty. From within, it is full of small, urgent beginnings.
