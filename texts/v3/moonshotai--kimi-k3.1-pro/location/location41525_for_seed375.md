## The Unoccupied Town

At the lowest point of a black mineral plain, a small town has recently come to the surface. It looks as though someone has drawn a city with a knife: two narrow ring streets, a handful of radial lanes, walled courts, stacked chambers, and thin bridges crossing slots in the ground. The whole settlement covers only a few hundred paces. Its highest roof stands barely above the surrounding plain, and from there nearly every other structure can be seen at once.

The basin containing it is shallow and raw. Its sides have not slumped into slopes, and the fractures around its rim still match across their gaps. There are no rounded stones, no old banks of sediment, no softened foundations. The town’s outermost walls end in combs of mineral needles. Fresh stairs have edges sharp enough to seem unfinished. Whatever age the architecture suggests is an illusion; even its oldest exposed surfaces are young.

Water is absent in a more absolute sense than drought. There are no dry watercourses, because nothing has ever flowed here. The pale powder along the curbs is not dried mud and has never been mud. The white crust on the walls is not salt left by an evaporating lake, nor frost left by a cold night. Night brings no dew. The basin contains no buried ice. Even the town’s life, such as it is, proceeds without sap, blood, or any other circulating liquid.

The building material is a milk-grey substance somewhere between shell and ceramic. It forms in laminae so fine that a broken wall resembles a stack of pages fused along one edge. Fresh layers have a faint pearl brightness; older ones darken toward blue and iron-black. Thin walls transmit a little light, while thick ones seem to absorb it. Every beam is continuous with its supports. Every stair rises out of its floor without a joint. There are no fired bricks, no cut blocks, no fastenings, and no tool marks.

Nothing portable has ever been made here. There are no vessels, doors, cords, blades, furnishings, or machines. The conduits beneath the streets are simply longer versions of the hollows in the walls. The rooms contain no objects at all.

Emptiness is the town’s principal building material. Arcades open onto other empty arcades. Chambers stand within chambers, their nested doorways framing successively smaller patches of dim light. Some roofs cover nothing but a deep, clean shaft. Elsewhere, a whole succession of bridges crosses the same narrow court at different heights, though there is rarely anything below them but a single drifting grain of dust. For all its partitions, the town contains far more vacancy than matter.

The light never becomes bright. A small, veiled star hangs behind high mineral dust, and the sky remains the colour of old pewter. Its cycle produces no true day or night, only shallow dimness and deep dimness. In shallow dimness, the walls are pale and the streets almost black. In deep dimness, that relationship reverses: the plain disappears, while the newest layers of the town remain faintly visible.

The air is cool, still, and dry, with a slight smell of metal and sulphur. Cold gathers most strongly around active seams, where fresh material is being laid down. There, a wall may be several degrees colder than the air touching it.

The town itself makes no sound. Dust moves without hissing. Walls grow without grinding. Occasionally an outer shell separates into plates and settles into the street, but it does so as a slow dispersal rather than a collapse. The lamellar surfaces swallow vibration, and even a loud sound brought in from elsewhere would find little echo. The silence is not peaceful exactly. It is simply unrelated to the amount of activity taking place.

For the town is alive, or is the product of something alive.

Beneath the basin lies a colonial organism that has never been seen entire. It is convenient to call it the foundation, though it is also the mason, the quarry, and most of the population. Its living tissue exists as extremely thin mineral lattices following the fractures in the plain. At its growing fronts, it deposits the grey shell material around itself and then withdraws, leaving cavities behind. What appear to be streets are the intervals between advancing lobes. Rooms are abandoned feeding spaces. Towers are narrow vents through which charged dust can rise. Stairs are corrugations formed where a growing front repeatedly meets resistance and changes level.

The urban order may be no more intentional than the order of a honeycomb, but it is less repetitive and therefore harder to dismiss. Lanes branch around obstructions. Courtyards recur at useful intervals. Upper galleries connect precisely where lower passages narrow. Layered construction accumulates beneath the streets and across the roofs, not as successive occupations but as successive states of one physiology.

Growth is quickest in deep dimness. A doorway may narrow by the thickness of a fingernail over several hours. A new tread appears on a stair as a pale ridge, then hardens. A bridge thickens from below while its upper surface remains apparently untouched. Every few cycles, an older district sheds its outer layer, exposing architecture whose edges are once again perfectly new. Nothing here mellows into ruin. A chamber becomes obsolete by narrowing, filling, or sealing.

The weather participates in the construction.

There is no rain and usually no wind. Instead, changes in temperature and charge lift dust from the plain in broad, motionless-looking sheets. The sheets separate into filaments over the town. Dust then travels along the streets in shallow streams, climbs particular walls, and gathers in vertical columns above the vents. It can move uphill or hang motionless over a step. At junctions it divides cleanly, as though the curbs were banks and the whole city had been designed to channel it.

During one part of the cycle, the courts fill ankle-deep with this pale material. During another, the charge reverses and the courts empty. Some of the dust is carried back to the plain; some is taken into active seams and becomes wall. Thus the streets are repeatedly buried and swept without ever being disturbed in any ordinary sense. Their floor channels are not drains. They are weather courses.

The sparse vegetation lives where these streams divide.

Here and there, usually no more than once in a courtyard, a bronze-black fan rises from a joint. Each fan consists of stiff, branching filaments, some broad and flat, others fine as wire. They have no green parts and contain no moisture. Charge moves through them in slow patterns, and they grow by replacing and adding material atom by atom. In shallow dimness they remain tightly folded, resembling small pieces of dark ornament fixed to the walls. In deep dimness they open, turn toward the dust streams, and develop a temporary bloom of coppery scales along their edges.

There are long stretches with no fans at all. One whole outer street may contain only three. A smaller, soot-coloured growth occasionally forms low crusts in their shade, but even together these living surfaces cover very little of the town.

Animals are rarer still. The most frequently encountered are the scalebacks: flat, hand-sized creatures whose segmented mineral bodies can lie so closely against a wall that they seem to be shallow inscriptions. They feed on the fans’ new scales and on the charged films left by settling dust. A scaleback may remain motionless through several light cycles, then cross a court in a series of movements so smooth and widely separated that its progress is easier to infer than to watch.

Somewhere in the settlement, usually in a floor channel, there is also a hunter. It is long, narrow, and pale, with a body that can follow the joint between two stones without disturbing their dust. When extended, it resembles a fresh seam in the pavement. It takes scalebacks by folding around them and drawing them slowly out of their shells. There is no struggle audible from even a short distance away. Afterwards, only a small fan of empty plates remains, and the foundation eventually takes those as well.

Nothing rots. Death is a loss of organized charge, followed by flaking, dispersal, and reuse. The fans concentrate scarce elements from the dust; the scalebacks concentrate what the fans have gathered; the hunter concentrates what the scalebacks carry. The foundation incorporates whatever remains. The entire ecology can pass through a street without leaving a stain.

This gives the town its peculiar rules of age. A surface is not old because it is worn, but because it has many layers. A district is not deserted because something has left it, but because the foundation has withdrawn behind a newer wall. Maps have short lives. A passage used on the way inward may be only a shallow niche on the return journey, though no one ever sees it close.

All the present streets eventually approach the central court. It is the largest opening in the settlement and the emptiest: an irregular, nearly roofless space containing one bronze fan and a narrow black joint across the floor. Its rim is continually rebuilt, so the court changes from circular to many-sided and back again. During dust weather, columns rise around its perimeter but leave the centre untouched. No structure stands there, and no path crosses it directly.

The town is not waiting for inhabitants. Its hollowness is not an invitation. The openwork is how the foundation exchanges heat, gathers dust, sheds old tissue, and makes room for further growth. The shape of a city has emerged before any need for one.

In deep dimness, when it appears most deserted, the town is busiest. New walls cool and whiten. Dust turns through the conduits. The fans open. Somewhere a scaleback changes position, and the pale hunter lifts itself almost imperceptibly from a seam. Plates loosen along the outer ring, ready to be replaced.

All of this occurs within a few hundred paces, beneath a sky the colour of ash. And still there is no sound at all.
