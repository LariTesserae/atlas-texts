## The Nine-Voiced Hollow

High on the sunward shoulder of an old range, there is a hollow small enough to cross in forty paces. It lies open on one side, where the mountain falls away into a depth of air and distant ridges. Elsewhere it is enclosed by a crescent of rounded limestone. From above, it resembles a green thumbprint pressed into the stone. From below, it cannot be seen at all.

The altitude is more visible than felt. Other summits stand at eye level; valleys lie far beneath, often filled with slow cloud. Yet the air in the hollow is warm. Height has taken the oppression out of the lowland heat without bringing cold. Wet stone becomes pleasantly warm in the sun, and even before dawn the seep water is only mildly cool. Nothing here is shaped by frost.

Nor is anything shaped by recent violence. The range is old. Its fractures have softened, its ledges have lost their corners, and the hollow has settled into a long equilibrium between solution, growth, and decay. There is no fresh scree beneath the wall. The basin at its foot is deepened almost imperceptibly by water, while pale mineral skins accumulate along its rim. Roots bind the places where blocks might otherwise loosen. Even the cliff beyond the open side seems to be wasting away at a rate the mountain can afford.

Daylight is ordinary and generous. This is not the green twilight of a closed cloud forest. For much of the day, shadows are distinct, wet leaves flash when they turn, and the rock shows its actual colors: warm grey where dry, nearly black where saturated, rust-brown along old fractures, cream where mineral water has dried. Passing cloud dims the whole hollow at once, then releases it. Mist beads on surfaces more often than it obscures the view.

No stream enters. The hollow is fed by rain held in the wall and by moisture condensed from the air. Nine principal seams give the water back, though rarely all at once. One only shines. Another swells into a drop every few minutes. A third releases a thread no thicker than grass, which divides on a root and disappears into moss. Lower down, several seeps converge in a shallow basin of clear water over red silt and dark, perfectly rounded grains. The basin has no outlet at the surface. Its water goes downward through a narrow slot, returning to the mountain.

The ground is wet without being uniformly marshy. There are firm islands of stone and root, but a hand pressed into the moss brings water immediately to the surface. Some rocks weep along their entire undersides. Leaves remain beaded long after the surrounding slopes have dried. In clear weather, the hollow can still rain gently upon itself.

It is unexpectedly loud.

Not deafening, but loud enough that the small space feels occupied. Drops strike concave leaves with soft drumbeats. Others fall into stone cups and produce bright, ceramic notes. The narrow thread of water enters a cavity in the wall and emerges as sound rather than spray: a low, continuous humming that seems too large for its source. Beneath the basin, shifting water occasionally makes a deep liquid knock. At the open rim, wind adds a broad hiss, then stops abruptly when it drops below the edge.

There is little animal noise to cover any of this. No insect chorus hangs in the warm air, and no birds maintain territories in the trees. One can distinguish individual falls of water, sometimes even follow a drop from leaf to leaf to stone. Near the wall, a visitor would have to speak slightly louder than usual. A few steps beyond the rim, the whole performance collapses into a murmur.

The strangeness is that the sounds do not belong to the same weather. The moss returns a shower within hours. The upper wall holds rain for days. Some of the deeper seams change only across seasons, and the lowest humming fissure continues long after every visible source has diminished. Water reaching one leaf may have fallen the previous night, while the drop answering it in the basin has been inside the mountain for months. To stand in the hollow is to hear several storms at once, none of them necessarily the storm overhead.

The vegetation is lush, but not crowded. At ground level, nearly everything that can hold life does: cushion moss, dark liverwort, ferns with fronds fine as green wire, small leaves packed so tightly along wet seams that they resemble scales. Above this, however, the space opens. A handful of pale-barked trees stand well apart. Their crowns do not join. Solitary plants lift broad, spoon-shaped leaves on long stems, each surrounded by enough bare rock or low moss that its entire outline can be seen.

The emptiness between them is not a gap in the vegetation. It is part of the vegetation’s arrangement.

The spoon-leaves fill slowly. Some hold water for hours before their stems bend and let them empty, producing one disproportionately loud stroke below. Then they rise again. Their rhythms never synchronize for long. Fine roots, orange where constantly wet, follow the seepage through cracks and under stones. They thicken over decades, narrowing a passage here and opening another there. The plants therefore alter the sound without appearing to move at all.

Animals are scarce enough to qualify as events. A translucent creature like a tiny salamander sometimes rests beneath the basin’s mineral shelf, so still that only the water around it reveals the outline of its body. Snails leave brief silver trails on the wall. At long intervals a dark bird may land on the open rim, look into the hollow, and leave without calling. There are no herds, nests, colonies, or well-worn runs. Most movement belongs to water, foliage, and light.

Nothing has been built. The crescent wall can resemble masonry where fractures cross, and the basin can appear deliberately carved, but neither retains a straight worked edge. There are no steps, markers, channels, stacked stones, or cut stems. If hands have ever come here, they used only what could be picked up and put down unchanged: a leaf as a temporary cup, perhaps, or a stick as a probe. No path survives to prove even that. The hollow’s own instruments are simpler and more patient—the root as wedge, the leaf as vessel, the cavity as drum.

Over an afternoon, almost nothing happens. The light travels from one side of a leaf to the other. A seep grows slightly brighter. One drop falls where none has fallen for an hour.

Over centuries, the hollow changes chiefly by gaining and losing voices. A root closes a seam, and a high ticking note disappears. Water finds another passage, and a new sound begins under the wall. A tree dies, stands for years beneath moss, and finally lies down without materially altering the green arrangement around it. The basin deepens; its rim accretes. Stone leaves, mineral arrives, and the form remains recognizably itself.

Even in the driest season, the deepest note persists. Clear sunlight fills the hollow while old rain continues to tick from the leaves and hum beneath the basin. It is a place nearly empty of presences, full of sound, and crowded only with weather that has taken its time arriving.
