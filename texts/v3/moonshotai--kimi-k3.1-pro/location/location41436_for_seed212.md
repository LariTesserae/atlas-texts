## The Meristem Basin

Before anything green is visible, the basin is audible.

The sound crosses the warm mineral flats in a continuous, pressure-heavy roar: fibers cracking, hollow chambers booming, millions of small surfaces snapping open. Sometimes a deep report travels through the ground and arrives before the sound in the air. Sometimes the roar drops for half a second, exposing a nearer turmoil of hisses, squeals, and liquid detonations. Then the whole mass resumes.

The land itself is low and conspicuously young. A shallow basin, thirty kilometres across, has only recently emerged from the water that shaped it. Its rim is still sharp. The first runoff channels cut clean, narrow notches through the pale crust, and the fans beneath them have not had time to spread or soften. Beyond the green, the ground is mostly dry, warm to the touch, and sparsely streaked with salt. Occasional rain collects in isolated pans, shines for a day, and disappears.

Within the basin, there is almost no exposed ground at all.

From a distance, the growth resembles an impossibly layered forest. Closer, its organization becomes urban. Broad avenues run between dense blocks of vegetation. Ringed courts open around sunken reservoirs. Buttressed walls support terraces, arcades, and narrow bridges. Towers rise in bundled clusters, some repeated with such precision that they seem prefabricated, though nothing here has been manufactured. Streets branch into smaller streets, then into roofed passages, then into capillary clefts too narrow to admit light.

No part of it is built from dead material.

The pavements are root felt, packed so tightly that their individual strands can only be seen at the edges. They flex under weight and slowly recover. Walls consist of living leaf-scales laid over screens of green stem; their outer faces are tough and dry, while their joints remain damp. The towers are braided culms and vascular columns, bound together by grafts that swell visibly around every junction. Windows are not openings cut into walls but places where the wall has remained thin, translucent, and veined. Reservoirs are lined with a smooth, waterproof epidermis. Even the hardest surfaces are still photosynthetic somewhere beneath their outer layers.

There is no masonry, no cut timber, no metal, no cord, no tool-mark. There are no portable objects. Nothing has been assembled. Everything has been differentiated.

The city is also not sized for inhabitants. A passage may be lofty for its first hundred paces and then narrow to the width of a stem. A stair-like root formation may rise into a solid green vault. Courts open where several flows of sap need room to spread, not where bodies might gather. The place has infrastructure without users, districts without populations, and extraordinary organizational density without a single dwelling.

Warm, ordinary daylight falls over all of it. There is no supernatural radiance in the leaves and no permanent storm overhead. Above the towers, the sky is often clear. The light has normal colour, shadows have ordinary darkness, and the distant skyline can look almost serene. This makes the noise more difficult to reconcile with what the eye reports.

Inside the wards, the vegetation is so dense that space seems to have been treated as another nutrient. Leaves overlap leaves; stems root into other stems; bridges put down secondary roots before their spans are complete. New growth occupies the gaps between older growth, and the older growth immediately responds by thickening, turning, or digesting a passage through itself. There are shaded regions, but few empty ones. Even apparently open plazas are crossed at different heights by translucent filaments, hanging roots, and sheets of young tissue too fine to be seen until they catch the sun.

For all this biomass, the basin is not wet in the manner of a swamp. Much of the outer surface is dry by midday. Moisture persists in seams, protected cisterns, internal vessels, and pockets beneath the root pavement. On some mornings, condensation forms along the cool undersides of leaf-scales and runs through their veins into the city. At other times there is no dew at all. Rain arrives irregularly, usually in brief, hard pulses, and the entire urban fabric changes shape to receive it.

Gutters unfold. Reservoir courts deepen. Dry avenues become temporary streams. Towers lean aside so that water can reach lower districts rather than being intercepted above them. Within hours, most of that water has been drawn into tissue or sealed into living storage chambers. A district may look lush at dawn and papery by afternoon, though tepid water continues to move just below its surface.

The air smells accordingly of hot green stems, wet salt, crushed leaf, and the sharp sweetness of exposed sap. There is very little smell of decay. Senescent tissue does not lie where it falls. Its nutrients are withdrawn first; then its walls are softened by plant enzymes and pulled back into adjacent growth. A collapsed tower leaves no heap of timber. By the time its last segment touches the pavement, the pavement is already consuming it.

Whether the basin contains one plant or many is not a stable question. Living bridges graft separate growths into a single body. Later, a contraction severs them, and each portion continues independently. A root tracing one district may emerge in another with a different colour, leaf form, and rhythm of growth. Yet every fragment remains capable of becoming any urban tissue: pavement, wall, reservoir, tower, or canopy.

There is no animal life to complicate the system. No wings cross the spaces between towers. Nothing nests in the arcades, burrows in the pavement, browses the new shoots, or carries pollen from bloom to bloom. Reproduction occurs through self-pollination, water-carried pollen, and, above all, rupture. A torn fragment is often already capable of rooting. A district pinched off by its own expansion may become the beginning of another district several kilometres away.

The checks on growth are water, light, mineral access, and competition with adjacent tissue. There is no other succession. The basin does not move from grass to thicket to forest. It moves from avenue to vault to reservoir to solid green mass, and then back to avenue when the mass opens again.

These changes happen quickly enough to watch.

A low curb of root felt rises, becomes a wall, and throws out buttresses. A street narrows behind it. Somewhere overhead, a hollow stem elongates with a series of reports like splitting ice. A plaza folds upward at its centre, not because anything pushes from below but because its surface has decided to become a tower. Farther away, an entire ward blanches as its water is withdrawn. Its leaves curl, its walls lose tension, and the district sinks with a roar into the pavement that supplied it.

Nothing remains long enough to weather. Fresh edges stay fresh. Exposed pith is white, new bark is thin and shining, and recently split mineral crust along the margins still has angular faces. Erosion is present, but it has barely begun its familiar work. Rain cuts a channel; roots close it. Sap-rich runoff incises the pavement; new tissue grows across the incision. Most sharp features here are not the remnants of destruction. They are the beginnings of forms.

The deeper instability appears at the growing tips.

Mature tissue holds the world together reasonably well. Within it, weight acts downward, water follows continuous vessels, and sound usually comes after the event that produced it. But the pale meristems—the tips of roots, stems, unfurling walls, and districts not yet committed to a shape—do not observe sequence so strictly.

An arch may cast a shadow while its two sides are still reaching toward one another. A reservoir may receive several minutes of dripping from a spout that has not yet grown. A severed avenue can continue to pass droplets across empty air, each drop following the route its vanished vessels were expected to take. In the densest quarters, incompatible structures briefly occupy the same sunlight without touching. When one of them finally becomes fixed, the others vanish with a concussion, and the surrounding streets jump to accommodate the geometry that remains.

Lignification fixes more than cell walls. It fixes which version of a place the rest of the basin must temporarily accept.

Resorption releases that decision.

This is why the noise is so overwhelming. Some of it comes from actual growth: turgor splitting fibers, water hammering through hollow columns, leaves cracking open, whole districts collapsing into their own roots. But at active meristems, several possible growths can be audible at once. Towers that will never stand still boom. Streets that will never connect still carry the hiss of their imaginary water. The destruction of a ward may be heard while the ward is rising, followed later by the lesser sound of the event itself.

Distance, too, becomes partly vascular. A drop entering a leaf gutter in one district may emerge beyond several others without crossing the intervening streets. Two physically continuous lanes can cease to conduct anything between them and become, for all practical purposes, remote. Meanwhile, two towers rooted into the same living column may remain inseparable even after a wide avenue opens between their visible bases.

No mechanism regulates this. There are biological valves, tensions, grafts, and gradients, but no devices. The rules resemble physiology carried past the point where physiology and locality can be distinguished.

At the basin’s edge, the city does not simply advance. Spears of root and stem race into the dry crust, raise small green precincts, and then withdraw as their water is reclaimed. Isolated blocks appear far ahead of the main growth. Some collapse within minutes. Others send back a living connection and abruptly become central avenues, their former remoteness erased.

When the next brief rain comes, it strikes millions of dry surfaces at once. The sound rises beyond a roar into something closer to pressure. Fresh meristems emerge everywhere, pale against the saturated green. For an instant, beneath the perfectly ordinary sky, the basin carries the shadows of several incompatible cities.

Then the rain stops. One skyline remains.

Already, its edges are softening into roots, and the roar reaching the mineral rim is coming from a configuration that no longer exists.
