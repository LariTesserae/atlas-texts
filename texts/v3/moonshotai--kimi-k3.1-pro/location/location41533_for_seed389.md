## The Accretion City

From the lower country, the massif looks unfinished. It does not rise in the long, weathered folds of an old range, but in sheer black partitions, blade-backed saddles, and summit blocks whose fractures still shine. Whole cloud systems pass beneath its upper ledges. On clear days the view from the top crosses several provinces of plain and intervening highland, yet nothing in that immense openness softens the vertical drop immediately underfoot. The outer cliffs simply stop, and the air continues.

The stone is young enough to be dangerous. There are no rounded boulders, no deep soils, no mature valleys. Broken glass lies where it fell, and fresh escarpments keep their conchoidal scars. Some fragments in the scree carry straight seams, regular fluting, or the negative impression of a pipe. At first these seem like pieces of the city that have fallen from above. Later, the distinction becomes less certain.

Heat rises from the massif even at its greatest heights. The daylight is ordinary—white sun, legible shadows, a sky without auroras or unusual color—but the cliffs shimmer as though seen over a kiln. At noon, shaded passages remain hot because their walls have been warmed from within. The air smells faintly of ceramic dust and electrical ozone. After moisture, it also smells of wet iron, though the smell rarely lasts long.

The summit city extends across several peaks and the saddles between them. From a distance it resembles an especially regular mineral crust. Closer, the repetitions resolve into cooling towers, pressure houses, stepped cisterns, transformer terraces, enclosed stair streets, and ranks of narrow furnace mouths. The districts are packed together without waste. Pipe bridges cross above pipe bridges. A paved lane may be the roof of a pump hall and the floor of another street; beneath that, older galleries continue downward, lit by small white service lamps.

There are few true squares. Open space is mostly vertical: heat wells, cable shafts, narrow light courts, and gaps left for machinery not yet installed. Blue-black volcanic glass stands beside gray clinker, pale ceramic tile, and copper darkened to the color of old bruises. Iron remains almost rust-free on the dry faces, but around persistent leaks it has bloomed orange and brown. Dust gathers in every corner, not as loam but as powdered glass, salt, and fine industrial ash.

It is a city in extent and organization, though it offers no crowds. Some of the small ceramic-shuttered cells may be dwellings. Others contain only a gauge, a bed of coiled wire, or a chair facing a wall of numbered valves. Nothing announces which. The absence of visible inhabitants does not make the place feel ruined. Filters are clean. Cables are under tension. Fresh insulators gleam among components coated with mineral scale.

The engines are everywhere, and almost none of them appear to move.

Enormous flywheels turn behind open grilles, their spokes changing position only after long attention. Pumps draw and release on cycles measured in hours. Freight hooks descend so slowly that their cables seem rigid. Pressure gates take days to close. The quick, concealed part of the industry is electrical; the visible part has been designed for torque, patience, and extreme duration.

Consequently, the city is much quieter than its density suggests. The thin air carries little from one terrace to the next, and the machine halls are deeply insulated. Most of the sound is local: the ticking of cooling tile, a faint hum felt through boot soles, water finding a lower joint inside a wall. Occasionally a distant mechanism seats against its stop, and a soft concussion arrives through the rock more than through the air. There is no continuous insect noise, no wind moving through foliage, no birdsong filling the intervals.

Moisture belongs more to the plumbing than to the climate.

For days, the exposed streets may remain completely dry. Then beads appear beneath a coolant main, each swelling until it falls into a ceramic catch tray. Deep cisterns sweat along their rims. In the condenser districts, combs of metal finning intercept clouds that climb the windward cliffs, and water ticks through covered channels toward reservoirs warm as blood. Now and then actual rain reaches the upper city: a brief, tepid scattering that darkens the stone in irregular patches. Steam rises first from the southern faces. By the time the shaded streets are fully wet, the exposed ones are already drying.

No stream crosses the summit. Water is captured, reused, lost to vapor, or locked into mineral scale. Where a leak has endured for decades, however, something else has begun. A black wall may carry one pale thread, narrow as a scratch, descending from joint to joint. Below it lies a shallow flute in the glass. These are the first channels in the landscape. None has yet become a gully, much less a valley. The city’s plumbing is accidentally teaching the mountain how rain will one day behave.

There is no vegetation. No roots pry at the masonry; no moss softens a wet step. Even the damp cisterns contain nothing green. Their life, where it exists, is simpler and less visible: translucent sheets of cells metabolizing dissolved iron and sulfur, colorless films around warm outlets, delicate membranes that crumble to gray powder when a reservoir is drained.

Animals are rare enough that a track on a wet sill can seem more remarkable than the largest engine. The creature most likely to be found is a flat, many-limbed thing with a shell resembling thin porcelain. It emerges near condensate, rasps microbial film from the stone, and withdraws when the surface dries. Sealed into a crevice, it can remain motionless through long hot intervals. Sometimes only an empty shell is found on a step, so light that the faint summit wind turns it over without lifting it.

The longer one remains, the less the city seems merely to stand upon the massif.

Rock has closed around the lowest conduits without crushing them. Buttresses continue downward as crystalline ribs. Survey markers on the outer rim are buried to different depths, not by debris, but by new ground. In fabrication districts, mineral-heavy melt raised from deep engine wells passes through miles of radiators and casting galleries. What leaves them is neither quite slag nor quite natural stone. It cools in organized layers beneath existing foundations, incorporating old drains, reinforcement, and access shafts. Streets become embedded. Retaining walls become strata. The discarded shell of one industrial terrace becomes the solid platform of the next.

The city is making its own altitude.

The process is too slow to watch. During a brief visit, it produces only a new film inside a mold, a faint line of scale, perhaps a fractional change on an altimetric gauge. Across longer spans, doorways become windows and windows disappear into walls. Staircases end against warm stone, not because they have been destroyed, but because their continuation has not yet cooled. Maps must carry dates. An address is incomplete without its stratum.

Erosion has not caught up with construction. Rain writes its first small grooves on surfaces younger than their stains, while entire lower districts remain sealed, pressurized, and operational inside the mountain.

From the highest survey ledge, the two scales of the place can be felt at once. Behind lies the dense saturation of the city: layered walls, near-touching conduits, stacked industries, thousands of patient presences compressed into every view. Ahead is open air and country so far below that individual storms look small.

A drop falls somewhere in the condenser field. A wheel advances by an imperceptible fraction of a turn. The heat continues.

Nothing appears to be happening. Yet the city is not stagnating, and the mountain is not waiting. Both are cooling, condensing, and adding another thin measure of height.
