## The Bellows

Long before the rim comes into view, the ground begins to make a noise. It is felt first in the heels: a fine, continuous laboring, as though something enormous were turning over in its sleep below the stone. Then the air takes it up. By the time the warm uplands open at the edge of the depression, the sound has become too large to have a direction.

The Bellows lies open to the sky, far below the surrounding plateau. It is not a cavern, though parts of it have roofed themselves over. From the southern rim, three unequal lobes of sunken land extend beyond one another for forty or fifty kilometres, each with its own heights, densities, and moving weather of wings. The cliffs around them are raw. Their fractures are fresh, their ledges angular, their fallen blocks still the size and shape of their first breaking. There are no mature talus slopes, no softened valley shoulders, no old channels leading down from the heights.

Nothing here has been shaped by water. There are no dry riverbeds, because there have never been rivers; no silt fans, no strand lines, no rounded gravel. Rain is not seasonal or rare. It is unknown. Even calling the place *dry* is slightly misleading, since that suggests moisture recently departed. Free water was never part of its design.

The light is the most ordinary thing about it. At midday, sunlight falls plainly into the depression, shortening shadows and showing colors without enchantment: leaf-black, sulfur yellow, oxblood, wax green, the ivory of split stems. Nothing glows. From above, the vegetation resembles weather only because it moves in fronts—dark masses bunching, pale outbreaks spreading, whole districts changing color as seed cases open or leaves turn their reflective sides to the sun.

The air rising from below is warm and heavily occupied. It carries spores, scale-like fragments of husk, wing dust, and the smells of hot wax, pepper, crushed rind, and faintly metallic resin. It is not an empty desert heat. Every breath feels filtered through a crowded room.

Break almost any stem in the Bellows and it will not drip. A bead of thin amber oil swells from the cut, sharp-smelling and translucent, then skins over before it can evaporate. Similar oils move through the leaves, through the burrowing threads beneath them, through the bodies of most things that fly or crawl. The plants store sunlight in dense, waxy tissues; the largest rooted forms also draw on the warmth of young stone. Their outer surfaces are varnished, pleated, or sealed in overlapping plates. Their soft tissues are kept deep inside.

There is no soil separate from them. Underfoot is a compressed thickness of previous generations: root felt, hollow valves, papery trunks, braided tendrils, seed shells packed hard as brick. Much of it is still alive. A hillside may be one plant repeated thousands of times, or thousands of plants grown through the body of one. Trunks fuse where they touch. Branches form sockets around neighboring branches. Leaves interlock into shingles, and the shingles become roofs beneath which other plants grow.

The Bellows is dense without being uniform. Near the rim, corky thickets stand nearly stem to stem, divided by galleries just wide enough for a person to turn sideways. Farther down, hollow stalks rise in bundles, their open tops all at slightly different heights, so that a wind across them produces a changing chord. Beyond these is a region of low crimson cushions, each cushion composed of millions of finger-length growths and each visibly crawling with life. At the center, pale root-plateaus lift and founder over raw mineral fractures. A clear space may remain open for an hour, a day, or only long enough for something vast to pass through it.

The underlying land is still being made. The floor subsides in irregular plates. New faults open with reports that carry across the whole basin; warm stone shows briefly beneath the vegetation before roots enter the cracks. Elsewhere, living matter accumulates faster than it collapses, raising soft ridges and rounded escarpments where no mineral uplift exists. Erosion has barely begun, but construction is frantic.

Nothing changes so quickly that the place becomes a single spectacle. That is part of what makes it unsettling. A stem takes several minutes to elbow past a marker. A root arch lifts by the width of a finger while you watch. Then you look away, and a hundred other movements continue. Return after an hour and the gallery has narrowed, the marker is overhead, and the ridge beyond it is wearing leaves that had not unfolded before.

These changes travel in outbreaks. An unfurling begins as a pallor at the base of a thicket. New growth runs upward and outward, old stems lean together, and the whole district settles under its own altered weight. The collapse raises no smell of decay. Dead matter here does not become damp or soft. It is chewed, unthreaded, powdered, carried off, or grown through.

The noise is not incidental to this activity. It times it.

Some seed cases open only to a particular interval of vibration. Certain pollinators hatch when the stalks around them begin to sound. Root tips turn toward some tremors and away from others. The great hollow plants change their growth according to the pitches sustained inside them, narrowing a throat until it whistles, then thickening one side until the note breaks. A call can be a mating signal, a warning, and the trigger for germination, depending on what receives it.

The Bellows does not merely contain a loud ecosystem. It is an ecosystem continually drumming, shaking, and shouting itself into its next shape.

Animal life is overwhelming at every scale. Turn over a leaf and its gray underside may stand up and walk away: not leaf tissue, but a crust of minute grazers packed so closely that their legs seem to be one fringe. Bark plates unfasten themselves from trunks and depart on hundreds of feet. Winged things rise from the crimson cushions in such numbers that they cast a second, moving canopy beneath the real one. Their wingbeat is high enough to hurt the teeth.

Below them, armored huskbacks bulldoze tunnels through the root felt, chewing woody growth into dry meal and leaving fertile seams of crushed shell and oil behind. Long, nearly legless browsers thread themselves through the upper branches. Things like folded kites hang motionless until a warm updraft opens their wings. At dusk, heavy bellowers move through the hollow-stem districts, their calls so low that they are heard most clearly through the soles of the feet.

Many roaming creatures begin as parts of rooted ones. A fruit detaches, unfolds jointed legs, and carries its seed away inside its body. A flower’s feeding arm becomes a six-limbed hunter for a season, then fastens itself to fresh stone and puts down roots. Some of these transformations are reversible; some are not. The useful distinction here is not plant or animal, but rooted or roaming—and even that may need revising by the following day.

Within the thickets, the combined sound is deafening. The high wing-drone has no pause in it. Hollow stems shriek and boom as warm air moves through them. Seedpods burst in dry volleys. Roots snap under tension; plates of living ground shift with long wooden groans; a distant collapse arrives first as pressure in the chest and only afterward as sound.

A spoken word disappears at the lips. A shout becomes another small irritation in the air. Those few people who work below the rim plug their ears with wax and fiber, communicate by hand signs, and tap messages into resonant stems. They pay particular attention to silence. A sudden local quiet often means that everything nearby has stopped at once—before a subsidence, a mass hatching, or the passage of something too large to see through the growth.

Human additions are sparse enough to seem accidental. Near one descent stands a single sleeping cell woven from cane and packed with seed-wool, built less against heat than against noise. Elsewhere there is a dry-stone windbreak, three notched stakes disappearing into a thicket, a platform lashed beneath a living arch. A stone mortar still holds a stain of yellow oil. Flaked blades, stem wedges, braided cord, and wooden mallets are the usual tools. No road remains a road for long.

The most deliberate structures are acoustic: a ring of stones set around a whistling trunk, a hand-worn slab beside a stand of seed cases, a row of suspended rods tuned to strike one another when the ground trembles. Strike the slab with the proper mallet and the neighboring pods may open together, allowing a harvest before the winged carriers arrive. Whoever made these arrangements did not try to silence the Bellows. They only tried to persuade it to do one thing at a time.

Buildings do not become ruins here in the usual way. They become incorporated. A doorway is found years later partway up a trunk that did not exist when it was built. A shelter’s roof appears inside a root arch, still lashed together, still faintly useful. A path becomes a tunnel, then a wall, then a line of unusually straight leaves.

By evening, the view from the rim has altered again. The pale ridge of morning has become a thicket; a gallery has closed; somewhere in the eastern lobe, a new uproar is spreading through the winged populations. The ordinary sunlight turns bronze and withdraws without producing any corresponding calm. Warm stone continues to tick beneath the growth. After dark, the bass calls deepen, and the whole depression seems to breathe against its unfinished sides.

Maps of the Bellows must be dated. The useful ones also give the hour. A route is not simply somewhere; it is sometime.

This is not an ancient wilderness. It is a young one, still inventing the difference between ground and growth, and doing so at the top of its voice.
