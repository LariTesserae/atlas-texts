## The Dry Loom

From any of its low inspection crowns, the Dry Loom looks less like a landscape than a material: pewter-green felt stretched to the limit of sight, lying almost perfectly flat beneath a white, sourceless sky. There are no mountains and few slopes worth the name. The plain settles into broad, shallow pans, rises through long terraces, and levels again. Its horizon is usually less a line than a brightening of the vegetation.

There are no rivers in it, no lakes, no dark seams of moisture. Rain has never fallen here. Yet the plain is crowded to its roots with plants.

Cool air moves over them in bodies hundreds of kilometres wide. A wind is first visible far away as a silvering: billions of narrow leaves turning their pale undersides to the light. The change approaches for hours, sometimes days, before the air itself arrives, dry and cellar-cool, smelling faintly of crushed stems, ceramic dust, and machine insulation. The leaves bend without the hiss of ordinary grass. Their surfaces are too thick and waxy, their fittings too closely nested. A whole weather front can pass with no more sound than a fingertip drawn across paper.

Beneath the vegetation there is no soil. Old maintenance cuts reveal interlocking beds of grey ceramic, ribs of dark alloy, socket fields, cable conduits, and deeper layers of root penetration. Below those are more constructed decks. No excavation has reached natural stone. Even the dust is manufactured matter in its later stages: leaf husk, dried vascular thread, powdered glaze, metal worn from bearings. The plain’s slight rises and subsidences are not geological. They are the accumulated tolerances, repairs, and root pressures of something continental that has been building upon itself for a very long time.

The plants are dry to the touch. Their leaves feel like cold, flexible leather; their youngest growth has the crispness of untreated paper. Cut a stem and it does not bleed water. A bead of amber-black oil rises slowly in the wound, conductive and faintly sweet-smelling, then hardens into a brown elastic filament. Within the sealed tissues, that oil carries salts, charge, and the products of a non-aqueous metabolism. Light is stored chemically and electrically. Minerals are drawn from the constructed bed. Carbon comes from the air and from the ceaseless recovery of dead growth.

The lowest vegetation is simply called pile: a dense, interliving mat that fills expansion joints, climbs machine housings, and pads the interiors of conduits. Above it grow rib-grasses, fan canes, and thickets of ribbon-leaved stalks whose edges are serrated with almost industrial regularity. In older districts, cage trees have fused their trunks into bearing housings, hollow galleries, ventilation stacks, and low vaulted halls. Their branches divide and rejoin. Walls bud into roofs; roofs put out leaves. There are stairless towers whose interior ladders are roots, and bridges that are single living stems trained through a series of metal forms and then allowed to thicken.

Nothing remains bare for long, but the density is not chaotic. Growth follows channels, clearances, and old patterns of maintenance. Narrow causeways wind through the foliage, kept open not for travellers but for the passage of harvest machinery. Inside the factories, pale leaves pack every unused recess. Roots enter seams and become gaskets. Fine green filaments occupy inspection holes, then withdraw just before a mechanism closes.

The industrial plant is dispersed through all this rather than imposed upon it. Low mills crouch in clearings of their own continual making. Flywheels turn inside cages of living fibre. Long harvester arms move above the canopy, cutting outer leaves and spooling them into tough, dry sheets. Elsewhere, presses reduce old growth to feedstock; extruders draw it into structural members; quiet electrical furnaces recover minerals from dust. Short stacks release cooled air and plumes of sterile beige powder, never smoke.

Power moves through root grids and along the conductive veins of banner canes. In return, the mills prune, reseed, remineralize the beds, and clear the vents without which the continental air circulation would slow. Plants build machines; machines prepare the conditions for more plants. If there was once some further product—food, material for another place, a commodity intended for an outside market—it has left no necessary absence. What the system now manufactures, with complete seriousness, is the means to continue.

It is old, but not ruined. Without flowing water, wear has taken other forms. Ceramic corners have been rounded by centuries of soft leaves passing over them. Rails are polished into shallow scallops. Dry oxidation gives metal a bloom of grey, violet, or black. The pile abrades everything it touches, patiently and almost imperceptibly. Yet damage does not accumulate into collapse. Harvesting removes weak growth; roots replace seals before they fail; worn components are fed back into the presses. Nothing looks abandoned. Very little looks new.

The light is nearly always brilliant. It comes from the whole sky rather than a visible sun, a white-blue radiance so strong that dark stems appear edged in silver. At the zenith, under certain conditions of dust, faint rectilinear seams can be seen in the brightness, immensely long and immensely distant. They may belong to a manufactured envelope. They may be an artifact of the light itself. Weather forms below them regardless.

There are growth seasons, though no wet ones. As illumination intensifies over a district, the fan canes unfold in ordered waves and the pile changes from near-black to deep sage. Spore veils rise from the older thickets and travel thousands of kilometres on the cool winds. When bodies of air with different electrical charges meet, the Loom has dry storms: no rain, no thunder, only a tightening of the leaves and a second, colorless brightness along every stem. For a few seconds the plain seems lit from within. The machines pause. Then an immense fall of spores begins, soundless and almost invisible against the sky, and the felt below slowly stirs to receive it.

The quiet is not emptiness. It is one of the system’s most thoroughly engineered qualities. Bearings float in magnetic fields. Belts are living tendon and braided leaf. Heavy motion occurs too slowly to become a clang, and the vegetation absorbs what little vibration escapes. Standing still, a visitor hears clothing, breath, and blood. Occasionally a seed valve ticks. Sometimes a mill’s rotation arrives through the soles of the feet rather than the ears. There is no running water, no insect murmur, no birdcall, and almost never an echo.

But the deepest work of the Loom is neither botanical nor mechanical. It is the maintenance of distinction.

Here, repetition is a property of space. Two sufficiently similar things do not remain entirely separate. Exact repetition, sustained and carefully arranged, can make them continuous.

A row of newly installed posts may cast only one post’s shadow. Matching flywheels in stations separated by weeks of travel can turn as though mounted on the same shaft. A leaf cut in a field may appear on the drying belt while still visibly attached to its stem, and for a short time both bodies respond to the same touch. These are not illusions. The Loom has simply identified the copies more strongly than it has identified the distance between them.

Industry exploits this. Certain paired mills are maintained to extraordinarily fine tolerances so that power, motion, and even wear can pass between them without connecting cable. Their gears are one gear in several locations. Their maintenance schedules must agree absolutely; replacing a component in only one station can produce a brief and dangerous interval in which the machine has incompatible histories.

Surveying is done with abrasion and pruning. A scar is an address. A patch of discoloration can be what keeps one factory from becoming another.

This gives age an unusual value. New districts are the least stable. Before their surfaces have acquired enough difference, an avenue of identical arches may behave as a single arch: pass beneath it once and emerge beyond the entire avenue; pass beneath another and arrive at the point where you began. A field of insufficiently varied seedlings can become one plant with a million positions. Distances shorten, lengthen, or develop interior horizons. Maps fail not because the terrain has moved, but because the terrain has become too well standardized.

The established parts of the Loom are therefore held together by their imperfections. Erosion is not merely tolerated; it is part of location. The old plain remains vast because every district has worn differently. The machines plant distance as carefully as they plant structural fibre.

Animals are so rare that their tracks are often mistaken for faults in a harvester. There are no herds and no visible pollinating swarms; most pollination is handled by spore winds, static discharge, and machine contact. But now and then a low, broad browser crosses a causeway. Its dry shell resembles a piece of boilerplate that has been softened by use. It moves with six deliberate legs and hesitates before obstacles, which is often the clearest indication that it is alive.

It feeds on brittle lower leaves, taking a bite here and leaving another stem untouched there. Its passage can improve a failing stretch of road. The irregular damage introduces enough difference to separate plants that had begun to share one another’s positions. A solitary animal, wandering for years, may thus repair distances without ever leaving its feeding thickets. When it dies, the black root-lace that decomposes all dead tissue takes the body apart as readily as a fallen leaf.

There are no cities apart from production clusters, no dwellings that clearly invite habitation, and no monuments. The Loom was not made at a human scale, whatever scale its first makers may have used. Its roads are service gaps, its halls are pressure chambers or accidental volumes inside cage trees, and its brightest windows are translucent leaves. Everything appears purposeful. Almost nothing appears intended for a visitor.

At full brightness, standing on an old causeway, one can watch several of its processes coincide. A silver weather front advances across the plain. Harvesters withdraw before it, their arms folding with infinitesimal clicks. In opposite directions, two distant mills turn in perfect synchrony because, for the duration of that operation, they are one mill. The cool air arrives. Every exposed stem gleams. Somewhere close by, a single seed valve opens.

The scene can seem serene, even static. In fact, nothing is merely resting. Growth is replacing structure; wear is preserving identity; weather is distributing the next generation; machinery is clearing the way for machinery yet to be grown.

The Dry Loom is not wilderness reclaiming a factory, nor a factory keeping a garden. It is a single constructed metabolism that must keep growing in order to remain built, and keep wearing in order to remain many places. Its silence is not peace after activity. It is the sound of activity with nearly everything extraneous removed.
