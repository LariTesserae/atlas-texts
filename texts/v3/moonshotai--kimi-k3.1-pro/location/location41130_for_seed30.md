## The Unfinished Cirque

High above a cloud sea, on the shoulder of a mountain that has not yet grown a summit, lies a small cirque with no outlet. It is only a few hundred paces across. Three uneven arcs of dark stone form its rim; the fourth side opens directly above the clouds. Mist enters through that gap in slow, warm sheets, so the boundary is sometimes a cliff and sometimes merely the place where the ground can no longer be seen.

The air is thin enough to notice in the lungs, but warm. Heat comes up through the rock rather than down from the sky. At this altitude the clouds ought to carry ice; here they release a fine rain at approximately the temperature of skin. It collects in shallow, transparent lenses on the floor, beads on every ledge, and seeps from hairline fractures. Yet there is almost no mud. Nothing has had time to become sediment.

The cirque is too young for erosion. Its blue-black stone still has the clean, brittle edges of fracture. Pale green crystals protrude from it without weathering or staining. There is no scree beneath the cliffs, no rounded gravel in the pools, no streambed cut into the floor. Water simply sheets across the rock, divides around crystal faces, and vanishes into pinholes. Elsewhere it rises again as warm vapor. Even the cliffs look less carved than extruded.

In places, they are still being extruded.

A ledge may end in a dotted line of mineral grains suspended in the air. The grains appear motionless while watched. Look away, and when you look back the ledge has advanced a finger’s width. Along the newest seams, the air has a finely ruled shimmer, as though heat were passing through a sheet of inscribed glass. Stone accumulates there without rubble, noiseless except for an occasional sharp internal tick. It seems less assembled than persuaded: a volume of wet air is briefly convinced that it has always been rock.

Six structures stand scattered across this floor, separated by long stretches of shining emptiness. Three are slender, forked pylons, each no taller than a person, with no visible joint where they enter the ground. One is a black ring set on edge around nothing. Another resembles a small shelter: two upright slabs and a low bench, roofless until rain falls. Then the water bends around an invisible curve above it, outlining a dry dome before streaming down the outside.

The last structure might be mistaken for a broken stair, except that its four steps descend into solid stone. Nothing here is worn enough to be a ruin. The broken-looking things are incomplete.

The pylons and ring are made of a material that resembles ceramic until touched, when it feels more like a firmly held vibration. They contain no accessible mechanisms. Most of their machinery appears to occupy the mist: motes too small to see individually, visible only when a raindrop catches the weak light and shows a momentary spiral around itself. But those motes do not fully explain what happens. Mass arrives without excavation. Distances near the ring occasionally disagree with the eye. Once in a while, an echo returns from the open side of the cirque, where there is no wall to reflect it.

The light never becomes bright. A low pewter sky presses close to the rim, and the sun, when visible at all, is only a paler bruise in the cloud. There are few hard shadows. Wet stone supplies most of the highlights; the pools hold dim, wavering copies of the pylons. In the thickest mist, the whole place reduces to black mineral, gray vapor, and the faint copper colors of living things.

Vegetation is sparse enough to count. A cushion of ash-colored lichen here. Forty paces away, three blades of waxy grass growing from a seep. Beside a warm pool, an isolated cup-plant with translucent leaves folded around a thimble of water. Long stretches of ground support nothing visible. There are no roots binding the cliffs and no soil softening the transition between stone and life. The plants hold on where they can, clean-rooted and widely separated, like punctuation on a mostly blank page.

The apparent poverty is deceptive. Every wet surface carries a thin microbial film—violet in the older pools, copper-green along fresh mineral seams—and these films are crowded with grazers. The food web begins in water and bare rock, without waiting for a forest or even a meadow.

When the rain slackens, mica-fleas spring across the pools in their thousands, each one no larger than a grain of sand. Flat, many-legged sieve-crabs emerge from fractures to shave the films from new stone. Thumb-sized bell-newts climb onto warm ledges, their sides nearly transparent, their bronze throats swollen with eggs or air. Rain-midges hatch in sudden columns above the puddles. Then the kite-wings come out of the clouds: broad, membrane-winged predators that hang almost motionless in the updraft before folding into steep, silent dives.

The abundance is intermittent rather than crowded. From the center of the cirque, you may see only one newt, a distant glider, and bare wet rock in every direction. A minute later every pool is ticking with larvae, tiny grazers are flowing along the seams, and the air is full of wings. Then the predators feed, the rain returns, and the visible world empties again.

It is never quiet for long.

The bell-newts produce disproportionately loud, hard notes, like stones struck together underwater. Their calls carry cleanly across the cirque and return from the cliffs slightly flattened. Beneath them runs the hiss of rain, the sizzling of midge swarms, and the continuous low tone of the resonators. At intervals that tone deepens until it can be felt in the teeth. The pools develop concentric rings. Loose droplets lift from the rock and hang just above it.

Then the new stone arrives.

It does so with a series of cracks loud enough to hurt—small, dry detonations followed by the long settling groan of the rim. The sieve-crabs rush toward the fresh seams. The newts scramble onto higher ledges and begin calling all at once. Kite-wings fold close to the ground to avoid the upward burst of warm vapor. For several seconds, conversation is impossible. The whole cirque sounds like an instrument being struck while it is still being built.

Afterward, the acoustics have changed. A call that once returned from the eastern wall now comes back doubled, or half a beat late, or from somewhere beyond the open rim. Each addition to the terrain alters the note of the place, and the resonators alter their tone in response.

Weather, construction, and ecology have become entangled. Warm vapor rises into the colder air above the mountain and returns as rain. Rain feeds the films; the films draw grazers; grazers open bright trails across the new mineral. Subsequent growth often follows those trails. The bell-newts gather wherever fresh water is about to appear, though no water may yet be visible. Sometimes the resonators wait through several ragged choruses until the newts approach a common pitch. Only then does the next seam open.

Perhaps this cooperation was designed. Perhaps the machines have simply learned from their loudest colonists.

The cirque gives no indication that it intends to stop. Completion may not be its natural state. It may be a kind of landscape that remains mature only while arriving: stone still thickening beneath the pools, water still learning where to run, animals continually revising the acoustics of their unfinished shelter.

And when the next growth pulse ends, there may be a new pool behind you, shining on stone that was not there when you crossed. Three bell-newts will already be sitting at its edge. Their throats will swell, their hard little voices will strike the dim air, and after a moment the machines will lower their note to match.
