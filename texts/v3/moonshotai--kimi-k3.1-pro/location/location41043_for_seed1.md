## The Unbuilt Works

The lowland runs wider than a weather system, and almost perfectly level. One could travel across it for days without gaining more than a body’s height in elevation. There are no cliffs, no true hills, no channels cut deeply enough to be called valleys. Erosion has been at work for so long that it has worn away the distinction between bedrock and debris. Graphite-colored mud grades into rounded mineral nodules; the nodules grade into low, exhausted swells; the swells subside into basins of standing brine. Even fractures have soft lips. Even newly exposed surfaces look ancient, as though age were not something that happened to materials here, but one of the materials itself.

Water lies near the surface everywhere. Sometimes it is a clear film, sometimes a black mirror between crowded animal backs, sometimes only a metallic chill in the mud. Rocks weep from their undersides. Droplets condense in the fog, crawl upward along mineral grains, and vanish into the ground. Pools overflow without rising. Rivers are often no more than broad, moving differences in wetness, sliding patiently toward a downhill that can no longer be seen.

It is profoundly cold. Ordinary water would long ago have become a motionless country of ice, but the brines are heavy with dissolved salts, and freezing is only intermittently compulsory. Skins of ice appear without the surrounding water losing heat; they vanish without thawing. A pool may be liquid at its center, solid along one moving stripe, and crowded with swimming animals in both states. Cold gives every odor a narrow, clean edge: salt, wet iron, faint sulfide, the inward sting of air that has been compressed too often.

The light is a dim, zinc-colored twilight. It has no obvious source and casts no dependable shadows. If there is a sun above the low fog, its only contribution is a slight graying of the whole sky. The brightest illumination comes from below: slow blue currents beneath pools, white sparks traveling through wet ground, momentary electrochemical flares inside translucent bodies. Fog mutes even these. Distance is usually inferred by sound rather than sight.

The openness of the Works contains no emptiness.

Every surface is occupied. Broad, low animals stand flank to flank on the swells. Smaller ones move over their armored backs, and still smaller ones inhabit the water film between their legs. The pools are dense with threadlike swimmers packed so closely that a current passing through them resembles a shudder in a single body. The air carries heavy, flat fliers, drifting juveniles, condensed motes that unfold when they strike something cold. There is no bare mud for more than an instant. A newly exposed patch is touched, tested, entered, and claimed before the water has finished settling over it.

There is no vegetation. Nothing roots, leafs, or forms a green film on the pools. No algae soften the stones; no fungal threads occupy the dead. What looks from a distance like a dark stain on the ground is usually a gathering of minute animals. What resembles a crust is often a closely packed generation holding still between feedings.

They are animals by behavior more than by composition. They seek gradients, avoid injury, compete, reproduce, and die, but many have no stable tissue and no enduring complement of atoms. A body may be a brine-filled interruption in a mineral film, a loop of current that carries sediment with it, or a persistent rhythm that borrows solid material as it passes. Some become solid only while feeding. Others leave their original substance behind entirely and continue as the same animal in new matter. Decomposition is merely the loss of a boundary, after which the smaller fauna move in.

Nothing here has been built. There are no housings, cables, roads, tools, ruins, or discarded components. And yet the Works is industrial in the most complete sense: generation, transmission, assembly, distribution, and waste recovery all occur at regional scale.

Deep beds of metal-rich minerals and ionic brine form immense electrochemical cells. Contraction in the cold upper crust bears down on saturated layers below. Pressure accumulates until the whole lowland gives a slow, subterranean stroke. Mud vents open with a concussive bark. Brine is forced through mineral membranes; charge separates; currents enter the pools and the bodies waiting in them. Fog is expelled in low, fast clouds. Then the pressure releases, water returns through another set of passages, and the next stroke begins somewhere else.

No single part can be removed and still function. A stone carried away from a vent is only wet gravel. A swimmer taken from its current collapses into dirty water. The engines are relationships among terrain, weather, chemistry, and fauna. Their power grid is partly migration. Their production lines are temporary arrangements of flow. Their output is not an object but the continuation of conditions in which output remains possible.

The rillbacks are among the most visible workers in this arrangement, though nothing in them intends work. Each is a broad, wet dome supported by hundreds of short, piston-like feet. Herds move so densely that their flanks grind together, the sound softened by mud and condensed water. They feed by pressing charged silt through their bodies and stripping it of usable potential. Their passage squeezes brine into shallow sheets, aerates the mud with cold fog, and drives burrowing fauna ahead of them in glittering waves.

In the pools, wire eels align head to tail for astonishing distances. They are less like cords than like transparent tensions in the water. When a current passes, the whole alignment flashes blue-white. New lengths precipitate along older ones, shiver free, and join neighboring chains. Predators strike not at individual eels but at interruptions in their conductivity. A successful attack can darken an entire pool.

Above them, the fog herds swim by repeated small failures of buoyancy. They are visible chiefly as flat streams of condensation, or as places where the dim light arrives a moment late. They feed on airborne juveniles and on sound, diving through loud zones with their bodies spread wide. Where one has passed, water continues to drip from apparently empty air.

The largest predators are easiest to identify as hushes.

A hush enters a herd as a moving region of silence. Vent blasts stop inside it. Footfalls become visible but inaudible. Even the internal ticking by which many animals coordinate their bodies is consumed. Prey caught near its center are left as cold, porous outlines, already filling with scavengers. Those at the edge flee soundlessly, colliding with animals that have not yet received any warning. The silence moves on. The Works’ roar closes behind it like water.

At full activity, that roar is immense. The ground supplies a slow succession of detonations and deep mechanical groans. Vents shriek. Rillback herds clatter and boom. Electrical discharges crack through the fog, while millions of small bodies add scraping, knocking, whistling, and the patter of feet on other feet. Sound travels through the saturated ground faster than through the air, so distant events often arrive underfoot before they can be heard. Loud impacts make pools jump in smooth sheets. The densest noises acquire a physical grain, and condensation gathers on them.

Occasionally a sound occurs several breaths before its cause. The fauna treat this as useful information. Herds begin turning before the predator’s strike has happened; swimmers scatter from a lightning stroke that has not yet found its path. Now and then the warning is wrong, or belongs to another nearby stretch of world. The resulting casualties are absorbed with the same efficiency as everything else.

Activity follows no day and night. It follows overlapping shifts of pressure, condensation, charge, and migration. During one phase, the whole basin seems to inhale: fog lowers, water climbs into it, and animals draw together until the ground appears to be made entirely of backs. During another, pressure breaks loose in traveling fronts. Currents race through the pools, vents thunder, and the fog herds stream low over the swells. Later comes reverse rain. Droplets rise from the water in silver threads, join a suspended layer overhead, and remain there until some unrelated event releases them all at once.

The cycles never synchronize for long. There is always a migration crossing a compression front, always a feeding surge beneath approaching weather, always some predator following the heat or noise released by another process.

Warmth, especially, does not pass unnoticed. It is rare enough to function as both resource and scent. Small fauna gather around it in tightening layers, drinking the difference between warm and cold. Larger animals follow the gatherings. A warm body introduced into the Works would not simply freeze; it would become an event, then a migration, then a densely occupied feeding ground.

The breakdown of reality is most evident where the crowding becomes impossible.

When too many presences occupy one place, more place occurs between them. The ground does not stretch, split, or visibly move. There is simply an additional reach of wet, ancient lowland where a moment before there was no room for it. It arrives already inhabited. Its puddles are already rimmed with worn mineral crusts; its animals already possess scars, migrations, and old grievances. The herd continues, no less crowded, and the horizon has shifted slightly farther away.

Distance is therefore another product of the Works. It is manufactured on demand and immediately put to use. This is how the region can be both open and saturated, extending far beyond sight without offering a single unoccupied interval. Travel does not necessarily bring one closer to an edge. Sometimes it only increases the amount of lowland that must be crossed in order to remain where one is.

Other failures are just as old and just as orderly. Rain may fall before the cloud that carries it has condensed. Young animals may emerge ahead of the mineral pulse from which their bodies precipitate. A carcass can lie in a pool for several cycles as a warning before the death that produces it. None of this feels like an active catastrophe. The anomalies have banks and channels. They recur within limits. Generations of fauna have come to depend on their timing.

Erosion has worn even causality smooth.

The Works is ancient not because nothing changes, but because every change has been incorporated into the same settled circulation. Sediment exported by water returns in fog, animal bodies, or newly inserted ground. Charge spent in motion becomes heat, heat becomes a gathering, the gathering becomes pressure, and pressure enters another stroke. Nothing accumulates into a final product. Nothing is allowed to become empty enough for silence to settle permanently.

So the dim lowland continues: cold brine climbing over rounded stone, blue fire moving through packed pools, herds compressing the mud, fog streaming from invisible backs. Somewhere a hush crosses a migration and a small silence travels through the thunder. Then the saturated ground tightens beneath everything.

The next compression arrives.

The whole region answers at once—already too crowded, already making room.
