## Serein Basin

From the low rim, Serein looks less like a city than a city’s measurements laid across a young country.

The basin runs onward until its far side becomes only a gray alteration of the sky. No hill within it is high enough to interrupt the view. Long pewter causeways cross the open ground, straight for several kilometers before bending around nothing visible. At their intersections stand precincts: a ribbed condenser tower, a cluster of stepped galleries, a cistern court, gardens, a transit hall. Between these nodes are enormous intervals of darkness and unused stone. Yet the emptiness is not wilderness in the ordinary sense. Conduits underlie it, survey lights mark it, and the causeways hum faintly with routes that may see only one carriage in an hour. One is never quite outside Serein. One is only between its rooms.

The ground was made recently, and it has not had time to become tactful. Terraces end in knife-edged lips. Fissures are clean and glassy, without talus or rounded shoulders. In the newest districts the dark, basaltic ceramic still carries a fine hexagonal grain from its formation. There is almost no loose rock and, away from the gardens, very little soil. Even dust is scarce enough to have compositional value. The stone remains faintly warm during the coolest hours, as though retaining some instruction involving heat.

The air is warm too, but usually only lightly damp: warm on the skin, dry enough at the back of the throat to make the extravagance of green in the precincts seem improbable. Occasionally a cooler current slides along the basin floor and leaves beads of moisture on railings, leaf edges, and the upper surfaces of walls. These brief condensations are more important than rain.

Overhead, the Loom takes most of the daylight.

It is an orbital veil rather than a roof, though from below its hairline arcs seem close enough to touch with a tower. At local noon the basin has the muted bronze-violet light of late dusk. Shadows are broad and uncertain. Dark leaves look almost black; pale ceramics become the color of old bone. During the deeper hours, the brightest thing in a district may be the sky reflected in a covered cistern. Windows and path lights are kept low, generally amber or dull red. There is no glare anywhere, and no true darkness while the city infrastructure is functioning.

Serein is old as a network and young as a place. Some of its transit rights-of-way and foundation coordinates are centuries older than the ground beneath them. The oldest stations originally stood on slender supports while the basin was grown upward to meet them. In maintenance cuttings, their pale lower levels can still be seen below darker replacement masonry and the newest smoke-clear materials. Upper galleries project at angles that would be structural boasts elsewhere. Here they are simply permitted. Walls may be solid to rain but porous to roots; a floor can be rigid beneath a carriage and soft, a moment later, beneath a gardener’s bare foot.

At the basin’s growing edge, construction is stranger and quieter.

First a flat shadow appears on the survey field, although the light is too diffuse for anything nearby to cast it. Rain, if rain comes, avoids the outlined area. The air above it becomes slightly warmer. Hours or days later there is a terrace, a foundation, or the first course of a wall. Nothing is cut, so there is no dust. Nothing is hauled, so there are no tracks. The process is accompanied only by a tone felt in the teeth and ribs.

It is not quite accurate to say that the Loom prints matter. It changes the terms on which matter agrees to occupy a shape. Afterward, ordinary chemistry is usually sufficient to keep the result standing—provided no one gives the new ground a sufficiently persuasive reason to become something else.

Water is scarce, not absent; it is simply never allowed to be casual.

No river crosses the basin. Open water occurs in black cisterns, in clear-covered lenses beneath paving, and in small hollows where condensation persists for a few days. Rain arrives in measured islands when condenser ribs cool the air enough to precipitate it. A curtain may cross one court while the next remains dry, or wet half a street and leave the other half dusty. For several minutes the runnels shine. Leaves drip into stone throats. Then the water is taken below, and the district returns to silence.

The smell of these brief wettings is pronounced: warm mineral dust, green sap, wet ceramic, and a faint clean metallic note from the condensers. In low pockets, mist sometimes remains after the surfaces elsewhere have dried. It moves without much wind, dividing itself around walls and then slowly rejoining.

Vegetation follows the city’s water, its leaks, and increasingly its mistakes.

Around every inhabited precinct the growth is lush. Veilleaf trees hold broad, overlapping leaves in layered canopies, each leaf guttered toward a central stem. Copper ferns pack the shaded beds. Vines climb the warm sides of galleries and spread across unused transit arches. Moss forms deep wine-red cushions where condensation gathers. Beyond these green belts, however, the open basin can be nearly bare for kilometers. The result is not continuous forest but an archipelago of dense, humid growth separated by wide mineral quiet.

The plants make do with the diminished light. Their darkest tissues use the red remnant of daylight efficiently; some also draw a slow subsidy through their root mats from the same buried lattice that distributes heat and structural instruction. They are not merely decorations sustained by the city. They are part of what makes the land durable.

Freshly resolved ground remains too responsive. An empty terrace can be revised cheaply, and a foundation without biological history may accept a later command with inconvenient literalness. Rootlace—a white, branching mat of roots, fungi, and fine mineral filaments—complicates that obedience. It stores water unevenly, makes microscopic voids, introduces salts and acids, and binds thousands of small variations into the substrate. Microbes follow. Soil begins to form. The ground becomes harder for the Loom to describe simply.

Gardeners in Serein are therefore also civil engineers. They speak of young land as “agreeable” and do not mean it as praise. Mature land, by contrast, has begun to disagree. A city built entirely on obedient ground would be too easy to alter and too difficult to trust.

The human presence remains sparse. A complex sized for a town may contain a dozen residents, a few gardeners, and maintenance functions that have no separate bodies at all. One can cross a public hall without meeting anyone, then find three people quietly eating beside a cistern as though the hall’s emptiness were the most natural thing in the world. There is no atmosphere of catastrophe. Doors are maintained. Gardens are tended. Carriages arrive. The emptiness is capacity, not aftermath.

Animals have been slower to establish themselves. Dusk moths appear after rain, wide as a hand but nearly invisible until they cross a lamp. Pale, nearly blind newts live in some of the warmer cisterns, where they move beneath the reflected sky like small errors in the water. In the oldest garden belts there are occasional prints of a low, soft-footed browser, though weeks may pass between sightings. Mostly one encounters evidence: a stripped fern tip, a moth wing on stone, a narrow trail through dust. There is no chorus of birds or insects at any hour.

The quiet has distinct layers. Condenser towers produce a pressure more felt than heard. A drop falls into a cistern every few seconds. A carriage announces itself with a small ceramic click. During dry winds, veilleaf canopies make a long, subdued hiss that carries farther than voices. When a new terrace is resolved at the rim, the accompanying tone can be felt across an entire precinct, but conversation need not stop.

Several processes are usually underway, though few of them hurry. Condenser ribs open and close with the moisture cycle. Copper ferns uncurl within minutes of being wet. Unneeded facades become thinner over weeks as their material is withdrawn, while roots are permitted to keep the foundations. Causeways are extended toward precincts that still exist only as dated entries on a survey map. At the growing edge, those maps carry confidence estimates as well as distances.

Visitors are given three instructions. Keep out of unplanted survey polygons, however solid they look. Do not cut white rootlace. And if a shadow appears with nothing to cast it, move away; the Loom has usually already decided what will stand there.

Every so often, after several dry days, a warm wind crosses the open basin and gathers the little loose material there is. It lays this dust in sharp fans behind walls and survey stakes. The next rain darkens the fans and sometimes carries their finest grains a short distance downhill. These are among the first sediments in Serein that no one designed.

Near one mature precinct, a cistern has now overflowed often enough to establish a small channel. It escapes the garden, crosses a causeway’s maintenance seam, and wanders for thirty meters over ground that was once perfectly level. Rootlace has begun to follow it. So have the ferns.

The channel is less than a hand deep and usually dry. The city has left it alone. It is the first line in the basin that neither the surveyors nor the Loom drew.
