## The Root-Ward

At the lowest seam of a young bog, just below the water table, there is a settlement that can be overlooked from five steps away. Above it stands only a long, black tussock, swollen with rain and rimed at its edges. Water gathers in the hollows around it, hesitates, and disappears through the stems. Beneath that tussock lies the Root-Ward: a town inside the hollow storage root of a plant whose green parts never enter this place.

The bog is newly formed, a cold depression left by retreating ice, and the Ward is younger still. Nothing here has been weathered smooth. The chamber began as a split nine thaws ago, when the root swelled and its heart pulled apart along a seam. Its cut edges remain pale and sharp. Steps have crisp lips; abandoned doorways end in splintered points. Rootlets are always opening new hairline passages and splitting old ones, so the closest thing to erosion is growth itself. Even the oldest construction carries only a few thin layers of callus, repair, and renewed growth.

There is no mineral floor and no dead timber. The street is a flattened vascular rib, springy and cold underfoot. Houses are woven between living buttresses, their walls thickened by the plant where cords have pressed against it. Gutters follow natural veins. Cisterns are cups of tightly folded tissue. Doors are valves persuaded to remain open; windows are healed cuts, narrow as knife marks. Seen during the brief gray inspections when the outer plug is opened, everything is ivory, straw, or green-black, with clear sap beading along the seams. For the rest of the year, the darkness is nearly complete, and the town exists chiefly as texture, scent, and temperature.

For a place so small, it is unmistakably urban. Nineteen dwellings stand along a main lane and three cross-passages. There is a common cistern, a midden-lock, two raised footbridges, watch niches, and a sequence of thorn-and-reed sluice gates. Yet most of the Ward remains unbuilt. Long intervals of naked living floor separate the household clusters, and the central oval is kept entirely empty. No one builds there because the lanes have all shifted before, and everyone expects them to shift again.

The air stays only a little above freezing. It smells of cold water, crushed stems, wet shell, and the faint sweetness of sap. Condensation gathers on every downward surface. Sometimes a drop grows for hours before falling; sometimes the whole roof seems to exhale at once and the lane is briefly stippled with rain. The main gutter carries black seep-water deep enough to cover a hand, while clearer water weeps from the walls and threads between the houses. Everything has a tide mark, though the tides belong to no sea.

The Ward’s tools are few: shell scrapers, thorn awls, split-root wedges, bone needles, sinew, and hand-rolled cord. There is no metal, no wheel, and almost no fire. Smoke would linger in every house, and an open flame would injure the same tissue that shelters it. Construction is therefore less a matter of assembling materials than of timing cuts and pressure. A cord laid against the root may become the lintel of a niche. A scrape made too deeply closes within days. A house left unattended for a growing cycle becomes a low bulge crossed by the ghosts of its rooms. Maps are bundles of knotted cord, revised whenever a threshold narrows or a bridge no longer meets its bank.

The apparent emptiness between buildings is full of animals.

They are not an addition to the Ward; they are most of its movement and much of its surface. Pale springtails cross the curbs in sheets so fine they resemble water changing its mind. Mites inhabit every twist of cord. Transparent worms hang where the roof drips, and blind beetles graze in patient files along the sap veins. Shell-less snails leave cold, crossing trails. Beneath the gutter lie flat hunting eels, while many-legged predators wait in the ceiling pores, their legs so delicate that their approach is felt more often than heard.

The cold slows this multitude without diminishing it. When sap pressure rises, the animals emerge in layers. Grazers follow the wet walls upward; hunters follow the grazers; winged things hatch in soft, crowded clouds that make almost no sound. A hand held against a wall soon acquires a living outline. Corpses seldom remain whole for long. Root hairs enter them, beetles dismantle them, and the smallest fragments are carried into drainage pores. The plant feeds on the old drowned vegetation of the bog and on whatever dies within its hollow. It is shelter, food source, predator, and foundation without being notably gentle in any of these roles.

The residents endure because they are useful. They clear the drinking pores, cull beetles from the tender growth ring, and keep the principal veins free of fibrous blockages. In return, the root thickens around their dwellings instead of closing over them—usually. No one calls this an agreement. The plant has no need to agree.

Quiet is maintained with the same care as drainage. Foot wrappings are repaired before holes appear, house cords are greased with sap to prevent creaking, and voices rarely rise above the level required to be heard at arm’s length. When the gutter suddenly goes still, work stops altogether; still water means the blind eels are listening for vibration. Most days the loudest sounds are a single drop striking the cistern, the dry tick of a beetle turning over, or the small suction of a sluice being lifted.

Several times a day, a pressure pulse passes through the root. The far seal tightens first. The walls cool and draw inward by a finger’s width. Water rises in the gutter and, for a few heartbeats, may run back toward its source. Then the whole Ward becomes active: springtails climb, beetles unhook themselves from the walls, eels slide into side pores, and predators emerge behind them. The residents lift a bridge, open one gate, close another, and retie the survey cords. For a short while the settlement is intensely busy, though a listener hears little beyond dripping and faint clicks.

Afterward, the quiet returns, but nothing returns to quite its former place.

Above, there is only wet vegetation in a cold hollow, perhaps a wisp of vapor at the outer plug. Below, in darkness, nineteen households maintain their narrow lanes inside a young and growing wound. The Root-Ward is never finished, and it is not waiting to become a ruin. It is continually being swallowed, repaired, and inhabited—one cold drop, one new rootlet, one multitude of nearly silent feet at a time.
