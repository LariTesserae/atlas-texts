## The Held Fracture

Beneath a plain of yellow glass, where the surface shows nothing but a shallow dimple and a few upright splinters, there is a hollow no larger than a low-ceilinged room. Seven paces cross it. At the center, a tall person could stand; at the edges, the roof comes down almost to the floor.

It is not a cave in the ordinary sense. No passage leads into it, and no gradual weathering has enlarged it. The whole pocket is the inside of a single fracture, lens-shaped and newly opened. The walls meet in edges that could cut cloth. Plates of green-black stone lift from one another like the pages of a book struck sharply from below. There are no rounded cobbles, no polished corners, no drifts of soft sediment. Where the floor has broken, it has broken into needles and thin, bright scales.

Nothing here has ever been wet. The dryness is not the dryness of evaporation, or of some ancient moisture long since lost. It is more fundamental than that. There is no damp in the deepest crevice, no film upon the minerals, no swelling or softening after a disturbance. Dust behaves like a multitude of tiny hard bodies. It clicks, rebounds, and settles without clinging. Even the living tissues are dry: cut one of the green fronds, and it would part with a clean fibrous tear, releasing sweet-smelling powder rather than sap.

The air is warm, almost hot against the face, and perfectly still. Its warmth does not appear to arrive from deeper stone or from any central vent. It is simply the temperature of the pocket, as fixed as its dimensions. The atmosphere tastes faintly of brass and crushed pepper. Sound carries through it with unusual pressure, so that the loudest tones seem to touch the teeth and the small bones behind the ears before they reach any sense of hearing.

And the pocket is loud.

A furnace-deep roar occupies the floor. Above it runs a piercing, glassy note, almost too thin to locate. Between these are dry rattles, snaps, and a ticking like hundreds of small beads striking a drum. The volume is absurd for so small a space. Yet none of it escapes into the surrounding glassy plain. A thumb’s width beyond the wall, the stone is silent.

This is not an echo. An echo would imply an original sound that had finished. The pocket is still making the sound of its own opening.

Its only light is a low garnet radiance in the fresh fracture faces. The stone itself shines just enough to reveal the ridges of the floor and the undersides of leaves. Nothing flickers. The illumination is too dim for clear color at a distance, but near the walls it separates the minerals into oxblood, smoky green, and a pale, waxy yellow. Shadows are numerous and faint, crossing one another without ever becoming fully black.

Three groves grow in this dimness.

They are lavish groves, though small. In the largest, knee-high fronds overlap in dense fans, each leaf thin and supple as treated hide. Their surfaces are black-green at the base, deepening almost to blue, with sulfur-colored margins. Beneath them crowd soft cushions of branching filaments, dry velvets, and little spiraled blades that spring back when pressed. Another grove consists mostly of translucent cups stacked upon jointed stems. The third is a tangle of ribbon foliage and hard amber nodules, so closely interwoven that the stone beneath it cannot be seen.

The vegetation does not conserve itself. Its leaves are broad, thin, extravagant. There are no waxy reservoirs, no tightly closed pores, no armored bulbs. Nothing in its form suggests drought, because drought would imply the possibility of water.

The plants feed on warmth and vibration. Their broad blades are less like solar collectors than drumheads. Fine ribs divide each leaf into fields tuned to different portions of the permanent chord; the ribs tremble continuously, though the fronds do not appear to move unless watched closely. At the roots, where green tissue grades into pale crystalline forks, that trembling becomes chemistry. The plants clasp the rock rather than burrowing into it. Some have rooted along a single sharp seam, following its resonance in a perfect green line.

For all their lushness, the groves do not fill the chamber. Between them lie broad intervals of bare floor. One can stand in the center and see long reaches of unoccupied mineral on every side. A single frond fallen visually against that emptiness would seem enormous. The place balances profusion with vacancy: each living patch is crowded within itself, but life as a whole is scattered.

Animals inhabit the groves in considerable numbers.

Fingernail-sized browsers move along the leaf ribs in single file, their porcelain shells marked by one dark stripe. They have six legs and a pair of delicate mouth brushes, which they draw across the trembling foliage. Under the stacked cups live pale, soft-bodied creatures that inflate slightly when the deepest tone passes through them. Their bodies are otherwise almost invisible against the stone.

The most conspicuous animals are the runners: slim, hand-long creatures with four gripping feet and two folded vanes along their backs. They cross the open floor one at a time, always stopping at particular stones. When alarmed—or when performing whatever private necessity resembles alarm—they open the vanes. These are not wings. They vibrate in opposition to the chamber’s note, and for an instant the runner becomes difficult to look at directly, its outline lost among its own shadows.

A flat amber hunter rests beneath a tilted slab near the smallest grove. Tiny winged things gather around the amber nodules. Something many-legged lives inside the wall itself, audible as a quick scratch that travels from one side of the pocket to the other without any crack wide enough to contain it.

Yet there is no impression of teeming. Most of the animals remain within their own narrow territories, and the open spaces stay mostly open. One may see a dozen small lives around a single leaf, then nothing at all across the next two paces. The pocket has abundance without crowding.

Three structures stand at the edges of the bare floor.

One is a half-ring of fitted stone chips, none larger than a thumb. Another is a low screen made from split leaf-ribs and dry-braided fiber. The third consists of two upright flakes supporting a crosspiece, all joined without mortar. Near them are a pointed splinter, three smooth hammerstones, a coil of cord, and seven nodules arranged by size. A row of shallow notches marks the base of the screen.

Nothing here is metal. Nothing suggests machining or even fired craft. The work is careful but simple: selection, chipping, rubbing, binding. The builders appear to have discovered that certain positions within the chamber alter the sound. At those fixed acoustic nodes, one component of the roar drops away. The little structures occupy such places. They may be shelters, listening cells, offerings, or devices whose purpose has no outside equivalent.

They are not ruins. Nothing has fallen from them. Neither are they unfinished in any meaningful sense. The half-ring will never receive its missing stones; the cord will never fray; the notches will never be continued.

There are no young plants among the mature ones, no shed skins, no corpses, no leaf litter, no worn paths. Mouths move, feet cross the floor, vanes open and close, but every circuit closes without residue. Feeding does not diminish a grove. Movement does not compact the dust. Any census of the animals, whenever taken, gives the same count.

A displaced pebble is the clearest demonstration. It can be rolled away from the wall and watched until it stops. Some time later, it is simply back in its original place. It has not been seen to return. There is no visible correction, no small convulsion of the world. The chamber merely declines to retain the alteration.

Thus the fracture remains fresh. Not recently fresh, but permanently so. Its first instant has never been succeeded by a second.

The groves remain on the verge of spreading across the empty floor, without spreading. The runner remains warm and quick, without aging. The little structures remain scattered, without being abandoned. The garnet light neither fades nor strengthens, and the sound of opening never becomes an echo.

The pocket is not paused. It is alive in the way a held chord can be alive: rich, sustained, full of distinct voices, and incapable of resolving.

It is not waiting for anything. Waiting would require a next moment.
