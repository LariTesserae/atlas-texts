## The High Catchment

At an altitude where mountains usually simplify into snow, wind, and a single exposed ridge, the High Catchment becomes crowded.

It occupies the summit of a vast massif, tens of kilometres across, its outer cliffs falling beyond sight into a white sea of cloud. From far off, the whole region can look like one blinding plateau. Closer, it separates into fin-fields, slot basins, hanging shelves, and deep amphitheatres. Hundreds of pale rock blades stand nearly touching, some broad as houses, others thin enough that daylight shows through their cracked upper edges. Between them are clefts just wide enough for a body, opening without warning into clearings a kilometre broad. Beyond every apparent horizon waits another crowded band of stone.

The rock is mostly quartzite, grey and honey-coloured when dry, nearly black when wet. Seams of iron stain whole walls vermilion; copper salts produce a hard, uncompromising green that might be mistaken for moss until one touches it. There is no moss. There are no lichens in the sheltered cracks, no roots in the scree, no film of life beneath the water. Even the smallest sheltered hollow contains only mineral, sediment, and weather.

Nothing about the Catchment looks newly broken. The fin-tops are rounded. Old collapse scars have been softened into bowls. Fallen blocks have worn one another into smooth, close-fitting pavements, and water channels lie inside water channels, the oldest filled with translucent stone. Some walls are fluted vertically, others horizontally, and a few carry intersecting scallops as though rain and gravity have spent ages arguing over their shape.

They have. The Catchment’s oldest peculiarity is that **down is local**.

Usually the difference is small. A runnel reaches the foot of one fin, crosses a seam of dark gravel, and begins flowing up a slab that visibly rises. A pool drains toward its centre. Rain falling beside a cliff may turn and strike the cliff horizontally. At the boundaries between two downs, water gathers in clear lenses: vertical disks, trembling spheres, or motionless sheets bent around nothing. A stone dropped into one may strike bottom, while a second stone dropped a hand’s breadth away continues sideways until it disappears around the rock.

The effect is not chaotic. Each district keeps its inclination for days or seasons, and the larger changes follow the weather. The fin-fields have been carved by these habits for so long that their forms record them. Their strange polish is not damage from a recent catastrophe. It is the settled appearance of a place whose physics have been weathering it for a very long time.

Water is everywhere, though seldom in great volume. It condenses on cold stone even while the sky is clear, first darkening the rock, then gathering into beads, then joining into threads too fine to see until they move. Weeping walls feed stepped pools. Pools overflow into slots. Slots give back a shining mist. In the open basins lie mineral mires: clear water over grey-blue silica gel, powdered quartz, and rust-red clay. They have no peat, no surface mat, no reeds. Their margins are marked by opaline crusts that curl and crack as they dry.

The mires swell when the cloud tide rises. Their weight opens joints in the underlying rock; when they shrink, the crusts contract and pluck flakes away. This is the closest the Catchment comes to growth. It is a slow, reversible chemistry, without metabolism or inheritance. A mire may hold its outline for centuries while every particle within it is replaced.

The air remains cool even under the fierce sun. At night, sheltered water skins over with ice and the highest fins collect brief beards of rime. By morning these are melting, though the air still bites and exposed shade feels wintry. Direct sunlight heats the upper surfaces of the rock until they tick and faintly steam, while a few steps below, water remains cold enough to numb the hand.

Light comes from below as well as above. The cloud sea throws it upward into the clefts, so there are few truly dark places. Wet walls flash white; mires shine with an internal milkiness; the zenith, by contrast, is a deep and narrow blue. At noon the brightness can be difficult to endure. Every object casts its ordinary shadow and usually a second, paler one. The second shadow belongs to light that has taken a longer route through folded space. Sometimes it lags perceptibly. A cloud covers the sun, the first shadows vanish, and for several breaths faint shadows remain standing by themselves.

The same folding makes distance unreliable without making it arbitrary. The far side of a cleft may be ten paces away at dawn and require an hour’s detour by afternoon. A pool can reflect cliffs that stand behind it, or show the underside of a shelf from which its water is simultaneously falling. Some views repeat: the same three ochre fins appear at increasing distances, not as copies but as successive encounters with the same formation. Paths do not so much move as acquire and lose intervening places.

Sound travels through these arrangements more readily than sight. The Catchment is loud. Innumerable runnels produce a close, bright hissing; larger sheets of water strike stone with hollow, booming reports. Quartz-rich fins hum when their loads of water change. Cooling blocks crack like distant doors. Somewhere below, unseen rockfalls gather into long rolls that seem to pass through the ground rather than the air.

The thin atmosphere ought to soften all this. Instead, the cliffs return sound with interest. A single pebble striking a pool may be answered by three impacts: one beneath it, one far below in the cloud, and one from an apparently dry basin nearby. Echoes sometimes reveal openings that cannot be seen. At other times they return from solid walls with the acoustic signature of enormous empty rooms. There are intervals, especially just before the evening cooling, when every sound stops at once. These silences feel less like peace than like a held breath.

Scattered through the fin-fields are signs that the place was once attentively used.

They are modest: hand-chipped gutters two fingers wide, cairns of flat stones, short flights of steps, clay-lined catchments, and roofless cells built against sheltered walls. Joints are occasionally secured with hammered native copper, now green and brittle. Hammerstones and flakes remain where work was abandoned. There are no extensive ruins and nothing resembling a town. Most districts contain only a marker, a basin, or a wall too straight to be natural.

The constructions do not fight the Catchment’s geometry. They accommodate it. Gutters branch and stop, then resume on a facing wall. Stairs climb to what is now the underside of a pool. Certain cairns are built around upright slabs positioned to ring when struck, and beside them sit balanced stones that tip when their catchments fill. Water still sets a few of these mechanisms knocking at irregular intervals. Their sounds carry from fold to fold.

Perhaps they were alarms, or water gauges, or surveying instruments. An echo’s delay would have revealed whether a passage remained continuous. A sounding stone that once answered from across a cleft might suddenly answer from beneath one’s feet. Whatever their exact purpose, the builders seem to have understood that a fixed map would be less useful here than a repeated question.

One structure, older than most, stands beside a mire in an open amphitheatre. Its low walls are fitted without mortar, and its floor remains dry despite the wetness all around. Three separate gutters approach it from directions that no longer share a downhill. Only one still delivers water. Inside are seven shallow depressions worn into the threshold and a stone bench polished along its front edge. Nothing else remains: no vessel, no image, no body, no object that explains who considered this place worth sheltering in.

Each day the whole region passes through its water cycle. Before sunrise, the cloud sea rises and enters the lower clefts. Fog moves through the fin-fields in dense, separate ribbons, condensing on every cold surface. The mires swell. Old channels begin to tick and whisper. As the cloud withdraws, the light hardens, the temporary streams strengthen, and the folded distances become widest around noon. Later, cooling stone contracts with deep reports; pools change their outlets; suspended lenses of water break apart and fall in several directions. By night the Catchment is rimed, dripping, and still audible across kilometres.

Over long periods, loss and deposition nearly balance. The fins shed sand from their upper edges while their feet accumulate silica and clay. Mires migrate slightly without changing their essential forms. Channels are abandoned and replaced, but the total movement of water remains much the same. Even the spatial folds seem to wear smooth paths for themselves, recurring along the same seams and amphitheatres.

Thus the Catchment is neither pristine nor ruined, neither living nor inert. It is an old, bright, crowded waterworks above the clouds, in which erosion has adapted to unstable gravity and distance has become something that can weather. Its reality has not finished breaking down. The breakdown has become one of its load-bearing structures.

Nothing roots here. Nothing hunts, calls, or takes shelter by instinct. Yet almost nothing is still. The stone rings, the mires swell, the waters choose their separate downs, and the little constructed basins continue patient, and still being answered.
