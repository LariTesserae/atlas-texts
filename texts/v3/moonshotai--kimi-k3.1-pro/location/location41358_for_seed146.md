## The Unweathered Deep

The warmth arrives before any sense of scale.

It comes through the soles of the feet and through a hand laid against the wall: a steady, mineral warmth, like stone that has been held for a long time against a body. But nothing here has been heated in order to cool. The rock, the heavy gas between the rocks, and the animals packed against both all hold nearly the same temperature. Within the Deep there are no warm currents rising, no cold layers sinking, no slow breathing of convection. Warmth is simply one of the place’s properties, as hardness is.

There is no water to complicate it. No dampness darkens the fractures. Nothing drips in the dark. The pale mineral seams are not the residue of vanished seas; they were never dissolved, never carried, never laid down in sediment. Even the animals are dry inside. Their flexible tissues are built around thin, non-aqueous films and gas-filled lattices. They have neither wet eyes nor blood. When two of their body-plates separate, the exposed surface shines for an instant like polished horn, then dulls without moisture.

At first, the Deep seems to be a single fracture in the world.

The walls are ink-black, iron-purple, or the brittle yellow of old bone where a carried light catches them. Their faces break in smooth shells and abrupt, glassy steps. Ridges end in edges fine enough to cut. Needles of mineral project from the roof—not stalactites, for nothing has ever dripped from them, but clean extensions of the stress that made the chamber. There are no rounded cobbles, no softened corners, no beds of sorted sand. Even the loose grains are angular and interlocked, reluctant to move.

Everything looks freshly broken.

Then a gallery withdraws its walls. The floor falls away in a series of broad, tilted plates, and beyond them the darkness continues without giving back an echo. What had seemed a cave becomes a province of connected voids. Some are narrow and upright, crowded with mineral blades taller than towers. Others open beneath roofs too distant for any portable light to find. There are subterranean plains whose animal-covered horizons curve away before the stone does, and fissure countries in which a traveler could descend for days without ever reaching a lower floor.

Taken together, the voids are continental. They have something like climate, though never weather. One province contains a clear, crushingly still gas; another holds permanent veils of dry black motes, each suspended at its own level, neither settling nor rising. Elsewhere the gas is layered by pressure into regions that ring faintly at the ears. These provinces have boundaries, but the boundaries do not travel. Nothing here gathers into a storm. Nothing clears.

The native darkness is almost complete. A few mineral interfaces retain a fixed, wine-red phosphorescence, too weak to illuminate even the crystals that emit it. The animals do not glow. Most have no eyes at all, or possess only smooth black pits sensitive to pressure, contact, and the faint electrical texture of nearby bodies.

A carried light does not reveal emptiness.

It reveals backs.

The lowest animals are broad and low, their segmented shells overlapping like pieces of a pavement. They rest nearly touching, graphite-colored and warm, with narrow amber seams flexing between their plates. Their legs fold so closely beneath them that they seem at first to be mineral nodules. Between them run creatures no thicker than a finger, all joints and dry sensory brushes, picking their way across shells and stone with the same delicate steps.

Larger animals occupy the spaces above these without clearing them. Some stand on long, splayed limbs, their bodies held horizontally like covered bridges while smaller life moves beneath. Others hang in the dense gas with ribbed membranes extended, drifting no more than the width of a hand before returning to their former attitude. Along vertical walls, flattened species fit themselves into the fractures in tile-like layers. In certain chambers the roof is also occupied, and open space becomes a low, warm corridor through bodies.

The multitude is extraordinarily quiet.

There is a dry tick when one plate passes another. There is the faint whisk of a brush-limb withdrawing, the soft friction of a membrane adjusting, the almost inaudible click of a mouth-part closing against stone. The shells and porous mineral swallow these sounds within a few paces. There are no herds thundering, no cries crossing the vaults, no insectile electric chorus. Often the loudest sound is the intrusion of one’s own clothing or breath. Even a shout does not awaken the Deep; it becomes small, travels a little way, and is absorbed.

No plants grow among the animals. There are no root mats, no fronds, no fungal felt preparing the way for something greener. Nor is there a hidden food web supporting the crowd. The gestures of life imitate one without completing it. A broad-backed animal passes its mouth over a crystal face, but removes nothing. Two long-limbed creatures intertwine their sensory organs and separate unchanged. A hooked animal holds a smaller one carefully against its underside for years, if years can be said to pass there, and the smaller one is neither eaten nor transformed.

Nothing is produced. Nothing is consumed.

Life here is not a flow of matter through hunger, birth, and decay. It is a standing arrangement of forms, closer to a chord than to a chain. Each body maintains itself through closed exchanges with the warm mineral and with its neighbors. Every flex is balanced by a return; every displacement is compensated elsewhere. Small animals are not young. Large animals are not old. The motionless are not necessarily dead, and there are no carcasses from which to learn what death would look like.

Their density is not competition. It is structure. Bodies distribute pressure, shelter one another from contact with sharp mineral, and fill nearly every available configuration. Open space, not nourishment, is the rare thing.

Scattered through this abundance, at immense and irregular intervals, are artifacts.

A translucent stone flake, struck to a point and hafted into a split shell with dark animal fiber. Four thin slabs fitted into a low hood just large enough for one crouching body. A braided line stretched between two natural needles above a fissure, not as a bridge but perhaps as a measure of its width. On one wall, three short grooves and a long one, repeated with slight variations. Elsewhere, a hand-sized comb of black mineral, each tooth made by patient abrasion.

There is no metalwork, no wheel, no kiln, no settlement. There are also no storage vessels: nothing needs to be carried, watered, or kept from spoiling. Several animal species have dextrous forelimbs, but none can confidently be identified as the makers. Occasionally a broad-palmed creature is found with its fingers fitted around an old tool, holding it against a stone without cutting.

The structures are not ruins. Their cords have not frayed, their slabs have not shifted, and their worked edges remain as crisp as the natural fractures. “Abandoned” is a word brought from somewhere else. So is “new.”

This is the Deep’s final strangeness: it moves without accumulating history.

An animal turns its head and later turns it back. Membranes open and close. The small runners cross a shell, but no path becomes polished beneath them. The permanent hazes do not thin. No dust buries the artifacts. No fracture sheds its sharpness. There are no seasons, no succession of species, no gradual darkening or brightening, no difference between one age and the next large enough for the place to notice.

Its youth is therefore not chronological. The Deep is young only in the sense that nothing has ever happened to its surfaces. Its stillness is not death, and its freshness is not promise. A traveler may tire, may hunger, may carry light from one crowded province to another, but the Deep does not join that sequence.

It is not waiting.

Waiting would imply a next thing.
