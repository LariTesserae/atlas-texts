On the floor of a polar sea, just beneath the first skin of sediment, there is a hollow in newly fractured rock. It is no larger than a heart. A hand resting on the stone above it would feel nothing unusual: no warmth, no vibration, no answering movement.

The rock is still glassy along the fracture. Its edges have not softened, and there is no sand inside, no gravel, no silt laid down in patient layers. A few fresh black flakes lie where they fell. Along one wall, transparent mineral needles have just begun to grow, so fine and new that the dim light passes entirely through them. The place is not an old cave. It is scarcely a cave yet.

Nevertheless, it is full.

Water occupies every angle of it, without even a bubble trapped beneath the highest point. This is dense, cold brine, kept liquid by its salt. Near the walls it is clear; toward the center it becomes faintly milky with eggs, molts, organic dust, and creatures too small to distinguish from drifting debris. A capillary opening connects the hollow to the sea. Through it, changes in pressure arrive as a slow tidal pulse. New water enters; old water leaves. The little chamber breathes, though nothing in it has lungs.

No daylight reaches this far. Its illumination is biological and unreliable. Cold green-blue light seeps from bacterial films in blind alleys, and occasionally from the bodies of larvae drifting through the central water. The newest growth is almost black. The oldest districts glow faintly, because there the city is digesting its own dead. Light is therefore not a sign of safety or beginning. It is a sign of accumulation, decay, and reuse. Even at its brightest, the hollow reveals edges more readily than whole forms.

At first, the growth covering the walls looks like frost.

Then the frost resolves into arcades.

Tiny chambers share walls and open into one another. Narrow buttresses climb the raw rock and turn inward at their tips. Brittle bridges span fractures. Some passages are wide enough for several currents to separate within them; others contract to a single living pore. There are tiered nurseries, waste channels, roofed galleries, and open shafts through which larvae rise and fall. What seemed to be one encrustation becomes a crowded urban interior, with distinct quarters growing over and through the remains of earlier ones.

Nothing here has been cut, fitted, fired, or carried into place. The builders are colonial animals, each one small enough that its own body nearly fills the chamber it secretes. A builder makes only a cup around itself. Its neighbors make adjoining cups. When they die, other generations roof them over, partition them, or use them as foundations. Every arch is either alive or composed of lives that have ended.

The water supplies what little planning exists. Growth survives in sheltered seams and fails where the tidal current runs strongly. Sheltered seams become towers and banks of chambers; places where growth repeatedly fails remain open as avenues. Bridges persist only at angles where their inhabitants can feed without being torn away. The city’s map is a memory of flow. Its streets are not so much constructed as continually left unfinished.

There is no privileged floor. Architecture covers the ceiling, the walls, and the underside of every fallen flake. Six skewed skylines lean toward the central water. To the inhabitants, a surface is simply wherever their bodies can find purchase. The hollow’s only true open space is the water at its center: plaza, crossing, hunting ground, and weather.

Look closely at any one bridge and the abundance becomes almost oppressive. Its outer edge is lined with the builders’ feeding crowns, pale and branching, opening and closing by the hundred. Translucent crustaceans pick through the joints beneath them, their dark interiors visible through their shells. A ribbon worm lies along the underside, extended so thinly that it resembles a seam in the material. Egg cases crowd the sheltered angle where the bridge meets the wall. Something has recently died inside one of the lower chambers, and three different kinds of small animal are already taking it apart.

Every bridge is occupied in comparable layers. So is every arch, ledge, pore, and empty shell. Glassy jellies no larger than pinheads turn in the central current. Nearly invisible larvae gather around them, then scatter without apparent cause. Crustaceans molt, and before the discarded covering has settled, something else has entered it. Even the seemingly clear water contains traffic.

There is almost nothing that could be called vegetation. A few colorless, hair-fine filaments root in the fresh mineral seams, closer to bacteria than to plants. Brief olive films appear where organic matter settles, but grazers crop them almost as soon as they spread. There are no fronds, no soft canopy, no green shelter. The dominant materials are shell, clear mineral, black rock, translucent flesh, and the smoky water between them. The strongest color belongs to certain predators: a muted rust-red visible only when they pass close to one of the glowing bacterial seams.

Sound does not become an event here. The water is too small a world and too closely packed with soft bodies; vibrations die almost at their source. Rock shifts, and the inhabitants know it as pressure. A shell gives way, and they know it by the sudden cloud of fragments. Feeding, mating, collapse, and pursuit all occur without report. The result is not quiet, but silence.

This does not make the place peaceful.

When a rust-red flat hunter slides from beneath a bridge, the crustaceans nearest it press themselves against the wall. Their stillness spreads outward as each animal responds to its neighbor. A whole avenue becomes motionless before the hunter has crossed half of it. No alarm travels. The hunter takes one body, folds itself around the absence, and withdraws. Gradually the avenue resumes.

The hollow has weather, though no sky. A rich inward tide brings oxygen, minute food, and sometimes larvae from the open sea. Then the feeding crowns open everywhere, and the white arcades seem to flower. A strong current pins jellies against bridges and fills the lower channels with drifting fragments. During lean pulses, the water clears, growth slows, and the bacterial lights spread through neglected chambers. On the coldest pulses, delicate scales of ice form around the throat. Animals retract; whole districts become dormant. When the brine returns, the ice vanishes without dripping—there is nowhere for a drop to fall.

Over longer intervals, the rock itself continues to change. A new flake splits away and crushes a quarter of the city. Before its edges have dulled, survivors are growing across it. Elsewhere the builders thicken a bridge until it becomes a wall, and mineral gathers on the wall until it begins to resemble stone. It becomes difficult to say where construction ends and geology begins. The cave and the city are forming together, and may eventually close the hollow between them.

One day the capillary throat may seal. Or the original fracture may widen, admitting the open sea and dissolving the small differences that made this a world of its own. Nothing in the architecture anticipates either possibility. Its order is entirely in the present tense: feed where the current allows, build where the water is calm, occupy whatever has been left empty.

At the next inward pulse, thousands of pale crowns unfold. Eggs turn slowly in the central plaza. The old districts glimmer as decomposers gather in their hidden rooms, and beneath a bridge the red hunter begins to move again.

The unfinished hollow takes another silent breath.
