## The Unfinished Height

The mountains here are not old. They are not even, quite, formed. They are happening.

Across a province hundreds of kilometres wide, the crust stands in enormous unfinished gestures: a plain lifted on one side until it becomes a rampart; a fault scarp whose upper edge is still splitting; a ring of new peaks around a basin that did not exist the day before. Yet at any given moment, almost all of the region is motionless. The violence is scattered. One ridge may be rising along the horizon while, a day’s journey behind it, a glass plain lies without so much as a tremor. Somewhere, always, another front is active. The turbulence is in the geography, not the weather.

Height repeats itself. There is no single summit from which the country can be understood, only high country mounted upon higher country: obsidian steppes ending in cliffs several kilometres tall, fault terraces stacked beyond the reach of the weak light, and narrow uplifts rising from basins whose floors would elsewhere be considered plateaus. Some clefts have no visible bottom. A stone released into one simply becomes irrelevant. There are no snowlines, tree lines, or cloud bases to give the altitude familiar gradations. Height is measured by thinner air, longer falls, and the amount of sky a ridge can take away.

It is a darkness with very little in it. There is no moon, and the stars are too distant to do more than distinguish sky from stone. Fresh volcanic glass occasionally returns a pinpoint of reflection; matte rock disappears entirely. The eye learns to read silhouettes, interruptions, the faint gloss of one black surface against another. Active fissures seldom glow. Now and then a deeper seam opens briefly, showing a dull red line beneath the crust, but most of the interior’s energy arrives as heat rather than light.

That heat is everywhere. The exposed stone feels like a wall that has stood all afternoon in sunlight, though no sunlight touches it. Even the highest shelves are warm near the rock. At active fractures the air becomes distinctly hot and carries a faint mineral sharpness, but away from them the warmth is steady rather than fierce. It comes up through the soles, radiates from cliff faces, and keeps the darkness from feeling cold.

The dryness is more complete than the warmth. There is no water to freeze in the shadows, condense in the clefts, or hide within pores. No damp survives at depth. There are no clouds of vapour, no hydrated crusts, no brines seeping through fractures. What little dust exists is fresh fracture-flour: angular, pale, and unwilling to cling. It gathers in shallow pockets but never into dunes. Chemistry here proceeds through dry melts, sublimation, oxidation, and direct deposition from mineral gas. Nothing is carried in solution. Nothing has ever been rounded by a stream.

Different districts express the same rawness in different ways. On the glass steppes, broad blisters rise almost imperceptibly until their surfaces fail along polygonal seams, leaving tilted plates the size of city blocks. Elsewhere the land is all verticality: splintered ribs, blade-like ridges, and towers with unweathered edges. Farther on, huge domes have lifted and emptied themselves, becoming hollow crowns of concentric cliffs. Between these features are long intervals of nearly nothing—flat dark expanses, a single vent, an isolated crystal patch, perhaps one unexplained arrangement of stones.

An uplift begins without spectacle. A warm line opens across a plain. The ground on one side of it becomes fractionally higher than the ground on the other. Stars disappear behind a rim that had not been there an hour before. Plates buckle; buried rock emerges with its fracture faces still clean. A ridge may gain metres in an hour, then stop for days while another part of the province tears itself open. Collapse is equally abrupt. A plateau founders in concentric slices, or the side of a high dome simply ceases to be supported and drops into the dark. The debris remains blocky and sharp. Before it can accumulate into an ordinary slope, it is buried, lifted again, or incorporated into the next fracture.

For all this activity, the dominant condition is silence. The thin air carries sound poorly, and the largest changes often occur too far away to be heard at all. Close to an active front there may be a sparse ticking, like isolated stones responding to some immense, slow pressure. Occasionally a concussion arrives so low that it is felt before it becomes audible. Warm gas escaping through a narrow vent makes a threadlike hiss. After a collapse, a few fragments strike once or twice and then lie still. There is no continuous wind-roar, no foliage to whisper, no animal call, no running water. Silence returns while the event itself is still continuing.

The only things that grow are mineral.

Near scattered warm vents, dry vapours condense into needles, plates, and branching filaments. Some patches resemble tiny fields of ferns or white coral until examined closely. Their resemblance to life ends at their shape. As the temperature and composition of the gas change, one crystal form is coated by another: pale sulphur giving way to dark silica, then to a sparse metallic bloom. It looks almost like ecological succession, but nothing metabolizes, competes, reproduces, or adapts. When the vent shifts, the whole growth cracks, sublimes, or is carried away with the rising ground.

There are no plants in the sheltered fractures, no fungal films behind the glass plates, no microbial mats around the heat. No spores travel on the thin wind. There are no tracks, burrows, nests, or bones. The region is not merely uninhabited; it has no ecology to inhabit it. Its warmth does not make it hospitable. It only animates the chemistry.

This makes the rare built traces difficult to assimilate.

On one high terrace, five flat slabs have been stacked largest to smallest. Elsewhere, a knee-high arc of unmortared wall shelters nothing and faces no approach. Far away, two upright blocks support a third, forming a doorway with the same empty slope on both sides. A few other marks are even less definite: three stones placed as steps against a fracture, a straight line of small blocks interrupting an otherwise random debris field, a shallow rectangle visible only where new glass has not quite covered it.

They are separated by enormous distances and never accumulate into a settlement. There are no roads between them, no fields, no tools, no vessels, no metal, no mechanisms, no inscriptions. Their only sophistication is placement. If makers existed, nothing of their bodies or lives remains to explain them. The structures do not make the province feel formerly inhabited so much as briefly visited, at intervals that cannot be reconciled with the age of the ground.

Some of the marks are already being undone. A wall leans because the terrace beneath it is rising unevenly. A cairn stands on rock younger than the stones stacked above it. A doorway is found split cleanly in half, with one upright carried several metres higher than the other. Elsewhere, equally fragile arrangements survive simply because the turbulence has jumped over them. Survival here indicates luck, not permanence.

The laws themselves are ordinary; the rates are not. Stone still fractures according to stress. Heat rises. Falling objects fall. Nothing moves in defiance of gravity or cause. What is strange is that processes elsewhere distributed across geological ages have been compressed into hours and nights, with long stillnesses between them. Creation and destruction do not follow a tidy cycle. They overlap, interrupt one another, and leave almost no aftermath. Erosion has not yet had time to become a landscape process.

Consequently, a map can only be a record of an instant. A pass may become a pinnacle. A basin may acquire a rim. A cliff used for orientation may double in height without changing its outline enough to warn anyone below. Age cannot be read from smoothness, because nothing is smooth from age. Even the oldest exposed surface may look freshly broken.

From a high ledge, the openness is mostly inferred: black planes, blacker interruptions among the stars, distances too great for the weak light to measure. The stone remains warm. Somewhere far off, a single fragment clicks into stillness. Then, without any accompanying sound, several stars go out in a row as a new ridge lifts across them.

Nothing roots the debris. Nothing will carry the event forward as memory. The dark remains almost undisturbed, and the mountains move another small distance toward a form they will not keep.
