## The Cold Manifold

The Cold Manifold begins above the weather of the lowlands, and then makes weather of its own.

It is a table of uplifted crust, nearly continental in extent, bounded by escarpments that descend through successive decks of cloud. Nothing about it has had time to soften. Its valleys are tension fractures; its passes are places where one vast plate has shifted against another. Cliffs break in glassy, conchoidal steps. Ridges terminate in blades. There are talus fields composed entirely of sharp fragments, without a rounded stone among them, and no river has yet cut a coherent course across the interior.

The exposed rock is green-black, dense, and threaded with nickel, mica, and clear veins of mineral salt. Fresh faces glitter unbearably. Older faces—old here meaning only a few thousand years—are darkened by life.

At this height, the cold is not a season but a condition of matter. Unprotected blood would thicken, then cease. Bare water has three possible futures: to freeze, to vanish into the dry air, or to be taken immediately into something living. Yet the Manifold is not buried beneath ice. Its whiteness comes mostly from light.

The light is fierce, nearly sourceless on days when high ice haze spreads the sun across the whole sky. It strikes the metallic rock, returns from the cloud decks below, and enters again from every side. Shadows are sparse, hard-edged, and intensely blue. At brightest noon, ice does not soften; it merely becomes more difficult to look at. The thin air holds almost none of the energy pouring through it.

Storms form over the interior basins and sometimes remain there for decades, turning with almost geological patience. Cold air accumulates against the rim until it spills over in transparent cataracts, disturbing clouds far below. A single bank of fog can take years to cross a province. Distance on the Manifold is often measured less by changes in terrain than by changes in the pitch of its wind.

For all this, the country is damp.

Not wet: damp in the way cold machinery is damp, around seals, beneath covers, wherever warm chemistry meets cold air. Moisture condenses in fracture shadows and along the breathing surfaces of animals. Pressure occasionally forces salt brine from the mineral veins, leaving short black seams that remain liquid long after fresh water would have frozen. At long intervals, lowland air is driven up the escarpments as fog. It seldom falls as rain. Instead, the vegetation combs it directly from the wind.

Water does not so much run here as get handed on. A droplet may be condensed on a leaf, drawn into a root, released into an animal’s blood, exhaled onto a shell, drunk by something smaller, and carried for a century before it touches stone again.

Where that moisture gathers, the vegetation becomes lush. On exposed rims it forms a tight, springy pelt of overlapping leaves, almost black from the pigments needed to use the excessive light. In sheltered fracture basins it rises into groves of cableleaf: ribbed trunks sheathed in natural insulation, crowned by long, dark fronds that tremble only when the electrical tension in them changes. Clear bladders at their bases hold brine. Their tissues are cold to the touch, but not quite as cold as the air.

The vegetation does not give an impression of abundance by growing quickly. It gives it by refusing to leave. A cushion no wider than a hand may have been adding cells for centuries. Roots enter the rock directly, dissolving metals and laying them down again as conductive filaments. These filaments join root to root and grove to grove. Across a basin, they form slow electrochemical networks in which light is stored, charge is shifted, and mineral nutrients are prepared in forms animal guts can use.

This is where the Manifold begins to seem industrial.

Nothing in it has been built. There are no wires except roots, no furnaces except stomachs, no engines except bodies that must continue functioning in order to remain bodies. Still, the place mines, refines, insulates, pressurizes, transmits, and manufactures. Its industry is inherited rather than designed.

The most conspicuous workers are the mills.

They are low, broad grazers, some no larger than a boulder and others large enough that weather condenses differently on their backs. Flexible plates overlap along their bodies. Their limbs advance by ratchet: tendons tighten gradually, a joint locks, and then one heavy segment moves forward with a ceramic knock. Within each mouth, opposed rings of teeth revolve with mineral slowness, milling cableleaf and rock dust together.

They are not machines. Their plates grow. Their valves scar and heal. Their young are born soft, and for the first part of their lives must remain against the warm ventral surfaces of older animals.

Inside a mill, digestion proceeds through a sequence of living chambers. Fermentation produces gas; gas drives circulation; symbiotic tissues concentrate heat behind layers of insulating fat and shell. Metal separated from the mineral fodder is incorporated into new teeth and plating. Excess heat escapes through dorsal vents in long, restrained exhalations. In the cold air, each exhalation becomes a brief white plume, then settles as condensation along the grooves of the animal’s back.

Those grooves are crowded.

So is everything else. Translucent grazers slide through the condensed brine. Flat, many-legged predators wait beneath the warm edges of plates. Winged cleaners open and close their iridescent covers in the vent heat. On a single old mill, there may be pasture, drinking water, hunting grounds, mating swarms, and several distinct climates. The animal carries them through the larger weather without haste.

The smaller fauna are beyond enumeration. They occupy the leaf pelt so densely that much of what appears to be vegetation is actually movement. They cloud above warm vents. They hang in the sunshine as glittering sheets, using minute electrical charges along their wings to hold themselves in the thin air. Individually they are nearly silent; together they make a high, dry sizzling, like rain heard through a wall.

Above them move the sailers, vast filtering animals whose stretched membranes resemble weather damage in the sky. They adjust their surfaces by fractions and can remain almost stationary for years. Their shadows slide over the herds so gradually that they seem less like shadows than changes in the color of the rock.

A single mill is quiet for its size: one knock, a long scrape of teeth, an occasional hiss. A basin containing a million mills is not quiet. Their separate slownesses overlap into a continuous industrial drone. Beneath it runs the basal ticking of cooling stone, the subsonic pulse of charge in the roots, the grinding of plates, the venting of pressure, and the brittle reports of new fractures opening somewhere along the rim.

The thin air is a poor medium for so much noise, so the plateau conducts much of it directly. Sound arrives through stone and bone before it arrives through the ears. During the coldest contractions, whole escarpments ring. A particular cliff may fail only once in ten thousand years, but there are always cliffs enough that somewhere, at every moment, one is breaking.

Nothing local is in a hurry. A stride may occupy an afternoon. A herd may require centuries to cross an interior basin. Cableleaf groves store energy through long bright cycles and flower over periods that outlast most of the animals pollinating them. Yet to wait beside one creature is to see almost nothing happen; to listen to an entire basin is to be surrounded by happening without pause.

Occasionally, a basin comes into phase.

Enough mills stand upon the same root network to complete its larger circuits. Charge begins to pass through their legs and ventral plates, down into the conductive mat. Current moves through the groves for years. Root chemistry accelerates; sap warms slightly; condensation gathers in unusual abundance. The cableleaf flowers, and the flying fauna emerge in numbers that turn the air granular.

From a distance, the event looks like a dark bloom spreading patiently beneath the glare. From within, it is a prolonged thunder of wings, feeding, discharge, and reproduction. No animal intends the alignment. The system has no visible governor. It is simply that survival here is so difficult alone, and so much easier when bodies happen to close the necessary connections.

Vacancy is therefore the rarest substance on the Manifold. Every surface has a tenant, including the tenants. Even apparently bare rock carries transparent films of dormant eggs, spores, and salt-holding cells. When a large animal dies, it does not open an empty space. It becomes a crowded province, then a wet one, then a dark patch of unusually vigorous vegetation.

Seen from far enough away, the Cold Manifold could be mistaken for an untouched wilderness: white height, black groves, slow herds, no road-cut, wall, chimney, tool, or ruin. It is untouched, if that means nothing here was ever assembled by hands.

But nothing is unworked.

Roots mine the young stone. Leaves refine the merciless light. Herds turn charge into pressure, pressure into motion, motion into heat, and heat into just enough water to begin the process again. The country’s only large export is the cold air spilling from its rim. Its principal product is more Manifold.

Across the bright interior, the mills take another step. Somewhere along a thousand-mile fracture, fresh rock snaps. The sound travels through the roots, the herds, and the crowded ground, joining the low patient roar by which the whole continent continues, almost imperceptibly, to make itself.
