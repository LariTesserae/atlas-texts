## Sere, the Condensing Town

From above, Sere is easy to miss. The surface is a young sheet of basalt, broken into clinker and narrow fissures, its edges still sharp enough to cut leather. Rain comes seldom and leaves almost no channels; most of it vanishes into fractures before it can gather. The only signs of habitation are a cable disappearing into the ground, a line of ceramic vent-caps, and warm air lifting faintly from a seam in the rock.

An iron stair descends thirty-four metres through that seam. With each turn, the air grows hotter and the smell changes: first sun-baked stone, then wet iron, then something green and faintly sweet. The sound of the surface wind disappears. Below it there is another sound, so low and steady that at first it seems to come from inside the body.

The stair opens into a town compressed within a single inclined chamber. A person could walk its length in ten minutes if there were a straight route. There is not. Sere has no avenue, no central square, and almost no unassigned space. Houses stand four and five tiers deep against the basalt. Machine rooms occupy the gaps between houses; stairs wrap around pressure vessels; balconies support gardens whose roots descend past the windows below. Pipes pass through kitchens and sleeping alcoves, lagged in woven fibre where a sleeper might roll against them. From certain landings, the town looks less like a settlement in a cavity than like one enormous building whose rooms happen to have different addresses.

Its materials are dark and closely fitted: cast-basalt block, slag brick, glazed clay, black iron, and copper gone brown with heat. The oldest construction lies low, where former streets have become service passages beneath later floors. Newer rooms have been inserted above and between them rather than built outward. Nothing is demolished if it can be made to carry something else. Retired flywheel pits hold cisterns. A disused kiln is now the foundation of three dwellings. Condenser plates from an earlier power house serve as roofing, their old fins still collecting dew.

The geological shell is much younger than it appears beside all this layering. Its fractures have not been rounded by rivers or softened by soil. Glassy facets shine where sheets of basalt have recently spalled away. In places the town’s masonry ends against rock so fresh that it seems to have broken that morning, although the break may be older than anyone living. Water has begun only the finest work here: white mineral threads, shallow runnels, a satin patch behind a leaking joint. Roots do more damage than rain.

Heat enters at the lower end, through a deep seam sealed behind ceramic grilles. There the air rises well above blood heat, and bare metal cannot be touched. Sere’s engines use the difference between that seam and a small bore of comparatively cold mineral water. Steam circulates in a closed system; pumps, generators, wire-presses, and kilns draw from it in turn. There is no coal smoke and little visible steam. The industrial heart of the town is almost unnaturally clean, its moving parts enclosed in oil baths and heavy cast housings.

The largest flywheels turn so slowly that their motion is easiest to see in the painted marks on their rims. Power travels through shafts and insulated cables rather than through open belts. Even the presses are geared down to long, deliberate strokes. Residents cook against heat-exchange manifolds, dry fibre in wall niches, and conduct the hottest work in short shifts. Loose clothing and unhurried movement are practical necessities. In the dry engine courts, sweat vanishes almost as soon as it appears; near the planted walls, it remains on the skin.

Water exists in Sere less as weather than as a slow transaction between surfaces.

There is no stream. Open pools are few, small, and covered against evaporation. Most moisture is borrowed repeatedly: water from the bore, vapour from engines and cooking, human breath, the transpiration of leaves. At the chamber’s upper end, cool air descends through the vent-shafts and meets pipes chilled by the returning bore water. Condensation gathers on them in clear beads. It also gathers on roof tiles, the undersides of balconies, and certain exposed bands of basalt. A wall may remain dry for days, then begin to shine. An alley that is dusty at one hour can be damp underfoot by the next, without anything that could properly be called rain having fallen.

Occasionally, after a storm on the surface, water finds a more direct route. Days later it emerges from one fissure above the eastern galleries and falls for several minutes into a single lane. The residents know which roofs and gutters will answer first. The event is too unreliable to be a water supply, but it is still recorded.

Where condensation is dependable, Sere becomes densely green. Ferns with fronds nearly black from absorbing the dim light grow in beds of crushed slag. Moss fills the narrow space between pipes and walls. Vines with thick, waxy leaves climb the condensers, their roots held in fungal mats rather than true soil. Broad leaves overlap so completely in places that they form a second, living wall outside the masonry. Some gardens hang upside down from balcony troughs, their trailing growth drawing moisture from the warm air below.

This lushness is not evenly spread. It stops abruptly near the engine courts, leaving a bare margin of heat-blued metal and powdery mineral scale. Elsewhere it follows the plumbing in long, narrow bands: a stripe of fern along a cold main, a dripping curtain of roots beneath a bridge, a column of moss ascending toward a vent. Fallen vegetation is returned quickly to the beds. With so few animals available to break it down, the fungal layer and the gardeners perform that work together.

Light is treated almost as carefully as water. Low electric lamps burn at crossings and beside machinery, usually behind amber or milk-coloured glass. Their illumination is supplemented by pale glazed tiles that carry it a little farther into the packed lanes. Most interiors remain in a warm twilight. Wet leaves catch isolated points of brightness; the depths between balconies stay dark.

Once each day, when the surface sun stands correctly, a narrow blade of direct light enters through the upper fissure. It touches the chamber for less than an hour, moving across a prepared sequence of white tiles. This is the noon line. By long-standing building law, nothing may permanently obstruct it. Maintaining that prohibition has required open wells, staggered floors, and one astonishingly narrow seam of unused air through the densest part of town. It is too discontinuous to serve as a street. Its value is that it remains empty.

Sere is correspondingly quiet. The foliage, close walls, and layered construction swallow echoes. Engines are heard chiefly as a low vibration in handrails and floor plates. Individual sounds emerge from that background and then vanish: a drop striking a ceramic cup, a soft pneumatic closure, sandals on a stair, the single click of a cable pawl as the surface lift begins its ascent. Voices are brief and rarely raised. There is no dawn chorus, no calling of animals through the lanes, and no continual rush of water.

Animals are scarce enough to be noticed individually. Small translucent snails live in some of the wetter fern beds, but not in others that appear identical. A species of pallid moth occasionally appears around the food-vine flowers, having travelled through routes no one has fully traced. Most pollination is done by hand or by the slow air circulation from the vents. There are no birds, rats, or established colonies of bats. A scratch in a wall or a movement beneath a leaf is therefore an event, not background.

The town’s strangest feature is also one of its slowest. On the oldest cold surfaces, condensed water does not always follow the visible slope. A bead may creep sideways, or climb a finger’s width into a hairline seam, before finally falling. The deviation is slight, consistent, and always toward the deep heat. It is too weak to draw a stream or lift water through a pipe, but over hours it can make a drop travel a handspan in the wrong direction.

The engineers call this tendency *the draw* and disagree about whether it belongs to the rock, the heat, or the water. Sere’s gutters are pitched to accommodate it, which is why some appear badly built to visitors. Suspended roots gradually curve along the same damp paths. Mineral deposits record older routes in fine diagonal fans. The effect has never been strong enough to power anything, only strong enough that no careful builder ignores it.

Change in Sere is measured in similarly patient units: the thickness of scale inside a pipe, the migration of a condensation line over several decades, the width of a root entering a fracture, the number of new rooms that can be fitted around an old machine without making it inaccessible. Fresh basalt is exposed occasionally as the heated shell adjusts, but there is no rapid crumbling and no dramatic seasonal flood. The town becomes denser rather than larger. Its gardens shift by degrees. Its oldest machinery disappears into foundations while remaining part of the working whole.

Every new room must preserve access to the pipes around it and return what moisture it can. No one may seal a wall merely because it weeps. A damp patch is inspected before it is repaired; a dry patch where dampness is expected causes more concern. These rules give the settlement its peculiar intimacy. Maintaining a public water surface may require entering someone’s home. A private balcony may also be part of the town’s cooling system.

Seen from the lowest gallery, Sere eventually stops resembling a cavern that has been built over. It seems instead to have developed a single continuous anatomy: basalt for its shell, engines for circulation, pipes and roots sharing the same narrow passages, leaves multiplying the area on which air and water can meet. The inhabitants provide adjustment, memory, and patience.

On one cold pipe, a bead may take most of an afternoon to form. It swells, creeps slightly toward the heat, and detaches onto a black leaf below. The leaf bends almost imperceptibly. Somewhere behind the wall, a painted mark on a flywheel advances. The noon line crosses another tile. Nothing in the town is still, but almost nothing can be watched changing.
