## The Unfinished Hollow

Far below a slope of newly fractured stone, where the breaks are still glassy and rain has not yet darkened them, a root swells around something that should not be there.

From outside, the swelling could be covered by two hands. It has no mouth, no scenic opening, no approach. A pore among the root hairs occasionally exhales warm, damp air; sometimes it takes that air back with an audible sip. The surrounding rock is so young that its splinters have not had time to become gravel. There is no soil here worth the name.

Inside the swelling is a chamber.

When it is quiet, the chamber might be twelve paces across. Its walls are close enough to show their finest veins, yet the space between them feels untenanted on a much larger scale. Almost everything is at the edges. The center is clear air: no trunks, no hanging curtains, no islands of debris, no pool. A seed falling through it would have time to become conspicuous.

Every visible surface is alive.

The floor is a pale, dense root-skin, faintly ridged and slightly yielding, with the dry smoothness of a fresh nut shell. Along the walls, root-wood gives way to braided green stems and then to broad, paper-thin leaves laid over one another without quite touching. The vault is younger tissue: translucent yellow-green, tense as a blister, with ivory growth tips pressing through it. At a few seams the plant has not yet closed over the stone beyond. Those glimpses of mineral are angular, lightless, and irrelevant. Stone is only the outside of this place. It is not part of the furnishing.

There is no leaf litter. A damaged leaf does not fall and rot; it slackens, turns colorless, and is drawn back into the nearest stem over the course of a day. Even the fine hairs shed by the roots are retrieved. The hollow smells of crushed green stems, warm sap, and the faint metallic cleanliness of new fractures. It is an ecosystem with almost no refuse and, at first glance, only one species. More precisely, it is one plant performing many kinds of growth: bark, root, canopy, storage bladder, flower, trap, and wall. Whether these are separate individuals joined below the stone is a distinction the plant does not consistently observe.

The air is warm, about the temperature of a sun-heated wall, but seldom steamy. For long intervals it is merely soft and slightly moist at the back of the throat. Condensation gathers at scattered seams, each bead swelling until its weight pulls the surrounding tissue down. Then it falls. Most strikes are small. A few land with the hard, round report of a stone dropped into a wooden vessel.

At irregular intervals, the whole vault rains.

This is not seepage from above. Thousands of tiny openings tighten and release together, giving off a brief, driving shower that lasts seconds or minutes. The dry root-skin darkens in sharp patches. Water runs through grooves so new that they have no rounded corners, then disappears into the floor. Rarely, a thin film remains on one leaf for several hours, reflecting the chamber without rippling.

The rain is tremendously loud. So is the plant’s growth.

A fresh root tip forcing its way through old tissue sounds like a green branch being snapped beside the ear. Larger shifts produce deep wooden booms that seem to come from beyond the walls. Unfurling leaves make a sustained crackling, and the seed-valves knock as their internal pressure changes. During a shower, these sounds combine into a roar that visibly shakes fine moisture from the leaves. Yet the chamber contains so little that there is nothing to absorb the noise. Its emptiness is resonant.

Sometimes an echo arrives first.

A faint boom sounds in the middle distance. The plant pauses—or appears to pause—and then a seam splits with precisely that boom. Drops are occasionally heard striking the floor while they are still visibly falling from the vault. These are not separate events repeated. Only one amount of water is recovered afterward, and only one new fracture can be found.

The youngness of the hollow is not only geological. Relations that older places take for granted—inside and outside, size and distance, before and after—have not entirely set.

Sound seems to help decide how far apart things are. In the dry ticking between rains, the chamber is intimate. During the loudest growth, the walls remain visible but become difficult for anything falling or growing to cross. A root hair extending into the center may hang there for days, its tip no farther away, while its base thickens normally. If it finally reaches the opposite side, it often emerges from the wall behind its own starting point. The plant has learned not to fill the middle. Its tendrils stop at an invisible margin, or turn sharply along it.

Daylight, by contrast, is almost insultingly ordinary.

It is not the blue glow of cave life or the dim green murk beneath a canopy. For much of the day, normal sunlight falls through the chamber in clear, warm patches. It is bright enough to show dust-sized imperfections in new leaves and to make the older floor look cream-colored rather than white. The patches move. Cloud cover somewhere above softens them. At dusk they redden and fade, although there is no shaft through the rock and no opening through which the actual sun could be seen.

The shadows are less dependable than the light. A shadow may have the outline of a leaf that will not unfold until the following rain. Others lag behind their leaves, stretching after the light has gone. Occasionally the entire vault carries the shadow of a branching structure far larger than the plant that contains it. When this happens, growth accelerates along the shadow’s finer divisions, as though the plant were using an absence as a template.

Water, light, sound, and growth keep the place in continual alteration without making it cluttered. New hollows open at the margins: small, sharp-sided pockets, perfectly empty, their green surfaces still glossy. Older pockets narrow and vanish. A section of floor may rise into a wall while remaining underfoot in the same orientation. After especially hard rain, the chamber sometimes spends an hour behaving like the inside of a seed—close, curved, and full of pressure—before becoming cavernous again. Nothing is demolished in these changes. There is no rubble, only tissue reassigned and space reconsidered.

Animals are so rare that their absence is part of the ecology.

The usual visitor, when there is one, is a translucent moth no larger than a nail clipping. It drinks from sweet pores along the walls and lays nothing, builds nothing. Certain leaves respond to its wingbeat rather than its touch. They fold with a abrupt clap, and if they catch it, their fluid is returned directly to the plant. Months may pass between such events.

More rarely, a slender, mouse-sized animal with grasping forefeet squeezes through the root pore. It comes for the hard green seed-valves. Once or twice it has been observed holding a fallen thorn in its mouth and using the point to lever a valve open. The thorn is not shaped, carried away, or kept. Afterward it is slowly enclosed and digested like any other dead tissue. This brief, unshaped use is the whole of technology here. There are no nests, caches, paths, marks of habitation, or enduring artifacts. Nothing in the hollow has been built.

Even the plant’s apparent dominance is not quite settled. The chamber is not simply a cavity into which vegetation has grown. Increasingly, it seems to be something the vegetation is cultivating: an organ made of emptiness. The living shell feeds it, the rain flexes it, and sound stretches it. Growth at the edges produces not more tissue but more room, always clean-edged and unfurnished.

What that room is for remains undecided. On bright days it gathers and releases light like a leaf. Under pressure it contracts like a seed. In the loud rain it is only a young cave beneath a hill, dripping and cracking in the dark rock—except that the daylight continues to move across its walls, and another precise reach of nothing opens where, a moment before, there had been no space at all.
