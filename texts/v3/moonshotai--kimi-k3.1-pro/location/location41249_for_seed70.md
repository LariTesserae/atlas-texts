## The Upper Flood

The high country is full of water.

Not a lake cupped within it, and not quite an ocean resting upon it. The water occupies the altitude itself. It stands among a young range of mountains, fills their fractured valleys, submerges their passes, and continues above all but a few of the highest summits. From a distance, the region resembles a mass of clear weather that has settled too heavily over the peaks: a silver upper plain, a haze of warm rain, and green-black depths where the ridgelines ought to be.

There is no coast in the ordinary sense. At the margins of the flood, the water becomes rain before the rain becomes air. First comes a fine suspension of droplets, then isolated warm beads hanging among wet pinnacles, then only humid wind and bare stone. Most of the droplets eventually drift back. A few escape into the high valleys, but so slowly that the level of the whole body scarcely changes across a century. Water here is reluctant to separate from water. It will eventually yield to slope and gravity, but it has not yet finished doing so.

Even at this height, nothing is cold. Heat enters through the young crust below, not in violent vents but through broad, patient warming. The water is clearest over the open saddles and faintly mineral in the fault basins. Its currents are too weak to feel as movement; they can be read only in the consistent lean of old plants, or in the position of a veil of sediment that has taken decades to cross a submerged pass. Above, the air is warm and clean, with clouds resting close to the rain-boundary. The few summits that penetrate it are never entirely dry.

Beneath the water, the land is still close to the event of its making. Ridges end in splintered crests. Fault faces descend in abrupt, unweathered steps. Sheltered fractures hold seams of dark volcanic glass, and the scree beneath the cliffs remains angular, with almost no rounded gravel. Pale silica and rust-colored mineral films mark the paths of warm seepage. Some slopes look orderly enough to have been terraced, but the regularity comes from repeated fracture and from generations of plants trapping sediment on the same narrow ledges. Every straight edge is geological; every arch is stone or living growth.

The light is not strange. At noon it is ordinary white sunlight, passing through clear water and quiet rain. In the afternoon it warms toward honey and copper. Nothing glows to compensate for darkness, and at night the depths are simply dark. By day, the highest submerged ridges appear almost uncovered, their colors sharp beneath only a little water. Farther down, successive ridges fade through jade and blue-green until distance, rather than depth, seems to erase them. Above the tallest plant canopies are long open volumes of illuminated water, wide enough to give the whole region an airy spaciousness.

Below those openings, space becomes crowded.

The lit summits carry dense turfs of small, thick leaves, jade on their upper surfaces and bronze beneath. Their roots enter hairline fractures and bind the angular scree without softening it. Down the slopes, the turf gives way to forests of flexible stalks crowned with fronds divided so finely that each leaf seems mostly water and green edge. The crowns overlap, but not in a single roof. They occur in successive storeys, each taking a portion of the light and leaving enough for the next. In the middle layers, open water becomes scarce: fronds cross fronds, fine root-threads bridge the gaps, and old translucent growth hangs beneath the new.

Other plants never reach stone at all. They begin in the mesh of established vegetation and live as broad, rootless veils, catching mineral dust, pollen, and fragments of dead leaf. Some of these veils are delicate enough to tremble when the whole flood imperceptibly shifts. Others have accumulated so much material that they have become suspended soil, dark and spongy, supporting gardens above gardens. There are places where no single organism appears to hold the mass together. The layer supports itself by being intertwined.

Death does not quickly remove anything. A fallen frond may sink so slowly that it is colonized before it reaches the bottom. It becomes first a host, then a sediment-trapping shelf, then part of a new ledge. Older stalks gradually acquire clear rinds of silica, so that supple green growth stands beside brittle, transparent remnants of earlier forest. When two of those remnants touch, they make a tiny mineral tick. Deep basins contain seams of compressed vegetation between layers of young stone: the beginning of a terrain made as much by accumulated leaves as by uplift.

Animals are rare enough that entire forests can appear uninhabited. The most frequently encountered grazer is still uncommon: a flat, translucent creature that clings beneath old leaves and scrapes away their mineral film, moving so slowly that its path is easier to notice than its motion. At much longer intervals, a solitary ribbon-finned swimmer crosses the open water above the canopy. It is pale, silent, and gone without having disturbed more than a few fronds. There are no schools flashing through the passes, no animal choruses, no busy reef of competing mouths. Leaves persist with few bite marks, and the density of the place comes from patient succession rather than consumption.

Sound has long empty intervals. The rain at the upper boundary produces a faint hiss without a locatable source. Mineral-sheathed stems tick against one another. Occasionally a fracture deep in the range releases a low note that travels through the warm water and seems to come from every direction at once. Then the quiet closes again. There is no surf, no shoreline drag, no continuous rush of current.

The whole region changes by accumulation. A mineral film becomes a crust; a crust gives purchase to a root; roots catch dust; dust becomes a ledge; the ledge shades whatever grew beneath it. A plant layer may take centuries to become too heavy for its supports, and the gap of light left when it settles may take centuries more to close. The flood itself has no quick tides. It rises and subsides almost imperceptibly as its immense volume warms, cools, loses rain, and receives seepage from below.

This is not an ancient drowned world. It is a young world temporarily wearing an ocean. In time, new fractures will give the water a route downward. The level will fall, the warm forests will be stranded at altitudes they cannot survive, and their silica-sheathed stems will remain as dark seams and delicate impressions on dry stone.

That time is not close. For now, the summits are gardens, the passes hold green sunlight, and the high mountains lie beneath warm water and a rain so slow that the entire submerged forest seems to be growing inside a single, unfinished thought.
