## The Plateau of Quiet Engines

High above the cloud country lies a plateau with no true summit. It is simply the cold upper surface of a continent, so broad that weather is born upon it, travels for weeks, and weakens before reaching an edge. From any one place it appears almost flat. Only after long distances does the flatness resolve into immense swells of land, each days across, separated by valleys so shallow that their courses are visible mainly as changes in the color of the stone.

Height has not made the land sharp. It has been elevated for so long that its mountains have become rounded backs and its fractures have softened into seams. The bedrock is dark and fine-grained, crossed by pale quartz veins that sometimes resemble cables laid just beneath the surface. They are not. Nothing here has been laid, built, or repaired. Frost works loose a grain; wind carries it into a hollow; ice presses it down again. Over ages, erosion and deposition have come nearly into agreement. Boulders rest in sockets worn to fit them. Even the gravel is mostly rounded, as though the plateau has had time to reconsider every edge.

The light is dim without quite being darkness. For much of the year the sun remains behind a distant wall of cloud, and noon is only a brighter shade of pewter. Shadows have no firm boundaries. Wet stone appears almost black; dry stone takes on the color of old iron. When the sky opens, the stars may be visible beside a weak, low sun, and the land seems less illuminated than merely disclosed.

There is too little water for deep snow. Most of the surface is bare, yet it is seldom perfectly dry. At night, when a shallow tongue of cloud settles against the ground, condensation gathers on the cold rock. It beads in mineral pores, darkens narrow bands of gravel, and freezes before morning into crystals too small to whiten the landscape. In certain hollows, salt-heavy brine persists beneath a skin of clear ice. Occasionally, warmer air arrives from far below and releases a supercooled drizzle. The droplets freeze as they touch down, so the rain has no patter. A slope simply becomes darker and slightly reflective.

The plateau makes its own climate from these small exchanges. Dense cold air slides outward from the interior in slow, invisible rivers. Cloud banks climb the far rim and dissolve into mist. A fog may fill a basin the size of a sea and take a season to drain away. Beyond the next swell, the air may remain untouched and still.

Plants are scarce enough to be overlooked even when they are underfoot. There are black-green lichens in sheltered grain boundaries, olive crusts beneath overhangs, and occasional cushions of reddish growth no larger than a fingernail. Nothing stands high enough to sway. Nothing rustles. Most of the food web is not green at all: in damp pores within the rock, microbial films draw energy from iron, sulfur, and other faint chemical gradients. Their growth is extraordinarily slow, but the plateau offers them an almost unlimited surface.

At first, this seems insufficient to support anything larger than the lichens.

Then one of the boulders breathes.

A brief seam of vapor appears along its flank. By the next twilight, what looked like a broken extension of the ridge has turned its head. Other shapes begin to separate from the land: low backs, broad shoulders, legs sunk among stones. The herd has been present all along.

The great animals are millbacks. They are long and massive but carry themselves close to the ground, with hides of layered gray filaments that trap air and break up their outlines. Their feet end in broad, soft pads. Their mouths are narrow and undramatic, adapted for scraping mineral film and lichen from the rock. They often feed before dawn, when condensation has loosened the crust; every drink of water is also a meal.

Within each animal is a slow mill. Muscular chambers turn swallowed stones against one another, grinding what the lips have gathered. Other chambers maintain different conditions of warmth, acidity, and moisture, cultivating the microbes that make the animal’s diet usable. A meal may remain in some stage of digestion for years. Their pulses are faint, their movements economical, and their body heat is held so closely that snow would not melt on their backs—if enough snow ever fell to test it.

A single herd may contain hundreds of thousands of adults and still average hundreds of metres between individuals. From within it, only a few animals are visible at once, with long intervals of empty stone between them. Yet one can travel for weeks without leaving the herd. Its multitude is not crowding. It is continuation. Once the eye has learned to distinguish living backs from mineral ones, the plateau becomes difficult to see as empty again.

Smaller animal life is even more abundant. Beneath damp slabs lie congregations of translucent dormants, thousands packed into a handspan, waiting through frozen years for enough moisture to complete a single stage of growth. The wind carries eggs and larvae among the mineral dust. Millback hides support whole communities of silent cleaners, and their waste—mineral paste inoculated with gut cultures—becomes the beginning of new microbial pasture. In this way the herds do not merely consume the plateau. Across centuries, they prepare the conditions by which later herds will feed.

There are hunters, though little that resembles a chase. Pale, narrow scavenger-predators follow the herds at a distance, testing the sick and taking those already failing. Most of an animal’s death is accomplished by smaller mouths. Soft tissue disappears first, then hide, then the organic material within bone. Afterward there may be only a dark stain, a few worn teeth, and a slight enrichment of the hollow where the body lay.

Nothing on the plateau has been manufactured, but the scale of its transformations gives it an industrial character. Stone is intake; cold and pressure are part of the apparatus; digestion is a sequence of mills, cultures, and reservoirs. The herds transport material across provinces. Wind distributes the dormant living stages. Production is continental, repetitive, and almost entirely without waste. There are no engines to discover apart from bodies, no factories apart from the chemical chambers within them, and no network except the one made by migration, weather, and time.

There is also power in the more literal sense. Ice dust drawn across millback hair charges it electrostatically. During the long dry stillness, potential accumulates without visible effect. When condensation finally wets the herd, the charge leaks away in tiny blue-white branches along the filaments and across the nearest damp grains. No single discharge is bright or loud. But across a great herd, millions can occur out of phase through one night, outlining animals that daylight had concealed. From a height, a whole province seems to flicker faintly, as though some immense circuit were testing itself and finding no need to hurry.

The silence remains the strongest fact of the place. The wind has little to sound against. Millbacks do not call to one another, and their padded steps raise, at most, a brief friction of grit. Freezing rain does not splash. Even the hunters feed without cries. At long intervals, contracting stone gives a small tick, or an animal releases a breath with a softness just below hearing. Otherwise, sound seems to have been omitted from the design.

Change is correspondingly patient. A millback may take several steps in a day and then stand feeding for a week. Herd movements are better measured in decades than in seasons. A trail is not a constructed path but a slight difference in compacted grains and microbial growth, persisting after the animals have gone and gradually fading as new life colonizes it. The discharges are brief, the rare rains briefer, but neither alters the essential pace. Stone rounds; films return; young animals grow into the outline of the land.

Under the dim sky, the plateau can still appear abandoned: an old, high, water-poor expanse of dark rock with almost nothing growing on it. But somewhere condensation is forming in a pore. Somewhere a crop of swallowed stone turns once inside a body. Charge gathers along a gray back, and a herd too large to see continues its almost motionless passage.

Nothing has been built. The work has never stopped.
