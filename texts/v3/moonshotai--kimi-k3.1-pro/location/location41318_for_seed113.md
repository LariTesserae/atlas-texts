## The Ninth Cistern

Half a kilometre below the surface, the Ninth Cistern is warm from its own making. It occupies a single chamber in basalt, no larger than a cathedral and its close, though the ceiling rises high enough to disappear into condensation. The excavation is recent. Parallel tool-scallops still climb the walls; fractures retain their glassy, cutting edges; chips lie where they fell, white at the centre and unrounded. Water has stained some of the stone, but it has not softened anything. There is almost no silt. Even the floor seems too new to have acquired a history.

From the entry stair, the chamber appears nearly empty: a broad dark floor, a few lights very far apart, three slender service towers standing in separate pools of shadow. The air between them feels disproportionately large. Then the urban order begins to emerge. Curbs divide the floor into narrow streets. Slot drains follow their intersections. Shallow bridges cross thermal channels, and flights of steps lead to service galleries stacked three levels high in the wall. There are access plates, cable trenches, inspection ladders, loading recesses, and small ceramic plaques marking plots with no buildings on them. One pavilion stands beside a reservoir; another, windowless and squat, occupies the far corner. Nothing looks ruined or removed. This is simply a city with most of its matter omitted—a district at the stage of utilities, addresses, and intentions.

Every surface is wet. Warm seepage beads on the basalt and gathers into drops too heavy to remain hanging. Clear water crosses the streets in films, finds the fresh tool marks, and braids them into temporary channels. In the lowest plots it stands ankle-deep, reflecting the guide lights as broken amber threads. The reservoir is darker, its surface dimpled by condensation falling from the unseen roof. The air is nearly saturated and warm against the skin, smelling of wet mineral, hot metal, green sap, and a faint clean sharpness like ozone. Mist moves through the chamber in slow horizontal layers, sometimes revealing the towers, sometimes leaving only their humming.

Where the water is instructed to linger, vegetation becomes abruptly luxuriant. Black-green moss thickens into cushions along the expansion joints. Ferns rise from the curb drains, their waxy fronds unfurling over bare ceramic. Ribbon-leaved reeds crowd the warm channels, and broad floating leaves turn slowly in the reservoir without ever drifting far from their root mats. These gardens are lush but not continuous: long, dense bands of growth separated by wide empty pavement and dark water. Deep garnet light leaks from seams beneath the roots, feeding them while contributing little to the visible illumination. The city repairs lifted tiles around established roots rather than cutting the plants back. In several places, a drain and a fern bed have become the same structure.

Nothing animal lives among them. There are no insects around the lights, no larvae or fish in the basins, no webs under the bridges, no tracks in the young deposits. No wings disturb the upper fog. Dead fronds collapse into the root mats and are slowly reduced to translucent fibres by fungal and microbial films, but nothing browses, burrows, or calls. The abundance of leaves makes the absence more noticeable. Movement here comes from water, growth, ventilation, and machines—never from attention.

The light is deliberately meagre. Amber lines run just beneath the curbs, bright enough to define a street but not to illuminate its whole width. Occasional cold pulses travel through the reservoir, briefly exposing submerged steps and the precise geometry of the bottom. Most surfaces are visible only because they are wet enough to return these small lights. The upper galleries remain silhouettes. Beyond them, the ceiling could be ten metres away or could open into a much larger darkness.

Yet the Cistern is not quiet. Its sound begins with a broad water-hiss and a deep, nearly continuous hum that seems to come equally from the towers, the rock, and the reservoir. The three tower frequencies are almost alike, so their combined tone swells and fades without becoming softer. Drops strike ceramic, leaves, metal gratings, and open water with distinct notes. The chamber separates and repeats them until a brief shower can sound like rain falling in several adjoining rooms. At intervals a drain closes with a resonant knock, followed by the long, bass swallow of water leaving the floor. Near the channels, conversation would require a raised voice; everywhere, the noise is felt lightly in the ribs.

Every seventeen minutes, the district makes weather.

The tower tones slide toward agreement. A pressure wave passes through the mist, and condensation thickens overhead until the roof becomes audible before it becomes visible. Rain begins in scattered heavy drops, then strengthens into a warm, roaring fall. For three minutes the streets disappear beneath moving silver skins. Ferns bow and spring back. Construction dust still emerging from the newest cuts washes out in milky ribbons. The guide lights dim, and the chamber seems to contract around the noise. Then all the drains open together. The water level falls with surprising speed, the rain thins, and fog returns before the last drops have struck the pavement. There is no wholly dry interval and no true silence between cycles.

The drainage is the first clear sign that the room’s emptiness is also an engineering achievement. The grates have no appreciable depth. Water crosses their lips and ceases to occupy the chamber without a splash or a visible pipe, returning minutes later as mineral-bearing mist above. Outlet and inlet are adjacent in the machinery’s geometry, though far apart to the eye. The towers are not pumps so much as anchors, keeping the district connected to systems whose bulk does not have to exist locally. Most of the city’s substance is elsewhere; what remains here are the surfaces on which it touches ordinary space.

The built matter, too, is not entirely finished. Fresh curbs end in translucent layers thinner than glaze, each one adding a nearly imperceptible extension. A cracked access plate may soften into a grey, mobile film and reappear smooth over the course of hours. Streets continue to grow toward the raw chamber walls, where illuminated survey lines already run across uncut basalt. Doors are marked before they are opened. Conduits are named before they are connected. The Cistern is young not because it is incomplete in the ordinary sense, but because completion is one of its ongoing processes.

It does not feel abandoned. Abandonment would be a later stage. Warm rain falls on unoccupied addresses; roots enter newly made joints; the galleries hum to empty air. At the far wall, where an unbuilt avenue has already been given a name, a narrow strip of moss has crossed the survey line. The tower tones begin to converge again. Fog gathers, the lights lower, and the whole hollow district answers with water.
