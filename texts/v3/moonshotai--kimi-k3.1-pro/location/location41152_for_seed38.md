## The Old Green Height

At an altitude where a younger landscape would be all cliffs, ice, and exposure, the Old Green Height lies beneath warm rain. It is a plateau only in the oldest sense: a continental swelling of stone whose summits were rubbed away long before the present forest began its life. Its highest rises are broad, almost imperceptible swells. Its valleys are shallow basins full of black water and roots. From above, it might look nearly flat—a dark, softly corrugated skin stretching beyond the reach of a single weather system. From within, there is no horizon at all.

The elevation is known chiefly by the clouds. Along the plateau’s immense, rounded margins, they press against the forest and are combed into mist. There are few clean edges. The slopes descend so gradually, and through such dense growth, that the height seems less to end than to become difficult to distinguish from the weather below it.

Height does not bring cold here. The air arrives warm and remains warm, held against the living ground like breath. Even the rain has little chill. Moisture condenses on stone, leaves, skin, and the backs of motionless animals, and stays there.

Underfoot there is almost no honest soil. The surface is a deep felting of roots, moss, leaf sheaths, and softened vegetable matter, layered over older versions of itself. It gives slightly under weight and recovers so slowly that a footprint may remain as a damp depression for days. Water does not always rise into the print; sometimes it wells up several paces away, displaced through the body of the mat.

Beneath this, where stone can still be reached, it is dark, fine-grained, and worn without angles. Exposed pieces occur on the crowns of swells or at the margins of pools, rounded as though polished by a river that no longer exists. They sweat continuously. Fine roots enter their pores and come away mineral-stained. In some places the rock is so thoroughly penetrated that it resembles a fossil of the root system above it. In others, roots have become stone’s apparent support: remove them, and the low ridge would crumble into the basin it has held for ages.

Plants are not the covering of the Height. They are its structure, its surface, much of its interior, and a considerable part of its climate.

There is no single canopy. Leaf layer succeeds leaf layer, each adapted to a dimmer portion of the light admitted by the one above. The highest foliage is broad, thick, and nearly black. Beneath it are bronze and deep-green fronds, then translucent leaves with tissues so thin that the faint light seems to pass entirely through them. Columns that appear to be tree trunks are often bundles of different stems grown together around an empty center. Some of these hollow columns hold water. Others contain vertical gardens, their inner walls lined with small pale roots descending toward the damp.

Roots span ponds without touching them. Fallen wood becomes a raised causeway of moss and new growth before it has finished rotting. Even the open water usually carries a partial skin of floating leaves, fine as lace or broad enough to support a resting animal. There are open places—still basins, root-vaults, long dim corridors beneath buttresses—but none are empty. Every surface is occupied by something, and often by several things occupying one another.

Yet this is not a uniform green mass. The vegetation varies almost nervously at close range: waxy leaves beside velvety ones, rigid fans beside tissues that collapse at a touch, low copper-colored cushions, white root-flowers that never see the sky. At a distance these differences merge into a single patient body.

Daylight reaches the lower forest as green-brown twilight. At its brightest, the sky visible through a rare gap is only a pale region in the cloud, never a hard disc. Shadows have no sharp boundaries. Farther down, among the root arches, reflections from water provide as much illumination as the canopy does. The air smells of wet stone, crushed stems, and a faint sweetness that is neither blossom nor decay but something continuously passing between them.

Water is everywhere, but there are few rivers in the ordinary sense. The gradients are too weak, and the vegetation too complete. Instead, water beads on leaf tips, threads through root-felt, stands in hollow columns, seeps from stone, and collects in basins whose surfaces appear perfectly motionless. A rill may take an hour to reveal which way it is flowing. Rain falling on one part of the Height may emerge years later from a spring on higher ground.

This is the plateau’s strangest habit: its vegetation does not merely occupy the watershed. It is the watershed.

The immense root systems exchange water across distances that have no relation to the visible slopes. Over seasons or decades, one basin slowly diminishes while another, beyond a rise, begins to overflow. Springs weaken without a change in rainfall. Ponds fill beneath a cloudless sky. The movement is too slow to watch, but it can be inferred from lines of drowned growth and newly exposed roots. The forest drinks in one province and releases in another. A drop of water may cross the Height without ever running downhill.

At continental scale, this circulation makes weather. Moisture exhaled by the canopy gathers above the broad swales, where the air is slightly stiller, and returns as prolonged mist. Elsewhere, condensation slides downward through the foliage and falls as interior rain long after the clouds overhead have thinned. One region can lie under warm fog while another, beyond any visible horizon, is receiving a rain composed partly of water drawn from the first. Weather does not simply cross the Old Green Height. Much of it is born there, moves through its tissues, and returns.

Animal life is abundant, though rarely conspicuous. Its abundance becomes clear at close range: translucent swimmers in a leaf-cistern, feathery-gilled amphibians flattened beneath a water film, small many-legged grazers working along the veins of a fallen frond, winged things folded so closely against bark that their outlines appear only when they breathe.

Larger animals move through the root-vaults with the same restraint. The moss-backed browsers are broad and low, their feet soft enough that they make almost no sound on the saturated ground. Gardens establish themselves on their backs during the long intervals between wallowing. Small gliders travel with them, using the moving clearings of their bodies and feeding on the insects disturbed by their passage. In the deeper pools, hunters lie among submerged roots, distinguishable only by the occasional movement of gills. Some wait through entire seasons for the brief abundance that follows a change in water level.

Many creatures communicate without voice. They drum lightly against roots, alter the pressure in a hollow stem, release scents into water, or send vibrations through the mat. A dimple crossing a still pool may be a signal rather than a footstep. Courtship, warning, and territory pass through the ground in forms too subtle to separate easily from the forest’s own hydraulic settling.

The animals prune, carry spores, open passages in the floating vegetation, and return minerals to places the roots reach slowly. Their bodies are small warm provinces in the dimness. When one dies, it does not vanish quickly; it becomes a long alteration in local growth. Decades later, the site may still be marked by an unusual richness of leaves.

Sound is scarce enough that each noise remains separate. A drop strikes a leaf. Much later, another enters water. Somewhere a stem ticks as it takes up moisture. A wing brushes wet foliage, or a browser exhales behind a root wall. There are no steep torrents to provide a continuous roar, and the wind that moves the upper canopy rarely reaches the ground. A loud call, when one occurs, seems to travel only a short distance before the layered growth absorbs it.

The silence is not emptiness. It is the condition of many small lives avoiding one another successfully.

Very occasionally, the vegetation admits a deliberate angle. Three root-covered stones form a corner. A low wall runs for eleven paces and then disappears into a basin. Five worn steps descend into water and do not emerge on the other side. In one district stands a single roofless cell, its doorway narrow and rounded by age. Its floor supports a small pale plant found nowhere else on the Height.

These remains are scattered too widely to suggest towns or roads. There are no tools beside them, no metal, no mechanisms, no accumulation of useful debris. Some may have been shelters. Others may have been markers, reservoirs, or structures whose purpose was never architectural. Roots have entered every joint, but instead of pulling the stones apart, they often hold them precisely where they were left. If these things were built, they belong to a brief and now almost illegible interruption in an otherwise biological history.

Nothing on the Height is in a hurry to erase them. Nothing is in a hurry to do anything.

A heavy leaf may take seasons to unfold. A gap in the canopy remains a gap for generations, then closes from the sides rather than from beneath. Browsers follow routes inherited through the bodies of the forest, though no two journeys along them are separated by less than years. There are seasons of mist and seasons of clearer dimness, but no general leaf-fall, no sudden greening, no annual convulsion. The serious changes occur across durations in which weather becomes only a faint background variation.

Erosion, too, has nearly finished. Almost no sediment leaves the plateau. Stone dissolves grain by grain into roots; roots return the minerals to stems, leaves, animals, and eventually to the mat again. The Height is not being worn away so much as very slowly converted. Its ancient mineral skeleton is becoming living tissue while the broad form of the old mountain is preserved.

This gives the landscape its peculiar feeling of completion. It is not dying, and it is not young. It has passed beyond the age of raw cliffs, catastrophic floods, and rapidly shifting valleys. Disturbances still happen—a root arch subsides, a basin changes its outlet, an old column collapses into its own hollow—but each is absorbed into processes already prepared to receive it. The oldest things here are not individual trees or stones. They are arrangements: a basin repeatedly re-formed, a grove renewed around the same absence, a path kept open by bodies moving along it at intervals longer than memory.

At the vague hour when the cloud-light fails, the forest does not fill with night voices. It grows quieter. Condensation gathers on the upper leaves and begins its long descent. One drop falls, then another. A browser shifts in its sleep, and water trembles in a nearby hollow. After a while even these sounds cease.

The rain sinks into the mat. Somewhere far away, perhaps weeks from now, a spring will rise.

The Old Green Height settles into darkness, warm to its depths: still wearing the last shape of a mountain, and still, almost imperceptibly, growing.
