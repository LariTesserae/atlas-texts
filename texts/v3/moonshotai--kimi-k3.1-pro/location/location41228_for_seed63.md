## The Hushed Kiln

High in the summit of a volcano that has not produced open fire for ages, the mountain keeps one hot room.

Outside, the old cone has been worn into broad black ribs. Between them lie slopes of ash and volcanic glass, dry beyond the memory of weather, their sharp edges softened by grains carried on the thin summit wind. Near the highest rib, a crooked cleft opens onto a ledge. Beyond the cleft is a chamber seventy paces long and perhaps thirty across: a small, entire landscape, with walls for horizons and a roof of uneven basalt. A straight walk across it would take less than a minute, if there were anywhere to put a foot.

Nothing in the chamber looks newly broken. The entrance has been polished into an oval. Fallen roof-blocks have sunk partway into the mineral floor, and their upper corners are rounded. The walls rise in old flutes and scallops, some cut by wind when the mountain breathed differently, others maintained by the patient passage of bodies. The deepest fissure still has edges, but even those are crusted over with sulfur, iron oxides, and pale salts deposited directly from hot mineral vapor. They are not the salts of a vanished lake. No lake ever stood here.

Almost no light enters. The cleft admits only a little of the high night, and that little is absorbed within a few paces. At the rear of the chamber, the fissure sometimes shows a dull red line—the color of iron long after it has ceased to be useful as a lamp. It reveals no ceiling and casts no useful shadows. It merely establishes that the darkness has a bottom.

Heat is more informative than sight. It comes up through the floor and out from the walls, gathering under the roof until the upper air shimmers without visible light. Near the fissure, stone would blister skin. Near the entrance, the heat is less fierce but steady, and the thin air offers little relief. There is no drip, no damp mineral smell, no cool pocket where moisture might collect. The chamber smells of hot metal, struck sulfur, and bitter dust. Its weather consists entirely of pressure, temperature, and the slow movement of dry gas.

At first, the floor appears to have been paved.

Flattened stones lie fitted together from wall to wall, dark gray and brown, with smaller pale fragments filling the spaces between them. Then the fissure exhales, and the pavement changes texture.

Every stone is an animal.

The scalebacks range from thumbnail-sized young to broad, shield-shaped adults as large as a hand. Their shells are mineral-rich and dull, insulated by layers of fine internal spaces. They have no projecting heads. A crescent mouth opens at the front edge, just above the ground, and six soft-padded legs carry them forward with extraordinary care. Where space is scarce, they overlap like roof tiles; where it is scarcer still, the young climb over the old. There is no single floor level, only a moving average of backs.

They feed on the chamber itself. Each exhalation from below brings reactive sulfur and metallic dust, some of which settles into a thin, fresh skin over the older deposits. The scalebacks rasp this skin away. Within their bodies, in tissues lubricated by a sulfur-rich oil rather than water, slow chemical reactions draw energy from the mineral meal. An injured scaleback does not bleed in any ordinary sense. It leaves a brief dark gloss that stiffens, powders, and is eaten by something smaller.

The walls are occupied by other animals. So is the ceiling.

Veilwings hang wherever they can find a grip, their dry, many-spared membranes folded into narrow mineral-looking flakes. At rest, their wing edges touch so precisely that hundreds of individuals can resemble one continuous crust. When a warm current rises, they open in sequence. For a moment the wall appears to delaminate. They strain the air for shell dust, mineral particles, and the nearly weightless young of smaller creatures, then fold themselves back into the available space. Some never touch the floor during their lives. Their generations mature, mate, and die within a few body-lengths of the same patch of roof.

Nothing green supports them. Nothing photosynthesizes, and there is no hidden mat of plantlike growth beneath the crowding. The ecology begins with heat and chemical difference: hot stone below, cooler mineral surfaces above, fresh compounds arriving through the fissure. Everything with a mouth enters the process somewhere after that. Dead veilwings become drifting scales. Dead scalebacks are opened, emptied, and their shells ground into grit. The grit is ingested again, or carried into cracks, or lifted by the next exhalation. Nothing rots wetly. Things are unmade.

The chamber’s fullness extends into its air. Tiny animals drift through the updraft, too sparse to resemble a swarm but too numerous ever to be absent. Larger veilwings occasionally cross the open space in short, heavy glides. Beneath them, the scalebacks move in grazing fronts so gradual that an individual seems motionless, though over hours a whole region of the floor changes orientation. Now and then a patch of shells suddenly boils upward as young emerge from beneath it. Neighbors close over every vacancy. No bare surface remains bare for long.

And yet the Hushed Kiln is quieter than the empty slopes outside.

Its sounds are slight: a dry tick as one shell edge passes another, a papery intake when wings unfold, the low sigh of gas in the fissure. The accumulated movement of so many bodies produces a faint rustle that is easier to feel than to hear. There are no mating calls, no territorial cries, no alarms. Most communication passes through antennae, through shell contact, or as vibrations sent directly into stone.

This quiet is not peace. It is a necessity shaped by the listeners.

The listeners are pale, jointed predators that spend most of their lives folded into crevices. Folded, one may be no larger than a hand; opened, its limbs form a wide, flat net around a small central body. They detect discontinuity—the knock of a shell against stone, the tear of a wing, the scrape of a panic-stricken animal losing its footing. Continuous soft movement gives them little. A single hard sound can draw one from several body-lengths away.

When such a sound occurs, the chamber stops.

The grazing fronts halt. Veilwings freeze half-open. Even the drifting young seem to settle more slowly. A listener unfolds and travels toward the disturbance with long, deliberate limbs, touching shells without rattling them. Its feeding is brief and nearly silent. Afterward there remains a hollow husk, a slight rearrangement of the crowd, and eventually the resumption of that almost inaudible rustle.

The mountain’s longer cycles continue regardless. Heat gathers beneath a crust in the fissure until the crust bows and releases a dry exhalation. Mineral vapor rises; dust lifts; veilwings open from floor level to roof in a dark ascending wave. Fresh deposits form in sheltered seams and along the cooler stone near the entrance. Then the scalebacks turn toward them. At other times the chamber draws air inward through narrow lower cracks, and the whole crowd leans subtly the other way. These cycles serve instead of day and season. They govern feeding, emergence, migration, and the brief flights by which veilwings exchange mates between walls.

Over long ages, the animals have become agents of erosion as important as wind. Their mouths lower the floor; their feet polish the entrance; shell grit abrades the fallen blocks. But mineral deposition raises the floor again, and the ceaseless traffic prevents drifts from closing the old passages. The chamber is therefore active without being unsettled. Its details continually change, while its form persists. It is not recovering from a catastrophe or approaching some final condition. It has been in this state, or something very near it, for a very long time.

A few deliberate alterations remain.

Nine steps descend through the entrance cleft, each tread worn into a shallow scoop. At the rear stands a rectangular frame made from two upright slabs and a single lintel, with no wall around it. It faces neither the entrance nor the fissure exactly. A shallow groove crosses one upright, interrupted where scalebacks have gained access to the stone. Elsewhere, half hidden beneath the living floor, is a short arc of laid blocks too regular to be natural and too incomplete to explain itself.

There are no vessels, hinges, metal fragments, or devices. Whatever hands made these things left only stone bearing the faint remainder of intention. The animals make no distinction between built and unbuilt surfaces. The steps carry a slow traffic of shells. The empty doorway is one of the densest veilwing roosts. The frame’s right angles are gradually becoming curves.

From just inside the entrance, after the eyes have exhausted themselves, the chamber can still be mistaken for a dark, mineral hollow containing nothing but heat. Then the fissure sighs. The floor, walls, and ceiling shift by almost nothing. The old stair seems to move under its burden, and the darkness acquires depth—not because anything becomes visible, but because countless separate bodies respond at once.

For an instant the soft rustle reaches every boundary and reveals the size of the room.

Then quiet closes over it again. Mineral by mineral, mouth by mouth, the mountain continues to be eaten and remade.
