## The Unweathered Town

Approach from below, and the first sign of the high country is warmth. It arrives before light, before any slope can be distinguished: a faint loosening of the cold, a mineral bitterness in the water, then a darkness with shape. The mountain rises out of that shape.

It is still being made. Its cliffs have not softened into slopes, and its ridges have not yet learned to be round. Lobes of black lava lie exposed along the summit. Fractures run through them with clean, glassy edges. There are almost no rounded stones, little sand, and no deep soil except where roots have trapped sediment in the lee of a wall. A rock displaced from a ledge may remain exactly where it lands for decades.

Although the summit lies far beneath the surface, it is unmistakably high country. On three sides the ground falls away in immense steps toward a plain too deep to be seen. Saddles connect it to other cones and broken ridges. The open water between them has the exposure of a high valley: no shelter, no nearby bottom, only depth beneath depth. The sea above is simply another kind of sky, heavier and much nearer.

The water is warm as a held hand, with colder seams along the outer cliffs. It is also clear. Darkness, rather than silt, limits sight. At local noon the black-green water thins to the color of old bottle glass, and the uppermost leaves acquire a narrow bronze edge. That is the whole display of day. Night brings only a slight further closing. Suspended mineral flakes hang nearly motionless, so slowly carried by the current that they seem uncertain whether to rise or settle.

Where warm seeps thread the fractured rock, vegetation gathers in tiers. Black-olive ribbons stream from crevices. Translucent crimson fans spread beneath them, and lower down grow dense velvets the color of tarnished brass. Some leaves use what little light arrives from above; others spread their roots through pale bacterial felts surrounding the seeps, living as much on dissolved stone and geothermal warmth as on daylight. The result is lush without being bright.

The growth is densest where the slope offers shelter. Fronds overlap above narrow pockets of open water. Root mats bind one mineral crust to the next. Leaves touch, part, and touch again over the course of hours. Almost nothing is torn or grazed. When a leaf dies, it often remains whole, slowly fading in place until someone gathers it for fiber.

The town first appears as regularity within this growth: a fracture made straight, a row of ledges too evenly spaced, three dark openings aligned across a cliff.

It is not a drowned town. Not one wall was built in air.

Retaining walls step around the summit shoulder, holding gardens and level courts against the raw slope. Above them stand roofless chambers, store cells, narrow lanes, and public terraces. Some streets began as natural fissures, widened only enough to admit a harvest basket. Elsewhere the masonry is so interlocked with the bedrock that quarry and foundation cannot easily be distinguished. The settlement is compact rather than vast, but it has an urban order: repeated gate widths, aligned drains, communal work courts, garden districts, and marked routes leading beyond the last houses.

Most buildings have no roofs. Underwater, shelter means a lee from the current, a restricted entrance, a chamber that holds warmth or darkness. A roof would mainly collect sediment. Doorways open at several heights; stairs are used for hauling stone and other heavy loads, while an unburdened inhabitant can simply rise past them. Many lanes are no wider than two outstretched arms, and vegetation closes over their upper ends. At junctions, small hollow stones contain cultivated patches of faintly luminous root-velvet. They do not illuminate the streets so much as interrupt the darkness.

Every block shows handwork. Basalt was pecked free with hammerstones, shaped with volcanic-glass flakes, and fitted by patient abrasion with sand. Ropes are twisted from the long leaves of the slope gardens. Stone weights, pivot poles, woven baskets, and fiber harnesses accomplish nearly everything. No edge is machine-true, yet the infrastructure is exact. A gate can be reached from either side. A harvest stair always meets a clear landing. Every garden wall includes a gap through which accumulated sediment can be removed.

The most intricate works are channels for carrying water through water.

Dense brine creeps along the lowest gutters, separated from the surrounding sea by a wavering, almost visible skin. Warm seep-water rises through open stone chimneys. In one public court, an inverted flume catches this upward flow and conducts it beneath a stone lip to the higher gardens. Excess water escapes in trembling threads and drops that ascend until they vanish. These are the town’s fountains. They are also its plumbing: by lifting or lowering a hand-cut gate, a gardener can send a different temperature or chemistry through an entire terrace.

For all this workmanship, bodies are scarce. A visitor might cross several courts and see only one gardener, motionless except for the hands tying back a frond. Farther on, two masons might be easing a block into place, their progress too gradual to watch. Many woven door screens remain closed. The town’s density comes from walls, leaves, stored tools, narrow passages, and layers of use—not from crowds.

Quiet has become part of the building practice. Water carries a knock farther than expected, so tools are padded and stones are never dropped. Masons strike in widely spaced blows, with long intervals for listening. Speech is uncommon outside the work courts. For hours, the loudest sound may be the granular whisper of brine crossing a rough channel, or a leaf stem drawing lightly along stone. Occasionally the mountain gives a low internal note, felt as pressure through the whole body. Work stops then. Afterward, the silence returns without ceremony.

Animals are rarer still. No schools of fish cross the open water above the roofs. A transparent grazer, small enough to rest on a fingertip, may spend an afternoon crossing a single garden. Blind, pale things coil deep in the sediment traps and are seldom disturbed. At long intervals something large passes beyond the ridge, visible only as a temporary absence of the faint light. The gardeners remember the years of such visits better than they remember the shapes.

The town’s age is recorded in addition rather than decay. Warm water lays a thin mineral rind on exposed surfaces. Roots follow, stitching masonry to bedrock. An unused threshold does not crumble; it thickens. Carved grip marks soften beneath ivory-colored stone. In the oldest quarter, passages have narrowed until a basket must be turned sideways to pass through. A building left unattended long enough becomes first a mound, then a ledge, then a small piece of the mountain.

Above the inhabited terraces, the newest district is still mostly quarry. Rows of pecked dots indicate rooms that may be separated from the bedrock by the next generation, or the one after that. No one calls the work delayed. A wall can remain under construction for three lifetimes and still be considered well attended.

Beyond the last marker stones, the dense summit opens into a broader region. A route crosses a bare saddle to outlying vent gardens. Farther away, other young peaks carry small cultivated patches and occasional clusters of masonry. Travel between them follows remembered gradients of warmth and slope more than sight. The intervening water is vast and uninhabited, a valley filled with night.

From the upper quarry, the town below looks less like an occupation of the mountain than like one of its slower processes. Basalt rises and fractures. Minerals gather. Roots bind the new surfaces. Masons answer a fresh ledge with a wall, and the gardens answer the wall with leaves.

Nothing here is waiting for the water to recede. The high country is not necessarily a future island, and the town is not the survivor of a flood. Both are learning, through almost imperceptible additions, how to exist submerged. It feels less like a place after a catastrophe than like an early morning that may last another thousand years.
