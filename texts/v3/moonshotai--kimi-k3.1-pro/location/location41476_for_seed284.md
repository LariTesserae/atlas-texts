## The Summit Vesicle

The highest point of the young range is a blade of black glass veined with quartz. It rises so far above the dry continental basin that the lower slopes are lost in atmospheric haze, while the summit itself remains painfully distinct: fresh fractures, translucent edges, splinters that have not yet been knocked loose. There is little talus and no rounded scree. The mountain has not had time to become old enough for ruins.

No water takes part in its making. There is no ice in the fractures, no brine in the mineral seams, no snow caught in the lee of the summit. Wind moves over the rock as a thin, cold abrasion, carrying dust that is nearly as sharp as the surfaces it strikes.

Near the very tip, sealed inside the glass, is a cavity smaller than a clenched fist. From outside it appears as a cloudy flaw. Within it is a city.

At the scale of its inhabitants, the cavity is not small. Its floor is a broken country of glass ridges, quartz outcrops, and industrial fill. Its walls rise in overhanging cliffs, and its roof is a distant, uneven vault. A narrow central shaft connects the lower terrain with chambers high in the opposite wall. The whole interior flexes almost imperceptibly when the pressure outside changes. For the city, that flex is enough to alter streets, open seams, and announce a change in conditions.

The enclosed gas is thin and cool, mostly nitrogen and argon. It carries a dry smell of crushed mineral, hot ceramic, and occasional electrical discharge. There are no damp pockets and no liquids in the machinery. Bearings use graphite and powdered ceramic; presses compact dry feedstock; induction furnaces soften metal without steam or smoke. Even animal bodies contain no water. Their tissues are lattices of silica, carbon, and conductive salts through which energy moves as carefully regulated electrical leakage.

The city occupies nearly every surface. Its oldest visible layers are stamping mills and accumulator houses crowded around the quartz seams. Above and between them are machine shops, cable ducts, freight galleries, sorting towers, storage hoppers, residential cells, and narrow passages whose original purpose has been forgotten. Streets cross one another at several elevations. Some run vertically. The central shaft is dense with lift cables, counterweights, maintenance rails, and temporary bridges, all close enough that a traveller could change routes without touching open ground.

This is not merely a factory. There are nurseries warmed by motor housings, communal feeding galleries around charged mineral faces, shelters built into insulation, and open clearances where animals gather to exchange grooming, charge, and fragments of useful material. But there is almost no unused space. A maintenance gap left empty for more than a few production cycles acquires a conduit, then a stair, then three small structures clinging to its sides.

The illumination is dim and local. A capillary opening through the summit glass admits a little daylight, not enough to light the chamber, only enough to give its darkness a direction. Around local noon, a gray-blue line creeps across part of the floor and is repeatedly interrupted by machinery. Elsewhere, furnace mouths glow dull red, accumulator indicators burn amber, and faulty connections produce brief white sparks. Many districts are visible mainly as silhouettes against other districts.

Sound is similarly withheld. The gas is too thin to carry a full industrial roar, and most of the machines are very small. There are relay ticks, the dry whisk of bearings, the faint tapping of presses, and now and then the ceramic click of something breaking. A collapsing gantry makes less noise than a fingernail drawn across glass. Through the surrounding stone, however, the city can be felt as a fine, continuous vibration, crowded with pulses too rapid to separate.

The animals are everywhere.

Seam-fleas gather on exposed quartz in glittering concentrations, probing the crystal with their forelegs and drawing off the charge generated by stress in the mountain. Wiremice nest inside switchgear, their conductive whiskers permanently dusted with metal powder; they carry their enamel-shelled eggs away whenever a power line begins to overheat. Veilwings hang beneath lamps and along warm conduits, each one a mica-thin flake with legs. When disturbed, hundreds rise together in a soundless gray sheet.

The dark freight ducts contain longer, many-jointed hunters that follow the electrical leakage of feeding animals. Latch-spiders stretch themselves across busbars and wait for the tiny interruption caused by a seam-flea touching down. Scavengers patrol the sorting floors, stripping broken machine parts and dead bodies with equal attention. Even the apparent dust on a ledge may prove, on closer inspection, to have legs.

There are no plants and nothing that photosynthesizes. The foundation of the ecology is geological. Compression in the quartz produces electrical potential; the smallest animals tap it, larger animals consume them or strip charge from their remains, and scavengers return shell and conductive tissue to the material cycle. Industry draws from precisely the same source. Slag heaps become nesting terrain, stray current feeds hidden colonies, and factory collectors compete with seam-fleas for access to a fresh fracture. The boundary between infrastructure and habitat is therefore never clean. A cable may be power supply, roadway, hunting ground, and nursery at once.

It is also a turbulent place.

Power does not arrive steadily. It comes in surges as the summit glass flexes and the quartz seams take up or release stress. During a small surge, production accelerates: conveyors reverse, sorting gates chatter, presses stamp out parts faster than the hoppers can receive them. During a large one, the city changes shape.

Gantries detach and crawl to new anchor points. Whole street sections are lifted aside. Towers are cut down into sharp, reusable blocks while new structures are pressed from powder elsewhere. A bridge may become feedstock before anything has crossed it; a factory may be dismantled because its site is suddenly required for a freight corridor. Sometimes an entire district comes apart in a cascading glitter of components, leaving, for a short interval, no architecture at all—only dense traffic moving through a cloud of material.

The animals respond immediately. Wiremice evacuate eggs in long lines. Veilwings coat the remaining lamps. Seam-fleas leap from one charged surface to another, and predators follow the confusion into passages normally too well guarded to enter. From a distance the chamber seems to seethe, though nothing boils and very little can be heard. Its violence is architectural and animal rather than meteorological.

Then order returns, but never the same order. Freight corridors emerge first, followed by accumulators and mills. Shelters accumulate around their waste heat. New seams are opened, old ones abandoned, and fauna colonize the altered gradients. The city is not malfunctioning, exactly. Its controllers are still enforcing practical rules—keep the central shaft passable, preserve charge, replace worn machinery—but each district responds independently, and their solutions collide. Maps expire almost as quickly as they can be etched. Navigation relies instead on current, vibration, and the position of a few stubbornly maintained routes.

Nothing weathers into picturesque decay. There is no rust, rot, moss, or softened masonry. An abandoned wall remains hard-edged until it is crushed. Slag does not mellow; it waits to be sorted. The youngest surfaces and the oldest surviving surfaces can look equally fresh. Age is recorded not in patina but in reuse: a single beam may contain ceramic from a hundred previous structures, none of which left a monument behind.

Outside, a climber could rest a hand on the summit and feel only cool stone and a vibration too faint to identify. Beneath that hand, in a space smaller than the palm, presses would be striking in dimness, wings rising around dead lamps, streets vanishing under new construction. The highest city in the world would be crowded, industrious, and almost silent. It would have no sky and no horizon—only the enclosing glass, and the next surge moving through the rock.
