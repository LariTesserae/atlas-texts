## The Rootworks

The Rootworks are still making the hollow they will occupy.

Far below the surface, an excavation some sixteen kilometres long passes through beds of pale anhydrite, graphite, and black salt. It is not a natural cavern later inhabited, nor an ancient one falling in. Its walls are fresh. Their fractures have splintered edges, and the dust beneath them is angular, bright, and unrounded. Nothing here has been shaped by flowing water. There is no water to shape it: no seepage in the deepest cuts, no condensation on the pipes, no hidden reserve behind the walls. Even the engines run without steam. Their sealed circuits carry gas, dry lubricant, or powdered mineral charge.

The air stays cellar-cool. It smells faintly of crushed dry leaves, crushed stone, and, near the switchyards, ozone.

Light comes from long banks of lamps suspended between root-grown pylons. It is ordinary, workable light—soft white, widespread, bright enough to distinguish the green of a new shoot from the green of old fiber. There are dark service recesses and unlit cuts beyond the frontier, but the inhabited region is not a place of perpetual gloom. From the central galleries, colours remain legible into the distance: sage-grey growth, iron-red machinery, lilac mineral faces, the matte yellow of freight carts. The roof is always present overhead, sometimes close enough to show its reinforcement bolts, sometimes several hundred metres above the floor.

Vegetation covers nearly everything, but it does not crowd the hollow.

A continuous plant skin lies over the settled geology: a dense, grey-green felt of tiny leaves, braided rootlets, and hard, papery stems. It climbs walls, wraps bridge piers, pads stair treads, and fills the spaces between sleepers on the tramways. Rock is visible only where it has just been exposed or where cutting tools keep it bare. Roads are compacted layers of this growth. Building walls are thickened stalks, grown around frames and then planed, drilled, and bolted. Even discarded machine housings eventually acquire a green seam along every sheltered joint.

The plants are dry all the way through. Their living chemistry takes place in mineral gels that never become wet; nothing drips from them, and nothing evaporates into the air. Their leaves are stiff wafers, often pleated like folded metal. Their trunks resemble bundles of horn and wire. At growing tips, the tissue is translucent and slightly flexible. A few days later it has hardened sufficiently to bear a load.

Above the continuous skin, however, there is great emptiness. Tall growth is concentrated in separated districts, with kilometres of low, open floor between them. A single power pylon may stand alone in the middle of a broad basin. A herd of grazers may take several minutes to cross a patch of ground containing no other upright presence. The settlement is urban in its organization rather than in its crowdedness: streets, switching stations, freight lines, presses, warehouses, and stacked residential galleries, all arranged with precision and separated by long intervals of space.

In the oldest district, bundled-stalk towers rise around a generator hall. Their lower storeys are square and machined; higher up, where later extensions were grown rather than cut, the walls curve and fork. Retired tram rails have been incorporated into foundations. New avenues pass over older ones on living trestles. The ground underfoot is layered with generations of compressed root-felt, but none of it looks abandoned. Old material is continually being planed away, harvested, or instructed to grow into something else.

The factories manufacture more of the city. Fiber mills comb excess growth into cable. Presses laminate dry husks into structural sheet. Cutting shops produce standardized joints, window frames, and gear blanks from hardened stalk. Elsewhere, metal components are cast and machined in the ordinary industrial manner: cylinders, bearings, conductors, flywheels, tools. The largest buildings contain slow lines of equipment rather than crowds. Their motors are enclosed in fiber-damped casings, and much of their sound is felt through the floor as a low, intermittent vibration.

Electricity does more than power the construction. It instructs it.

Root tips turn along charged conductors and fork in response to timed pulses. To lay out a building, the garden-engineers first arrange a circuit in the shape of its intended loads and passages. The surrounding vegetation follows, thickening where current is steady and leaving hollows where the pattern alternates. A district plan therefore resembles both a street map and a wiring diagram. Construction crews prune, tension, bolt, and correct, but they do not place every piece. Between their visits, walls gain thickness and empty frames become rooms.

At the eastern edge, the city thins to survey lines, isolated work lamps, and a freight track ending beside a bud the size of a house. A future platform number has already been stencilled on one of its closed valves. Beyond it, the continuous plant skin becomes a scattering of pale filaments. Beyond those is raw mineral.

This is where the hollow advances.

Fine roots enter weaknesses in the wall and harden inside them. Their swelling, together with the patient pressure of mechanical wedges, opens fractures deeper into the bed. Cutting engines remove the larger slabs; small animals and decomposer cords clean the fissures. Dust collectors gather the rest. Only afterward do the roof arches arrive, grown outward from either side until they can be joined. The process produces no smooth, finished frontier. Raw geology, first growth, temporary infrastructure, and permanent architecture overlap in a band several hundred metres wide. The whole region has the appearance of an embryo whose different tissues are developing at different rates.

Twice during each long lighting cycle, the ventilation plants reverse the pressure in the hollow. This is the closest thing the Rootworks have to weather.

At intake, cool air moves through the open basins in broad, gentle currents. Loose mineral dust rises in thin veils. Pleated leaves unfold, exposing pollen surfaces, and dust moths emerge from crevices in the industrial walls. At exhaust, the collector fronds close and the dust settles onto them in pale lines. The change is usually quiet. Sometimes, when millions of leaf wafers flex together, an entire district makes a soft sound like many pages being turned in separate rooms.

Otherwise, sounds remain distinct and widely spaced: a relay closing; two tram wheels crossing a joint; the brief chatter of a cutting head; a dry seed valve snapping somewhere underfoot. The cavern does not fill with machinery noise. The plant felt absorbs footsteps and small impacts, while the larger engines turn too slowly to roar. Even the frontier is often silent except for an occasional tick within the wall, so small that it could be mistaken for an insect.

Animals are abundant once the eye stops looking only for large ones.

Copper-shelled mites move through the felt in immense numbers, stripping dead fiber and leaving fertile pellets for the decomposer roots. Pale, knee-high grazers travel in loose groups, cropping routes through the vegetation and keeping some of the lesser service paths open without any human intervention. Their hooves are hollow and their footfalls barely audible. Moths with broad, matte wings carry pollen between districts on the ventilation currents. Small, many-legged hunters pursue them through the machinery, while larger membrane-winged predators roost in the high gantries and launch when a moth concentration thickens.

None of these animals drinks. Their bodies use variations of the same closed, non-aqueous chemistry as the plants. They communicate chiefly through scent, touch, and vibrations transmitted along stems or rails, so a busy patch of ecology may appear almost motionless from a few paces away. For all their numbers, animals make up very little of the living mass. The city outweighs them; the city is alive.

Industry continually makes new habitats. Waste heat around the pressworks produces small pockets where leaves remain unfolded through the cool rest period, attracting grazers and then predators. Spilled fiber feeds mites beneath the mills. Moths nest behind lamp housings. Burrowers enter improperly insulated cable runs, and maintenance crews can often locate a fault by watching where the small hunters gather.

From a high gallery near the centre, the whole place can be held in one long view: a dense knot of towers and factories nearby; an open grey-green basin beyond it; a solitary freight tram crossing toward another district; then scattered lamps, half-grown arches, pale fans of excavation dust, and finally the raw wall. Above everything, the lamps maintain their clear, unremarkable day.

It is not quite an underground forest, and not a city being reclaimed by one. Almost nothing here has had time to become old enough for reclamation. Geology, vegetation, and infrastructure are different stages of the same continuing manufacture, though not always an obedient one. Roots outgrow their circuits. Machines cut them back. Animals redirect the pruning. Districts designed for one purpose develop useful hollows for another.

Nothing in the Rootworks is waiting to be watered. The place is waiting for current, for the ventilators to turn, and for the next nearly inaudible fracture in the wall.
