## The Old Choir

The mountains around the Old Choir are high, but no longer tall. Their ridges have softened into broad, lichen-colored backs, and their valleys are filled with the gravel and powdered stone of peaks that no longer exist. There are no young cliffs, no raw scarps, almost no angular rock. Even loose fragments have been rounded and settled into place. The whole range seems close to the final shape it will keep.

The Old Choir occupies a shallow basin on one of these ancient shoulders. Cloud-filled drainages lie below it on either side, and the air is thin, dry to the taste, and cold even under direct sun. Yet the basin itself is small: a few hundred paces across, with a rim so low and worn that it appears less like a wall than a change in the color of the ground. One cloud shadow can cover the entire place.

From the rim, it looks nearly vacant. A dozen or so pale forms stand far apart on gray bedrock. Between them are long stretches of bare pavement, dark seams of vegetation, and occasional black flecks of water. Nothing moves at the scale of an animal. The tallest growths cast shadows across empty courts; the smaller ones seem lost in the spaces around them.

Only closer does the arrangement become architectural.

A lane passes beneath two joined shells and ends at a blank, mineralized wall. Beneath that wall, the opening of an older lane remains visible, half sealed by later growth. Elsewhere, a narrow bridge spans a hollow; broad, stair-like spillways climb to a terrace containing nothing but sky; low partitions divide a court into cells, some roofed, some open. There are arcades, raised galleries, sunken basins, covered channels, and walls built over older walls. It has the organized complexity of a settlement, though a small one, and almost none of its contents.

Most of its volume is air.

No stone was quarried to make it. Nothing was cut, carried, fired, fastened, or fitted. There are no tools, no furnishings, no hearths, no stored objects, not even loose debris arranged for a purpose. The entire borough was grown by a colonial organism whose living tissue resembles a thin olive film and whose dead body resembles porous, bone-gray stone.

The film spreads over moist rock and old growth, taking minerals from dust, rain, and the bedrock itself. Grain by grain, it deposits a hard skeleton around pockets of air. When the living surface retreats, the skeleton remains: a hollow rib, a vault, a perforated wall, a basin with a folded rim. New tissue uses the old shells as support. Over thousands of cold seasons, repeated growth and partial death have produced streets without surveyors and repairs without repairers.

What look like masonry courses are growth bands. Some are no thicker than paper, laid down in dry or bitter years; others are swollen and green-stained from periods of frequent cloud. The oldest material is almost indistinguishable from the mountain. The newest is thin enough to flex slightly in a hard gust.

In ordinary daylight, the place is visually restrained. Gray walls throw gray shadows. Rust-colored stains descend from old mineral seams. Wet growth is dark green; dry growth fades toward olive and straw. Water is black where it lies deep and colorless where it films the stone. There is no unusual radiance. The strangeness of the Old Choir does not depend on strange light.

Water arrives mostly without rain. Cloud presses against the basin, and condensation gathers on thousands of fine ribs above the shells. The drops merge, run downward, and disappear into narrow folds. Occasionally a colder, heavier shower crosses the summit. For a few minutes every open surface streams, the spillways become staircases of white water, and the covered lanes fill with the sound of hurried drainage.

There is never a river. The largest pool would barely cover the floor of one of the smaller courts, and most reservoirs are no more than cupped lenses of water sheltered beneath stone lips. Between wettings, exposed surfaces become powder-dry. Only the deepest folds remain damp. At night, water skins over with clear ice; on bright days, some of it loosens again by afternoon. A single day can include blowing dust, dripping arcades, liquid black basins, and frozen galleries.

Where moisture persists, vegetation becomes abruptly lush. Cushions of moss pack themselves into the seep lines, layered so deeply that no mineral surface shows through. Tiny ferns grow among them, their fronds no longer than a finger but densely clustered. Ribbonlike liverworts lie glossy in the basins, and reddish spore stalks rise from the wettest mats. Inside a few sheltered shells, hanging growth forms thick green curtains around openings in the stone.

A pace away, there may be nothing but bare gray pavement.

No trees live in the basin. Its lushness is low, concentrated, and almost fierce: narrow zones of crowded green separated by wide expanses of mineral emptiness. After cloud-water has been absorbed, the colors deepen across these zones in visible succession, as though a dark stain were moving through the settlement. As the surfaces dry, the green withdraws into cracks and beneath overhangs.

The Old Choir can be heard before the basin comes into view.

Each hollow shell opens to the weather through pores, slits, and abandoned growth chambers. Wind crossing them produces sound at several scales at once: a low pressure in the largest vaults, a softer booming in the courts, a thin whistle along the lanes. In a strong westerly, the whole basin roars like surf against a cliff, although it holds no body of water large enough to make surf. The empty spaces carry echoes from shell to shell, so that a gust seems to approach from several directions.

Rain changes the music without quieting it. Resonance dulls under the wet living film, but drumming, trickling, and the impact of drops in deep basins take its place. A single drop falling from an upper gallery can strike with the report of a small stone. Hundreds fall out of rhythm. Afterward, when the drains begin to close one by one, isolated notes sound from distant parts of the borough.

At night, freezing water produces sharp cracks that travel through the arcades. The living skin ticks as it contracts. Complete silence is possible, but rare—usually a brief, windless interval before dawn, when every accessible drop of water is ice.

Life other than the builder is scarce. A translucent snail occasionally grazes the wet films, leaving a faint, interrupted trail. In the warmest part of the year, a handful of midge larvae may appear in one sheltered basin; a pale spider living beneath its rim takes whatever reaches the surface. These are events, not a background of activity. Most basins contain only water. Most courts hold no track, nest, feather, or bone. A bird may cross the basin as a shadow, or call from the slopes below, but nothing nests in the towers.

Almost every nutrient remains within the small system. Dust feeds the films. Organic matter washes into the basins. Moss and liverwort use what the water brings; their dead tissue is washed deeper or incorporated into new growth. Even the rare snail shell eventually disappears into the colony’s mineral fabric.

The oddest habit of the Old Choir concerns its sound.

New growth does not follow water alone. It also thickens along ribs and margins exposed to repeated vibration. A narrow opening that whistles too violently is gradually constricted until its pitch drops. A chamber that tears at its seams in storms is buttressed; one that merely resonates tends to remain open. Over long periods, damaged shells lose their voices, while new openings appear elsewhere in the connected colony.

No intention is necessary to explain this, though the result is difficult not to hear as listening. The settlement is not pleasantly tuned. Sometimes its notes beat against one another in an unpleasant pulse; sometimes one shell blares above all the others for an entire season. It is tuned only to persistence. What can hold water without rotting, and wind without breaking, remains.

When an old vault finally collapses, its note vanishes with it. For decades there may be an audible absence in the basin’s weather. Then another throat opens in a younger wall, and the chord changes.

The mountain beneath all this scarcely participates. A grain shifts; a rim stone settles; wet gravel compacts a little more tightly. On geological time, the basin is close to stillness. On the time of an afternoon, however, the Old Choir passes through drought, condensation, flow, flourishing green, and ice. On the time of centuries, lanes close and galleries rise. Its slowness and activity depend entirely on where one stands to watch.

It is easy to call the place abandoned because it has streets and no footsteps. But nothing has departed. There were never inhabitants in the sense its shapes suggest. The openings were always for wind and tissue; the basins for cloud-water; the streets for the intervals the colony left between its own growths.

It is not a city that became a landscape. It is a landscape that acquired some of the habits of a city without acquiring citizens: mostly hollow, locally luxuriant, perpetually cold, and loud far beyond its size.

The ancient mountain around it is settling toward silence. The Old Choir is not.
