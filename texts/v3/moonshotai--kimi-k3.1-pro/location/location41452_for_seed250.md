## The Unworn Sea

There is an ocean above the weather, but it is not serene.

It occupies an unfinished basin near the summit of a continental uplift, higher than many of the world’s older mountains. From its rare exposed rocks, the lowland atmosphere can be seen far below: layers of cloud stacked over a dim blue gulf, storms crawling across distances that have no visible bottom. Closer at hand, the sky is ordinarily clear and pale. The sun is white. Daylight falls without unusual color, though the water breaks it into silver, slate, and a deep, cold green.

The cold is clean rather than bitter. Spray stings; exposed stone gathers thin rime in the wind; the water itself remains liquid, moving with the heavy sluggishness of a large cold body. At the surface, rain and sea are often difficult to distinguish. Wind lifts whole roofs of water into the air, and rain returns in slanting curtains so dense that they seem to be the ocean standing up.

Nearly everything is submerged.

The basin floor is not a floor in any settled sense. It is a sparse geography of newborn rises separated by immense intervals of open water: blue-black swells of glassy rock, unfinished ridges, broad blisters of mineral crust, fissures whose sides have never been touched by sediment. Some slopes descend for only a few hundred meters before ending in clear water and apparent vacancy. Others continue down beyond the reach of daylight. There are no gravel beds, no sand flats, no rounded boulders. Fractures remain sharp enough to throw back narrow flashes of sun. Pale crystalline seams run through the dark rock with unsettling straightness, then vanish where the crust has folded or foundered.

A rise may lift a ridge through the surface over the course of a day. For several hours it becomes an island: streaming, steaming faintly where cold air meets newly exposed mineral, inhabited by nothing. Then the support beneath it spreads, or the rock changes phase under pressure, and the island sinks without leaving a shoal. Elsewhere, a vertical wall appears in the sea, not as a wave but as the water draining from a mass that has risen beneath it. Before rain can gully that wall or surf can wear its foot, it has usually broken apart and gone under again.

Erosion has barely begun. The sea is full of force but almost empty of the small materials force usually makes. A little milky dust sometimes surrounds fresh fractures. Currents take it, dilute it, and leave the water clear. There is no soil anywhere.

This clarity makes the place feel emptier than a turbid ocean would. Sunlight enters in broad, slanting volumes. Near the surface, every disturbance is visible: bubbles ascending in isolated silver ropes, shear lines crossing one another, distant columns of disturbed water turning slowly in the blue. Farther down, objects do not crowd the view. They occur. A rock pinnacle several kilometers away is a major presence. A drifting ribbon of mineral foam is an event. Between such things there is only depth, light, and the occasional faint movement of a boundary between waters of different density.

The open sea has weather within itself. Internal fronts, hundreds or thousands of kilometers across, travel beneath the surface like submerged atmospheres. Along them, one layer of ocean slides over another. Where the layers rise against the young crust, they break into slow, submerged storms: vortices with hollow, nearly motionless centers; descending sheets of colder water; upwellings that carry no nourishing cloud of life, only dissolved minerals and a few green cells. A difference in temperature too small to feel against the skin can set an enormous volume in motion.

At the surface, several such systems may coincide. Then the horizon disappears. Water arrives from incompatible directions, making steep pyramids, collapsing domes, and sudden level pans surrounded by chaos. Whole provinces of sea tilt by almost imperceptible degrees, and that slight tilt is enough to send a moving wall of water across distances greater than a mountain range. Calm never means stillness. It often means that the largest motion is happening below the reach of sight.

The sound is continuous and frequently overwhelming.

Wind supplies only the highest part of it: a hard roar through spray, the hiss of rain striking rain. Beneath that are wave detonations, long pressure booms, and the dense cracking of new rock cooling under water. Some fractures ring. The sound begins with a bell-like note and descends until it is no longer heard so much as felt through the entire body of the sea. Other noises have no identifiable source. They arrive after traveling for days, layered and distorted by depth, so that a disturbance beyond the horizon seems to occur simultaneously above, below, and inside the listener.

There are no calls among these sounds. Nothing answers anything. No animal swims through the open water, burrows into the fresh crust, or shelters beneath the stones. There are no shells, no grazing scars, no nests on the temporary islands. Even the loudest eruption of noise remains purely geological and hydraulic.

Vegetation exists, but so sparsely that the sea can appear sterile. Where a sunlit mass of rock survives for more than a few seasons, it may acquire a veneer of olive-black scales, each thinner than paper and almost translucent at the edges. They lie flat against the mineral surface, drawing energy from ordinary daylight and taking the rest of their substance directly from dissolved salts. In sheltered fissures, the scales sometimes lengthen into soft, branching filaments, dark green at their bases and bronze where the light reaches them. They never form a meadow. A patch the size of a hand may be separated from the next by hundreds of kilometers of bare water.

A second kind drifts freely: isolated cellular threads, barely visible except when they cross a shaft of sun. They reproduce slowly in the cold and are often carried away from the light before they can divide. Storms tear the anchored films from their rock. Nothing eats them. Nothing competes with them. Their scarcity comes from the land’s refusal to remain still long enough to be colonized.

Yet the sea is not simply chaotic matter. At irregular intervals, usually where deep water meets the newest crystalline crust, its behavior changes.

A turbulent region may suddenly develop a perfectly smooth boundary. Within it, waves cease breaking and instead pass as exact deformations, preserving their shape across impossible distances. Heat moves without the rolling convection that should carry it. Dissolved minerals separate into impossibly pure layers. For a few seconds, or occasionally most of an hour, a volume of ocean larger than a sea on an ordinary world behaves less like countless colliding molecules than like one continuous condition.

During these intervals, new rock can appear with the precision of fabrication. Crystal extends through the water in flawless sheets; faults close without rubble; a ragged submerged ridge becomes smooth along curves no natural erosion has had time to cut. Nothing directs the process, and nothing remains that could be called a tool. There are no engines beneath the crust, no networks in the water, no constructed objects of any kind. The sea and stone simply enter a state in which matter is sorted, placed, and transformed with an exactness beyond any familiar engineering.

The state never becomes architecture. It does not stabilize into towers, platforms, or convenient permanent forms. When it breaks, it breaks completely. The smooth boundary wrinkles; separated layers mix; stored pressure returns as a chaotic front. Flawless sheets of crystal may shatter before they have fully emerged. The sea becomes louder than before, as though the brief order had merely folded several storms into one another.

These episodes give the landscape its only repeatable rule: precision is temporary, but scale is not. A small patch of coherent water is rare. More often the change crosses a horizon, and the observer sees only one local consequence—a sudden plane of silence, a straight line passing through broken waves, a ridge shining below the surface where there had been fractured rock.

The silence of those events can be more startling than the noise. For a moment, sound is conducted away so efficiently that the water seems hollow. Then the coherent front fails, and the delayed roar arrives from every direction.

From above, the Unworn Sea would look almost blank: a vast, cold brightness on the high back of the world, crossed by weather and interrupted by only a few dark points. From within, it is an immense interval between presences, repeatedly disturbed by the appearance and disappearance of matter. Nothing in it is old enough to be a ruin. Nothing is finished enough to be destroyed in the ordinary sense.

It is not a flooded land, and not quite a young ocean settled into its basin. It is the basin, the water, and the rising crust still arriving together—high above the lower clouds, nearly empty of life, without a single made thing, and loud with the work of becoming solid.
