## Vey, Above the Cloud Country

On a plateau where the air ought to be thin and cold, the town of Vey bakes beneath a white sun. Heat arrives from both directions: downward in hard, almost colorless light, and upward through the buried fractures of the rock. At the escarpments, clouds move far below, dragging their shadows across a lower country that is rarely visible. Beyond them, other highlands stand at eye level—pale, flat-topped, and distance-blue.

From a distance, Vey’s plateau looks dry and nearly empty. A few condenser stacks rise above the stone. Cistern towers catch the sun. Long aqueducts cross the open ground on slender piers, sometimes going for a kilometer without meeting a building. The old surface is an uplifted floor of salt beds, volcanic ash, and black glass, worn into broad basins and rounded ribs. Gullies have soft shoulders. Nothing appears freshly broken. Even the industrial cuts have been weathered until they seem to belong to the same slow topography.

Up close, nothing is dry.

Water stands in shallow pans no deeper than a hand. It slides in transparent sheets down tilted pavements and gathers in the seams between salt and basalt. The cliffs weep at their bases, not in streams but in innumerable bright beads. A footprint left beside a channel fills slowly with warm water. In some places the ground gives off steam without any visible vent; in others, brief rain falls from clouds so thin they are visible only when they pass across the sun.

The air tastes of wet iron. Salt dries on the lips while clothing remains damp. Black slag paving is too hot to touch, even in shade, yet its underside is slick with seepage. The whole plateau seems to be sweating through its skin.

Vey is less a town surrounded by waterworks than a town distributed through one. Its districts are small and widely separated: a pump hall, a condenser house, several stacks of dwellings around a shaded shaft, perhaps a depot where ceramic pipes are repaired. Between them lie open pans, empty fields of gypsum, and long reaches of unmarked stone. Raised causeways connect the inhabited islands. Older conduits run beneath them, some still in use, others visible as mineral-choked ribs where later builders cut through their foundations.

The engines work constantly, but their industry is mostly something seen or felt rather than heard. Flywheels turn in sealed oil baths. Vapor screws rotate behind inspection glass. Pistons travel with slow, deliberate strokes inside geothermal wells, and their vibration passes faintly through a bridge underfoot. Spent mineral salts emerge on silent conveyors and are crushed into paving. Thin plumes of white vapor stand above the stacks, luminous in the hard light.

There are no crowds. A keeper in loose white clothing may cross a court, or two figures may be visible high on a condenser gantry, tiny against the glare. Most inhabitants remain in the cooled lower rooms during the brightest hours. The loudest ordinary sounds are a drop striking a metal cup, the small click of a valve, or the faint granular tick of cooling ceramic. Sometimes several minutes pass in which nothing seems to sound at all.

There is no plant life. No reeds soften the margins of the pools; no roots lift the paving; no green film gathers in the shaded cisterns. The clear water can therefore look sterile, especially where it reflects the empty sky.

It is not sterile.

Life begins invisibly where hot, mineral-rich brine meets oxygenated condensate. Microscopic organisms harvest that chemical difference, and the larger animals harvest them. Brine fleas stipple the warm shallows in their millions, each one a bright point beneath the surface. Glass eels follow, transparent except for the dark thread of the gut and a quicksilver line along the spine. At certain outflows their young pass in numbers so great that the water appears to contain a second, moving current.

Mirror crabs occupy the warm rocks. Their broad, shallow shells are plated with a reflective mineral layer, so that a colony at noon resembles a scatter of broken mirrors. They feed quietly at the pool edges, drawing brine fleas and eel eggs into bristled mouths. Where the overflow is hottest, their shells become nearly white; in cooler channels they darken to bronze.

The predators are pale, long-legged waders with smooth skin and narrow, articulated feeding limbs. They stand so still that they can be mistaken for parts of the pipework. One will remain above a channel for an hour, then fold downward with no splash and come up holding an eel. A mirror crab taken from a rock makes one small shell-click. There is no chase, no call, no visible alarm. The other animals simply continue.

Vey’s oldest structures accommodate them. Sluices have narrow bypasses worn smooth by eels. Condensers include warm, shallow ledges where crabs gather. Some inspection pipes are clouded with transparent bodies, and keepers clean around them rather than clearing them out. The town’s engines and its animals use the same gradients of heat, salt, and water; neither can be entirely separated from the other.

Three times a day, the well-field changes pressure. Vey calls this the Turning.

No siren announces it. The first sign is a darkening of the high condenser fins as their surfaces cool. Moisture beads on them, then on the roofs, pipe bridges, and tower rims. Within a minute, water is running from every elevated surface in town, though the sky may remain cloudless and white. This is Vey’s private rain: thousands of vertical threads falling into channels, with scarcely more sound than a fingertip drawn across cloth.

The animals anticipate it. Eels gather at the gate mouths. Crabs turn their shells toward the coming flow. Waders take position along the causeways. For a short while the town is full of movement—water descending, vapor rising, gates opening, animals entering new channels—while remaining almost perfectly silent.

Then the heat returns. The fins brighten. The last condensate flashes from the black paving, and the separate pans begin to reconnect through their seeping margins.

Vey’s oldest building law concerns that water. Every roof must return what it catches. Every engine must leave a wet margin. No channel may be sealed merely because it is inconvenient. A settlement can own a cistern, but not the passage between cisterns.

From the outer escarpment, after a Turning, the town looks less constructed than precipitated: pale towers, salt terraces, scattered mirrors of animal shells, and vapor lifting through empty distances. It is neither ruined nor newly finished. The plateau has worn Vey into tolerable shapes, and Vey has learned to work within them.

Below, the clouds continue their slow passage. Above, the light remains fierce. Water rises through hot stone, passes through engines and transparent bodies, falls from bright metal, and enters the ground again. In the silence, the entire visible region seems to be breathing very slowly through its machinery.
