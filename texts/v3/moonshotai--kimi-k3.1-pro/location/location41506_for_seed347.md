## Below the Rim

The plateau gives almost no warning. It is a cold table of limestone and thin turf, combed by rain, with nothing taller than a cushion of heather. Then the ground ends in a white, splintered rim.

Below it lies a basin nine kilometres long and, in places, three kilometres wide. Its floor is far beneath the surface of the surrounding country, but it is not a cave. The depression is open along its length, and broad enough that daylight reaches the bottom without difficulty. At noon the light is simply daylight: cloud-silvered, colourless on wet stone, green on the leaves. Only in early morning and late evening, when the walls hold long blue shadows, does the place feel sunken.

It is visibly young. The rim has not weathered smooth. Fresh fractures run through the pale rock, and the talus below is made of blocks rather than boulders, their corners still sharp enough to tear a hand. Water has not had time to round the gravel into cobbles. New collapses expose bright scars among older, rain-blackened walls. Elsewhere, whole slices of the rim have recently slipped, leaving raw slabs tilted over the wetlands beneath.

From above, the floor first appears to be an unusually crowded marsh. Then its repetitions become clear. Ponds lie at definite heights, one above another. Curved banks hold them. Channels turn at maintained openings rather than wandering freely. Islands have low, layered roofs. Narrow embankments connect patches of higher ground, and dark openings pass beneath them at water level. What looked like vegetation and flood debris resolves into retaining walls, weirs, houses, paths, and canals.

The whole basin is built.

Water is not confined to its waterways. It seeps from fractures in the walls, gathers in translucent beads beneath overhangs, and moves through the peat without showing itself. Some hillsides are dry to the eye but give underfoot with a slow, cold exhalation. After rain, hundreds of temporary threads appear on the rock, too small to be streams. They join existing runnels, disappear into moss, and emerge again far below. At the lowest end of the basin, the remaining water enters a chain of still black pools and drains through a narrow limestone slot.

The cold is wet rather than white. Through most of the growing season, the air stays only a little above freezing, though the shelter of the walls allows brief, mild afternoons. Breath clouds at dawn. Old snow remains in shaded clefts while new shoots emerge a few steps away. Stone steals warmth from anything resting on it, and moisture condenses on fur, leaves, and the undersides of wooden roofs.

Yet the basin is intensely green. Its lushness is not height but thickness. Moss and liverwort join one surface to another until bare rock becomes uncommon. Sedges stand densely along the channels, their old brown blades still caught among the new. Horsetails colonize fresh silt almost as soon as it settles. Water crowfoot streams beneath the surfaces of slow pools. Dwarf willows and low alders fill the sheltered ground, repeatedly gnawed back and repeatedly sprouting, so that feeding produces thicker thickets rather than clearings. In protected cracks there are ferns with cold, water-heavy fronds. Even the submerged stones wear films of algae and diatoms, olive, rust, or near-black according to depth and current.

The builders are broad, dark rodents with webbed hind feet and iron-orange incisors. They work alone, in pairs, or in loose family groups. A stem is gnawed through, dragged into position, and forced into mud. Stones are rolled or carried short distances and wedged where the current can press them tighter. Mud is brought against the chest and patted home with the forepaws. Nothing is fired, forged, hinged, or machined. No tool lies beside any structure. The largest dam in the basin is the product of teeth, bodies, water pressure, and persistence.

Their houses begin as simple heaps over submerged entrances, but few remain simple. A chamber is enlarged; a second is added above the flood line; a food cache is sunk in cold water nearby. When part of a lodge becomes too wet, it is sealed with silt and built over. Neighbouring houses acquire common walls. Paths cross their roofs. Drift caught against them becomes soil, and willows root there, binding the whole mass more securely. The oldest foundations are dark with waterlogged wood and peat, some lightly cemented by calcite deposited from seeping water. New work on top is pale, sharp, and fragrant with sap.

The middle of the basin is the densest. Lodges have merged into terraced masses whose slopes contain more chambers than their outlines suggest. Canals run between them like streets, though each canal is also a feeding ground, an escape route, and a means of moving building material. Small ponds serve as open junctions. Spillways carry excess water from one level to the next. Low, muddy paths link the few places where an animal can remain dry above ground. There is almost no unused interval between one function and another: a house wall is also a bank, a bank is also a root mat, and a root mat shelters a nest.

Toward the head of the basin, the city becomes younger and more experimental. Fresh rubble interrupts the canals. Short dams stand where broader ones may follow; others have already been breached and abandoned. New springs appear after small collapses, and builders gather around them within days. At the lower end, by contrast, the works are broad and deep. Water moves slowly through old, heavily vegetated ponds, depositing fine sediment before disappearing into the outlet slot.

There is no central plan and no single city-wide authority. Hundreds of family territories overlap and abut. Nevertheless, a raised dam changes the water upstream, and a neglected spillway can flood neighbours. Repairs therefore spread by necessity. A breach draws workers not because anyone has commanded them, but because the changed current exposes a food cache, deepens a route, or threatens a chamber. Water level is more important than any boundary on land.

Life occupies nearly every remaining space. Small fish hold beneath overhangs and dart into the rooted shallows after insect larvae. Snails and freshwater mussels line the quieter margins. Dippers walk underwater along the stony runs, while diving ducks use the deeper canals. In the brief warm weeks, the air above the sedges fills with midges, and swallows work low over the ponds to take them. Voles tunnel through the upper banks. Frogs gather wherever a shallow pool warms slightly faster than the rest.

Predators enter along the same routes as everything else. An otter can pass through several districts without leaving the water, moving from fish shelter to fish shelter. Herons wait beside spillways where the flow concentrates prey. At night, owls drop silently from the rim toward movement on the lodge roofs. The builders answer most disturbances with a disappearance rather than a fight: a swirl, a few bubbles, and an empty canal.

For so much activity, the basin is extraordinarily quiet. Its water descends in many small steps, so there is no single river-roar. Moss, peat, and dense vegetation absorb echoes, and much of the wind passes overhead without reaching the floor. At first a visitor may hear only a faint, generalized seeping. Then individual sounds separate out: a twig turning in a spillway, wet gnawing inside a thicket, the tick of one small stone against another, the single note of a bird. Occasionally a tail strikes water and the report travels startlingly far. The silence afterward seems to spread outward with the ripples.

Nothing remains settled for long. Rain raises a pond by the width of a finger, and banks are heightened before morning. Fish move into newly flooded vegetation; predators follow. Winter ice grips the tops of dams and pulls material loose when it shifts. Thaw sends limbs, silt, and whole sections of abandoned wall downstream, where much of it is immediately reused. Freeze and thaw widen cracks in the basin walls. A fall of rock may erase a district and create the foundation of another.

Nor does vacancy last. An empty lodge becomes a bird roost, then a root-bound island, then the base of new construction. A dry niche gathers seeds and is green before it has time to gather dust. An unused channel narrows with sedge, while another is deepened by paws and current. No map of the basin would remain accurate for many seasons.

Seen from the rim, the place can almost be mistaken for roofs, streets, reservoirs, and terraced gardens. Down within it, every category changes. A wall is a nursery; a street is a fishing ground; a ruin is a peat bank; a garden is also a food store and a building site. Construction and ecology are not separate activities here.

It is not a settlement imposed on a wetland. It is a wetland that has acquired, through water, roots, teeth, paws, eggs, and continual repair, the density and organization of a city.
