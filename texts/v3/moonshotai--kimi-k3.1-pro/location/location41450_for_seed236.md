## Sere, the Grown Town

Sere stands so high that the country beneath it has lost its detail. From its rim, the lower world is an expanse of ochre salt and black mineral dunes, crossed by no river and softened by no distant haze of rain. The summit itself is young: a small upthrust of pale tuff, sulfur stone, and blue-black glass, only a few hundred paces across. Its fractures have not had time to round. Crystals still keep their first clean faces, and where the outer cliffs have split, they shine as if broken yesterday.

Yet the summit is warm. Gentle heat rises from slow mineral reactions deep below, and the dark terraces hold the sun. At an altitude where the surrounding slopes are cold, Sere’s sheltered basin feels like a sun-warmed courtyard. The air is thin, still, and completely dry. There is no dew at dawn, no frost in the shaded crevices, no water sealed in the rock. Life here does not merely conserve water. Its chemistry has another basis altogether: clear oils, resinous salts, and warm, slow reactions that never require a drop.

The light is ordinary daylight. Sunlight falls white-gold across the upper courts; the shadows beneath the roofs are blue and sharp. Nothing glows. Nothing needs to.

Seen from the rim, Sere looks like a dense green thicket filling the summit cup. Entered through one of the narrow clefts, it becomes a town.

Lanes turn between stacked vaults. Small courts open unexpectedly beneath latticed roofs. Stairways rise through hollow towers, and bridges join upper chambers whose walls are green on the outside, ivory within. Some passages are barely wide enough for the animals that use them; others broaden into high, cathedral-like naves where the canopy admits long diagonals of sun. The settlement has the footprint of a hamlet but the density of a city built upward and downward rather than out. From its highest bridge, every district can be seen at once. Exploring all its levels would take much longer.

Nothing here has been cut, fired, hammered, or fitted. Every wall is alive, was once alive, or was secreted by life.

The principal structures begin as bundles of hollow stems. They lean together as they grow, fuse at their points of contact, and thicken into arches. When older stems die, their tissues slowly mineralize, becoming lightweight structural stone. New growth climbs over them, so that each generation of vaults stands partly inside the skeleton of the last. Floors are layered with fallen stem-sheaths and the smooth nacre secreted by small wall-dwelling animals. Steps occur where roots have thickened at regular intervals. Ventilation shafts are the empty hearts of old towers. There are no hinges, tools, lamps, or machines—not even a sharpened stone. Nevertheless, there are thresholds, conduits, drains for dry dust, storage cells, and streets that remain open from century to century.

The streets are not exactly planned and not exactly accidental. Neighboring colonies of stems chemically refuse to touch, leaving narrow seams between them. Browsing animals keep those seams cropped. A lane is therefore both a boundary and a feeding trace; a market-like court is simply a place where several boundaries meet. The same growth that produces leaves produces roofs. The same appetite that sustains the fauna maintains the roads.

The vegetation is lush without being moist. Plump leaves hang in overlapping curtains, their fullness coming from pale oil and tiny gas chambers rather than sap-rich flesh. Their surfaces range from soft bottle green to bronze, violet-black, and the pale green of new buds. Some are waxy and reflective; others are covered in velvet so fine that two leaves can slide against one another without a whisper. Beneath them, low cushions of coppery growth fill every fracture. Root filaments penetrate the mineral faces, dissolving what they need and leaving delicate white traceries on the glass.

In the deepest shade, the plants grow in thin, broad ribbons, turning nearly all their surface toward borrowed light. Higher up, they become dense and leathery, folding at night and opening again over several hours of morning warmth. Their flowers are small but numerous: waxy cups filled with dry, fragrant pollen and a few beads of golden oil. The scent of the town is warm resin, crushed green stems, and, near the oldest vents, a faint mineral sharpness.

Animals are everywhere.

Veilwings drift beneath the vaults, broad fringed wings moving so slowly that their flight seems closer to falling upward. Hare-sized browsers stand nearly shoulder to shoulder in the narrower lanes, cropping shoots with padded jaws. Their six soft feet make no sound on the nacre floors. Colonies of masonbacks occupy the walls, each no larger than a grain of rice, smoothing the chambers around them with layer upon layer of secretion. Suede-skinned hunters lie motionless under leaf bases for days, their outlines visible only when an eye changes color. Fine pale scavengers move through the litter in such numbers that a fallen frond seems gradually to dissolve into the floor.

There are animals in the roofs, animals inside the walls, animals sleeping in the hollow stairs, and animals suspended in the still air between them. Their abundance is visible rather than audible. None sing. None buzz. None cry out to establish territory. They signal through scent, touch, and slow changes in the color of their skin. Even the veilwings’ wings are fringed to silence the air.

Consequently, Sere is almost perfectly quiet. There is no running water, no rain, no wet foliage to drip or rustle. The basin is sheltered from the high winds, and the felted leaves absorb what little sound remains. A footstep on the central glass court carries startlingly far. At long intervals, deep within the young mountain, a mineral seam completes some years-long adjustment and gives a single subdued tick. The sound is felt through the floors as much as heard. Then the silence closes again, without drama.

The town changes at nearly the pace of its stone.

During the warm day, the living oils thin, flowers open, and the animals move. At night the oils thicken; the vegetation folds, and even the hunters become difficult to distinguish from the walls. A new arch may take generations to close. An old doorway narrows by less than a finger’s width in an ordinary lifetime. Nothing is repaired. It is overgrown, reinforced, or gently abandoned while another passage becomes convenient.

Death does not produce wet decay. Dead tissue dries further, becomes papery, and is gradually unmade by scavengers into oil, fiber, and mineral meal. Roots take back the meal. Masonbacks use the finer dust. Because so little is lost and nothing is weathered away, the past remains crisp beneath the present: older vaults lie under newer ones with their edges intact, their chambers filled with odorless powder and the delicate skeletons of former roofs.

Sere is therefore young without looking bare, ancient in its layering without being ruined. Beyond its outermost root, the cliff is raw and empty. Within that boundary, nearly every surface is occupied. The whole settlement could be crossed in twenty unhurried minutes, yet it contains thousands of lives and centuries of unfinished construction.

It is not a city reclaimed by a forest. No builders came first, and no inhabitants will later return. It is a settlement whose architecture, ecology, and citizenship are the same patient process. From above it resembles a town; from within, a grove; in the depths of its walls, a single vast anatomy.

At noon, sunlight crosses the open court. Pollen hangs nearly motionless in the warm air. A veilwing passes beneath an arch that has been closing for two hundred years. Nothing hurries to complete it.

The city is the slowest animal in Sere.
