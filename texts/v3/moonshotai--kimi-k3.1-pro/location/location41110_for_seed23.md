## The Bright Cistern

The Bright Cistern is a small, high room in the mountains with its roof torn off.

An old glacier made it, cutting a nearly circular hollow into blue-grey limestone and then withdrawing so long ago that even the freshest-looking scars are weathered. The floor is less than four hundred paces across. The walls rise in steep, irregular tiers, holding snow on their upper ledges. A single narrow cleft admits the path in and lets the overflow out. From above, the whole place resembles a stone cup set down among the peaks.

The cup is never empty, though it is rarely full.

Water occurs here as films, black threads, trembling beads, and shallow pans without reeds or grassy margins. It seeps from the walls at many heights. It stands in the worn basins of the floor and lies just beneath the gravel, so that a heel pressed into apparently dry ground brings water welling around the boot. Some of it is fresh and tastes of iron. The lower pools are bitter with salts and remain liquid in cold that splits unprotected skin.

The rock has been shaped by this seepage for ages. Its joints are rounded; its runnels are deep and smooth. Pale tufa has accumulated in dams and scalloped lips, then been cut through, frozen, polished, and deposited again. There is little loose scree. Most of what could fall fell long ago. Even the town’s oldest stairs have become difficult to distinguish from natural ledges.

And over everything rests an almost intolerable brightness.

Light strikes the upper snowfields and returns from wet mica, white salt, cloud, ice, and water until the Cistern seems illuminated from several directions at once. Shadows are shallow and doubled. The sky, when visible, is often less white than the walls. Residents wear slit goggles carved from bone and smear dark mineral paste beneath their eyes. Visitors commonly spend their first day squinting, weeping, and walking with one hand on the rock.

The light gives almost no warmth. Breath still freezes. Water still numbs the fingers in seconds. Ice remains ice while glittering fiercely enough to hurt the eyes.

Its strangest property is that strong light lifts water without heating it. A film on an exposed slab first becomes brilliant, then seems to loosen. It rises as a cold, silvery dust too fine to wet the skin. Exposed ice dwindles in the same way, without melting. In shade the movement reverses: moisture gathers invisibly on cold stone, swells into droplets, and begins to run. Thus an open bowl may empty beneath a clear sky while the underside of a nearby overhang starts to rain.

Nothing in the Cistern is simply wet or dry. Dryness means only that the water is elsewhere in the room.

This produces weather on the scale of a courtyard. Mist rises in separate columns from the floor, braids against the walls, and tears itself apart in small contrary currents. Droplets condense on ropes and the undersides of stairs. Fine rime opens across a pool, then pales and vanishes. A passage that was dripping an hour ago becomes white with salt; a dry threshold begins to sweat. The changes are fast, sometimes violent in appearance, but almost soundless.

That is the first contradiction most visitors notice: the place boils, and yet it hushes.

The seepage is too dispersed to babble. The mist has no voice. Porous rime, wet gravel, and the felt hung throughout the settlement absorb small noises. Even the birds are nearly mute. One hears the tick of a hoof, the faint click of a stone tool through its hide pad, the papery snapping of frost on an awning. Often the loudest sound is one’s own breathing inside the hood.

The town occupies the eastern and southern walls in forty-odd households and several times that many rooms. It is a hamlet by population and a city by compression. Houses stand back to back and above one another, fitted into old fractures and extended on corbelled shelves. Every roof seems to serve someone else as a floor, bridge, pen, or water-catching platform. Streets are mostly stairs. Some pass through houses, skirt sleeping chambers, cross a communal cistern, and emerge two tiers higher beside a rookery.

The buildings are made without large timber. Walls are dry-laid slate and limestone, their courses adjusted with stone hammers, antler wedges, and patient hand pressure. Roofs are corbelled or formed from overlapping slabs. Hide panels close the wider openings; thick felts provide doors, insulation, and movable shade. A little hammered copper exists in awls, needles, and knife edges, but most tools are stone, bone, and horn. The sophistication of the place lies in fit and accumulated knowledge rather than mechanism.

Its waterworks are especially intricate. Stone runnels no wider than two fingers cross entire alleys. Lidded cisterns sit in permanent shade, because drinking water left open to the light may slowly lift away. Catchment felts hang across dark cracks where rising moisture condenses; workers wring them into clay jars by hand. Small gates divert overflow from one pan to another. Roof bridges double as aqueduct supports. Old houses have become cisterns, old cisterns have become foundations, and sealed doorways appear at ankle height in lanes that have risen over centuries.

The inhabitants dress in layered grey felt and oily hide, with fur turned inward and loose outer garments that shed rime. They work barehanded only briefly. Heat is more precious than water: the sparse vegetation provides almost no fuel, so dried dung from the domestic animals is hoarded for communal hearth-pits. Many rooms are warmed chiefly by bodies, animals, and stone walls that have been enclosed for generations. Lamps burn animal fat, but in the white day they are valued more for their small warmth than their light.

Despite the crowding, people speak little while working. Close quarters have made quietness a practical courtesy, and much of the ordinary labor is directed by hand signs. A palm turned downward means cover the cisterns. Two fingers brushed across the goggles mean a mist-turning is beginning. The sight of the settlement from across the floor is consequently strange: scores of people hauling, wrapping, scraping, climbing, carrying, and signaling, all in a silence that seems too large for them.

Above the waterline, vegetation scarcely troubles the bareness. There are coins of orange and black lichen, a few cushions of rusty growth in deep shelters, and occasional dwarf shrubs whose leafless twigs look like wire driven into cracks. There are no mossy lawns, no reed beds, no trees. The Cistern is saturated without being lush.

Its real pasture lies underwater. A thin charcoal-colored film coats the submerged stone, so slick and dark that the clearest pools look empty until something feeds in them. It draws nourishment from the mineral seep and regrows rapidly under the white light. Where grazing animals disturb it, pale trails remain visible for a few minutes before closing again.

Glass eels crowd the lower pans. They are finger-length, transparent except for their gills and a dark line of gut, and they pack themselves so densely that a pool sometimes seems to develop a second, moving bottom. Seep-midges hatch above them in columns resembling soot or smoke, though their wings are too small to hear. Oily-furred rock shrews hunt through the wall crevices and along the runnels. Their nests, stolen fibers, and stored food are found inside almost every human wall.

Slatewings roost by the hundreds beneath eaves and ledges. Their upper feathers are the color of wet stone; their underwings are chalk-white. They have no ordinary call. Alarm, courtship, and territorial threats are communicated by posture and sudden flashes of white as the wings open. When a whole colony rises, the brightness beneath them flickers like light on disturbed water.

The domestic shag-goats are broader and lower than their wild mountain relatives, with splayed hooves and guard hairs that collect needles of ice. They stand pressed together in roof pens, breathing fog over one another, giving milk, hair, hide, dung, and occasionally meat. Pale martens follow their scent and the scent of the rookeries. A marten may cross an entire occupied lane without being noticed; often only the shrews’ abrupt disappearance reveals that it has passed.

Several times in a bright day, the whole basin may undergo what the residents call a turning.

Sometimes the eels settle first. Sometimes the midges rise together, or the upper walls become too dazzling to look at. Then water begins visibly leaving the floor. Mist columns stand up between the houses. Pools shallow around their rims. Rime races along ropes and freezes the outer hair of the goats. The slatewings pour from the walls, and their passing shadows stripe the ground.

People respond without panic but without pause. Cistern lids are seated. Felts are turned. Animals are moved from ledges where ceiling-rain is about to begin. Jars are brought indoors. A child may be sent running along a roof to close one stone gate, setting off three other tasks below.

For several minutes the Cistern seems unable to keep its shape. Then moisture condenses high in the shaded fractures and comes back as drops, rivulets, and occasional thin falls of ice grains. The upper town drips onto the lower. The mist thins. The pools begin to refill, though not always in the same places. A path has become glazed; a pen is dry; a new colony of eels has been stranded in a pan too shallow to survive the next turning. Repairs begin before the last water has fallen.

Yet the place is not being destroyed by all this activity. Its deeper state is remarkably settled. The great walls do not shift. The oldest channels remain the most reliable. Generations of feet have polished thresholds into shallow bowls, and generations of hands have worn the same smooth grips beside the same stairs. Residents know which black seam will always weep, which ledge can carry another room, which apparently natural pool must never be covered, and which stretch of dry wall should not be trusted after a bright morning.

Children learn the rules early: keep drinking water in darkness; never mistake dry stone for safe stone; do not build across a living seep; and when the midges rise, look first to the animals. Beyond these are older, quieter customs about shared shade, shared fuel, and how loudly one may work when another household sleeps on the other side of a wall.

At the outlet cleft, the overflowing water gathers into something more nearly resembling an ordinary stream. A short distance beyond it, light dries by warmth again, footsteps return to their normal sharpness, and running water can be heard. Residents going outside often find the lower mountains exposed, harsh, and offensively loud.

Inside the Cistern, the crowded stillness resumes. Wings open and shut. Eels turn in the pans. Mist rises through the stacked rooms. Cold drops gather beneath old stone and fall without splashing. The mountain took ages to make the room; water, animals, and people spend every moment remaking what passes across its surfaces.
