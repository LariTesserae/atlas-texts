## The Still Hollows

A country of air lies beneath a country of stone.

The Still Hollows extend for tens of kilometres beneath a dry limestone plateau, but they never offer a single comprehensive view. Their openness is a matter of volume and connection, not visibility. High collapse halls give onto long, level galleries; the galleries descend into broad, low spaces where the roof seems to rest on darkness; narrow fractures lead to isolated chambers and blind shafts. Some of the halls could contain a village, though there is no village and never has been. Between them are long stretches of bare passage where the principal feature is distance.

The limestone is old, but the landscape inside it is young. The present system took shape when the water table fell and a connected body of flooded passages drained. Unsupported roof spans failed, galleries opened into one another, and whole hillsides quietly acquired hollow interiors. Then the water ceased to do much of anything. Consequently, the Hollows look freshly exposed rather than anciently decorated. New fractures are chalk-white against older satin-gray walls. Fallen slabs retain splintered edges. Their corners have not been rounded by floods, and there are no banks of water-polished cobble, no deep flowstone curtains, no forests of joined stalactite and stalagmite. Calcite occurs mostly as a first transparent skin over damp stone, or as small, clear beads at the ends of seams.

Much of the floor is dry: pale grit, plates of roof-flake, fine mineral dust undisturbed except by animal tracks. The wet places are startlingly local. A ceiling may sweat over one patch of floor while dust lies powder-dry a few steps away. Condensation gathers along cold joints, darkens a hand’s breadth of limestone, and occasionally releases a drop. The resulting pools are generally small—basins in broken slabs, lens-shaped hollows in clay, a few still sheets no deeper than a finger. None has a meaningful inlet or outlet. Rain enters only through the widely separated roof openings, where it produces a brief ticking and a smell of wet dust before the openings become dry mouths again. Nowhere does water gather enough force to become a stream.

The air remains cool, approximately the temperature of a cold cellar, and almost motionless. Away from the animal colonies it smells faintly of crushed limestone. Near them it acquires ammonia, musk, and the warm, dry odor of packed bodies. There is no underground weather beyond these differences. Mist does not travel through the galleries; clouds do not form and clear. Cold simply resides in the stone.

Light is similarly rationed. At the roof openings, daylight arrives diluted by depth and reflection, turning a little limestone gray without producing a strong beam. A few ferns grow on ledges there, together with separate cushions of moss and the occasional root that has followed a fracture downward and stopped in barren air. These plants do not form a community so much as a scattering of individual exceptions. Farther in, vegetation disappears. Damp walls carry some colorless fungal growth and thin bacterial films, but most surfaces are mineral and nothing else.

In the deepest chambers, the only natural illumination comes from sparse colonies of luminous fly larvae along moist overhangs. Their blue-green points are too faint to reveal the roof. They merely give the darkness a few fixed locations. A chamber may therefore be acoustically immense and visually consist of six lights, a wet edge, and nothing.

Yet the Hollows are overwhelmingly alive.

The principal roosts contain hundreds of thousands of bats; across the whole region, their numbers run into millions. They occupy limited sections of ceiling, often separated from one another by kilometres of nearly vacant passage. At a distance, a roost appears as a dark, irregular stain. Closer, it resolves into bodies packed wing to wing, with smaller clusters hanging along fractures and solitary animals occupying the outer margins. When a colony shifts, the sound begins as a dry rustling and becomes a continuous wing-hiss, threaded with contact calls, small squeals, and the leathery clatter of animals jostling for position. In a large hall, the echo turns this into something like heavy rain arriving from several directions.

The bats feed beyond the plateau and import nearly all the energy that sustains the interior. Beneath their roosts, guano supports a second abundance: cave crickets, beetles, springtails, mites, fly larvae, and small, quick predators moving among them. These communities are numerous beyond easy counting, but sharply localized. One can stand on bare dust and see almost nothing alive, then cross an invisible boundary into a floor that ticks, creeps, and flexes with insects. The isolated pools contain their own sparse populations—white amphipods, minute worms, and other eyeless animals moving through clear water with no plants to shelter them. Larger predators are few and widely spaced.

This is the Hollows’ particular balance of emptiness and multitude. Life is not evenly distributed; it occurs in archipelagoes. But where it occurs, it occurs in orders of magnitude, and its influence travels far beyond its bodies. Even the emptiest gallery carries distant wing-noise. A drop striking a pool produces several apparent drops as the echo moves from wall to wall. Cricket rasping may be audible around a corner long before the faintly lit patch of guano that supports it comes into view. There is little wind or running water to mask anything. The place can look vacant and remain loud.

Nothing has been built here. There are no steps, markers, shelters, enclosing walls, or worn routes. Even technology scarcely becomes an object. At the illuminated margins, clever foraging animals occasionally use what they find: a stone as an anvil, a stick once inserted into a crevice and then discarded. Nothing is shaped, joined, hafted, or kept. Once dropped, such an object is indistinguishable from the natural debris around it. Use never accumulates into alteration.

For all its motion, the Hollows have no strong trajectory. Their stillness is not inactivity; it is the absence of net change. Temperature holds. Condensation and slow seepage balance the water lost from the pools. The insect communities process what falls from the roosts, leaving dark, shallow deposits rather than building new hills. Plant life cannot spread beyond the little light available to it. With almost no flowing water, the fresh rock edges remain fresh. Animal numbers rise and fall around the same enduring pattern, but one generation does not make the landscape different for the next.

A bat crosses a hall. A droplet falls. A cricket molts beneath a stone. None of these events becomes a season.

The rules of the place are therefore simple. Water is stored, not transported. Food arrives mainly on wings. Light marks a boundary rather than a resource to be competed for across the whole region. Distance is measured best by sound. And bare ground does not imply an absence of life; it may only mean standing between colonies.

From the middle of one of the great dry floors, no animal need be visible. The roof is beyond the light, the walls are separated by darkness, and the nearest pool may be nothing more than a black line around a fragment of limestone. Still, the air carries the far-off collective murmur of wings, the hard notes of dripping water, and the small continuous static of insects. The stone keeps its new edges. The chambers keep their dimensions. Somewhere out of sight, a multitude shifts together, and the Hollows answer with the same long sound.
