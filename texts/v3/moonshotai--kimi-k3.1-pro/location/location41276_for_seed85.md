## The Summit Ward

At an altitude where mountains ought to give way to ice and thin air, there is water.

It is not rain, cloud, or a flood passing through. It simply continues: upward beyond the height of former weather, downward around the roots of the mountains, outward beyond useful measurement. There is no surface. A swimmer ascending from the highest peak does not approach daylight or air. The darkness merely becomes more complete.

The water is uniformly cool, with the clean mineral chill of shaded stone. It has no thermoclines, no brackish layers, no warmer places sheltered among rocks. Pressure does not increase with depth. Instruments carried from the summit into the abyss return the same reading, as though the water has declined to make any use of altitude. It is extraordinarily clear, but clarity is difficult to appreciate where there is almost no light.

One mountain reaches up through this suspended ocean.

It is young, a splinter of black volcanic glass raised so recently that its fracture faces still conchoidal and sharp. Needle-like ridges run from its summit into water too dark to follow. Nothing has rounded them. There is no scree beneath the cliffs, no sediment in their crevices, no pale abrasion where currents might once have dragged sand. The mountain looks less formed by an ancient eruption than cut, moments ago, from a larger piece of night.

A small town occupies its top.

The Summit Ward consists of nineteen buildings on three incomplete terraces, all contained within a boundary that a slow swimmer could circle in under an hour. It has streets, addresses, service conduits, dwelling cells, switching stations, and a civic hall with the proportions of a narrow cathedral. Yet most of the town is interval. Buildings stand tens or hundreds of meters apart, separated by unoccupied water and portions of exposed summit. From any one structure, only two or three others can be seen even when a lamp is carried.

The largest open space is the central plaza. It contains no monument except a metal disc the size of a coin, set flush into the glass. Every civic distance is measured from it. The town’s roads radiate from that point and then stop, sometimes at buildings, sometimes at the edge of a cliff, and sometimes in open water where their purpose has either been fulfilled or forgotten.

Nothing here was assembled in the ordinary sense. The walls have no courses, welds, or fasteners. Where black ceramic meets the mountain, their crystalline structures continue into one another; architecture and geology are different expressions of the same fabrication event. Window corners remain acute. Stairs have no worn treads. Even the slender conduits that leave the pumping stations are polished and unmarked, though several end abruptly after a few meters. Their functions continue invisibly through the water.

The Ward’s machinery is dispersed rather than installed. It borrows molecules when necessary, arranges them into brief computational or mechanical relationships, and returns them to being water. There is no central engine and no visible moving part. The entire town may be a pattern sustained in the medium around it, with the buildings serving only as its more permanent annotations.

Some of its conveniences are immediately apparent. The streets supply weight. A person standing on one feels a pavement underfoot, though there is nothing but water beneath the boots. At intersections, “down” rotates smoothly to meet the next street. Step off the pavement, and weight becomes a local arrangement in which you no longer participate. One can drift beside pedestrians who are walking at right angles to one another, each accompanied by a private and perfectly well-behaved gravity.

The buildings are flooded. Their doors were never intended to keep water out. A closed door instead refuses to translate an approach into an entrance; an authorized visitor passes through without observing a moment at which the barrier opens. Inside, the same cool water fills every room. Within the municipal boundary, it can also be breathed. The first breath remains an act of trust: cold liquid enters the lungs, and some distributed civic process removes the practical distinction between respiration and drowning. Beyond the boundary, that courtesy ends.

There is no day at the summit. If a sun exists above the water, nothing of it reaches here. The Ward has seven small lights, fixed at street junctions and on the highest surveying mast. To the eye they are almost identical: pallid points, weak enough that the hand can obscure one without casting a noticeable shadow. They are address markers rather than illumination. Each identifies a location to instruments, not to sight.

A carried lamp reveals edges one at a time. Its beam barely scatters, so there are no soft underwater halos and no suspended particles shining within it. A wall appears, then a stair, then the black line of a conduit. Beyond the beam, objects return to darkness without seeming to move. The water tastes faintly metallic and carries no organic odor.

Nothing grows on the town. There are no algae in its joints, no encrusting colonies beneath its ledges, no film of simpler life clouding the windows. There is nothing to root, nothing to flower, and no food web in the usual sense. The material world is almost entirely mineral and constructed.

Fauna are present only as an unlikely exception.

The creature occasionally seen near the outer terrace is called a glass eel, though it is neither glass nor confidently an eel. It is a transparent ribbon about two meters long, thickened at one end around a single black joint. A row of dim points becomes visible inside it when it passes close to an address light. It swims without flexing and leaves no wake; water ahead of it appears to exchange places with water behind. It has never been observed feeding, resting, reproducing, or dying. It may be an animal maintained by the town, a maintenance process that has acquired the habit of an animal, or the last surviving citizen of a settlement whose other inhabitants require no bodies.

Sound is nearly absent. There is no current to hiss around the cliffs, no hull to groan under changing pressure, no weather overhead. The mountain carries a continuous vibration too low to be called a note, felt more in the teeth than heard. Voices lose their echoes almost immediately, even inside the civic hall. When a visitor becomes still, the loudest presence is usually the heartbeat, returned faintly by the surrounding water a fraction late.

The stillness is more than inactivity. It is conserved.

A stone released away from a street remains where it is released. Water displaced by a swimmer closes without turbulence. Heat introduced by a body disappears without warming anything else. No sediment settles because none is produced; no light dims because none consumes its source. The town does not repair itself in any observable way. Repair would imply that damage had been allowed to become part of its history.

In the civic hall there is a clock with many concentric hands, all motionless. It does not count hours. According to the single inscription beneath it, the mechanism measures the settlement’s accumulated departure from its completed state. Its reading is zero.

Consequently, the Ward’s youth is not a stage through which it is passing. The mountain will not gradually soften. The ceramic will not develop a patina. Doors may admit visitors, streets may lend them weight, and the glass eel may cross the outer darkness, but when each interaction ends, the place resumes the same exact relation to itself. It does not repeat a cycle. It declines to accumulate one.

From the boundary, the whole settlement looks small enough to extinguish with a hand: seven faint lights, a few sheer faces caught in a carried beam, and the black summit falling away beneath them. It does not feel abandoned, exactly. Abandonment is something that happens after habitation. Nor does it feel unfinished.

It feels like the completed moment of a town, held open and given the standing of a natural law.

The highest light does not blink. Above it, the water continues upward, with no surface and no promise of air.
