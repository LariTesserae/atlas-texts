## The High Clamor

At the height where the lower country disappears beneath its own weather, the mountains do not narrow into peaks. They open into a chain of broad basins, and every basin is full.

Not full of water, though water is everywhere. Full of stone.

Pale, quartz-rich rock crowds upward in domes, lobes, and rounded backs, each mass pressed so closely against its neighbors that there is no ordinary ground between them. Some crowns are broad enough to stand on; others rise no higher than a knee. Between them run wet seams, slots, and narrow wells of shadow. There are no open flats, no fields of loose gravel, no soil softening the intervals. Every level surface is the top of something solid. Everything else is a fracture or another stone.

Ancient jointing divided the uplifted bedrock into this dense mosaic. Water has since rounded every exposed edge without freeing many blocks. The high rims are blunt, the basin walls worn into overlapping shoulders, and even the deepest accessible fissures have smoothed lips. There is no fresh talus and very little evidence of sudden breakage. From a distance, the terrain looks finely corrugated, almost uniform. Close up, all regularity disappears: no two crowns match, no seam continues straight for long, and nothing has been placed for the convenience of passage.

The main basin extends far beyond comfortable walking distance. Its far rim is reduced by distance to a chalky line, though it stands higher than the near one. Beyond it, saddles and crowded hollows catch the light in succession, so that the whole region seems to continue by repetition rather than by empty space.

It is not a drowned landscape. Few pools are broad enough to suggest a lake. The wetness is finer and more pervasive. Moisture condenses on the cool rock and gathers in beads along minute crystal boundaries. It slides off in films too thin to see until they flash, collects in shallow cups, and spills from one stone to the next in thousands of short, white threads. Seams that appear dry from above reveal water just below their lips. Some masses weep continuously from their upper surfaces, though no stream enters them; others remain almost dry on their crowns while their bases are polished by currents hidden underneath.

The water is cold and exceptionally clear. Where it rests, it holds faint amber, slate, and rust-colored depths, all mineral in origin. Where it moves, it breaks the landscape into small pieces of reflected brightness. Spray hangs low over the more active seams, but the mist never gathers into fog or lifts into weather. It maintains a nearly constant height, continually removed by the thin upper air and continually replaced below.

Above the region stands a vast, luminous overcast. Moist air feeds it from the unseen valleys; its substance is always being renewed, yet its outline remains essentially the same. The light filtering through is intense and almost shadowless. Dry stone glares white. Wet stone throws the brightness back in harder, more concentrated flashes. Even the narrow shadows between masses are filled with reflected radiance, leaving only the deepest underwater recesses genuinely dark. The light carries little warmth. The air remains thin and cool, with colder, denser breath rising from the wet clefts.

And over all of this is the sound.

It is not the roar of one great waterfall. It is the combined voice of innumerable small descents: water hissing over stone, tapping into cups, striking submerged ledges, surging through constrictions, and falling into cavities whose depths cannot be seen. The packed rock reflects and returns every note until separate sources cease to exist. At the rim, speech is already difficult. Within the basin, the sound becomes a pressure against the ears and chest. The deepest slots contribute a continuous bass; the surface films add a piercing hiss; somewhere between those extremes is a dry, rattling thunder made by water striking stone inside hollow chambers.

The roar has almost no direction. Turning the head changes its texture but not its presence. It comes through the air, through the wet rock underfoot, and seemingly through the bones themselves. There are no intervals of quiet in which the place could reconsider itself. Nor are there crescendos. The sound is deafening because it is total, not because anything is happening suddenly.

Exposed life is nearly absent. There is no green on the crowns, no lichen in the sheltered angles, no dark algal velvet along the watercourses. Nothing roots, flowers, or spreads visibly across the mineral surface. The colors that might suggest growth are oxides, fine precipitates, and old stains laid down where water once followed a slightly different path.

A sparse ecology persists out of sight. Where deep seepage brings dissolved iron and sulfur into contact with the air, films of chemosynthetic cells occupy the wet rock. These support a few colorless animals, flattened and translucent, rarely longer than a finger. They cling beneath overhangs and move against the current with deliberate slowness, grazing without making any visible difference to the film. One might examine a hundred pools and find none. Then a single pool will contain one, folded against the underside of a stone like a drop that has learned to hold its shape.

Nothing flies over the basins. Nothing calls. No tracks cross the crowns. Life has not softened the region or given it a seasonal rhythm; it survives in a few protected chemical gradients and otherwise leaves the landscape to water and stone.

The whole place initially gives the impression of having been caught in the middle of an event: just after rain, just beneath a passing cloud, just before some flood subsides. But this is its settled condition. The cloud does not pass. The pools do not rise. The same runnels glitter in the same grooves, and the mist keeps its accustomed level. Water is always arriving, always leaving, and never accumulating.

Its stillness is therefore not the stillness of ice. It is motion without development. The same drops do not fall forever, but the pattern of their falling does. Erosion continues so slowly and so evenly that it no longer alters the visible arrangement on any meaningful timescale. No new channel opens with a rush; no old channel collapses; no crown becomes an island. The basin is not eternal, but change has retreated beyond the reach of observation.

To stand within it is to feel two contradictory kinds of exposure. The eye commands an immense region, while the body is allowed only the width of one wet crown. Distance is visible everywhere and available almost nowhere. There are no made paths, no steps except accidental ones, no clear route except the water’s—and water does not make routes for feet.

The strongest impression is not emptiness but occupancy. Every niche has its stone, every stone its film or dryness, every current its note, every note its place in the unvarying roar. There seems to be no room left for a new thing, and no sign that a new thing has ever been required.

Yet touch the water, and it runs cold and immediate over the hand. The High Clamor is not frozen. It has simply found a motion it can repeat without becoming anything else.
