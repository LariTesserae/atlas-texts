### The Bead on the Needle

At the summit of a mountain still being pushed up out of the earth, the rock narrows to a black needle above the cloud deck. Just below its tip, sheltered by a fresh fracture, there is a wet cavity no wider than a thumbnail. From outside it shows three things: a sharp lip of volcanic glass, a trembling bead of water, and ordinary sunlight on both. That is the whole landscape.

The needle is too young to have softened. Its edges split fingers of light into clean white lines; its fractures have conchoidal, shell-like faces and no weathered rind. There is no scree, no soil, no sand. Even the finest fragments are angular. Water enters a new crack before the crack has quite finished opening, so the stone seems to bleed clarity. Despite the altitude, there is no frost. Heat comes up through the summit in slow pulses, and the thin air immediately around the cavity shimmers with it. Beyond that small shimmer, the mountain is dry and nearly windless. Within, everything is damp: hot glass, mineral scale, the bodies of animals, even the apparently empty air.

Inside the cavity, ledges no thicker than hairs step around a central seep. Water moves through them without ever becoming a stream. It stands in pinhead pools, creeps in bright capillary veins, and gathers beneath overhangs until surface tension lets go. The hottest seams simmer; higher up, vapor condenses on the glass in colorless beads. Between pulses there are warm, almost dry ribs where the heat smells of iron and crushed stone. Nothing glows. The colors are those of an ordinary sunlit place—black rock, white reflections, watery gray, small stains of rust and sulfur.

The cavity is not larger within. Its dimensions are honest. What fails is the simpler expectation that each of its surfaces should have only one neighbor.

In clear light, reflections are passages. A pool reflected in a hanging drop can be reached through that drop; the dark underside of one ledge may open beside another ledge several terraces away. Distances remain minute, but routes multiply. Water uses them as readily as animals do. A rill can enter its own source without traversing the space between, and rain occasionally falls from a ceiling pool into the seep that supplied it. At noon, when every wet face holds a sharp image, the cavity has more geography than it does at dusk. Under passing cloud, adjacencies vanish. The place becomes smaller without changing size.

Nothing here is a plant. There is no green, no root, no leaf, nothing rooted in photosynthetic patience. The base of the food web is a nearly colorless film feeding on chemical gradients in the hot seepage. It is less a mat than a continually reorganizing stain. Over and through it moves the fauna, in such numbers that bare stone is usually a temporary condition.

The veil-fleas are flattened, transparent discs with legs around their entire circumference. They graze in crowded sheets, each one overlapping several others, their passage clearing brief dark trails through the film. Glass limpets cluster along the wet ribs, their soft silica shells so close that they lift and settle against one another with the heat. Smaller things live in the seams of those shells, and still smaller things ride on them. Thread-mouths—long, flexible loops with no visible head until they feed—wait in pools and reflections. One may hang beneath a ledge while putting its mouth through the image of a pool above it. For the prey, the attack appears to come out of a bright patch of sky.

When all these animals move at once, the cavity appears to be changing material rather than merely stirring. A black ledge becomes silver; a pool becomes opaque with bodies; a dry rib develops a fringe of translucent legs. If everything were removed, little would remain but three small shelves, a seep, and a few sharp mineral ribs. Much of what looks like terrain is only a temporary arrangement of crowding, feeding, and rest.

The largest animals, the palm-backs, are no longer than an eyelash. Two of their forelimbs end in opposed digits. They use tools, though the landscape contains no artifacts. A palm-back will test several naturally broken glass needles, reject some, and choose one with the right balance to lever a limpet from its hold. Another may use an unmodified hard grain to crack a shell. The tool is never hafted, cached, or deliberately reshaped. Once released, it is indistinguishable from any other fresh fragment. There are no nests, walls, arranged stones, or shelters; eggs are deposited in the seams of living shells. Technology exists only while it is being held.

The overwhelming life makes almost no noise. The loudest event is the minute tick of a grain against a limpet shell. Otherwise there are soft shell-closures, the papery contact of feet, and the occasional wet dimpling of a surface when a thread-mouth strikes. Thousands of animals feeding together produce less sound than one drop condensing on the outer lip. Steam escapes through pores too small to whistle. The silence is not peaceful or empty; it is crowded.

Several times an hour, heat rises through the needle. The lowest pools tighten into vapor, the limpets clamp shut, and veil-fleas migrate upward along routes that may cease to exist if the light changes. Then condensation begins. Water returns to the ledges, grazing resumes, and predators take up positions at the newly opened adjacencies. On longer cycles the summit fractures. A ledge breaks away, or a fresh one appears, wet and chemically rich. It is occupied before its sharpness has dulled. Nothing here erodes gently enough to become old; surfaces are dissolved, eaten, or broken first.

And so the landscape remains at once minute and multitudinous: a hot wet notch on a young stone needle above the clouds, without vegetation, construction, or lasting tools, yet full of appetite, choice, weather, and pursuit. In plain daylight its beasts cross distances that do not exist, water returns by impossible shortcuts, and the whole crowded country continues almost silently beneath a single bead’s reflection of the sun.
