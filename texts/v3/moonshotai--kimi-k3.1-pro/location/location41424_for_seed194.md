## The Underhusk

At the lowest point of a salt basin so old that its rim has been worn to an almost imperceptible swelling, there lies a husk small enough to hide in a closed hand. The surrounding plain is cool, level, and mineral, with no stone large enough to cast a useful shadow. The husk might be mistaken for a flake of thicker salt or a piece of clinker. Its seam admits no light.

The entire landscape is behind that seam.

Inside, the darkness is complete. It is not night, because there is no alternating day; not shade, because there is no nearby illumination from which shade could be taken. No tissue glimmers, no mineral vein phosphoresces, and none of the city’s slow friction makes a spark. The place has never needed eyes. Its surfaces are distinguished by resistance, temperature, texture, and the way they return sound.

The enclosed air is cool and nearly motionless. It smells faintly of crushed rind, dry oil, and the astringent dust inside old seed cases, though there is almost no loose dust left to smell. Nothing drips. Nothing is damp at depth. There is no hidden reservoir, no capillary moisture, no water held in reserve against a drought. Life here is not merely good at surviving without water; it is not organized around water at all.

Its cells are closer to lattices than to fluid-filled sacs. Charge creeps through mineral-bearing fibers, and fixed enzymes rearrange bonds along their surfaces. Growth resembles crystallization until it branches, turns toward a stimulus, or heals a wound. A shoot may require generations to thicken perceptibly. There is no sap to rise, no soft green flush, no seasonal wilting. The plants draw their remaining energy from a slight chemical difference between the inner rind and the tissues around the central hollow—a weak inheritance from the husk’s formation, spent with extraordinary thrift.

The husk itself is the oldest plant. The floor is its living interior; the ceiling is another of its organs. Everything solid within is vegetation, whether active, dormant, or in the slow process of being reclaimed: the glassy ribs, the corklike pavements, the cables under tension, the thin vanes, the hard little tools in their niches. Even the fine matter worn from the streets is plant tissue, and even that is eventually gathered again.

It is total vegetation without profusion.

A plan of the interior would look urban. There are districts, concentric service passages, narrow gates, retaining walls, low bridges, shafts, chambers within chambers, and several generations of repair laid over one another. Some avenues follow the old growth rings. Others cut across them with obvious deliberation. There are places that can only be called thresholds, and others that correspond to warehouses, workshops, reservoirs, or commons—though nothing was ever stored in the reservoirs but pressure, and nothing gathered in the commons.

A journey through it would feel almost empty.

The districts consist mostly of the spaces they enclose. Long intervals separate one buttress from the next. A chamber may contain a single column, or a bowl-shaped depression, or nothing at all. Whole streets have no object beside them. Bridges cross dark ducts and arrive at blank walls. Doors open into rooms whose only feature is their echo. The map is intricate; the terrain is sparse. The city is built around absence as carefully as around structure.

Its relief is very low. The central floor descends so gently that it appears level until the sound changes. Elsewhere, old steps have become shallow swells. Curbs are rounded ridges. Towers have thickened and settled into squat fluted piers, while the upper vaults have relaxed toward the floor without quite collapsing. There is no rubble. Anything that breaks loose is met, sooner or later, by a filament too fine to feel, which begins the centuries-long work of taking it apart and carrying its substance elsewhere.

The materials vary more than the forms. Old rind has the dry resistance of unfired ceramic. Inner bast yields slightly, like densely pressed linen. Pavements are fine-grained and corky, warm only by comparison with the cooler mineral ribs. Some beams are so densely layered that they ring when struck; others have been deliberately made soft and fibrous, swallowing vibration. The earliest construction was grown in one continuous piece. Later work was cut, scraped, wedged, braided, and stitched. The most recent repairs sit over ancient damage like sutures over a scar that has already closed.

The tool caches are unmistakable: flaked thorn scrapers, slivers of silicified rind, fiber saws, smooth wedges, small spools of cord, and hooked implements made from hardened root. There is no metal and no sign that metal was ever needed.

Yet no hand held these tools. The builders were rooted organs of the same vegetation that formed the city. Opposed pads once gripped a scraper while a torsion stem drew it forward, one imperceptible fraction of a turn at a time. A fiber saw might spend sixty years completing a single cut. A wedge was not hammered but grown in place, thickening until the surrounding tissue separated along the intended line. When the work was finished, the manipulating organs were usually reabsorbed. Sometimes only the tool was left, fitted neatly into a recess beside the task it had performed.

The technology is primitive, but not careless. It is suited to a civilization for which a century is a workable interval.

And the city is loud.

The first thing its architecture does with movement is make it larger. Somewhere in the rind, a fiber slips by less than the width of its own wall. The click enters a rib, passes into a vault, and returns as a hard report. A beam tightening over centuries releases a note that makes an entire district boom. Dry membranes tick in rapid, irregular volleys. Beneath these sounds runs a lower laboring tone, sometimes almost a roar, sometimes a pressure felt through the pavement more than heard.

The emptiness gives the noise room to gather. An avenue is also an acoustic duct. A hollow tower is a resonator. A sealed room is a drum with no membrane visible. The leaflike vanes attached to the walls do not collect light; they flex at particular frequencies, dividing one note from another. Some doorways are dampers. Some stairs, despite their uselessness as stairs, are tuning structures: each worn tread changes the path of a returning vibration.

At times, one district answers another—not with a voice, but with a sympathetic resonance that begins only after the first sound has crossed the hollow. The result can seem like a vast exchange of calls. Nothing is calling. Nothing is fleeing, approaching, mating, warning, or declaring territory. There are no animals here, not even mites in the dust or burrowers in the rind. Nothing scurries away from a sudden crack. No wings interrupt the echoes. The city’s loudness has no animal source and no audience apart from its walls.

Those walls do, in their own way, respond.

Where vibration is stable, the plants lay down dense, narrow ribs. Where resonance becomes destructive, they produce mats of softer tissue. Certain root tips grow toward a repeated tone; others turn aside from it. A note sustained through the right chamber can determine, over the following ages, whether a seam closes, a passage narrows, or a vane becomes thick enough to alter the note itself. Construction and repair are therefore difficult to separate from listening. Whether this constitutes language is less clear. The city modifies itself according to sounds that it also produces, and each modification changes the sounds that follow.

Its ecology is similarly closed. Producers, builders, sensors, and recyclers are all plants, often grafted so thoroughly that the boundaries between individuals are no longer meaningful. Some tissues unmake older growth and return its elements to the living lattice. Others exist almost entirely as structure, metabolizing just enough to remain available for future use. Nothing photosynthesizes. Nothing rots in the ordinary sense, because there is no separate wild community waiting to consume a corpse. Death is a transfer of custody.

The city consumes its past, but with so little waste that the process looks almost still.

That stillness makes the noise deceptive. A single boom may mark the conclusion of a contraction that began before the surrounding basin lost its last pronounced slope. Afterward, the tension may take another age to accumulate. A vane moves through its full range over hundreds of years. A door closes by the width of a fiber in a lifetime. The newest visible construction is a brace across an old seam: three braided attachments, two complete and one still narrowing toward its anchor. It is neither hurried nor neglected.

This is not a city in active ruin. Ruin would imply that collapse were still the main process. Here, nearly everything destructive has been accommodated, softened, or recycled. Sharp corners have disappeared because sharp corners concentrated stress. Towers settled because their height no longer served the chemistry. Streets flattened because their partitions were unnecessary. What remains is approaching a form that requires almost no correction.

Eventually the last useful chemical gradient will be exhausted. The final fibers will slip, the last chambers will answer, and the resonances will decay without being renewed. The city need not then crumble. There is no soil to receive it and no other ecology eager to begin. It may simply stop altering itself, preserving its streets and empty rooms as a dry, completed structure.

Until then, the husk gives no sign on the cool salt plain. Inside, under absolute darkness, an almost vacant city continues to thunder and tick. It is urban without crowds, vegetal without lushness, ancient without decay, and immensely loud while doing nearly nothing.

It is not waiting for dawn, rain, or citizens. It is patiently finishing itself.
