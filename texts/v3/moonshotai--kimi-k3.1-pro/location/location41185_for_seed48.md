## The Low Saltern

The basin lies only a few metres above the sea, behind an old barrier of rounded mineral dunes. It is so flat that water seems to deliberate before moving. Shallow pans, drainage cuts, and embanked reservoirs extend for kilometres, separated by low causeways whose height matters more than their length. A rise of two metres is enough to make a building site; a fall of half a metre is enough to reverse the usefulness of a channel.

The city here was built to extract salt and other minerals from warm brine. It is not compact. Pump houses, crystallizing sheds, substations, rail depots, workshops, and blocks of workers’ housing stand far apart, each where the water grade or the railway required it. Gathered together, the buildings would make a modest town. Spread across the basin, with its reservoirs and service roads, they constitute something closer to a dispersed industrial city. A freight train needs most of an hour to cross it.

There is no vegetation. No reeds interrupt the waterlines, no salt grass holds the embankments, and no weeds emerge from the rail joints. Heat, salinity, and repeated flooding and drying prevent anything rooted or leafed from establishing itself. Even the sheltered sides of buildings remain bare. Colour comes from salt white, concrete grey, iron red, black slag, and the wine-dark or ochre staining of microbial films in some of the shallows. The only green in the central works is old corrosion on a copper roof.

Water is seldom deep, but it is nearly always present. Warm brine rises through faulted ground and is tempered with seawater before entering the pans. Pipe flanges weep. Rain collects in shallow depressions and stands on the paving because there is almost nowhere for it to soak away. Cooled process pipes carry continuous beads of condensation, which fall with the regularity of clocks. After a shower, freshwater lies briefly over the denser brine, making two waters that reflect light differently before wind and pumping mix them.

The heat is persistent rather than dramatic. The brine, the powerhouse, and the exposed masonry all contribute to it. Before dawn, when the air is at its coolest, walls still radiate the previous day’s warmth. Rain arrives warm. Sweat and condensation become difficult to distinguish on skin, and salt dries in pale lines on any cloth repeatedly wetted. Near the hottest intakes, heat distortion softens the outlines of pipes; farther out, the pans are merely warm enough to steam faintly when cooler rain crosses them.

A low, salt-laden overcast is common. Evaporation, powerhouse smoke, and fine mineral dust thicken it until distances lose their contrast. There are few hard shadows. At several hundred metres, a pump house becomes a grey arrangement of planes; at a kilometre, it may be only a dark interruption in the haze. Work lamps remain lit through the day, not in continuous rows but as isolated points at doorways, gauges, and rail crossings. At night, most of the city remains dark. One can see great distances without seeing much.

Nothing looks newly ruined. The Saltern has been weathering for a long time, and its damage has acquired settled forms. Wavelets have rounded the exposed corners of the causeways, while salt has cemented their lower stones into hard, porous reefs. Broken concrete has been knitted into place by mineral deposition. Old slag heaps have stable, rain-worn slopes. Roofs that failed decades ago now lie as low, encrusted mounds rather than jagged wreckage. Newer work sits plainly on top of older work: brick culverts beneath concrete tanks, steel pipe racks above both, electric cables clipped to whatever support was available.

The works are maintained, but selectively. Some embankments are repaired every season; others have been allowed to become mineral ridges. Buildings stand on raised plinths, electrical equipment is mounted well above splash level, and pipes are designed to be uncoupled before corrosion welds them permanently together. Everything important is arranged according to slope, salinity, and access. Streets double as dikes. Drains are also sampling channels. Railway sidings serve pans as well as sheds. It is a practical city, repeatedly revised without being substantially replanned.

Only a small operations staff remains where housing was once provided for thousands. Most of the residential streets are used for storage or not used at all. A lit canteen, a parked maintenance vehicle, and washing beside one occupied block are enough to indicate habitation. Freight leaves on an irregular schedule. For long intervals, the only visible machine movement is the slow travel of a salt harvester along a distant pan or the rotation of a ventilator above a roof.

Inside the pump halls, the engines are considerable. Outside, their heavy walls reduce them to a low hum, sometimes felt through a handrail more clearly than heard. The predominant sounds are small: water dropping from a pipe, a valve ticking as it expands, brine moving through a narrow gate, the dry tap of insect bodies against a lamp housing. Occasionally a pump changes speed or a conveyor starts. The sound carries across the empty basin, continues for a while, and then stops without an echo.

The apparent lifelessness is most convincing from a moving vehicle. On foot, beside the right water, it disappears.

In the cooler pans, brine shrimp occur in such numbers that the water seems to possess a second, slower current within itself. Their bodies cloud clear patches and turn the margins reddish where they gather against a windward shore. Brine-fly larvae work through the microbial films below them. Discarded pupal cases collect in dark lines along the waterline, deep enough in places to crush softly underfoot.

When the adult flies emerge, they rise in sheets. From across a pan, an embankment may appear to be smoking. Up close, the phenomenon resolves into innumerable separate animals, most travelling only a short distance before settling again. They do not bite, but they cover railings, instrument glass, sleeves, and the warm backs of maintenance signs. Walking through a dense hatch produces a faint ticking on clothing, like the first few grains of a rain shower.

Fish wait in the seawater channels, especially near the sluices. Crabs occupy the cool spaces beneath culvert stones and emerge where pumping strands small prey along the margins. Predatory beetles patrol the shallows. Spiders have established themselves in the angles of pipe bridges, where the fly hatches provide a reliable, if seasonal, abundance.

The birds gather beyond easy counting. Long-legged waders stand in the drawn-down pans, sometimes so numerous that they form a pale, shifting margin where the actual shore is difficult to distinguish. Gulls occupy the dikes and roof ridges. Other birds follow the intakes and outfalls, moving from one feeding ground to another as the water levels change. Most feeding is done quietly. When a large flock lifts, the principal sound is not calling but air passing through many wings—a broad, soft disturbance that fades as the birds settle one pan farther on.

At dusk, bats emerge from the roof spaces of the older industrial buildings. They feed above the warm channels and around the lamps, taking flies and other insects. Their departure can continue for a long time without becoming loud. By then the waders are tightening into quieter groups, fish are working the darkened sluices, and the pump houses have begun to gleam with condensation again.

The abundance is uneven, and that unevenness preserves the basin’s emptiness. The hottest intakes and most concentrated brines are nearly sterile. Some vast salt floors hold nothing but crust and standing rainwater. Most of the air, most of the tall shed interiors, and nearly all the space between stations remain vacant. Animal life occupies thin, intensely populated layers: the upper water, the cooling margin, the underside of a roof, the first metre above a hatching pan. The Saltern is therefore both crowded and hollow, depending on whether one looks at a surface or through a volume.

Its processes continually alter those surfaces. A sluice opens and shrimp accumulate along the new current; fish gather below them; birds begin walking in from the farther shore. A warm rain cell crosses the basin, briefly freshening the upper pans and sending runoff through old street drains. Flies shift to newly exposed margins. Pumps respond to the changed levels. Hours later, evaporation begins restoring the salt crust, and the harvested channels start their slow return toward stillness.

From the final rail embankment, the city can still be mistaken for an abandoned industrial relic: a few roofs, stacks, and isolated lamps in a dim wet plain. But the stillness is largely an effect of distance. Every tolerable water surface is being grazed, filtered, hunted over, or roosted above. The works persist in a slow accommodation with heat, corrosion, and water. Humanity remains as maintenance; the city’s overwhelming population is animal, and most of its activity is too small, too dispersed, or too quiet to announce itself.
