## The Serein Catchment

From the southern rim, the catchment looks wet. It is not, quite.

It lies high among pale, splintered mountains: three linked hollows, broad enough to hold several towns, opening one into the next as they descend toward a dark fault wall. The air is thin and cool, and so clear that distances collapse. A condenser mast on the opposite slope appears close enough to touch until a walker tries to reach it and loses half a day in the intervening folds of ground.

The light is extraordinary. Even early in the day it has a white, nearly sourceless intensity, striking the mica in the rocks and the wax on the leaves until the whole basin seems outlined in fine bright wire. Shadows remain sharp and cold. Exposed stone can be dazzling to look at and unpleasantly chilly to touch.

The land is too young to have softened. Fractures end in splinters; blocks freshly detached from the rim still show the geometry of their breaking. The scree has no rounded edges. There are no gravel bars, no undercut banks, no old flood lines, because there has never been a river here to make them. The hollows were shaped by uplift and faulting, and erosion has only begun its patient work. In places the vegetation stops against bare rock in a line as abrupt as a cut.

Elsewhere it is almost impenetrable.

The central hollow supports a forest without much height but with extraordinary depth. Slender, many-stemmed trees interlock above broad ferns. Beneath these are shrubs with cupped, blue-green leaves; beneath the shrubs, shelves of moss and small silver fronds; beneath those, a black and white felt of roots and fungal threads. Each layer touches the next. A person pushing through disappears within a few steps, and the foliage closes behind them with hardly a movement.

There is little true soil. A hand forced through the root felt finds stone immediately, veined with pale fungi that slowly open its minerals. Dead leaves do not so much rot as flatten, remaining intact for years in the cool shade until newer growth compresses them into thin black sheets. Much of the forest spreads by division rather than seed. What appears to be a crowded thicket may be one very old organism, its stems emerging hundreds of metres apart.

The basin’s moisture is similarly dispersed. On the open rim the air dries the lips; beneath the canopy, clothing gradually darkens at the cuffs and knees. At dawn, cold leaves carry a film of condensation. By midday most of it has been drawn into stems and root chambers. A few natural reservoirs exist—a black pool beneath an overhanging slab, water deep inside a fault cleft, a clay-lined hollow that remains damp long after rain—but none connects reliably to another.

Rain comes only occasionally. Clouds may spend days massed beyond the western rim before a small part of one crosses over. Then rain falls almost vertically, without wind, and vanishes into the vegetation so quickly that the exposed paths remain merely speckled. Within an hour there may be no sign of it except the weight of water in the leaves.

The straight lines in the forest reveal the other system at work.

Widely spaced among the trees stand condenser masts: narrow metal frames carrying combs of dull silver tubing. Their surfaces are kept colder than the surrounding air, even under the hard sunlight. Moisture beads on them in clear weather. Once a day, or less often, a collector travels down the frame so slowly that its movement can only be confirmed by watching it against a fixed branch. It wipes the accumulated water into ceramic cups. From there, buried pipes distribute it through the root mat.

Beneath one mast there may be a brief, local dripping onto dry stone—the nearest thing the catchment has to ordinary rain. A few steps away, the rock remains dusty.

The structures are sparse, but their influence is not. A walker may go an hour seeing nothing made, then find a numbered valve beneath a fern, a porcelain insulator clasped in roots, or two concrete steps leading to a door set into an otherwise empty slope. Service paths are narrow and discontinuous. There are several windowless maintenance shelters, a short length of rail ending at a rock face, and no settlement anywhere.

On the northern fault wall stands the only large building: a low condensary of pale concrete, metal fins, and recessed doors. It has no chimney and no visible smoke. Indicator lamps burn behind thick glass. Insulated mains leave the building and return to it through separate portals, and electrical lines descend from installations higher on the rim. Its principal product seems to be cold. Water is what the valley yields in response.

Whether the forest was planted to use that water, or merely arrived and found the system favorable, is no longer evident. Roots have grown densest around the distribution pipes. Condenser frames serve as trellises. In some places old trunks have enclosed valves so completely that the machinery must now operate through the living wood.

Everything is quiet.

The silence appears to be part of the design. Sudden motion would shake condensate from the tubing; vibration would waste it into the ground. The heavy equipment is isolated and counterweighted, and its moving parts work at speeds closer to growth than to ordinary manufacture. The interleaved vegetation absorbs what little sound remains. Even on the rim, wind is often only a cool pressure against the face.

Occasionally there is a single tick as a pipe changes temperature, or the distant, enclosed note of one drop falling into a cistern. These sounds do not break the silence so much as give it dimensions.

Animals are scarce. There are no birds in the bright sky and no grazing trails through the moss. Under the leaves one might find a transparent snail, a few nearly colorless mites, or the husk of a small grey moth. The pools beneath the slabs contain pale, slow-moving creatures that react to light but make no disturbance at the surface. The forest does not depend on them. Fungi perform most of its decomposition, and its spores and fragments are carried by dripping water, root growth, and the patient passage of maintenance mechanisms.

Change is easiest to see where something made and something living meet. A pipe support acquires a sheath of roots over decades. A service door remains accessible because a concealed shear trims its seam once each year. A metal marker darkens, not with rust but with a thin bloom of lichen. In the shelters, pressure gauges and water-level indicators still function, though some of their needles would have to be watched for months to prove that they had moved.

The catchment is becoming wetter, but almost imperceptibly. Each year the root felt holds a little more moisture; each century, a few more fractures remain damp through the bright season. Isolated hollows approach overflow. Empty channels have been prepared below them, their sides already green though their beds have never carried more than a brief stain of water.

Nothing here looks abandoned, and nothing looks finished. It is a young landscape being patiently taught the habits of an older, wetter one: to hold a drop, then another; to make soil around a pipe; to shade a machine until machine and forest approach the same temperature.

For now the open basin lies under its hard white light, lush and nearly waterless, its immense industry proceeding in silence—one condensation bead, one root tip, one cold stone at a time.
