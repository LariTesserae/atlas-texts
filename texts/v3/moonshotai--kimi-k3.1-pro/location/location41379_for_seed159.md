## The Calving Basin

The first thing beneath the plateau is not darkness but noise. It rises through the descending fissure as a dry, continuous rattling, with wetter sounds underneath: slaps, gulps, the clatter of hard bodies against stone. Now and then something booms in the distance, and all the lesser noises hesitate before resuming.

The passage ends on a ledge a hundred paces below the surface. Beyond it, the Calving Basin opens not as a single cavern but as a region of broad halls, collapsed courts, roofed valleys, and connecting galleries. The far wall is a pale interruption in the dust haze, a day’s walk away. Nearer ground descends in shingled flats and low, swollen domes; beyond those lie sump country, ribbed terraces, and a white district of fresh fractures where the roof seems to have been broken only moments ago. Some chambers are intimate enough to touch both walls. Others contain weather of a sort—dust clouds drifting through sunlight, condensation mist trailing from a cold fault, herd shadows moving over ground too crowded to see.

It is ordinarily, almost startlingly, well lit. The plateau above is thin and riddled with openings. Sunlight enters through skylights and long roof-cracks, strikes the pale mineral terraces, and reflects from chamber to chamber. At midday the colors are true: chalk white, honey yellow, wet umber, the dull red of old animal stains. Shadows have ordinary edges. Nothing glows. At night the Basin becomes properly dark except for stars in the roof-openings and the occasional animal-fat lamp, but the noise continues without any change of shift.

The land is young, and looks it. Its large spaces were made by faulting and collapse rather than by the patience of rivers. Water has scarcely begun to soften them. Fractures remain sharp; fallen blocks keep their original angles; even the dust is angular under the tongue. Where a surface is smooth, it is usually because something has grown there, not because something has been worn away. Warm, mineral-heavy seepage lays down aragonite and gypsum in blisters, thin shelves, and blunt, branching crusts. A pool begins with a white rim. The rim becomes a wall. Water finds another course around it, and the wall becomes the beginning of a terrace or a maze.

Much of the floor is not bedrock at all, but shell: generations of shed plates, crushed carapaces, dung, and bone cemented into a tough, uneven pavement. In places it rings underfoot. In others the crust flexes over a hollow and emits a high, warning tick.

Water is everywhere as a possibility and nowhere as a guarantee. Most of the Basin is dry enough for pale dust to rise around each footstep. Then a pressure change draws cooler air through the roof faults, moisture beads on the ceilings, and a local rain begins—straight silver stitches falling onto one terrace while the next remains dusty. The rain may last minutes or hours. It produces brief, hand-deep streams, warm brown pools in shell hollows, and dark streaks down walls. These streams do not converge into a river. They soak through new cracks, are drunk by animals, or vanish into sumps that may themselves drain without warning.

The larger pools are brackish and crowded, none extensive enough to be called a lake. The best water collects in small gypsum cups beneath condensation drips. The air is warm rather than hot, though the lower sumps feel close and animal-heavy. It smells alternately of wet chalk, crushed shell, ammonia, hide, and a faint mineral sourness. Dry breaths of air come through one set of passages while damp air exhales through another, so that a traveler can pass from dust to mist and back within a few minutes.

There is nothing green. Even beneath the brightest skylights there is no grass, moss, or rooting thing. The foundation of the ecology is a film: cream-colored when dry, nearly black when saturated, spread thinly over fresh mineral and seepage stone. It is microbial, faintly threadlike, and draws its energy from sulfur and iron in the warm water. It never raises itself into stems or fronds. It simply spreads, is eaten, and spreads again.

Everything above that film seems to have arrived at once.

At a distance, parts of the floor appear paved with fine gravel. Then a large animal steps into it and an entire sheet of gravel flows aside. These are scale-lice, flat, hinged creatures no bigger than thumbnails, often layered several bodies deep. They scrape the film with their undersides and make a hiss like fine sand being poured. Disturbed, they spring, tumble, and strike the ground with thousands of minute clicks. They occupy wall cracks, the rims of pools, the undersides of ledges, and the shells of larger animals. A newly exposed white surface may remain bare for a few hours; by the next day it is usually filmed, and soon after that it moves.

The molt-oxen move through them in herds. A molt-ox is low and door-broad, with six columnar legs, a face largely given over to soft rasping lips, and a back piled with overlapping mineralized plates. The plates thicken as the animal ages and are shed in awkward, ringing armfuls. Several hundred oxen together sound like a stone building being slowly taken apart. Where separate herds join, thousands may be visible across the flats, their bodies hiding the ground and their dust softening the distances. Young animals cling beneath the adults or in the grooves between their back plates, adding small peeping cries to the deeper grunts and bellows.

Between their legs run narrow scavengers with too many joints. Patient predators lie almost indistinguishable beneath shell grit. In the sumps, pale eel-things and purse-shaped swimmers churn the water until it seems to boil. The roof is occupied too: membrane-winged animals hang in dense, overlapping colonies, periodically pouring from one skylight district to another in clapping masses. Fliers hunt the airborne stages of the scale-lice; long-legged things hunt the fliers when they land. Carcasses rarely remain recognizable for long.

The result is not merely a great number of animals, but a shortage of unoccupied surfaces. Bodies absorb echoes, so an empty new chamber sounds strangely sharp and wrong. Near a herd, speech must be shouted. Farther away, the noise separates into useful layers: water under stone, oxen on shell pavement, wings near the roof, fresh rock ticking as it dries.

Stone and animals continually make work for one another. Mineral crust grows on shells, boots, bones, and sleeping places. The oxen grind it off or shed it. Their discarded plates become sediment; seepage cements the sediment into ground; collapses break the ground open again. Anything left motionless is first investigated, then colonized, and eventually either eaten down or incorporated.

The large changes come often enough that no one calls them rare, but the small changes never stop. A floor sags. A sump begins to roar and empties through a crack that was not visible the day before. Elsewhere, warm brine wells up and spreads in a thin sheet, already whitening at its edges. A roof block calves loose with a concussion that whites out a whole court with dust. Herds pour away from it, then wheel back toward newly exposed seeps. Roof colonies fall silent for several breaths before returning in a storm of wings. Within hours, scavengers are on the rubble. Within days, a pool may have become a dry basin, a dry passage may have become a sump, and a ridge may stand where a track had been.

A chamber’s life can be measured in years, a pool’s in days, a safe route’s in an hour.

Signs of deliberate hands are scattered so thinly that hours can pass without one. There is a cairn beneath a skylight, half swallowed by mineral growth. Three red-ochre slashes mark a descent. Elsewhere, a few steps have been pecked into soft tufa, and a crescent of stacked stone remains from a windbreak or herd baffle. The most substantial structure is a low lodge beneath an old overhang: rib bones bent into a frame, hides stitched over them with sinew, flat stones weighting the edges. Its floor is layered with shell grit, old grease, and broken tools. It is empty as often as it is occupied.

The tool-users make nothing here that requires permanence. Their cutting edges are flaked chert; their awls and needles are bone; their scoops and vessels are shell or stitched gut. Cordage is sinew and split hide. There is no timber and no metal. Sleeping platforms are raised on stacked stone, shelters are struck and cleaned before crust can bind them, and routes are drawn in removable shell grit rather than committed to durable maps. A line of perforated shells hung across a passage serves as a crude alarm, clattering when the air changes or something large pushes through.

Experience matters more than equipment. Travelers walk in the wake of the oxen, where the film has been cropped and the small animals driven aside, but never directly before a herd. They distrust both very dry new floors and very inviting pools. They navigate by skylights, old perimeter rock, and the direction of sounds. Most importantly, they notice when the scale-lice stop. Such silence may mean a predator, a pressure shift, or a roof beginning to move. Whatever the cause, it is not treated as peace.

By evening, the view from the entry ledge has already altered. A white rim has thickened around a morning puddle. A fresh fall of shell has buried the lower steps. The temporary stream is gone, but a wet stain has appeared on the opposite wall, and the first scale-lice are gathering beneath it. Far off, another collapse rolls through the galleries, followed by the immense, agitated answer of the herds.

The Basin is not unfinished in the sense of awaiting some final, stable form. This is its finished condition: continual almost—almost a river, almost a road, almost a roof, almost silence. It does not seem to be alive as one organism. It only seems so because stone, water, and animals keep borrowing one another’s shapes. Stand still long enough and even your footprints begin to whiten at the edges.
