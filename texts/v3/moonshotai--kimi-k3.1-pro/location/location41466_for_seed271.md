## The Seep Hollow

Below a low, almost featureless plain lies a hollow small enough to walk around in a few minutes. Its rim is not a cliff but a rounded shoulder of limestone, weathered back until the descent feels less like entering a depression than stepping into a broad, shallow room. An old watercourse opens through the southern side, though it has not carried a stream for a very long time. Its bed is level with dust, its banks softened and partly buried. From the plain above, the whole place can be overlooked in a glance.

The floor is mostly bare. Pale silt lies in wide, uncomplicated sweeps, interrupted by fine gravel, isolated stones, and shallow patches of clay. Nothing is crowded. A stone the size of a skull may have several paces of open ground around it; a low tuft of vegetation stands apart from the next with no scattered seedlings between. The spareness gives small things unusual definition. A single curved slab, a dark seam of moisture, or three stacked stones can hold attention simply because there is so little competing with it.

Every exposed form is old in its present shape. The limestone has lost its sharp bedding edges. Pebbles are rounded not by vigorous water but by long handling in gentle, intermittent flows. Former channels are broad, shallow discolorations rather than cuts. There are no fresh landslides, raw scarps, or undercut banks. Even the stones that have fallen from the rim look finished with falling: half sunk into the floor, their upper surfaces weathered to the same subdued gray as the walls. Erosion continues, but without drama. The hollow is not being rapidly destroyed or remade; it has settled into a form that water and gravity can only refine very slowly.

Water enters at the western side, where a joint in the rock opens beneath a shallow overhang. There is no spring in the usual sense, no bubbling mouth or continuous rill. The stone is simply dark there. Moisture gathers along its underside, swells into occasional drops, and falls into a basin little wider than a footstep. The basin is shallow enough to show every grain on its bottom. From it, a nearly invisible thread of water reaches two smaller hollows and then disappears into a fan of damp clay.

That fan marks the practical limit of the water. Beyond it, the ground lightens abruptly from umber to dust-gray. Occasional rain briefly extends the dark margin and fills a few shallow depressions elsewhere on the floor. For a short while, slender films of water follow the old gradients, joining and separating without cutting channels. Then they sink or evaporate. The same few pockets remain. A single pale mineral line around the largest basin suggests how little its level changes; there are no stacked shorelines recording a succession of deeper pools.

The air is warm even in the shade, though never fiercely hot. It carries the smell of wet limestone close to the seep and, farther out, the dry, slightly sweet odor of warm dust. Sometimes the two scents meet without either overcoming the other. Condensation forms on the coolest stone beneath the overhang, giving the sheltered rock a brief darker polish before it dries again. There is little wind at floor level. Moving air is usually heard along the rim first, a faint passage over stone, and often that is where it remains.

Direct sunlight reaches the hollow only briefly. For much of the day, illumination arrives indirectly, reflected down from pale upper surfaces or entering as diffuse light through the opening in the rim. The result is dim without being gloomy. There are no hard black shadows; the deepest recess beneath the overhang is a soft brown darkness. Wet stone holds a low luster, while dry silt absorbs the light almost completely. The small patch of sky above can seem much brighter than the place itself, like a doorway opening onto a different exposure.

Vegetation follows moisture, but cautiously. Closest to the seep are a few fern rosettes and narrow, grasslike plants with dark bases and muted green tips. Their roots occupy cracks and thin pockets of soil rather than a continuous bed. Along the damp clay grow scattered sedges, each clump compact and separate. Farther away, where the ground is dry for most of the time, there are only small leathery-leaved shrubs and a thin crust of lichen on selected stones. Nothing forms a mat. Nothing drapes the walls. There is no accumulation of leaf litter deep enough to become soil.

From the center, one could count the larger plants without much effort. Their limited number is not the result of recent damage. They are simply held at that density by the available water, the thin substrate, and the subdued light. A fallen leaf remains where it lands for a long time, gradually paling and breaking apart. A stem that dies may stand through several rains before collapsing. Growth and decay are both quiet enough to be easy to miss.

There are no animals. No insects hover over the basins, no birds cross the opening of sky, and nothing moves beneath the shrubs. The wet silt bears no tracks; the walls contain no nests, webs, or burrows. The plants are unbrowsed, and the water’s surface is undisturbed except by falling drops and rare rain. This absence gives the silence a particular quality. A small sound is not automatically the approach or concealment of something living. Usually it is water, a grain of stone settling, or wind touching the rim.

The few built traces are so slight that they can initially be mistaken for geology. Near the eastern wall stands a crescent of unshaped limestone blocks, two courses high at most. A short distance away, several half-buried stones outline a small rectangle, its interior filled to the same level as the surrounding ground. They may once have formed a windbreak and a place to sit or store something, but their use is no longer legible. There is no mortar, no paving, no roof material, and no path leading away. There are also no tools, vessels, mechanisms, or worked objects. Stone, weight, and placement were the whole method.

Nor do these remains feel freshly ruined. The stones that were going to fall have fallen. Those still standing are locked firmly enough to have acquired the same lichen and weathering as natural outcrops. Silt does not pour through their gaps; it has already found its level. The structures do not introduce a second, human landscape on top of the first. They merely add a few straightish lines to a place otherwise composed of curves and slopes.

It would be easy to call the hollow abandoned, but that implies a departure still somehow in progress. It would be equally easy to call it waiting, but nothing in it suggests an approaching event. The seep is neither obviously failing nor strengthening. The vegetation is not advancing across the floor. The old watercourse is not about to reopen. The walls are not on the point of collapse. Even the basin’s reflection, when disturbed, returns promptly to the same dim image of stone and sky.

This stability is not a suspension of ordinary processes. Water still falls. Rain still comes occasionally. Dust still shifts, plants still replace themselves, and evaporation continually removes what the rock releases. But these movements repeat around a remarkably fixed condition. Wetting is followed by drying; small growth by small loss; deposition by an almost equally slow wearing away. On any human timescale, the proportions remain essentially unchanged. Time passes without producing a story of succession, decline, or recovery.

The dominant sound is the interval between drops. When one falls, the note is small and clear, briefly amplified by the stone hollow beneath the overhang. Rings cross the basin, touch its mineral rim, and vanish. Then there is only warm air, the faint mineral smell, and the visual distance between one sparse thing and the next.

The hollow’s completeness lies in those distances. It has enough water to keep a little stone dark, enough vegetation to reveal where the moisture goes, and enough building to register, once, as a human scale. Beyond that are long stretches of warm, bare ground and settled rock. Its stillness is not an emptiness awaiting fulfillment. It is the stillness of a balance already reached.
