## The Unfinished Sea

Above the last snowfield, where the ridge narrows to a spine of dark stone, the mountain has a mouth.

The opening is only eleven paces long, a rain-filled cleft easily mistaken for a shadow. Clouds pass below it. The wind up here is thin, cold, and needling, and the surrounding rock is bare except for a few gray buttons of lichen. Yet when a visitor leans over the cleft, there is no shaft beneath them and no bottom in sight.

There is water, and daylight shining through it.

The cleft contains a sea.

Its water stands almost flush with the broken rim, held in a slightly raised meniscus but never spilling. Rain falls into it without appreciably raising the level. To look down is to look through the roof of an ocean: near walls descend in sharp, wet facets, then incline away into clear green-gray space. The far wall cannot be seen. This is not because of darkness. There is simply too much water between one side and the other.

Nothing glows. At noon the place is lit by plain high-mountain daylight, broken into pale, shifting panels by the movement of the surface. Cloud cover dims the whole hidden gulf at once, as though it were a single room. At dusk the upper water becomes briefly copper-colored. Farther down, objects do not so much fade as cease to be distinguishable from distance.

The water is cold and tastes faintly of iron and fresh stone, with no salt. It never freezes, though a cupful taken onto the rim becomes ordinary meltwater and can ice over by morning. Left undisturbed, that cupful does nothing remarkable. The sea, apparently, exists only in its pocket.

The basin is not being eroded around it. It is being built.

Every visible surface is raw. The walls are black-green and glassy, veined with unstained quartz; their edges can cut skin, and nowhere has water had time to round a boulder or lay down a quiet bed of sediment. Ledges swell out of the rock over the course of hours. At first they look dull and almost soft. Then they darken, harden, and develop seams. Some split while still forming. Others thrust farther into the open water, narrowing it, before an entire section shears away and drops without reaching any visible floor.

The fragments do not accumulate. They are drawn down in long pale trails, becoming grit, then milkiness, then nothing.

The sea is not filling a finished cavity. The cavity is being assembled hurriedly around the sea, and the work keeps failing.

Currents reverse without first slowing. A smooth upwelling may rise into a glassy hill, fold sideways, and become a descending sheet. Broad reaches of water remain uncannily still while, far away, a narrow seam of turbulence races through them. Air cavities open against the walls and are crushed shut. Whirlpools sometimes form vertically, their axes lying parallel to the stone. Bubbles usually rise, but not always: strings of them occasionally dive, wobbling like bright beads, and vanish into water that had appeared empty a moment before.

Because there is so little suspended matter, much of this violence is nearly invisible. It reveals itself only when it catches a bubble, a loose stone, or one of the place’s rare plants.

The noise, however, is unavoidable.

At the rim it is a waterfall roar pressed directly against the ears. Beneath the surface it does not soften; it separates. There is the continuous hiss of mineral grit, the shriek of water forced through new fissures, the slam of collapsing air pockets, and beneath everything the slow detonations of stone forming badly and breaking under its own weight. Some impacts are so deep they seem to arrive before they are heard, felt first in the teeth and breastbone. A shout disappears completely. Covering the ears changes the quality of the pain but not its force.

Twenty paces down the ridge, most of this is gone. The mountain conceals its sea and nearly all of its voice. Only a faint buzzing remains in the soles of the boots.

Life has established no durable strategy here. Vegetation consists mainly of single cords of olive-colored algae, each no thicker than yarn, anchored to fresh rock by a small, tough disc. Some stream out to many times a person’s height; others are short, bleached tufts clinging to ledges already beginning to fracture. Hundreds of body lengths of empty water may separate one plant from the next. There are no weed beds, no floating mats, nothing that could be called a forest.

Tiny transparent crustaceans occasionally appear around the algal anchors, so few that a handful of water usually contains none. The only larger animal regularly suspected is a pale, gill-fringed swimmer about the length of a forearm. It moves slowly, with the flexible body of a folded leaf, and grazes the mineral film on newly hardened stone. No observer has reliably reported seeing two together. It does not flee so much as cease to be findable, turning once within a current and becoming a fleck of distance.

Human work is equally sparse. On the leeward side of the cleft stands one roofless cell of stacked slabs, low enough that its entrance must be crawled through. There is no mortar and no squared stone. Inside are a shallow lens of charcoal, three fire-cracked pebbles, and a hammerstone worn smooth on one side. A few chert flakes lie outside where the wind has not yet taken them.

At the water’s edge, a mineral-stiffened fiber line is tied around a natural knob of rock. Pierced stone sinkers remain attached at irregular intervals. The line ends in a clean break. Nearby, three small cairns mark places that were once considered stable; one now overhangs open water, and another has been split neatly through its base.

A baked-clay cup in the shelter bears bundles of shallow cuts: five, five, seven, then a crowded group crossed by a single long scrape. They may record depth, rainfall, the survival of ledges, or simply the number of times someone returned. Whatever was being measured, the notches become less orderly toward the end.

Nothing here suggests an attempt to settle the sea. The structures look more like equipment for asking whether it would remain the same long enough to be understood.

From below, the summit appears entirely ordinary: snow in the crevices, cloud on the slope, a shepherd’s ruin beside a narrow crack. But at the crack itself, cold spray touches the face, the stone shudders, and ordinary daylight falls into an almost empty gulf where new rock breaks like close thunder. Somewhere above the hidden turbulence, one strand of algae lashes against a ledge that did not exist yesterday.

The place feels less like a flooded mountain than like the first local attempt at an ocean—set too high, made too clear, and begun before the world had finished learning how to contain it.
