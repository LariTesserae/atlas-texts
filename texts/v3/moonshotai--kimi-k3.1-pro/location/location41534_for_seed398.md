### Serein, in the Summit Needle

The Needle rises forty-one kilometres above a plain of black volcanic glass, its point standing above nearly all weather. It is so young that its fractures still resemble broken crockery: concentric, glassy, sharp enough to divide light. Nothing has rounded them. There is no sand to blast them smooth, almost no wind, and no water on the outside except for a phenomenon so slight and intermittent that it might be mistaken for a flaw in vision.

At this elevation the sky is the colour of cold iron, though nothing here is cold. The sun hangs as a brown, weakened disc, giving more glare than illumination. Heat comes up through the Needle itself and radiates from every ledge. The thin air shimmers without brightening. Sometimes a fracture contracts with a faint, solitary tick; sometimes a plate of glass splits from the summit and falls soundlessly out of sight. Otherwise the spire might be a dead thing.

Twenty metres below its point is an opening no larger than a thumbnail.

Beyond that opening is Serein.

It is not a miniature city. The threshold simply disagrees with the mountain about measurement. What should be a cavity inside the rock contains a vertical settlement of avenues, pressure locks, landings, drains, stacked transit lanes, and districts marked by thin bands of coppery salt. Its central space is a hollow with no visible bottom. Streets cling to parts of it; others stop at the darkness and continue, after an interval, somewhere else.

Serein is urban in its organization and almost empty in its substance. Eleven principal structures stand at great distances from one another: narrow towers of white ceramic laminated with obsidian, a ribbed reservoir, several windowless halls whose walls are only thick enough to cast shadows. Between them lie immense reserves of untouched space. Some streets consist entirely of a handrail, a gutter, and an illuminated address. A bridge may cross the hollow in three disjoint sections, each section complete according to its own survey.

There are older layers beneath the ceramic—black arches, sealed stairwells, heavy conduits from the city’s first construction—but nothing is ruined. Ruin would require neglect, and Serein is continually attended to. It merely has very little in it.

The light is dim and reddish, leaking from expansion joints and from the mineral seams beneath wet surfaces. It never reaches the centre of the hollow. Hot air moves slowly there, carrying a faint taste of metal and clean stone. No plants root in the joints. No animals nest in the ducts. There is no cloth dust, no organic film, no decay. Even the fine powder on the dry walkways is abiotic: glass flour, ceramic salt, and trace metals worn from mechanisms that have never been touched by a hand.

Occasionally a blue-white line travels along a rail, or a lens-shaped distortion crosses an empty district, briefly making the far wall appear nearer. These are surveys, not inhabitants. They do not explore. They verify.

Serein alternates between parching dryness and a close, mineral damp.

For long intervals the walkways are dusty and the towers radiate a steady, almost invisible heat. Then pressure deepens in the lower streets. Vapor enters through capillaries too fine to see, and the air becomes heavy without becoming cooler. Certain structural ribs turn cold by exporting their heat into the folded space around the city. Condensation gathers along them: first a silver roughness, then distinct beads, then rivulets that follow grooves cut for water before the city had any.

The rain, when it comes, is sparse and warm.

Gravity in Serein is supplied locally, like lighting or pressure. Every bridge and street has its own down. The central hollow has none unless a transit lane is opened. A drop leaving an eave therefore falls only as far as that street’s jurisdiction. At the boundary it stops and hangs in the dark, trembling slightly, until a drain or lower district accepts it. A shower can take hours to cross the settlement. Seen from a balcony, rain appears as widely separated vertical threads, interrupted by motionless constellations of water.

It makes almost no sound. A tick on ceramic. A softer tick in a gutter. Once in a while, the low pressure gates adjust and produce a single note felt more in the teeth than heard. There is no machinery hum, no continuous running water, no voices.

Pockets remain after each rain: black little mirrors in expansion joints, warm pools no bigger than fingerprints, a skin of moisture beneath the reservoir stairs. The water dissolves minute quantities of salt and glass. In the sumps these are separated, instructed, and returned to the structure. During the next dry interval a new railing may precipitate along a previously empty edge, or a curb may extend several centimetres into the dark. Elsewhere an unused wall is reduced to pale dust and carried away.

This is the closest thing Serein has to an ecology: heat, water, mineral, and instruction circulating without an organism. Every rain leaves the city slightly altered. Nothing erodes in the ordinary way; surfaces are revised. A fresh edge remains razor-sharp until the city decides it should be old.

During the wettest phase, the municipal census briefly becomes enormous. Every suspended drop is registered as a temporary structure, assigned an address and a load value. For an hour Serein may contain millions of components. Then the last pools are taken into the capillaries, and it returns to eleven buildings, kilometres of vacant infrastructure, and the hollow between them.

The city was not made as shelter. Its architecture is the visible form of the calculation that keeps the Needle standing. Avenues are load paths; empty districts are tolerances; the rain redistributes stresses that ordinary materials could not survive. If Serein ever completed the calculation and stopped revising itself, the summit would become fully measurable. Altitude, gravity, and the rest of the waiting world could then find it.

So the city remains slightly unresolved.

From outside, all of this is only a hairline opening in hot glass. Once in a while a bead of water appears there, impossibly high above the weather. It shines dull red, draws back into the rock, and leaves no wet mark.

Within, a single drop hangs between two districts, waiting to be told which way is down.
