## The Hollow Orchard of Senn

In the lowest part of a warm salt basin, the crust swells around a dome of gray mineral no wider than a market square. There is no doorway in its upper surface, no vegetation around it, and nothing to suggest that the swelling is hollow. A traveler could pass within a few steps and remember only the heat, the white ground, and the unusual smoothness of the stone.

Beneath it is Senn: not a cavern containing a town, but a town folded into a single enclosed room.

Its floor is a shallow bowl of compacted mineral dust. Against the curved walls rise tier after tier of dwellings, seed mills, granaries, public ovens, stairways, storage niches, and lanes just wide enough for two people carrying baskets to pass. Some streets are open ledges; others burrow through the thickness of the wall and emerge three levels higher. The bricks are made from crushed salt and shell grit bound with vegetable pitch. Beams of dense black fiber support the upper balconies. Hammered native copper appears only in pins, knife edges, and the collars of tools.

All this construction crowds the perimeter. The center is empty.

It is an immense absence by the standards of the place, rising from the lowest floor to the luminous roof, unbridged and unroofed. Most of Senn’s volume is simply warm air. The architecture gives the impression of a crowded settlement until one looks inward and realizes that a city has been arranged around almost nothing.

The light is fierce. The mineral shell is translucent, and at external noon the whole interior becomes white-gold, too bright for an unshielded gaze. The salt bricks throw light back into the deepest passages; the gardens break it into hard green reflections. Even when the basin outside darkens, the shell releases what it has stored, and Senn remains amber-bright until morning. There is no true night, only white hours and honey-colored hours. The air never becomes cold. It smells of hot resin, crushed seed, dry leaves, and the faint metallic sweetness of warm dust.

The sound is worse than the light.

It begins as pressure in the teeth and chest: a foundational tone too large to seem located anywhere. Above it are the hard buzzing of the wall flues, the hiss of salt grains moving in expansion joints, the dry clatter of millions of leaves, and irregular concussions like stone doors being slammed in another world. Speech is useless. A shout disappears an arm’s length from the mouth.

The shell makes most of the noise. It is very slightly out of round, and as it warms and cools, its buried ribs expand and contract against the surrounding salt. The friction excites a resonance that the town’s builders did not create and could not now reproduce. Their descendants have merely learned to build around it. Streets terminate in baffles. Ovens double as damping chambers. Stairwells carry disruptive harmonics away from dwellings. Certain rooms are shaped so precisely that a seed husk dropped inside them will hop for several minutes without being touched.

There is another peculiarity: sound in Senn does not vanish cleanly. A little of it settles. It sinks through the streets and collects in low places, so the oldest, deepest lanes are louder than the open center. The gardeners say that every sound ever made inside the shell is still present, though most has been worn too fine to distinguish. In the central void, where almost no dust exists, standing vibrations sometimes gather what little there is into motionless discs and vertical curtains. These hang in the brightness like diagrams of an invisible weather.

And everywhere except that central emptiness, the city is extravagantly green.

Dry vines pour over the balconies in ropes as thick as a torso. Fan-leaved shrubs crowd the public stairs. Black-green cushions cover entire roofs, while orchards of narrow, coppery trees rise from deep beds of powdered brick and salt. The foliage is not merely tolerant of the dryness. It has no relationship with water at all. There has never been a well in Senn, nor a cistern, nor a cup made for drinking.

A cut leaf does not bleed sap. It yields a little clear amber oil, which quickly hardens to varnish. The plants’ tissues are dry and flexible, their circulation non-aqueous, their roots chemically patient. They mine sulfur and other minerals from the shell grit, bind loose dust with resin, and use the city’s ceaseless vibration to move oils through their stems. Sound also shakes useful material free from the walls. The gardens therefore flourish not despite the roar but because of it. Where a flue is accidentally blocked, nearby leaves become rigid and pale. Where one is opened too far, growth turns lush almost immediately—and the brick beneath it begins to powder.

This is Senn’s principal work: keeping the green abundance and the resonance from consuming the architecture.

The inhabitants are few. One may cross several inhabited tiers without encountering anyone, and there are fewer residents than public ovens. They tend the city with stone adzes, copper knives, bow drills, fiber scrapers, and hand-turned seed mills. There are no engines, unless the shell itself is counted as one. Repairs are made by hand, a few bricks at a time. Pruned vines become cordage and structural fiber; pressed seed oil feeds both inhabitants and lamps; exhausted garden dust is mixed with fresh pitch and returned to the walls.

Their language is manual. They sign face to face or send instructions by tapping patterns through floors and railings, to be read by touch. They wear plugs of waxed fiber against the loudest frequencies, though these reduce the roar rather than silence it. A gardener judging a wall does not strike it and listen. She lays her cheek against it and feels whether its note is clean.

Nothing here weathers in the ordinary way. There is no rain to cut channels and no flowing water to round stone, yet every edge is softened. Vibration has worn the parapets smooth, cupped the thresholds, and polished the stair treads into shallow curves. Roots pry at mortar while the constant tremor rubs the loosened grains away. Even recent repairs are given blunt corners, because sharp projections quickly fracture.

Still, Senn does not look ruined. Its erosion and repair have been matched for a very long time. What falls becomes garden soil; what the gardens produce becomes mortar, fiber, and food; what the inhabitants build is slowly returned to dust. Balconies sag and are lifted. Streets narrow under foliage and are opened again. The city’s present shape is old not because it has resisted change, but because change has repeatedly arrived at the same form.

Animal life is almost absent. No birds cross the central void. No insects provide a background hum beneath the greater noise. The only creature regularly recorded is a small, colorless moth whose wings appear powdered with shell glass. Sometimes several emerge in a season; sometimes years pass with none seen at all. It lays its eggs in discarded seed capsules, and its brief flights are considered events worth climbing several tiers to witness.

During the white hours, the shell’s note rises and the orchards open their leaves. During the amber hours, contraction deepens the resonance and the driest seed capsules split. At certain transitions, thousands open within a few heartbeats. Their individual reports vanish into the din, but the result is visible everywhere: black seeds slipping through green foliage, gardeners moving along the ledges, fresh dust descending in thin threads from the upper walls.

The center remains untouched. No permanent bridge may cross it, no roof may cover it, and no garden may extend far enough to interrupt its volume. This is the one planning law that every generation has preserved. The resonance needs a place to assemble itself. Without the hollow, the pressure would distribute unpredictably through the town, and the settled balance of garden, brick, and sound would fail.

So Senn keeps its heart vacant.

From outside, there is only the low salt basin and a smooth swelling in the ground. Within that hidden shell are blinding light, warm mineral air, lush dry orchards, a city built with hand tools, and a noise sufficient to overwhelm thought. Yet most of the interior is still emptiness—an emptiness carefully maintained, green around its edges, and deafeningly alive.
