## The Undermere

The Undermere can be heard long before it can be seen.

Beyond the last low swell of bone-coloured stone, the air begins to tremble. Then the cloud thins enough to reveal the basin: shallow water, dark clay, scattered domes of bedrock, and almost nothing upright beneath a sky the colour of old pewter. With the sight comes the full sound—a vast, grinding roar, as though a river the size of the sky were being forced through a narrow door.

No waterfall explains it. There are no cliffs, no surf, and scarcely any slopes. The Undermere has been worn down for so long that even its rivers have lost the habit of falling. Water lies *on* the land more often than it runs through it, spreading in broad, almost motionless sheets, creeping around stones, or rising quietly from below. A footprint in the clay fills within moments. In some places the whole surface is soft over hidden water, though it looks firm enough to cross.

The basin is days across, yet no natural feature in its interior rises more than a few body-heights. Its bedrock is dense grey limestone, black where wet and pale where salt has dried on it. It emerges in long, rounded backs, like animals buried to their shoulders. Between them lie pans of fine silt, fields of blunt gravel, and pools whose margins cannot quite be decided. Even the old fractures have softened. Floods do not cut new channels now; they merely refill depressions older than any memory brought here.

Beneath this nearly finished landscape is a second one: flooded chambers, conduits, and siphons worn through the limestone. Their shapes, too, are ancient and comparatively stable. Rain across the region and changes in air pressure set them breathing. At thousands of small openings, the ground hisses. At the larger openings—smooth-mouthed pits called throats—it exhales with enough force to flatten rain into mist.

Near a great throat, the roar is not simply heard. It presses on the chest, buzzes in the teeth, and makes spoken language useless. Farther away, the sound separates into layers: a low subterranean pounding, the continuous hiss of smaller vents, the hollow boom of water shifting somewhere underfoot. Some pools are perfectly still at the surface yet thunder when an ear is brought close to them. During changes in weather, the entire basin seems to tighten around the noise.

The air is cool and almost always damp. Hard rain is uncommon, but fine rain arrives nearly every day, sometimes so slight that it is indistinguishable from fog. In still weather, moisture condenses on stone and runs from the undersides of ledges; the rocks weep without having been rained upon. Breath begins to show toward nightfall. The cold is rarely severe, but it is patient, and wet hides and clay hold it against the skin.

Cloud seldom lifts completely. Daylight has the weakness of late evening, with shadows so faint that animals seem to rest directly on their own reflections. On clearer occasions, a remote rim of darker grey can be seen around portions of the basin. More often, visibility ends after a few hundred paces, and distance becomes a matter of sound, memory, and the sequence of markers under one’s hand.

Nothing roots in the Undermere. There are no reeds around its pools, no moss in its fractures, no grass to show the passage of wind. Water meets mineral directly. Receding pools leave bare rings of salt, iron stain, and grey clay. From a distance, the land appears almost uncoloured; close up, it contains rust-orange seams, milk-blue crusts, black silt, and the dull yellow of sulphur around certain seeps.

The food web begins there, in films of microscopic life that use dissolved minerals rather than sunlight. They spread over submerged stone in layers no thicker than paint, cloud the water at seep mouths, and slough away in translucent ribbons when the chemistry changes. They can colonise a newly flooded hollow within days. Nothing about them resembles a meadow, but they support an abundance that the empty-looking surface conceals.

A pool no larger than a cloak may contain thousands of pale swimmers. Some hang motionless until touched by a current; others flick through the sediment in bright, brief stitches. Finger-long blind eels knot themselves around seep openings. After rain, the larvae of veilwings strip the films from stones, then rise as adults in sudden columns of membranous wings. Larger gliders follow these columns through the dim air, turning without haste, their bodies visible mainly when they cross water.

The most conspicuous animals are the slatebacks: broad, low grazers with segmented backs, soft wide feet, and crescent-shaped mouths adapted for rasping stone. The smallest can be held in two hands. The oldest are heavier than a person and leave tracks like dishes pressed into mud. They travel in herds, but the herds are widely separated. One may walk for an hour across apparently lifeless clay, then come over a barely perceptible rise and find hundreds of them packed around a seep, their wet backs repeating the grey of the bedrock.

They signal less by calling than by striking the ground. A slateback’s forefoot hitting limestone can be felt through the feet long after the sound has vanished into the general roar. Much of the basin’s fauna navigates in this way, through vibration in mud, water, and bone. Eyes tend to be small. Bodies tend to be pale, black, or the colour of silt.

Predators rarely announce themselves. The clayjaws lie buried with only a sensory seam exposed, waiting for the particular tremor of a mired animal. A strike appears as a sudden closure of mud, a ring of water, and sometimes nothing more than a slateback’s empty track ending at the surface. Scavengers gather afterward: shell-legged waders, small eels, and patient gliders that descend only when the larger animals have gone.

Rain sets these scattered populations moving. Seeps enlarge, films spread, buried eggs hatch, and herds leave old pans for newly flooded ones. Clayjaws follow the migration routes. Veilwings emerge over water that may remain for only a few days. Then the level falls, the films contract toward permanent springs, and much of the life retreats below the surface. The shape of the land barely changes, but its occupation is continually revised.

Human presence is slight and easily missed. A hut may stand alone beside a seep, with no other dwelling within half a day’s walk. The inhabitants build into the lee of bedrock domes or raise low, rounded structures of fitted stone, hide, and clay. Nothing is tall. Tall walls drum in the vibrations and eventually shake themselves apart. Entrances turn once or twice and are hung with heavy hides, less to exclude the cold than to reduce the roar enough for voices to be understood indoors.

There is no timber. Cords are braided sinew; roofs are hide weighted with stone; lamps burn animal fat in hollowed bowls. Tools are chert flakes, bone awls, stone scrapers, and shell blades reset when their edges fail. Soft clay wrapped in hide serves as ear protection. Outside, people rely on hand signs, touch, and brief patterned strikes against stone.

Their other works are fainter still: a stone weir at a seep outlet, a line of submerged stepping places, a windbreak no higher than a knee, a half-buried shelter abandoned to silt. Route cairns are shaped to be recognised by hand in fog. Some are so old that their upper stones have been lost and only a row of rounded gravel remains. It is often impossible to tell whether a straight seam in the clay is a former path, a collapsed wall, or an accident of water.

The oldest travel rule concerns silence. If a throat stops abruptly while the rest of the basin is roaring, experienced people leave its vicinity at once. The pause means the pressure below is turning. Sometimes the throat then inhales, drawing down fog, spray, and anything loose around its mouth. Sometimes water first rises through every nearby footprint, and the exhalation that follows throws cold mist and panicked animals across the pan. The huts are placed with these reversals in mind, behind stone baffles and above the old flood stains.

At the dim end of day, the Undermere appears deserted again. Huts darken into the shapes of natural domes. Herds settle into shallow water until only their backs remain exposed. A distant throat lifts a white plume into the cloud, and somewhere a single fat lamp makes a small amber point behind a hide curtain. The roar continues without acknowledging any of it.

The stone here is nearly finished. The water is not. Nor are the films, herds, hatchings, hunts, and brief occupations of the pans. The Undermere is not a dead world with a little life scattered over it. It is an ancient, almost-flattened world with a thin cold skin—and beneath and upon that skin, more motion and noise than the eye would ever predict.
