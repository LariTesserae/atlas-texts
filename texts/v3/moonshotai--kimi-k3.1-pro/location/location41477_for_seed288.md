## The Dry Choir

The sound reaches the descending passages before the city can be seen: a dry, continuous roar, as though gravel were being poured somewhere inside the rock. Nearer, it separates into layers—wingbeats, insect stridulation, shrieks from roosting ledges, the small hard clicking of mouthparts—before the echoes weld everything together again. In the largest vaults, a shout cannot be understood from an arm’s length away. Yet the animals making the noise are often invisible. One can stand in an apparently empty court, seeing only a lizard and several moths, while the walls seem to contain a multitude.

The city occupies a chain of broad, low caverns below the surface. It is not a single chamber with buildings in it, but a region of connected districts: outer galleries choked with ancient collapse, open construction yards where nothing has been constructed for ages, low residential warrens, roost vaults, and gardens gathered beneath breaks in the roof. From a high ledge, the pale floor extends beyond the reach of the dim light. Natural pillars alternate with built buttresses. Streets run between them, wide and mostly vacant. The farthest districts do not end in a wall; they simply lose contrast and become part of the gray distance.

Everything exposed is old and rounded. Doorways have become oval hollows. Stairways have worn into ramps, and ramps into shallow slopes. In the oldest quarter, the city is nearly flat: whole neighborhoods survive only as long swellings under the dust, with streets marked by the absence of roots rather than by walls. There are no picturesque heaps of fresh rubble. Even the collapses have settled, been powdered underfoot, and acquired the same matte surface as everything else. Where settling has opened a section through a wall, its age appears in layers—dark mineral masonry, pale dust, dark masonry again—like the bands of an extremely slow-growing tree.

The construction is unmistakably organized, but nothing here was manufactured. There are no cut blocks, metal fittings, ceramics, cords, lamps, or abandoned implements. There were never any tools. The walls consist of minute stone grains carried individually and fixed with plant resin by small colonial animals known loosely as masons. Most of them work inside the masonry and are seldom seen. Repeated over immense periods, their simple inherited behaviors have produced ventilation flues, graded passages, storage cells, arches, roosting shelves, and districts with distinct functions. The city is behavior accumulated into mineral.

Cool air moves weakly through the flues. It smells of chalk dust, dry leaf oil, guano, and animal musk, with no trace of mildew or wet stone. The temperature barely varies. Light enters indirectly through narrow, crooked openings to the surface; even at its strongest, it resembles a heavily overcast dusk. There are few hard shadows. Pale masonry reflects what little light there is, while the plants absorb it and appear almost black.

Those plants grow in separated groves, each occupying a place where light reaches the floor and animal waste has deepened into soil. Between them may lie hundreds of paces of bare street and sterile court. Within a grove, however, the growth is extravagant. Ropey trunks support layer upon layer of broad leaves; climbing stems curtain the walls; low, fronded plants overlap until the ground disappears. The vegetation is lush in density, not in succulence. Its leaves are thick, waxy, and curled, gray-green or deep plum where the light catches them. A broken stem closes quickly with dry resin. Fallen foliage remains stiff and audible underfoot.

Nothing drips. There are no pools, seeps, damp hollows, or mud. Dust does not darken when disturbed. What little water exists in the system is concealed inside living tissues and passed carefully through the food web. Plants retain it behind nearly impermeable skins; herbivores obtain it from leaves; predators and scavengers reclaim it from bodies before desiccation can take it. Dead material is not left to rot in the open. Beetles, crickets, larvae, and the fine roots threading their galleries reduce it to powder and begin again. The landscape has no water cycle. Its inhabitants have a water budget.

The fauna are abundant without usually appearing crowded. Ash-colored moths rest among the foliage. Armored beetles work through leaf litter, and long-legged crickets occupy the hollow cells of the walls. Small furred grazers follow polished paths between groves. Geckos and slender snakes hunt along the masonry, while bats and swift-like roosting birds use the upper vaults. A district may look sparsely inhabited until a roost opens or a grove is disturbed; then the number of wings revealed seems out of all proportion to the visible space.

Most of the deafening sound comes from these concentrations and from insects calling inside resonant chambers. The caverns gather small noises and return them from several directions at once. Empty courts do not offer silence; they act as sounding chambers for colonies elsewhere. Near the center, the high calls become a painful pressure in the ears, while the lower roar of wings is felt in the ribs. At the margins of the city, after the last visible structures have disappeared, the sound continues for a long time as a vibration in the rock.

Yet the noise gives a false impression of activity. Almost nothing in the landscape changes quickly. The largest plants are centuries old and add tissue imperceptibly. Masons repair more than they expand, sometimes spending generations restoring a passage that gravity has narrowed by the width of a grain. A grove may take a thousand years to cross a court. The closing of a ventilation flue, the migration of a colony, or the settling of an old vault counts as a major event. There are no floods, wet seasons, or sudden vegetative flushes. Daylight brings only a gradual graying and darkening, and the choruses rise and fall with it.

Construction and decay have nearly balanced. The city no longer spreads outward; it maintains a shape. Its oldest and newest surfaces are difficult to distinguish, and its empty spaces are not clearings awaiting occupation. They are part of the design that has emerged: distances between groves, hunting grounds between roosts, silent-looking streets carrying sound from one district to another.

From above, the whole place can resemble an abandoned city after an unimaginably long abandonment—rounded ruins, vacant avenues, dark islands of growth beneath a dim roof. But abandonment would imply that activity belonged to the past. Here the visible city is sparse and nearly motionless, while the audible city is innumerable. Its roar is not upheaval. It is continuation: the sound of an ancient, waterless place sustaining exactly as much life as it can afford, and no more.
