## The Finished Garden

Near the summit of a black phonolite needle, at an altitude where the horizon is visibly curved and the plains below have faded to mineral abstractions, there is a chamber seven paces long.

It is easy to miss. Six iron staples climb the outer rock and stop at a seam no wider than a shoulder. Beside them is an enamel tag, its number worn down to a single pale stroke. There is no door, no path, no larger ruin. The needle itself has been weathered into smooth flutes and rounded lips, but nothing loose remains on it. No grit gathers in the crevices. No dust lifts from the ledges. The dry world below may possess plains of salt and ranges of powdered stone, but up here even erosion appears to have finished its work.

Outside, sound is almost unavailable. The atmosphere is too thin and cold to carry it with any conviction.

Inside, sound is unavoidable.

It begins through the rock: a pressure in the hand, then in the wrist, then in the teeth. At the entrance it becomes a close industrial chord—a mill’s low grinding, the dense drone of a transformer, and a high metallic note like a whetstone touching a blade that never quite reaches its edge. It is loud without being explosive. There are no crashes, no intervals of relief, no rhythm that could be used to count time. The chamber holds one complex, continuous note.

The entrance admits only a little sky. Three caged lamps, set deep into the rear housing, add a dim amber light. Nothing flickers. Beyond the threshold, stone, metal, and foliage all share the same even coolness. There is no frost, because there is nothing here to freeze: no dew on the leaves, no dampness beneath the roots, no sap sealed in the stems. Even in the deepest recesses, the chamber is absolutely dry.

At first, there seems to be no room to enter.

Vegetation occupies the pocket completely. Broad, bottle-green leaves overlap in dense shingles; black fern-work unfolds in layers so fine that the fronds seem to have been drawn inside one another. Moss like velvet wire packs the joints between larger plants. Roots, cables, and narrow ribs of stone form a springy pavement underfoot. The walls are not visible except where leaves thin to translucent scales. The ceiling is a compressed darkness of stems and undersides.

Every space between these growths contains a finer growth. Every space between those contains root-hair, wire, or woven mineral filament. Only beside the rear housing is there enough open room to hold out both hands without touching something.

The colors are lush but subdued: verdigris, deep green, violet-black, pale sulphur along the veins. Where the lamps shine through a leaf, its interior appears not as wet tissue but as an intricate dry lattice. These plants have no fluid cells. Their flexibility comes from microscopic frameworks of carbon and silica; their veins are solid conductors. Their roots penetrate seams of powdered salt and native copper, while their leaves and stems harvest the vibration that fills the chamber.

They are not stone imitating vegetation. They are soft, pliant, elaborately formed—and unmistakably botanical. But there are no flowers, fruits, or seed pods. Nothing here pollinates, browses, nests, burrows, or scavenges. There are no insects in the foliage, no animals at the opening, not even old evidence of them: no web, husk, track, shell, or dropping. The garden has never required an animal to complete any part of it.

At the back stands the engine.

It is industrial in the heavy, legible sense: three finned iron cylinders, copper busbars thick as wrists, ceramic insulators, small circular gauges, a handwheel set into a housing of cast metal. It resembles equipment removed from a factory and reduced to its essential core. Yet no pipes lead away from it, no exhaust leaves it, and nothing emerges that could be called a product. None of its visible parts move. The sound comes from stacks of dark crystal seated between the cylinders, held in uninterrupted resonance.

The vegetation passes through machined ports in the housing. At first the roots seem to have invaded it, but they occupy their channels too precisely. They neither force the casing apart nor avoid its moving parts. Root and busbar join without a clear boundary. Leaves brace components that would otherwise shake loose; the engine’s vibration, returned through a hundred dry tissues, keeps the garden supplied with charge.

The engine is not infested, and the garden is not merely being kept alive. They are two halves of one closed industrial ecology. The engine makes the garden possible. The garden keeps the engine in tune. Between them, they manufacture nothing but continuation.

A few human-made things remain outside that circuit: a threshold slab worn smooth at its center, a ceramic fuse half visible beneath the root mat, three empty bolt holes, a faded survey line crossing the rock and disappearing under foliage. The rear housing could be called a building, though only just. There is no settlement to explain it and no road by which its pieces could conveniently have arrived.

Age has softened everything without reducing it to ruin. The iron has a dry plum-colored tarnish rather than flaking rust. Paint has faded but not peeled. The aperture is rounded, the floor polished, the lamp cages dulled. There are no fresh scars and no recent debris. Roots fill the stone’s fractures without widening them. The old work of weathering has not been resisted; it has reached a form in which there is nothing more it is permitted to take away.

That completeness extends to the garden. Lushness usually implies events—unfurling, shedding, rooting, rotting, replacement. None of those events occurs here. Every leaf is open. Every stem has its final length. There are no buds waiting under the scales and no dead leaves beneath the roots. The gauge needles do not tremble. The lamps do not brighten or fade. A particular translucent frond casts the same narrow shadow across the same insulator, indefinitely.

Even the cold has no gradients. The engine is not warmer than the stone; the entrance is not colder than the rear wall. Touch offers no way to orient oneself. The sound, too, remains fixed: no beats develop between its tones, no resonances swell and recede. The chamber is not a moment frozen in anticipation of release. It is a finished state, with no available next shape.

It is easy to mistake the place for a survivor of some busier age. Instead, it seems to be what busyness became after every unnecessary process was removed. One engine. One garden. One dim, crowded room above a waterless world. One loud note, held without effort.

It is not waiting to be discovered. Discovery would not complete it.

It is already complete.
