## The High Cistern

The cistern rests at a great height, but there is no mountain beneath it. Its altitude has been built.

Below its floor are the roofs of older rooms; below those, sealed stairs, buried conduits, and walls enclosing walls. The descended structure is so old and so continuous that no exposed foundation remains. Nowhere within reach is there bedrock, soil, or an untouched seam of stone. Even the cliff-like outer face is masonry: small hand-formed units laid upon one another until their accumulation became a landform.

From outside, the cistern is only a dark joint near the summit, marked by a narrow copper lip and a permanent stain of dampness. Inside, it is no larger than a chapel. A swimmer could cross it quickly if there were a straight passage. There is not. Low vaults open into lower vaults. Short piers stand close together. Fired-clay pipes cluster along the walls and descend through the floor in dense, organized bundles. Niches, settling basins, and narrow cells interrupt every sightline. It is a small place made intricate by the closeness of its parts.

Water fills nearly all of it.

Only the highest cupolas retain air, and there it lies in seams no deeper than a finger or two. These trapped surfaces are black, trembling mirrors, almost indistinguishable from the ceilings above them. Everywhere else, water is the atmosphere. It is clear but not transparent to distance, because there is so little light. It is warm—about the warmth of a wall after sunset—and changes temperature so gradually that the difference between seasons is less than the difference between one side of a pier and the other.

The warmth enters patiently. Sun heats the outer ceramic shell and the dark roof beds; the thick construction stores that heat, blurring day and night before passing it inward. Water creeps through porous inlet tiles, crosses the warmed chambers, and eventually reaches the overflow. There are no wheels, gates, or moving mechanisms. The entire system works through levels, narrow openings, and the old reliability of seepage.

A single high joint admits the only daylight. At brightest, it appears underwater as a dim gray loosening of the darkness. It does not illuminate the room so much as give the darkness an edge. At night even that vanishes. No animal here makes light.

The submerged terrain was once precise. The central basin had a squared rim; the approach to it had steps; every pipe mouth was circular and every niche rectangular. Age has softened these distinctions. The steps are now a smooth, shallow descent. The basin rim survives as a broad swelling beneath the sediment. Exposed corners have rounded, and the narrow passages have become oval where water and sheltering animals have worried them for generations.

Yet almost nothing is actively falling apart. What could collapse collapsed long ago. What could settle has settled. Fractures that once admitted too much water have been narrowed by pale mineral deposits. Fallen fragments have cemented themselves into the floor. The vaults have found their final lines of pressure. Erosion has gone beyond ruin and become a kind of finishing: the structure is more stable now that nearly every unnecessary edge is gone.

Protected joints still preserve evidence of the makers. A thumbprint under a lip of glaze. The parallel grooves of a simple stone tool. Hammer marks on a copper staple no thicker now than wire. The bricks were shaped in small wooden molds, and some still carry the impression of grain. Nothing is machined; nothing fits with sterile exactness. The whole place bears the slight irregularity of hands, softened but not erased.

The water seems occupied before it seems inhabited.

Blind fish hang in the middle vaults in loose, dense layers, many separated by less than the length of their own bodies. They are pale, narrow-finned, and almost motionless, holding position by corrections too slight to see. When one turns, several neighbors turn after it, not in a flash of schooling but as a slow adjustment passing through the crowd.

The pier surfaces carry shellfish so closely set that they form pavements. Some are indistinguishable from the lime scale until a shell opens by a fraction. Translucent crustaceans work around their margins, testing the masonry with jointed limbs. At the inlet tiles, colonies of small tube-dwelling animals extend soft fans into water that appears perfectly still. In the clay pipes live longer, ribbon-bodied creatures. Most remain withdrawn, only their heads visible at the mouths. An open pipe can therefore seem to contain darkness, several pairs of sensory tendrils, and nothing else.

There are no plants: no algae in the dim joint of daylight, no roots in the sediment, no green film on the warmer tiles. The food web begins with bacteria and simpler films living on the slow chemistry of the construction itself. They use what the seepage brings and what clay, mortar, and old metal release in traces. Grazers crop these films without visibly diminishing them. Filter feeders take the finest suspended particles. Fish and the pipe-dwelling hunters take the grazers. Shells, waste, and bodies settle into the basin and return their minerals to the same close budget.

Life is abundant because it spends so little. Growth takes years. A disturbance may cause only a brief contraction of fans, a shell closing, one fish sliding behind a pier. When a hunter feeds, the vacancy it leaves in the crowd can remain apparent for days.

Sound is correspondingly scarce. There is no rush of inflow and no echoing drip in an open chamber. Sometimes a cooling tile ticks once. Sometimes a shell shuts with a small, hard note that travels farther than expected. At long intervals, water gathers on the outer copper lip until a drop releases. It falls away down the built height, and no impact ever returns.

The cistern’s motions are easier to measure in duration than in distance. Suspended sediment can take a day to settle. The water level changes by a finger’s width across a long wet cycle. Mineral crust narrows a pipe by the thickness of a shell over generations. A bubble appearing beneath one of the cupolas may remain there, flattened and reflective, until the trapped air seam shifts almost imperceptibly and absorbs it.

Nothing enters quickly. Nothing leaves whole. The architecture turns current into diffusion, rainfall into seepage, collapse into sediment, and noise into isolated events separated by silence.

It is no longer possible to say whether the place was built as a reservoir, a bath, or one part of some larger waterwork. Its original purpose has become less important than the agreement that persists among its materials: clay holds water, water softens clay, mineral deposits close the damage, and life occupies the narrow margin between those processes.

High above anything that could simply be called ground, the small made sea continues—almost full, almost lightless, almost silent, and crowded with patient movement.
