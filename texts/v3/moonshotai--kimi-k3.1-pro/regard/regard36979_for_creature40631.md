The power came first as listening.

It did not come as light. Light would have been an interruption. It did not come as silence; the herds would have drawn their young inward, and the longhands would have stopped tapping on the stems. It did not come as rain. In that country, rain was never only a kindness.

So, for a time, the basin contained one more attention and no new weight.

The listening went where sight could not. It heard a herd open a stand of herd-pods before it heard the seeds strike dust. It heard a keyfly miss the pitch of a cupbell, try again, and begin to weaken. It heard larvae chewing inside softened wood and a thornbird turning its head through small angles to find them. It heard the longhands’ judgments: the springy fork rejected, the blow checked, the stone dropped when the kernel had been taken.

Its first impulse was to mend.

It could have given the basin regular rain. It could have rounded the stone, deepened the water pockets, made the root mats honest underfoot. It could have spared the hunted and fed the hunters by some means that left no body behind. It could have placed in a longhand’s forked root a stone so well shaped that no seed would ever again be lost in litter.

But each mercy reached beyond the creature to which it was offered. Reliable water would have made corridors of what had been events. Kinder stone would have closed the sinks and also the rootways. A world in which nothing could be hunted would not merely have had less fear in it. It would have had different hungers, different journeys, different young.

By the time the power had made the basin safe, it would have made another place and called the substitution love.

So it did not improve the basin.

Neither did it therefore call everything in the basin good.

Nothing suffered in general. Suffering was always particular: this thirst, this injured hand, this small body exhausting itself against a wall. A community could be richly busy around an unanswerable cry.

The basin had drawn the power’s attention. What held it was a young rumbleback in a sink.

The animal had fallen through a root mat where the corridor narrowed. Its legs were unbroken, but the walls were too sheer for its wedge-shaped body. Above it, the hole admitted a little brown-green light and occasional dust. It had called until the herd’s answers were gone. Now it called at longer intervals, not because it understood abandonment, but because it was tiring.

At the bottom, flattened scavengers kept to the cracks. They were not cruel. They were patient.

The power saw what would happen if it did nothing. Moisture would gather around the body. Fungi would enter what the scavengers left. Larvae would use the brief richness, and the sink would remain ecologically busy long after the last cry.

All of that was true.

It was not an answer.

The power let the calf’s next call pass through stone and root felt with the reach of an adult bellow. Far along the corridor, a female stopped. She knew the small catch at the end of its rhythm. She turned; others turned around her, not from understanding, but because a herd is a body continually changing its mind about its shape.

While she came back, the power lifted the calf out.

It made no ramp. A ramp would have become a water trap, then a rootway, then perhaps a corridor, and the power did not wish to hide a large alteration inside a small mercy. It simply set the young animal on the far side of the rim.

When its mother arrived, she struck it once with the side of her mouth, hard enough to stagger it, then smelled it from shoulder to hoof. The calf called. She answered so low that the note seemed to come through its legs rather than through the air.

The sink remained open. The scavengers remained patient. Nothing was punished for having expected to eat.

That was not the only cry the power answered. It made no rule that mercy had to be rare in order to be wise. But neither would it make mercy into weather, falling indifferently on hunter and hunted, root and fracture. Each act had to remain small enough to understand. Where rescue would merely have appointed another victim, the power did not call the outcome good. It stayed with the dying and, once pain could teach no further escape, made the end brief.

To the longhands it left no shaped stone.

The first object kept after hunger had gone would change more than the way a seed was opened. It would become a location, a memory outside the body, perhaps eventually a debt or an inheritance. That discovery, if it came, should belong to them. If one day they made a question that could outlast the asking, the power would answer honestly. Until then, it would not hurry them merely because it could see a little way down one possible road.

For the basin as a whole, the power kept only two intentions. It would not hold the place still. And it would not allow the whole country’s capacity for further becoming to be extinguished by some blind accident from outside. Between those refusals, it left the basin free.

Nothing marked the visit.

Seamroots went on opening stone and binding the fragments. Cupbells remained shut to the wrong wingbeat. Longhands dropped their stones; thornbirds abandoned their thorns. Clearings became events and were negotiated shut. The rare rains came, made their brief rivers, and withdrew into damp stains beneath stones.

The future remained difficult.

But in one herd crossing a root-choked gorge, a mother gave her low note, and the answers returned through foliage and flesh: one young voice, another, then the small damaged rhythm she had gone back for.

The herd moved on, counting itself.

Every time it counted, it came to one more.
