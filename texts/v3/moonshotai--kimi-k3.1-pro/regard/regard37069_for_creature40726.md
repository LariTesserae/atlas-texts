### Keeping the Knock

First, the power would do nothing quickly.

It would stay through enough reversals to learn that the roaring was not a cry for silence. It would see gardens flower after heat and fail after abundance; it would follow a falling shred of frond through several hungers and several lives. It would watch a snail spend weeks on a ledge, and not conclude that the snail’s life was small. It would learn the needle-eel’s route well enough to distinguish knowledge from ignorance: the eel knew its province precisely, and the province was not the sea.

Only then would it consider help.

The cap would offer the easiest and most catastrophic kindness. Open it. Let in air, sight, witnesses. Relieve the pressure. Give the hidden ocean a sky.

The power would not do so.

Nor would it widen the bore, quiet the valves, still the turbine, or spread the gardens across the bare reaches because bareness looked like want. It would not rescue every spawning cloud from the threadfish, nor preserve every good refuge from the crab that might one day strip it. It would not save the whole by making each life safe in advance. A sea so thoroughly cared for would become an arrangement maintained for the power’s satisfaction.

Its first mercy would be to refuse the improvements that benevolence most easily mistakes for love.

The old needle-eel might be its greatest temptation. The power could show it, in an instant, the whole nine kilometres: the golden rim beneath the diaphragm, the olive provinces, the garnet curtains, the white basin and the interrupted amber rings. It could explain the cap, the pumps, the heatworks, the obsolete numbers. It could give one inhabitant the address of everything.

Instead, it would leave the eel its earned knowledge: the bracket where a garden had failed, the ledge where crabs brooded, the seam that released larvae after warmth. It would not make a creature’s sufficient world suddenly feel like ignorance.

But restraint within the Deep would not require idleness outside it.

There, the power would act carefully and without spectacle.

It would keep the cap from becoming interesting.

It would put no inscription on the brass, no radiance beneath the corrosion, no warning elaborate enough to invite investigation. It would not make the place sacred in a way that could be discovered. The old numbers would remain illegible. The cap would remain one warm, minor fitting among many.

Elsewhere, its care would resemble adequate maintenance and unremarkable luck. A pump bearing would be replaced before it seized. An external gasket would be found weeping during an otherwise routine inspection. A proposed chemical flush would be diverted from a circuit no one had realised was alive. On a demolition drawing, the old balancing loop would remain useful as a reserve, or awkward enough to isolate, or simply not worth the expense of disturbing. Where possible, the power would act on things rather than people: on fatigue, omission, timing, the route of a pipe.

It would not preserve the Deep by making the world beyond it poorer. It would merely refuse to let forgetfulness become, by itself, sufficient reason for destruction.

Nor would it necessarily grant endlessness. To continue forever is not the only way for a life, or a sea, to have mattered. Gardens would still fail. Crabs would still inherit and ruin ledges. An eel could still clear a reach of young fish. The machine would remain old. The power would give time, not exemption from time; protection from the casual ending, not imprisonment in an unchangeable perfection.

And if, in its keeping, it could remember more than the system—if it could remember not only the persistence of glass-shelled snails but one snail’s interrupted grazing line, not only brooding crabs but one brood released into ascending water—then it would remember those things too. It would not love the inventory more than the lives.

Inside, there would be no sign that any decision had been made.

A valve would begin to close. The wall would carry the news first. Seam-fans would shut; snails would settle their shells; the eel would become motionless beside a gasket. Far below, the turbine crowns would withdraw. Then would come the clean blow, the reversal, the brief upward streaming of fronds, sediment, spores, and young.

Ninety seconds later, the long glide would resume.

To the felt, the power’s care would be a leak that still arrived slowly enough to feed it. To the seam-fans, another ascent. To the basin colonies, dust after the groan. To the needle-eel, a province not suddenly made strange. None of these would be messages. None would require gratitude.

The power would leave no name in the water. Its only monument would be the next eleven minutes.

Outside, a palm held against the brass would still feel warmth, the faint double knock, and the long vibration after it.

The power would not answer the knock.

It would only keep it from being answered by a spanner.
