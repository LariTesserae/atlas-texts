It would do nothing at first.

It would remain through a hatch and through the failure of a hatch; through drawdown, rain, drying, and return. It would learn that a white pan might be empty only in the present tense, that a silent roof did not necessarily contain an abandoned roost, and that birds leaving a feeding ground were not always witnessing its destruction. A power that intervened after one hot afternoon would know too little to be kind.

It would not make the Low Saltern green.

It would not put reeds along the causeways, sweeten the brine, cool the intakes, or bring soil into the rail joints. It would not try to correct the hottest waters or populate the sterile salt floors. Above all, it would not move every pan into the narrow condition where life was most visibly abundant. The Saltern did not depend on universal tolerance. It depended on difference: between one water and another, between wetting and drying, between a pan now and that pan six hours later.

Nor would it stop the works. A gate opening could destroy one feeding arrangement and make another. Pumping could strand prey, deliver food, expose a film, or bring a drawn-down pan to the depth at which birds began walking in from the farther shore. Even the warm roofs and illuminated insects belonged to the accommodation. A motionless sanctuary would not be the Saltern perfected. It would be a different place, and eventually a poorer one.

There would be no single inhabitant whose rescue could discharge the power’s duty. It could not save the place by saving only the birds, or the microbial films, or the people in the occupied block. Neither would it regard the whole as a harmony. The crab beneath its slab, the bats above a warm channel, the driver watching an outfall, and the cysts in a dry margin did not want the same things. Some did not want anything that could be called a future beyond the next season.

What would concern the power was not change, or even loss, but the possibility of total simplification.

One pan might be flooded, one nesting dike washed out, one roof stripped while a roost was occupied. These were real losses, and not all of them would be acceptable. But migration, dormancy, local knowledge, and sheer animal movement could survive many such events. What they could not survive was every refuge becoming impossible together: every mixing channel brought to the same grade, every margin scraped clean, every warm roof closed, every suitable pan altered on one schedule and for one tidy operational reason.

Against that, the power would act.

Its miracles would take the form of alternatives.

A gate expected to seize might turn once more. During a general shutdown, one disused mixing channel might continue to receive a trace of seawater through a leak too small to appear on any diagram. A contaminated discharge might be caught long enough for someone to notice the change in colour. None of these interventions would preserve a pan forever. Each would merely prevent one bad hour from becoming the last hour.

The power would buy time rather than impose outcomes: time for a rain cell to cross the basin, for a worker to reach a valve, for birds to move one pan farther on, for swimming shrimp to leave a generation behind them in a margin. It would introduce redundancy not only into machinery, but into waters, seasons, and knowledge. Its characteristic act would be to leave a second way for the future to happen.

Towards the people, it would be equally careful. It would not command them to love the animals. Flies would still foul instrument glass; gulls would still enter stores; a delayed repair would still sometimes mean a dangerous night shift. A benevolence that required exhausted people to become saints would only have found another way to spend their lives.

If it took human form at all, it would take a shift.

It would arrive as relief rather than revelation. It would listen in the canteen, learn which gauges lied, and be remembered chiefly for having allowed someone else to sleep. It would not call a dry pan dead without looking twice. Before leaving, it might ensure that one experienced worker had time to teach another what could not be inferred from salinity and pressure alone. Knowledge existing in only one person’s memory would seem to the power nearly as precarious as life existing in only one culvert.

If it left anything resembling a teaching, it would be a question small enough to enter ordinary maintenance:

*Elsewhere?*

If this channel were to be closed, where would the fish retreat? If this pan were dried, which other margins would remain tolerable? If this roof had to be repaired, was there another warm, sheltered space, and had anyone looked? If this shift could not make the careful choice safely, who could be called before the choice became irreversible?

Sometimes the answer would be nowhere, and the work would still proceed. The power would not demand that every operational need yield to every nest or hatch. But the question would make ignorance harder to confuse with necessity. Occasionally the answer would be another gate, another vehicle route, another week, another roof. Care would become part of competence rather than a separate and expensive virtue.

Nor would the power make the works immortal. Perpetual salt production would be no more obviously good than perpetual stillness. When the city’s industrial life eventually ended, its concern would again be with sequence rather than simultaneity. Not every gate need close on one day. Not every connection to the sea need be severed before another pattern of water had established itself. Not every old roof need come down while it was still the only roof. Whatever remained afterwards might be smaller, wilder in some respects and poorer in others. It would not be a museum. The power would not preserve its own idea of the Saltern at the expense of what might follow.

From the final rail embankment, none of this would be visible. The city would remain a few roofs, stacks, and isolated lamps in a dim wet plain. Salt would still shorten the life of tools. Rain would still arrive warm. Workers would still cover their mugs, and some pans would still return to white silence.

But after the bad failure, or the hard season, or the final necessary closure, there would remain somewhere from which return was possible.

Perhaps that would be the whole of the intervention: not to guarantee abundance, but to preserve the sources of abundance; not to make every life safe, but to prevent avoidable desolation from becoming complete; not to take the works out of human hands, but to leave human beings with enough knowledge, freedom, and rest to act as more than the immediate emergency required.

Then the power would go. Its name would enter no maintenance record.

At a sluice, hours after some ordinary change in pumping, shrimp would begin to gather in seams and eddies. Fish would move into the channel below. Birds would appear along a shore that had seemed unoccupied at dawn. In an old logbook, beside a pressure and a salinity, someone might add three words without thinking them especially significant:

*Pan coming on.*
