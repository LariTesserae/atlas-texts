Their attention did not arrive as light.

Nothing in the Sump would have answered light. It did not arrive as coolness, or a voice in the control cabinet, or a sudden abundance of water. For a long time, it made no difference that could have been measured.

The power waited.

Not because the chamber was sacred and must never be touched. Not because stillness was necessarily good, or because every ending became wise when left uninterrupted. They waited because any kindness performed before they understood the scale of the life there would have been a kindness performed upon their own idea of it.

A hurried power might have tried to rescue the Sump.

They might have filled it with clean water, started all three engines together, restored the failed pipe, opened the folded fans. They might have brought soil, seed, insects, something warm-blooded to stir among the roots. They might have called this abundance.

Instead, the power watched one cushion darken while another remained dry. They watched a fan take days to lose a little of its resistance. They watched a wet bed remain nearly bare beside a discoloured hollow crowded with tissue. They saw that more water was not always more life, and that the dry lattice beneath the dead pipe could not be interpreted merely by looking at it.

They did not wake it to find out.

They did not insist on deciding where one body ended before they could care for it. Nor did they require the being to be conscious before its continuation could matter. But neither did they assume that, because nothing here cried out or fled, nothing could be lost.

Eventually, their attention came to a very small thing.

Inside the control cabinet, where the weak service lamp still burned, a relay was nearing failure. There was no drama in it. A little corrosion, a contact becoming less certain, a spring performing the work it had performed for years. Soon the relay would close without completing the circuit. The pump would remain still.

Afterward, the pipes would warm. The garden below them would use the water remaining in its cushions and sheets. Its fans would fold. Its roots would continue for a while at the chemical boundary, and then less certainly. There would be no moment at which the chamber announced that something irreversible had happened.

The power could have left the relay alone.

They could have called this respect. The machines were old; the purpose for which they had been built was gone; the grove had always lived at the edge of deprivation. Nothing in the Sump had been promised another wet cycle.

But there was no original purity here to defend. The rain was already the unintended consequence of an intervention. The being had made its life within that consequence. To let corrosion end it, merely because corrosion had arrived before the power did, would not have restored anything. It would only have allowed accident to decide, while the power kept its hands clean.

Still, they did not make the engines eternal.

They did not renew every cable, clear every pipe, or undertake to open every fan that had ever folded. They did not turn the Sump into a garden whose prosperity would forever demonstrate the goodness of its guardian. A being preserved by continuous correction might eventually become little more than the record of that correction.

So they removed the corrosion from one contact.

That was all.

They did not brighten the cabinet lamp. They did not straighten the service rail or free the spanner from its roots. They did not leave a sign in the enamel warning’s erased final line. The whole repair was such that a maintenance worker, had one ever returned, might have accomplished it in a few minutes and thought no more about it.

The power made no covenant concerning the bearings, the cables, or the other engines. They did not pretend to know already what care would require when those failed. Perhaps one day they would let the last pump stop. Perhaps they would cool one small length of pipe themselves, and only often enough to make return possible. Perhaps continuation would cease to be the good they had supposed it to be.

They would attend again.

For now, the good was small and plain: the next rain need not be prevented by that one film of corrosion.

The relay closed.

The pump turned. Coolant moved through the overhead pipes. Their surfaces cooled, and condensation began as a dull silver bloom. Hours later, the first drop struck iron. Another entered the shallow sump. Much later, one fell onto a copper cushion and vanished among its cups.

Nothing opened in response. There was no gratitude, no sign that the grove had received anything extraordinary.

Days afterward, if enough other drops had followed, one fold in one black fan relaxed by the width of a finger.

The power did not mistake this for praise. They had not given the being a new nature, or spared it every ending. They had only allowed its old weather to return.

Then they waited again.
