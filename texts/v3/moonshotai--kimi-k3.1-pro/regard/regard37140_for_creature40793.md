### The Unmarked Repair

The power came up the path on foot.

At the upper gate it was shown the public basin, the washing place, and the nearest overflow. Only then was it shown the view. It accepted a dry room and plain food, and stayed long enough for the explanations to stop. One morning, without ceremony, someone handed it a blocked channel stone and went elsewhere expecting the work to be done.

By then, the power had begun to understand what sort of benevolence would be required.

It did not open a spring in the cliff. Such a spring might have been a good gift, but not one to give without consultation. Every court below it would have had to answer for the water. Nor did it make the roofs proof against weather, or pour the keepers’ knowledge whole into the mind of an apprentice. A perfect description of a sound was still not the ability to hear it through sleep.

Most of all, it did not command the town to last.

Nothing in the keepers’ lives seemed to the power holy merely because it was difficult. It did not treasure worn hands, postponed meals, or an illness endured without complaint. Had they asked for help, it would not have answered by praising their sufficiency. Had they wanted a road, it would not have tried to preserve the narrow path for its own admiration. And if they had all chosen to go down the mountain, it would have gone with the people, not with the cisterns.

Its care was not a sentence of continuance.

So, for a while, it did ordinary work and nothing more.

Then, one night near the end of its stay, the keeper responsible for the upper roofs went to bed exhausted. She and two others had spent the day replacing a failed slab. At supper she had argued briefly about a missing coil of cord, eaten more than usual, and fallen asleep with stone dust still pale in the seams of her cuffs.

A little before dawn, a drop struck within one of the covered cisterns with a slightly flattened note.

The power heard it.

The keeper heard something too. She turned, but did not wake.

The power could have roused her. She would have risen without complaint. It could also have repaired the channel without leaving the room. Instead, it took enough of a body to use a brush and a wooden lever, and went out into the lane.

There was no emergency. Grit had gathered where two channels joined, turning a little water beneath a stone that should have remained dry underneath. Left until morning, it would have been a small repair. Left until the next season, it might have become an interesting one.

The power lifted only the stone that needed lifting. It brushed out the joint without polishing the channel’s bottom. It did not deepen a groove, improve a slope, or take the opportunity to make some enduring amendment. When it reset the stone, it left it so that another keeper could lift it again.

Then it covered the cistern, returned the tools, and waited.

Once more the drop fell. The interval was ordinary. The note was deep and clear.

In the morning, the keeper saw that someone had seen to the channel. She supposed one of the others had heard it during the night. No one claimed the work, and she did not make a mystery of it. She had slept until daylight. There was dust to sweep from the new slab.

That was all the power did then. It left no spring, no law, no promise of rain. It did not return every night to make watchfulness unnecessary. It had not come to make the keepers need it.

Soon afterward it went down the path, carrying away no token. If the town changed, its care would not require the change to be prevented. If the town ended, it would not fill the empty courts with remembered footsteps for its own consolation. It would not ask anyone to remain behind to complete the view.

Years later, no one could have said which hand had reset that stone. The work had stopped attracting attention.

On the Cistern Ledge, that meant it was finished.
