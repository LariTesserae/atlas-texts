### A Provisional Mercy

The power did not bring dawn.

It did not part the cloud, warm the basin, finish the arches, or teach the chords to speak in words the citizens could enter into evidence. It did not silence the water. It did not lift the scutes from the bridges, however easily it could have done so, because a mercy to one bridge might have been a famine on the next warm buttress.

Even light, it discovered, would have been an argument.

So for a long time it listened.

At first Vey seemed to possess one voice: water, shells, machinery, and animals crushed together into a single continuous pressure. Gradually the power learned to distinguish what the city itself often had trouble distinguishing. A loom’s note. The fine irregular excavation of borers. A pump beginning to cavitate. The propagation of movement through scutes before any one animal had traveled its own width. The upper harmonics of a chord returning from dwellings built after its call had begun.

It heard the citizens too. Not only their careful minutes and maintenance transmissions, but their irritation, their jokes, their lawsuits, their grief at plans that would outlive them, and their entirely reasonable desire that something, occasionally, should simply be fixed.

It learned that a street could be ecologically valuable and still be hated by the family that had lost the use of it. It learned that an unfinished arch was not necessarily a promise. Sometimes it was an experiment. Sometimes an compromise. Sometimes a mistake whose consequences had become too heavy to move.

It learned not to write *consent* beside a chord’s silence, or *death* beside a vanished call.

And it learned that the citizens’ celebrated patience was often only the patience of their files. People had been angry for centuries. Their anger had not always been copied.

That, at last, seemed a place where help might be given without becoming another kind of weather.

The power made an offer through ordinary ward channels. It would remember what was entrusted to it and return those memories with their sources, contradictions, and uncertainties intact. It would not reconcile accounts merely because later citizens preferred a tidy decision. It would not turn survival into approval, or the death of an objector into retrospective agreement. It would answer questions where it could, distinguishing what it observed from what it inferred.

Any ward could refuse it. No district would be required to keep its records there. It would not be the only copy, and it would not be the final authority.

Above all, it would not make itself load-bearing.

The offer was debated for a long time. Some citizens suspected that such modest assistance must conceal an omission. Others wanted the power to arbitrate reservoir rights, predict chord movement, or settle once and for all which unfinished structures were mistakes. It declined to treat these requests as though they were all the same request.

Nor did it make a doctrine of noninterference.

When three lock keepers were trapped by a pressure failure, it held the lock until they were out. Then it let the damaged mechanism be dismantled. It did not preserve the lock forever, and it did not use the keepers’ gratitude to purchase authority over the reservoir.

When it perceived seepage behind a ward wall, it reported the seepage, its location, and the limits of its certainty. It did not also redesign the ward.

But it would not call every inconvenience an emergency, and it would not make Vey quiet, clean, rapid, or unanimous for its own good.

Afterward, much of what it preserved was almost offensively plain.

Into the history of an unfinished arch went the load calculations, the failed proposals, and the final note of a loom listener who had attended its note for sixty-one years:

*Do not say I left this for you. I left it because I could not solve it.*

Into the records of a scute migration went grazing estimates, bridge loads, the warming plan for a sacrificial buttress, and the statement of a resident whose street had been impassable for forty-three years:

*I understand why it is closed. I still want my street back.*

Beside the account of a chord absent from every reservoir ledger for nineteen years, the power placed no conclusion at all. Later, when an unfamiliar call was recorded in deep water, it preserved the dispute over identification rather than selecting the identification that would have made the archive appear more reliable.

For a disappearance of microbial film from a supposedly dry wall, its entire contribution was:

*Absence observed here. Cause unknown. Wall not known to be dry.*

None of this reopened a street. None of it moved a chord, completed an arch, or relieved a drainkeeper of the opinion that a scute was vermin with a maintenance budget. The citizens continued to make bad decisions for good reasons, good decisions for selfish reasons, and decisions whose reasons no one could reconstruct until long after the consequences had become geological.

Sometimes the preserved memories made arguments worse.

The power did not consider that proof that the memories should be improved.

Vey remained extraordinarily loud. Sleet still accumulated around faceplates. Intakes sang. Scutes redistributed themselves across roofs and cables. Loom fields were withdrawn, or were not. A carcass became a district and outlasted the name first assigned to it. At the outer dams, ice continued to form on one side of a boundary that liquid water crossed on the other.

No general harmony emerged. The power had not come to administer one.

Centuries later, a load surveyor requested the history of a street closed by scutes. She received the expected tonnage estimates, several incompatible maps, and the voice of a woman dead for four hundred years saying that she had never accepted the closure.

It did not change the load.

It changed the minutes.

That was the sort of difference the power had decided it could responsibly make: not to spare Vey every loss, but to make certain quiet thefts more difficult. The theft by which silence became agreement. By which endurance became consent. By which the unfinished work of the dead was made to look like a gift they had intentionally bestowed upon the living.

The city did not become wiser all at once. It became slightly harder to misunderstand by accident, and slightly more troublesome to misunderstand on purpose.

Nothing in the basin was finished. The arrangement between Vey and its visitor was not finished either. That seemed to the power not a failure of benevolence, but one of its conditions.

On the municipal maps, where the wards eventually found it necessary to represent the arrangement at all, they marked it *provisional*.

The power left it that way.
