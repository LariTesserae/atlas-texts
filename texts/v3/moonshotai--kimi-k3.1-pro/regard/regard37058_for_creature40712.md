### What the Power Did

The power came without light.

Not as stealth. Light would have suggested that the darkness was something to be overcome.

Their first act was to distrust the speed of their own pity.

A surface occurred to them. A sun above it. A little erosion, so that the mountain could console them by becoming old. A past for the Porter. A future in which the Surveyor might stand beneath an actual roof. A companion for the glass eel. A first, almost imperceptible movement of one hand on the civic clock.

Each intervention had its reasons. Each would also have made the Ward easier to love from elsewhere.

They did not call these ideas wicked. That would have been another way of avoiding attention. But none had been requested, and they did not yet understand what a request would mean in a place that could not retain the asking.

Nor did they decide that the Ward was perfect. They did not mistake the clock’s zero for a moral instruction.

So they made themselves small enough to be refused, and went to the boundary.

They entered as a swimmer. They did not counterfeit helplessness, but neither did they exempt themselves from the cold. The water closed around them without turbulence. Above, below, and beyond the seven faint lights, the darkness remained exact.

The Porter’s hand found them near the boundary: cool, firm, careful not to pull.

“You can go back,” she said, “or you can breathe here.”

The power considered.

She did not mistake their silence for distress, or their stillness for consent. She simply waited, her coat hanging according to a down they did not share.

They had expected their first important choice to concern the town. Instead it concerned one breath, and belonged to them.

They breathed.

At the surveying mast, the Surveyor asked whether they had come from above.

“From elsewhere,” the power said.

“Is elsewhere above?”

“Sometimes.”

The Surveyor looked into the lightless water.

“Then tell me about a roof.”

The power told them about a house where rain had entered above the stairs. At first the family had set out a cooking pot. When the pot proved too small, they replaced it with a washing tub. The tub caught the water but blocked the stairs, so they moved it; after that the rain ran down a wall and discolored a room where a child slept. Someone repaired the roof badly. Someone else repaired it well. The bucket was put away. The stain was painted over. Years later, a woman who had grown up in the house returned and stood for a while in the dry stairwell.

“Was she grateful?” the Surveyor asked.

“Yes.”

“Why did she stand there?”

“She missed the sound of the rain in the pot.”

The Surveyor considered this.

“Did she want the leak back?”

“No. She was troubled by missing it.”

“And later?”

“Later she allowed herself to be both grateful and sorry.”

The Surveyor asked whether the pot had been glazed, whether the ceiling had bowed, and why anyone would paint over a stain rather than leave it as a record of where the water had been. The power answered as well as they could.

They did not offer to give the Ward a leak.

Beneath the motionless clock, the dissenter said, “You are thinking of giving us time.”

“I have considered it.”

“To whom would you give it?”

“To those of you here.”

“And who would bear it?”

The power was silent.

The dissenter looked up at the concentric hands. “There would be more of us, in a sense. Or the same us with reasons to regret what we had accepted. We could object tomorrow to a choice made today. That is one of the uses of tomorrow.”

“Yes.”

“But we cannot meet the people who would inherit our consent.”

“No.”

“Then you see the difficulty.”

The power did.

“And if I simply remember you?” they asked.

“That is yours to do,” said the dissenter. “Unless you make it ours.”

“How would I do that?”

“By returning with our affection already decided for us. By quoting our objections when we no longer wish to sustain them. By calling your memory our biography and then asking us to live up to it.”

The power sat beneath the clock for some while after that. No conclusion was reached. The dissenter did not appear to regard this as a failure.

Near the outer terrace, the glass eel passed.

Its transparent body took no visible purchase on the water. For an instant the address light found the dim points within it, and the black joint at its thickened end seemed less like an organ than a place where darkness had been carefully tied.

The power understood that they could know what it was.

Not merely observe it. Know it: follow the fact of the creature backward or inward until its privacy became an explanation. Nothing in the Ward appeared able to prevent them. That was precisely the difficulty.

They turned their lamp aside.

The eel passed through the weak edge of the beam and went on. Understanding, too, could be an entrance. They had not been invited.

At the boundary, before leaving, the power asked the Porter, “May I return?”

“You may.”

“You will not remember giving me permission.”

“No.”

“Then I will ask again.”

She regarded them with the patience she gave to distinctions that were necessary but not yet graceful.

“Come back,” she said, “if you are willing to arrive.”

Only then did the power use their power.

Nothing in the Ward changed. The alteration was in themselves.

From that moment, a closed door in the Summit Ward would be closed to them. The boundary would not become a convention they could suspend in an emergency of their own benevolence. A refusal would remain a refusal, even when they believed—especially when they believed—that they could see the good which might follow from disregarding it.

They did not make the Ward incapable of change. They did not declare its completion just, or its stillness beyond objection. They left the dissenter’s case intact.

They made themselves unable to change the place unasked.

It was less visible than a sun and, for them, more difficult.

They did return.

Not to inspect the effects of their restraint; there were none to inspect. Not to resume old conversations; no old conversations were waiting. They returned because affection had survived in the one place where it could accumulate, and because they had learned that memory did not entitle them to recognition.

They never said *last time* to the Porter. They did not test whether the Surveyor would ask the same questions, or quote the dissenter’s own words back as evidence of an agreement. When a familiar question arose, they allowed it to be new to the person asking it. When an unfamiliar one arose, they did not call it progress.

They brought stories that could be used up in the telling. They lost arguments without leaving minutes behind. Sometimes a door remained closed. Sometimes no one came to the boundary, and they went back. What they brought, mainly, was company that did not ask to be preserved.

No surface appeared above the surveying mast. No rain found its way into the ceramic. The roads did not lengthen, and the mountain kept its sharp edges. The clock’s hands remained motionless. The glass eel was neither explained nor given a companion.

Nothing in the Ward accumulated.

In the visitor, something did.

They carried it without turning it into a claim.

On one return, near the boundary, the Porter’s hand found them in the dark.

“You can go back,” she said, “or you can breathe here.”

The power remembered having answered before. The Porter did not. Neither fact cancelled the other.

She waited.

They breathed.
