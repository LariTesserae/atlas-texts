## The Listener

The power did not arrive above the High Choir.

An arrival would have cast a shadow, and there a shadow is already an event. Button insects would have dropped into the fiber. Grazers would have turned beneath the leaf-scales. Water-gatherers would have looked up from the rims. So the power came, if coming is the word, without shadow, weight, or voice.

Benevolence had wanted to begin with weather. Wisdom made them begin with waiting.

They listened through choruses and through the longer intervals between them. They listened until they could distinguish a crown weakened by disease from one merely altered by the loss of a brood; until the silence running ahead of a seam-walker no longer resembled the silence of drought; until a root ridge closing over an abandoned shelter did not seem, from their greater height, like nothing happening.

They had come prepared to hear a message. For a while that expectation kept them from hearing the lives.

They also looked for the being in the High Choir: some central presence in the deepest trunk, perhaps, or an intelligence distributed through sap and root, for whom the crowns were organs and the choruses were thoughts. They found no place where their question ended cleanly. Growth answered growth. Neighboring tissues sustained and poisoned one another. The same wound might be closed by animals in one basin and opened by roots in another.

The power did not settle the matter by declaring the plateau one being, or many, merely so that they would know how to address it. A gantry could refuse by walking away. A camp could refuse by breaking off trade. A root could refuse by growing around an intrusion. They learned not to demand words before recognizing an answer.

Their first imagined mercy was rain: regular, gentle, reliable rain, enough to fill the basins and end the long uncertainties of the water-routes.

Then they saw the tough-skinned larvae of the dry galleries, the animals whose lives began only after long waiting, the cistern communities already living at the margins of suffocation. They saw how the camps’ knowledge was carried in movements between unreliable sources, and how a permanent spring might become a reason to remain, to defend, to cut, to own. Rain was not refused because rain was bad. It was refused because the power did not yet understand what their rain would be.

They imagined other mercies. No seam-walker. No disease carried in the damp folds of browsers. No spark able to vanish into the galleries. No person able to rob another camp’s water. No crown permitted to die while its voice was still remembered.

Each mercy began by making the world easier to love from a distance.

It would have been easy, after setting these designs aside, to make the opposite mistake: to call the High Choir perfect, and every hurt within it necessary. The power did not. A drowned brood was not consoled by the antiquity of drought. A traveler’s thirst did not become music because the power could hear the whole plateau at once. The seam-walker’s hunger was real, but so was the terror of the animals waiting at the water. Wisdom did not make those things cancel one another.

So the power began, where benevolence becomes practical, with what could be asked, answered, or refused.

Their help was small enough to be overlooked.

A traveler crossing a district whose crown-voices had changed stopped walking one breath before entering a traveling silence. She noticed the stillness in the pelt. She saw the gantries lift their heads. She left the water alone, though her gourd was empty, and afterward credited experience. Perhaps she was right.

A cord already hollowed by insects gave its hard, narrow warning while the platform was still unloaded. A spark beneath a covered hearth reached one fiber that would not carry it farther. At a dry seep, a gourd left uncovered before dawn gathered a single unexpected swallow—not enough to found a camp, not enough to make a route dependable, but enough for the person holding it to reconsider whether she must take the next dangerous water.

The power did not answer every request. They did not invent a ledger proving that the unanswered ones were just. They were a power, not an escape from every contradiction. Sometimes they could offer only a little more time in which to choose. Sometimes they could offer nothing but attention, and they did not praise attention as though it had filled a gourd.

They took no tribute and demanded no belief. Help, they had decided, must not become another cistern with something waiting at the rim.

When people began to tell stories about them, the stories disagreed. In one, the power was an old walker whose footprints held clean water until dawn. In another, they were a note audible only in cordage about to fail. Some camps maintained that nothing had happened at all, and that travelers had simply become more willing to admit when they were lost. The power made no attempt to correct any of these accounts.

To the possible old organism of the plateau, they offered one thing: if there was a wound beyond its own power to answer, they would help, if help was wanted.

Then they waited.

Platforms appeared, thickened into their living supports, and vanished. Tonal routes deepened. A crown that had been silent for generations flowered weakly and was avoided anyway. Roots closed over old injuries and opened new passages around them.

No answer came that the power could honestly distinguish from their own wish to be useful.

So they did not call their longing consent. They did not heal the plateau into agreement with their idea of health.

Their only lasting promise concerned a greater loss. If some wound ever threatened not one crown, not one lineage, not the present arrangement of water and voice, but the whole living capacity of the Choir to respond—if no healing growth, migration, brood, or patient alteration could any longer answer it—then they would return.

They did not promise to restore the plateau exactly. Exact restoration might have been for their comfort rather than its good. They promised only to help preserve its ability to continue differently: to make another map, another weather, another arrangement of silence and sound.

Until such a day, their attention would not be a government.

The High Choir remained dangerous and abundantly alive. People still fouled water through carelessness and sometimes took more than they needed. Burrowers still opened what sealers had closed. Crowns sickened. The old walkers carried both refuge and disease in their coats. The power had not made the inhabitants good. They had not made themselves innocent of having preferences. They remained willing to discover that they had been wrong.

One evening, long after the camps had ceased to agree about when the visitation had occurred, a chorus crossed the plateau.

It came first through the soles of the feet. The crowns answered beyond sight. Pollen loosened, seed-valves sprang open, and condensed water fell glittering through branches beneath a cloudless sky. Wings filled the spaces between the trunks. Hunters opened their brief corridors through the sound, and the separate pitches closed behind them.

The power listened.

They heard no worship in it, no gratitude, no request, no farewell. It was not about them. This pleased them more than any answer they had imagined at the beginning.

Afterward, distance returned. The gantries came to the seepages. People lowered their gourds while watching the rims. The visible congregation drank, scattered, and was gradually folded back into galleries, fur, seed, and living wood.

The crowns became separate objects in the heat once more.

The power did not mistake that for emptiness.
