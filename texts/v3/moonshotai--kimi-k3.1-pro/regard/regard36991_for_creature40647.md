The power would begin by listening, which would look, for a long time, like doing nothing.

It would not bring rain. It would not lay a cooling hand upon the roof, clear the roots from the conduits, return the shutters to their first true hang, or fill the central vacancy with gardens. It would not see dark, oily leaves and imagine thirst. It would not see empty streets and imagine loneliness.

Those kindnesses would be answers to questions the Thrum had not asked.

So the power would remain through one entire exhalation. It would hear the first tone beneath the paving and the other flues joining it, each altering the immensity of the note. It would feel the draft draw sand across the floor. It would watch the curtains stream toward the walls and the shutters rise without hands. When the capsules opened, it would let the seeds pass. It would catch none, plant none, and call none wasted.

Then it would stay longer.

One exhalation would teach it the weather, but not the life. It would have to remain long enough to see a fan change its angle, a cane thicken without growing taller, a root loosen a stone while binding the wall around it. It would have to notice a conduit’s voice lowering over centuries and the seed valves near it changing in answer. Only then would it begin to understand that repair might be indistinguishable from injury.

To restore an original note could leave a generation of capsules shut. To cool the floor could diminish the difference on which the oldest roots fed. To clear the center could close the road between colonies. Even to preserve everything exactly as it was would be a violation: the Thrum lived partly by abrasion, deposition, collapse, and slow adjustment. Its balance was not stillness, and its present form was not a promise.

The power had arrived, perhaps, with the thought of a place and the being who lived in it. That distinction would become less certain the longer it attended. Root and conduit, conduit and seed, seed and vacancy: each entered the conditions of the others. The power need not decide whether the Thrum was one being, many beings, or something for which those categories were clumsy. Care did not have to wait upon a census.

Nor would it find a simple verdict of suffering or contentment. There was damage here, and great age, and failure without number. Most seeds accomplished nothing. Tissue split; colonies narrowed; flues changed beyond the responses of the growth beside them. But none of this, by itself, told the power that the Thrum wished to be otherwise. Persistence was not necessarily consent, and silence was not necessarily peace. Still, the power would not invent a misery merely because it knew a magnificent cure.

For the present, then, it would make no change within the chamber.

It would leave no spring, no sentinel animal, no eternal lamp, no seed of its own devising. It would not even mend the next block that was going to fall. It would take no specimen and leave no shrine. Nothing in the Thrum would be required to accommodate a miracle.

If the power kept watch afterward, its watch would be narrow. It would not undertake to keep every wall standing or every flue at its present pitch. It would not make the Thrum immortal and call that mercy. But if appetite, carelessness, or some outsider’s well-meant restoration threatened to end the place outright, the power might act—with the smallest sufficient gesture, and after listening again. It would not turn one wise refusal into an eternal commandment. There might come a time when doing nothing would be only another way of failing to hear.

Then, for now, it would withdraw.

Above, the salt pan would remain white at noon. Dust would rise from the warm seam in its windless thread. Below, the pewter light would touch the chalk-pale ledges and leave the doorways black. A root would divide a mineral grain. A fan would turn by no angle anyone could have measured. Dead lace would continue bearing the weight of living fronds.

At the next exhalation, the town would become loud again. Pollen and pale seeds would enter the hollow. One seed would cross nearly the whole settlement and fall where nothing could grow. The power would not save it in order to demonstrate love.

Nothing below would know that it had been considered, or spared, or understood even as far as it had been. There would be no gratitude, and none would be required.

The power’s gift, for the present, would be the absence of its handiwork—and an accurate memory of the roar: not a lament, not a summons, not music made for a listener, but the sound of a place still becoming itself.
