## The Untied Cord

The power came with its hand already raised.

It had seen the three stone blades in their basket and the cord lying halfway through its work. To finish one lashing would take less than a thought: a pull, a tuck, a small neatness. It seemed the sort of kindness beyond objection.

Then the spider’s web trembled.

One of its anchors was the unfinished handle. The power considered what happened in the brick when a thing received its final gesture. It saw the neat handle departing into exterior clay, the open fan of silk left with nothing to hold it, the hollow diminished by almost nothing—and diminished all the same.

It lowered its hand.

That was not yet wisdom. It might only have been hesitation. So the power stayed, and listened.

It listened through ceiling rain and upward rain, through the mallet’s heavy residence in the air and the later arrival of its dimple. It heard the brief, unsafe silences. It watched roots enter joints not to ruin them but to keep them capable of weeping. It saw the vine use its own earlier life as a ladder, and the fern retain, in its glossy adult frond, the pressure of a neighbor that had moved away.

Little by little, the power abandoned its first gifts.

It would not give the mountain bedrock. It would not teach the water a single down. It would not clear a path through the leaves, open the furled shadows by force, or make the spider’s silk unbreakable. It would not even preserve everything exactly as it found it. A garden kept forever in one condition would have been only a more courteous kind of brick.

But neither did it decide that, because completion was dangerous, everything unfinished must therefore be good.

Hunger also remains open. So does fear. The midge’s struggle did not become beautiful merely because the chamber was beautiful, nor the spider’s hunger sacred because its web sang so delicately under the rain. The power refused to call pain necessary simply because it could not yet see how to relieve it without causing some greater replacement.

Gradually, its restraint became something other than refusal.

It would touch only where a touch could return possibility to a creature, rather than substitute its own purposes for the creature’s. It would not mistake silence for permission. It would not invent a voice for the garden and then piously obey the voice it had invented.

Once, it touched.

A silence came. The basin lifted. Roots loosened in their channels, and the far wall began to clear toward that other, differently crowded garden. The spider had already gathered what silk it could against the tool handle, but the small bundle rose now in a thread of water that had forgotten weight. The spider reached after it. Between its legs and the bundle was all the distance there was.

The power did not stop the silence. It did not put back the web, or hold the gutter and handle at their former angles.

For one breath, it let one drop remember down.

The bundle sank with it. The spider caught the silk against the handle and drew it close. Then the drop, released, resumed the general disagreement.

When the work returned, nothing stood quite where it had stood before. The spider still had to begin again. The power did not choose its anchor points or show it a better design. It had saved no structure. It had only kept a small practice from losing the material of its next beginning.

For the garden itself, the power made no promise of endlessness. It did not know that endlessness was wanted, and would not call its own attachment a request made by the garden.

It promised something narrower.

No ending would be arranged for the place merely because some other being could not bear to see it continue as it was.

Ordinary surfaces could still receive their last gestures and depart as brick. Terraces could vanish. Walls could swell, collapse, and reveal newer clay. The mountain could accumulate beneath the summit until every former depth had become only a recent one. Even the garden’s own ending, if it ever truly came, would not automatically be resisted.

But if a hand approached to fire the brick for safekeeping, to seal the vent against danger, to clear the leaves for the sake of room, or to smooth the final wall because the work had gone on long enough, that hand would find the clay less obedient than expected.

Not thunder. Not punishment. Only wetness beneath the thumb. A root crossing the intended seam. A drop arriving from the wrong direction. A small resistance in which there remained time to listen.

The power’s guardianship would consist largely of preventing other guardians from improving the place out of existence.

After that, no sign of it remained.

No new handprint appeared. No leaf opened early to fit its shadow. There was no fourth midge, no second snail, no addition to the census except, perhaps, the uncountable fact of one drop that had briefly remembered down.

Warm rain leapt toward the ceiling. The vine tightened around an older stem. The snail approached the point where one more grazing stroke would have joined its trails into a clean, closed circuit, and after a while it turned aside.

In the basket, the three handles remained half-lashed. The loose cord stirred faintly when the spider plucked water from its new web.

The power had almost tied it.

Its blessing was the almost: the space left for the next gesture to be someone else’s.
