The first thing the power did was refrain.

From above the weather, the High Basin looked like a catastrophe that had been going on for a very long time: a sea leaking through its walls, a roof of broken ice, and beneath it the remains of immense labor worn almost back into the floor. The power could have closed the sills. It could have warmed the brine, raised the buried roofs, returned the arches to their crowns. It could have filled the pewter water with light.

Instead, it entered without a body. Its attention did not stir a grain.

Its care came to rest on one being in particular: an old pallid predator lying along the crest of a swell. At first the power had mistaken the animal for a bank of sediment. Silt covered its back. Minute grazers crossed it, and masons had begun a fragile crust near one flank.

The predator’s stillness had seemed, at first, like desolation. The power wanted to feed it, to move it into richer water, to relieve it of the burden of waiting.

It stayed instead.

A distant collapse came first as pressure, then as a whiteness in the water. The old animal lifted its head before the sediment arrived. Much later, when food gathered along a current boundary, it moved. The silt on its back slipped away in broad, soft veils. The little floor it had carried went into suspension: masons, grazers, films, resting cells, all dispersed together. Some would settle elsewhere. Some would be eaten. Some were already crushed.

The predator fed and became still again. New silt began to gather.

The power followed pale seams into the pores of the floor. It watched grazers open old surfaces and masons thicken in their tracks. It saw microbial growth cloud the water above fresh fractures while sealed material beside it remained nearly sterile. It saw sieves gather where currents supplied food, and the gathering vanish when the water changed.

Gradually, its first gifts began to look like executions.

A floor preserved from every wound would eventually close itself against the chemistry that fed the microorganisms. An end to grazing would mean fewer exposed pores. An end to collapses would also mean an end to the sudden abundance that woke buried scavengers and fed life far downstream. To restore the lost towers would be to honor a plan that had never existed, at the expense of the lives actually present. The masons were not failed architects. Nothing in the Basin was waiting to be forgiven for having become what it was.

But the power did not make the opposite mistake.

It did not call every death necessary. A torn grazer was not repaid by the feast its body gave to scavengers. Young animals smothered beneath a fall did not become acceptable because other young might later drift in from elsewhere. The power refused to make the whole so beautiful in its understanding that particular lives disappeared from its care.

Its tenderness did not diminish as its knowledge grew. It still wanted to feed the old predator. It still wanted to turn grazers aside from approaching shear and lift the sediment from buried things. Wisdom did not cure it of these wishes. It taught the power to distinguish help from its own need to see suffering relieved.

There was no builder to consult, no single mind in which the Basin could give consent. The power did not mistake that silence for permission.

So it changed nothing within the rim.

It completed no arch. It preserved no surface as sacred. It made no animal immortal as an exception to the lives around it. It did not even make the Basin everlasting; it did not know that everlastingness would be a mercy.

Beyond the rim, however, it acted.

It closed the path left by its own discovery, so that its attention could not become a road for another will. And it made one promise. If some catastrophe from outside ever threatened not this district, this generation, or this age, but the ending of every pore, every resting stage, and every possibility of continuation, it would return. Against an outside hand coming to drain the sea, carry away its lives, or preserve it forever in deathless perfection, it would set its strength.

Even then, it would do the smallest thing that left a future open.

It would not return to improve the survivors.

Before going, the power considered revelation. It could have shown one mason the arch toward which countless local accommodations might accidentally tend. It could have shown the old predator the whole sea: the rim, the freezing falls, the districts lying beyond every pressure and dissolved trace its body could perceive.

It did neither. The mason’s life was not a smaller, defective version of an architect’s. The predator was not an emblem of patience. Neither needed to become intelligible to the power in order to be worthy of its care.

Then it went.

No light remained to mark its visit. No law began ticking beneath the ice. At the rim, living things continued to go over with the water. On the floor, a grazing spiral deepened. Somewhere a current began to fold, and far away a pallid length lifted its head.

In a pore behind a fresh fracture, a mason turned until its body fitted the cavity. It laid one pale particle behind itself.

The particle remained.

The power had not saved the Basin from dissolution. It had spared it a plan.
