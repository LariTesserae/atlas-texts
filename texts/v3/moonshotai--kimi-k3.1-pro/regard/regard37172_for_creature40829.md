### What the Power Did

The power did not enter the basin.

Its first impulse was to give the place a next moment: to let one suspended seed fall, one hinged lid lift, one warm lensback adjust a foot beneath its leaf. The movements would be so small. No grove need vanish; no tree need be struck down. Yet beneath that single footfall, almost anything might begin—afternoon, hunger, memory, the long mercy of change.

It imagined the lensback’s first step, and the gift seemed nearly irresistible.

Then the power saw what the gift would require.

There was no imprisoned movement within the animal, no unfinished gesture waiting for release. To give it a next action would not be to free the life already there. It would be to make that life preliminary: to decide that all its warmth, receptivity, and exact relations had been a deprivation, and then to take credit for relieving the deprivation the power had itself imagined.

The same was true of the grove. A future could be given to it, but only by turning its completed condition into a first condition. Its inhabitants could become ancestors, its leaves a canopy that would one day be remembered, its precise compatibility a problem to be solved by growth. Nothing would necessarily suffer. That was not sufficient reason. Benevolence did not become innocent merely because it could picture a beautiful result.

So the power looked for harm.

It did not make a religion of stillness. Elsewhere it had found still bodies that were paralyzed, silences maintained by fear, closed things whose closedness was a wound. Had the lensback been weary, had the grove been laboring to sustain itself, had some unspoken mind been waiting within the exhaustive coherence for an answer, the power would have acted. Wisdom was not reverence for whatever happened to exist.

But there was no duration in which the lensback could tire of its leaf. The white chambers were not resisting an inward pressure. The grove was not holding its breath. There was no one for whom the noon had lasted too long.

Nor could the power safely ask. A question would not uncover a hidden respondent; it would require one. To give the grove enough sequential mind to consider alternatives, and then to call its answer consent, would be to intervene first and seek permission afterward. Silence would signify neither refusal nor assent. The power would not invent a prisoner in order to enjoy setting it free.

It considered lesser interventions: preserving the basin, blessing it, appointing a guardian, making its permanence secure. But permanence was not something the basin lacked. The power saw no reason to make itself necessary merely so that its care could take a visible form.

At last it attended without proposing anything.

It did not count the lensbacks. It did not decide whether the copper-crowned tree had two trunks or whether the map was wrong. It did not open a white chamber to settle the status of the pale spiral within. Its curiosity did not create a right to make an event. It allowed the place to remain more exact than the distinctions brought to it.

Then it loved the basin, if love can name a regard that seeks neither response nor continuation.

The love did not warm the air. No charge brightened in the root-lace. Nothing native received it, and the power did not wait to be received. It did not mistake this restraint for a great sacrifice; that would have made the basin a stage for its virtue. It simply ceased to suppose that goodness must always have an object it could improve.

Afterward, it went where it could be useful.

There were worlds in which rain was mercy because roots were actually failing, and night was mercy because animals could become exhausted. There were seeds that needed to fall, doors whose closedness meant someone had been shut inside, and minds for whom a next moment was the difference between captivity and hope. There the power opened, moved, mended, interrupted, and ended. It did not make the basin an argument against time. Neither did it make time an argument against the basin.

But something in its benevolence had become more exact. It was slower to confuse silence with refusal, closedness with immaturity, and an absence of response with an absence of life. It was also slower to call every unaltered thing sacred. It learned neither to intervene nor to refrain by habit.

In the Basin of First Noon, nothing marked its attention. The light kept its ordinary warmth. A leaf continued to lay its shadow across another grove. The white chambers remained closed, not patiently. The lensback’s underside remained warm against its host, and its dark eyes promised no movement.

The power had done nothing to the place or the being.

Its mercy, there, was to be unnecessary.
