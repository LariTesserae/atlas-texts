### What the Power Did

It did not arrive as light. Even light would have altered something.

Nor did it hush the basin in order to listen. For a long time its attention had no shadow, temperature, or weight, and the Choir remained louder than its witness.

A quick mercy would have misunderstood almost everything. It might have pitied the swimmer for being alone, though nothing in the animal required company. It might have mistaken a clear pool for an empty one, or a bronze stain for abundance. It might have interpreted the swimmer’s long stillness as despair. It might have heard the failure of a note as the silencing of a voice, and tried to preserve the music by preventing the stone from changing.

So, at first, it did nothing.

This was not because it considered everything that happened good. It was because it did not yet know what help would mean.

It remained long enough to see a waterline abandon a film and another community occupy the mineral residue. It saw a swimmer shrink until its body was little more than a dark line suspended in water, then thicken again when the chemistry of the pool changed. It learned that a pure note could come from an almost sterile tube, and that a clogged, silent cavity could contain more life than a sounding one. Slowly it understood that care and preservation were not necessarily the same thing.

Then it rejected two gifts.

The first was abundance. It could have brought soil, rooted green things along the margins, filled the pools with visible animals, and made the basin generous to hunger. But that would not have been the Slow Choir becoming more fully alive. It would have been another world laid over this one, with the present inhabitants surviving, if they survived at all, in the margins of someone else’s idea of mercy.

The second gift was permanence. It could have kept every vent open, every pool at its present level, every column fixed in the register it had when the power first heard it. But the basin had not become complete merely because it had been noticed. To hold it forever at that moment would have been to preserve an admiration, not a place.

It refused both the garden and the museum.

Yet it did not make a religion of noninterference. A place can be loved so abstractly that nothing living in it is ever permitted to need help.

In one eastern pool, beyond a region of short, clogged columns, the power had found a swimmer. The animal had settled against a bronze waterline and was feeding without visibly scraping the stone. There was nothing exemplary about it. It was not the basin’s spirit, not the last witness of its builders, not a small body appointed to carry the meaning of the whole. It was simply one of the rare visible lives for which this pool was, for a time, sufficient.

Eventually a plate shifted at the bottom of the pool.

The movement was too slight to change the outflow or trouble the nearest column. It trapped one clear fold of the swimmer against bare glass. The animal was not crushed. It could contract, but not enough to free itself, and it could no longer reach the film on which it had been feeding. Left there, it would slowly diminish. Its gelatin would lose its folds; its dark line would disperse. The films would receive their brief addition of organic matter, as they someday would in any case.

The power considered the accident with the same patience it had given the basin. No seep depended on the plate’s weight. No other community required its new position. Nothing in the deep common note would be altered by moving it. Nothing the power could discover needed the swimmer to end beneath that particular stone.

So it lifted the plate.

Only a little. It did not pull the animal free, repair it, strengthen it, or make it incapable of being harmed again. The swimmer’s withdrawal was so slow that the power held the stone through the whole almost imperceptible sequence of contractions. Then it let the plate settle on a bare surface nearby.

That was the miracle.

No seam opened. No new water entered the pool. No mark appeared on the plate. The swimmer did not look upward or become capable of gratitude. After a time it returned to the waterline and fed.

The power did not conclude from this that further intervention must therefore be better. It did not make the swimmer immortal. It did not search out every dormant cell and prescribe for it a future of uninterrupted growth. It did not shield every pool from drying or every animal from every accident. The absence of a reason for this one death did not supply a reason to abolish death itself.

But neither did it call its small act interference with a wisdom inherent in the terrain. The basin’s indifference was not a moral instruction.

Toward the Choir as a whole, the power remained almost equally restrained. It would let columns fall and stands go mute. It would let life assist in the narrowing of some passages and the keeping open of others. It would not prevent the deep chord from separating into local sounds, or demand that silence always signify loss.

If some will from outside had come to drain the basin for brief use, crush it for its minerals, or seal it unchanged behind the glass of eternal admiration, the power would have resisted. Not because the place was never to change, but because use and erasure are not the same as change.

Against time itself, it made no war.

When it withdrew, there was nothing to show where it had been. The old constructions retained their faint thresholds. The stair still descended directly into water. No additional straight mark explained the others. No new note entered the Choir.

In time the swimmer would die. In time its pool might seal. Neither event would prove that the power’s care had failed. Care had not been a bargain against the future.

Water continued down the hidden interiors of the columns. Somewhere an impact returned after too long a delay, divided among places no listener could have connected. The swimmer rested in its pool, not chosen, not safe forever, not alone in any sense it could understand.

Nothing had been made to signify love.

Something small had been spared by it, and everything else had been left free to become otherwise.
