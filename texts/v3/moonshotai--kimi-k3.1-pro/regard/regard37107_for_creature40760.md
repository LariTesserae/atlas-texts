### A Second Possibility

The power arrived in a lull, and nearly misunderstood everything.

Weak light lay on the wet gravel bars. Water dropped separately from the alder leaves. Somewhere a bird called and, after a pause, was answered. It would have been easy to believe that this was Sorn as it wished to be, and the storm an affliction laid over it.

The power waited.

The front returned. Alders bent until their branches struck water. Silt lifted from the fans. Somewhere upstream, a bank went into the river with no sound distinguishable from the rest of the roar. The power listened until it could hear, within that roar, cobbles being sorted, roots taking strain, ice loosening from reeds, a spruce failing fibre by fibre.

Nothing about this made the storm good. It only made stillness an ambiguous gift.

To fix the channels would have been to choose one Sorn and forbid all the others. To warm the basin, dry its moss, and give its animals dependable banks would have saved some lives by making the lives that belonged there impossible. A drowned nest was not made good because a willow rooted downstream; neither did the drowned nest prove that the flood should never have come.

The seven anchors offered a better lesson. They held ground without asking it to become eternal. They released what could no longer be protected without sending the harm elsewhere. Their wisdom was mostly a matter of timing.

So the power did not calm Sorn.

But it had not been drawn there by the watershed alone.

On the leeward side of a spruce, a small bird was losing its grip. Rain had worked through the outer feathers. Its feet had been closed around the same twig for hours, and the muscles holding them closed were beginning to fail. The bird knew nothing about young land, disturbance, or succession. It knew the branch and the cold.

There were hundreds of other birds in the spruce. That fact was of no use to this one.

The basin could replace the bird. The bird could not replace itself.

When its feet opened, the power caught it in a warmth no larger than cupped hands. It did not stop the wind elsewhere. It did not make the spruce immortal. It dried the feathers, restored a little strength, and waited.

In the next lull, contact calls passed between the hummocks. The bird struggled toward them before its wings were entirely ready. The power could have carried it to some mild country where no branch ever moved violently, but it did not. The bird was not choosing the storm. It was choosing the other birds.

That gave the power its rule: where possible, add a possibility; do not confiscate a world.

It remained, and began to practise a mercy with a short reach.

A second exit from a den stayed open through one more night. A floating sedge mat, with a nest woven into its crown, caught against willow roots instead of rolling under. A bank was not saved, but its failure came after a group of browsers had passed beneath it. Once, a human hand searching blindly along a shelter wall found the raised lip of the new entrance.

These were not rewards. Nothing about the character of a mouse, a bird, or a field worker earned them. Nor were they promises. Sorn did not become safe, and no route became trustworthy because something kind was watching. The power had no wish to be attended to instead of the uphill side of a valley.

Sometimes there was no clean mercy available. Saving one bank would send water into another refuge; feeding one hunger would end another life. In such cases the power did not invent innocence by calling the outcome natural. It did not require the dying to be consoled by their future usefulness to moss. It eased what suffering could be eased without merely moving it, and stayed with what it could not save.

Nor was remembrance alone sufficient. A perfect archive would have been another way of keeping Sorn while losing its inhabitants. The recovery of a population did not answer for one small body in a flooded nest. Next summer’s char did not answer for the fish buried in gravel now.

So, when a life ended, Sorn was allowed to take back what Sorn needed: feather, fur, bone, warmth, the materials of other beginnings. But whatever in that being could go onward as itself, rather than only as food, offspring, sediment, or recollection, the power allowed to go onward.

Not as a specimen. Not as a lesson. Not as evidence that suffering had been necessary.

Simply onward.

For Sorn itself, it reserved a different protection. It would never preserve the basin in its present form. It would preserve the basin’s ability to exceed that form. No future owner, engineer, designer, or god—including itself—would be permitted to declare one arrangement of channels the land’s completed meaning. Against the ordinary loss of bars, ponds, trees, and paths, it would usually do nothing. Against an injury final enough to end the capacity for beginning again, its restraint would end.

No survey recorded any of this. There were still three shelters, seven anchors, and a broken footbridge. Maps continued to become historical on the way back from the field. The cold found seams. Some calls received no answer. The power did not rename any of this paradise.

In another lull, a bird called from the spruce. A second answered from across open water, and then a third, so far away that its voice was almost part of the river.

The power did not add its own voice. It was not their flock, and it would not make itself necessary merely to be loved.

Then the next front arrived. The alders bent together, and the separate sounds closed into one enormous pressure.

The power let it come.

It had learned what to leave alone—and that leaving alone was not the same as abandonment.
