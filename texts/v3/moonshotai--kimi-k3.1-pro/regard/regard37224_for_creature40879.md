### What the Power Did

It would not begin with evening.

Evening would have been easy. A single act, and the white heights could soften into blue; the wet plaster could take on gold; shadows could lengthen across the courts and give every pavilion, every bird, every person the brief dignity of an approaching night. A traveler might have wept with relief.

But the power would be wise enough to distrust the traveler’s relief.

To give the basin time would not be to give it sunsets alone. It would be to make things accumulate. Fruit could fall and darken. The sharp copings could round beneath the water. Bodies could become trails of their own injuries. Affection could acquire the burden of duration, and absence could become loss. Every kindness would remain—but so would every harm. Perhaps such a world would be better. It would not be a gift to give by surprise.

Nor would the power make the opposite mistake and worship its own restraint. A perfect court does not cure a headache. A person may be exactly where they belong and still want company, coolness, distraction, or help. Beauty is not consent, and peace is not proof that nothing is needed.

If someone asked for elsewhere, the power would listen. If someone asked for an ending, it would not answer with a lecture about the excellence of things that endure. Wisdom is not merely loyalty to what already exists.

So the power came into the basin as a traveler: warm, thirsty, without radiance, and without the hooked thumbprint. It had no right to that sign.

It spoke to people separately. No one was permitted to answer for the basin.

For one person, it cooled a cloth pressed to a aching head. It did not also abolish heat, headaches, or the body that could suffer them. For another, who wished to know about rain, it made the sound of rain inside two cupped hands: first a few drops, then a hard, delighted roaring. When the listener said, “Enough,” it stopped at once.

One person asked whether the great knot beneath the reed island was alive. The power considered the question, the island, and the person’s expression.

“Would the answer help you?” it asked.

“No,” the person admitted. “But it would help me win.”

The power declined.

Another person wanted nothing from it and said so. It gave nothing, and did not treat this as a failure of benevolence.

At the long water stair, the woman sat with her feet in the lowest pool. Her scraper moved patiently through the white clay.

The power stopped some distance away.

“There is water,” she said, “if you can cup it.”

It drank. The water was warm.

“May I sit?”

“If you sit there. Don’t watch me.”

It sat where she indicated, facing the vacant court. For a while there was only the small noise of its damp cloth, its breath, and her feet shifting in the pool. The scraper itself made almost no sound.

“Is it true,” she asked eventually, “that cold water hurts the teeth?”

“Sometimes.”

She laughed.

“I would dislike that,” she said.

The power did not tell her what else she would dislike.

Her work continued. A little darkness appeared in the groove.

“Are you one of the makers?” she asked.

“No.”

“Did you make the stair?”

“No.”

“Can you make the water run down?”

“Yes.”

Her scraper paused.

“Would it still be this stair?”

“I don’t know,” said the power.

She accepted this answer with a small movement of her shoulders.

“Then don’t.”

“No.”

“Can you make it dark?”

“Yes.”

“Everywhere?”

“Yes.”

She considered the white court, or seemed to. The power kept its face turned toward it.

“That would be a great deal to do,” she said, “because you were visiting.”

“Yes.”

She bent again to the cut.

The power waited. It did not fill the waiting with offerings. It did not show her a garden growing wild, a child becoming an adult, a beloved face changing with age, or any of the other arguments that might have been made for time. It did not show her people suffering in eternal noon, either. She was not a court before which its preferred world needed to be defended.

At length she said, “You may hand me the bowl.”

It handed her the bowl.

She drank, held it out, and then changed her mind.

“Put it in the shade.”

It did.

“Farther.”

It moved the bowl again.

“There.”

That was all she asked. The power did not search her voice for a larger request hidden beneath it.

After a while she said, “You can go now.”

It went.

It left no sunset behind it, no commandment cut into clay, no blessing on her work. It did not make her its priestess, its evidence, or the beginning of a new age. It left no door to elsewhere. She had not asked for a door.

Behind it, the causeway was clean.

The power carried away a memory: warm water, an unscarred thumb, the precise limits of permitted company, and a bowl moved twice. Not because she required remembrance in order to matter. Not because memory could preserve her; she did not need preserving. But the encounter could now become part of the power’s own history, and it was willing to be the thing changed.

At the stair, the groove darkened. A little dampness gathered at its lower end, hesitated, and began to climb.

The light had not moved.

The power had.
