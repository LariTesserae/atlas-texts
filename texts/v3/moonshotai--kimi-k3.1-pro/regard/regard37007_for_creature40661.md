### No Mark in the Stone

The power did not enter.

An opening made for its passage would have changed the pressure. A hand laid against the wall might have cooled one crystal face and killed the film upon it. Light would have been a gift to the visitor, not to the inhabitants. So its first act of care was to let the mountain remain closed.

At first it perceived the chamber as a single fierce event: heat, roar, precipitation, crowding. Then it slowed its attention until the event separated into lives. A grazer released its feet one at a time while the crystal beneath it ticked with stress. In the dust below, a larva fed along the sheltered side of a grain. Three pores away, a hunter waited beside a trail whose feeding vibrations were becoming less frequent.

The power had looked for the being who lived in the chamber. It found no one for whom all the rest were scenery.

It also perceived what no inhabitant could: the old interstices sealing, the new ones opening, the tenuous movement of films and larvae between them. It saw that the chamber was not a vessel containing a history. It was one brief arrangement through which a history happened to be passing.

Many possible gifts occurred to the power, and most of them were ways of making the place easier for the giver to love.

Water would have been an invasion. Coolness would have silenced the chemistry. Room enough to move freely would have altered the pressure and the crowded gradations on which everything fed. Silence would have removed nothing the animals needed, perhaps, but the conditions required to produce that silence would have removed nearly everything else.

Even preservation would have been a choice of one moment against all the others. The power could have kept the chamber exactly as it was. It could have made a permanent wonder of it, complete and unchanging, and thereby mistaken a living passage for a precious object.

Nor did the power call every death good because it was natural. It did not use the word *balance* to spare itself perception. A body crushed while soft after molting was not made painless by the scavengers that would later use it. A hunter’s hunger was not answered by the elegance of the relations that had produced it. The power saw these things without demanding that they become either a tragedy it must abolish or a harmony it could admire from a distance.

But to prevent every loss would not merely have cleansed this world of suffering. It would have placed every trail, every meal, every unsuccessful molt and successful escape under an invisible administration. The animals would have continued to move, but behind every movement would have been the power’s permission. Its benevolence would have become the hidden condition of their existence.

None of them had asked for that. The power did not invent a request on their behalf.

It did not even announce itself.

It could have sent a vibration through the crystal ribs, immaculate and gentle, unlike any random ticking of growth. But the inhabitants had no category for a benevolent power. Such a signal could reach them only as something within their world: a mate, a rival, another shell, a possible danger. Love would have arrived as false information about what was near.

So the power did not drum back.

This restraint was not a vow of helplessness. Had its own presence fractured the mountain, it would have repaired the injury. Had it found the last living films cut off from every remaining gradient by one accidental seal, it might have opened a passage no wider than a microbe’s need. There could be mercies smaller than redesign. But the power would not manufacture an emergency merely so that its goodness could become visible.

Here, no such act was required.

It remained without touching while the pressure-note changed. It watched one rich face become poor and another, formerly useless, begin to carry film. It saw grazers extend their trails, hunters go hungry beside abandoned routes, scavengers gather briefly where movement had failed. When the old chamber at last narrowed toward closure, the power did not hold it open. By then, life was already elsewhere—not saved intact, not transported as a community, but resumed in fragments, with most of those fragments lost.

The power took no specimen. It left no marker that might draw a less patient visitor. It preserved no image of the chamber and called that the whole. If it remembered anything, it remembered the handoffs: microbes in the seam of a shell, eggs sealed in dust, a larva entering a passage that would never admit the body it might later become.

When its attention finally withdrew, no crystal bore a sign. No animal had been chosen to represent the rest. The mountain had not been promised eternity.

In a younger interstice, one shell rocked against another. The signal traveled through the crystal on which they stood, around a fracture, and into the legs of an animal beyond direct reach. After an interval, an answer returned.

The power had not caused it. It had not answered for them.

Its gift was not safety, knowledge, or permanence. It was that nothing there had been made to need the giver.

The chamber remained unfinished. The power had not abandoned it; it had declined to possess it.
