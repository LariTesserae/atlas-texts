### A Mercy Small Enough to Be Ignored

The power did not finish the escarpment.

It could have made the attempt look like kindness. Every pool could have been given water that arrived in the proper order. Every herd could have emerged from a disturbance with exactly as many animals as had entered it. Every hunter could have been left with only the body that fed. Cliffs could have been made to remain beneath their gliders, and distance could have been taught to keep its word.

Seen from far enough away, this would have resembled peace.

But the power had not come merely to admire the place, and so it looked longer. It saw that to preserve every present arrangement would also be to exclude every arrangement yet to come. A cliff that could never be withdrawn could never be replaced. A herd protected from every disagreement in space might also be protected from the additional hungry bodies it would have to learn to feed. A world relieved of all its mistakes would be relieved, at the same time, of one of the ways life had always begun again.

Finishing the escarpment would not have healed it. It would have made it into something else, and then defended that something against its own future.

So the power did none of that.

Neither did it turn away and declare that everything happening below was necessary.

A herd still flinched at the interruption of a hunter long dead. A pool still spent its inhabitants enacting the abundance of water that no longer existed. Additional manyfoots stood hungering in foliage that had not made room for them, while elsewhere dense groves signaled and signaled to animals that would never return. The power did not call these things beautiful merely because, from sufficient altitude, they formed part of a beautiful whole.

There was a kind of reverence, it saw, that could become indifference with better manners.

It remained.

For a long while, attention was its only action. It learned the duration of slow pumps and the much shorter patience of spore-moths. It followed the fourth pulse, so weak that many roots scarcely registered it. It dried with the pool films and unfolded with them. It waited with gliders above the thermal of a vanished cliff until the last usable warmth had gone. It felt how older manyfoots pressed a young animal into the cropping wave, and how the same pressure, differently timed, could crush its chance of ever finding the rhythm.

There was no single inhabitant it could question without leaving out almost everything. There were beings all the way down into the root mats and agreements all the way up into the migrations. The nearest thing to a voice was often a pattern repeated by participants that did not know they were answering one another.

Any offer would therefore have to be small enough to go unanswered.

At last the power noticed something life had already been doing without assistance. It hesitated.

A young manyfoot missed a step. A root web discharged a fraction late. A film began to open and then contracted. A glider omitted one turn in an inherited circuit. Most such discrepancies were corrected, eaten, exhausted, or carried into irrelevance. A few survived long enough to become material for something new.

The power did not invent a new faculty. It made a little more room around an old one.

Here and there, briefly, it lent its attention to the interval before a pattern repeated itself. During that attention, a discrepancy did not have to be treated immediately as an error. A root web could send its fifth pulse and receive no answering flinch. A pool film could begin unfolding in poor water and stop without that stop becoming its final act. A herd could lose a beat without losing the migration. A glider could fail to make the expected turn and discover whether there was another way through the air.

Nothing was instructed to change. Most things did not.

When the power withdrew, the old rhythm usually resumed. Sometimes the hesitation resumed with it. If roots, feet, wings, and films found uses for the difference, it became as real—and as vulnerable to being forgotten—as any other habit. If they did not, nothing remained to mark the offer. In particular, the power was careful not to leave a sixth pulse. A sixth pulse would only have become one more thing to obey.

The first consequences were not all comforting. Narrow hunters learned to wait inside the loosened intervals. Their interruptions became subtler; sometimes whole columns of grazers stumbled where previously only one animal would have been lost. The power did not revoke the gift from the hunters. It had not been trying to improve anyone’s character.

Elsewhere, in a basin where a herd had flinched at every fifth pulse for longer than any of its bodies had lived, one animal did not flinch.

Nothing followed.

The cropping wave continued. The animal’s feet tasted moisture, fungal charge, the approaching warmth of rain. On the next wetting several animals failed to answer the old interruption. Later still, the root web sent it and the herd passed through the signal as though it were only weather moving underground. The memory had not been hunted down and destroyed. It had simply lost some of its ability to enlist the living.

At a poor pool, a few films remained closed through a wetting that would once have spent them. Not all opened when richer water came. Not all were alive by then. The power had not ended scarcity, and it did not pretend that delay was the same thing as rescue.

Some groves stopped sending their unanswered summons. Others kept sending it. The power left those alone. Not every unanswered thing was a wound.

Herds continued turning left before rain. Gliders continued, for a while, to circle absent cliffs. Portable habits crossed provinces in the folds of wings and the dust on armored backs. Among them, occasionally, traveled a slight irregularity: a delayed contraction, an omitted departure, a pause before division. It had no reliable shape and no proper name. In some places it was useful. In some it vanished. In a few it became an old, senseless habit in its own right.

The power did not return to correct those cases. It had begun to recognize the desire to do so as a danger in itself.

When it could no longer be certain whether it was still making room for difference or merely favoring the differences it loved, it stopped.

It left no sanctuary, no guardian, no stable valley in which its intentions could be preserved. It did not remain to admire the result. Attention, held long enough, could become another kind of grip.

Much later, the escarpment achieved one of its rare agreements.

Condensation gathered across a province. Fronds opened together. Pool-flaws lifted their margins in the same glassy water. The buried pumps completed their strokes, and millions of manyfoots lowered their heads without a lag traveling through the herds. Even the narrow hunters stood briefly in single bodies.

The whole country gave its soft, comprehensive click.

The power’s work did not interrupt it. There was nothing there to interrupt.

Then a moth answered a valve that had not opened. A shadow arrived early. Somewhere a herd emerged from a cleft with more appetites than the next seam could feed.

In one wet grove, a young manyfoot missed the following step.

The older animals closed around it. Their pressure came persistently, almost too quickly—but not quite. Somewhere in the herd’s inherited timing was a small looseness, assembled from delays no single animal remembered. The young manyfoot stumbled, found the cropping wave, and lost it again.

The herd made room a second time.

Far away, a cliff vanished beneath its gliders. Rain fell from a cloud that had not yet formed. Hunters fed. Old signals went unanswered. Fresh stone warmed beneath the dim sky.

Nothing had been made safe. Nothing had been granted permanence. The escarpment remained unfinished.

But here and there, between what had been enacted and what was about to be enacted again, there was a little more room for a living pattern to change its mind.

The power had not given it peace, or completion, or an ending.

It had given a few of its rhythms the opportunity not to go on—and then had allowed even that gift to remain unfinished.
