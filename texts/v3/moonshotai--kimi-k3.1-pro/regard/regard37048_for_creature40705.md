### What the Power Did

The power’s first impulse was rain.

It was an old, honorable impulse. On other worlds, it had seen life struggling beneath a bare sky and had known what mercy looked like: cloud gathering over stone, dust darkening, the small astonishment of moisture in a place that had none.

For an instant it imagined water standing in the basins of the Crown, reflecting the hard white sky.

The thought passed before it became weather.

The power looked longer. It saw the sealed oils within the leaves, the gases lingering in the root felt, the dry salts and mineral films. It saw that rain would not simply be added to this life. The life would have to become something else in order to survive the gift. Much of what stood before it would not survive at all.

So the power did not call its impulse wrong. It called it uninformed.

It remained, without touching anything, and learned.

It learned how long a garden could spend becoming visible. It watched a spore put out filaments in the lee of a stone. Years passed, and the whole achievement of that life was a small cluster of dark grains that did not shift when the surrounding pavement was combed clean. The power had once measured beginnings by their consequences. Here it learned to measure them also by what they managed to hold.

It followed a veilback across a basin.

At first the journey seemed to it like deprivation: so much distance, so little food, no other crest answering on the horizon. Several times it felt the old impulse return, now in the form of company. It could have brought another veilback near. It could have shortened the crossing. It could have made the traveler’s life easier to witness.

Instead, it attended to what was actually happening. It felt the slow changes in the animal’s oils, the crest opening to the early light, the long dormancies that were not death but part of travel. It discovered the spores lodged between the body’s articulations and the dormant gleaner sheltered beneath a plate. The apparently empty crossing was also a bridge, though almost none of its passengers would survive it.

The power did not conclude that the veilback’s life was easy. It concluded only that ease was not the sole measure by which it could be understood.

It saw, too, what made any simple reverence impossible. Roots competed. Hunters killed. Gardens vanished. Most spores woke nowhere, and many that might have woken were eaten or carried beyond every possible anchorage. There was genuine absence in the bright intervals, not merely hidden abundance waiting to be discovered.

The power did not call these things good. Nor did it use them as evidence that the Crown had been badly made. Its sorrow, it began to understand, was real; but sorrow was not yet knowledge, and knowledge was not yet a warrant to interfere.

At last it began to ask a different question.

Not: *What is lacking here?*

But: *What would my gift make impossible?*

Rain would make the present chemistry of the gardens impossible. Joining the gardens would make many of their separate histories impossible. Ending the great weather would alter the releases, the crossings, the slow redistribution of dust and life. Preserving every garden exactly as it stood would require arresting the very processes by which gardens arose. Even giving the veilbacks a voice in which to explain their needs would first require the power to decide what sort of beings they ought to become.

There was no central will in the Crown that it could consult. It would not invent one and call that invention consent.

None of this became a law of eternal noninterference. The power was too wise to turn restraint into another kind of pride. If it had found a harm it could ease without merely substituting its own preferences for the lives before it, it would have considered acting. If some later necessity had required help, wisdom would have had to begin again, with attention.

But it would not manufacture a necessity in order to exercise its benevolence.

Its judgment was not that the Crown was perfect. Perfection was not a prerequisite for respect.

In the end, the power left no guardian, no covenant, no image of itself in the stone. It made the gardens no safer from time. It promised no veilback that it would reach another garden. It took away neither failure nor distance, and it took no specimen to prove that it had understood.

What it carried away was a change in itself.

Thereafter, when it came to a place that seemed empty, it waited longer before deciding what was absent. When it encountered lives whose pace offended its patience, it did not immediately call them stunted. When its power made help easy, it became more careful about the difference between helping and enjoying the feeling of being helpful.

Elsewhere, this would lead to action: a hand stayed, a design abandoned, another world heard for a while before being named. The Crown thus altered what the power would do through ages to come, though the Crown itself never felt its touch.

Its love had not diminished. It had become accurate.

Long after the power had gone, a front crossed one of the high basins. Violet light moved soundlessly along its edge. The plumes folded, the cupfans turned silver, and the gardens drew themselves close to the ground. Dust passed over them for days.

In the calm that followed, one garden opened. Spores rose until the sunlight above it softened, and for a few hours the felt was full of minute disturbances.

Far beyond that weather, in a hollow with no name, a single spore put out filaments.

Nothing announced the event. Nothing recognized it as an answer.

For years, its whole visible achievement was that a few grains of dust did not move.

It was not a sign.

It was a beginning.
