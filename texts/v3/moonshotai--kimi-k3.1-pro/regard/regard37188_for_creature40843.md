### Rain, When They Were Ready

They would come quietly, and at first they would do nothing.

Their benevolence would want to give water. Their wisdom would ask where the water would go.

A spring might seem the obvious kindness. But a spring opened in the wrong joint could empty a cistern, loosen a terrace, or turn a shared seep into something people fought to own. Soil could be laid over the spoil like a blanket, soft and deep and ready for roots—but it would also bury the small evidence by which the inhabitants read their town. Even the tree could be given companions, shade, a whole green court around it, until the oldest terrace belonged more to the power’s idea of mercy than to the people who had made it.

Nor would the power decide that hardship was beautiful and therefore ought to be preserved. Skill did not make an injury desirable. Cooperation did not make an empty cistern good. The New Cut was neither a wound awaiting repair nor an idyll to be kept under glass.

It was a town. That made things difficult.

The power stayed long enough to hear two people disagree about an abandoned channel and understand that both were partly right. They watched a haul crew stop in the particular silence that meant something had caught. They saw a child warned away from a fresh face, an old wall patched with material from an older one, and a broom destroy in three strokes a pocket of damp ground that had taken several rains to make.

They had expected, perhaps, to consider a place and the being who lived in it. Instead they found many beings, each altering the conditions of the others. There was no single inhabitant whose good could safely stand for the good of the whole.

So, at last, they asked.

On the oldest terrace, an old inhabitant was sorting chips into two baskets: pieces small enough for packing, pieces worth carrying to the rubble stack. The power sat beside her in a form that cast an ordinary shadow.

“What would help?” they asked.

She did not look up immediately.

“That depends on what you mean to change.”

“Nothing you do not choose.”

“Then you had better ask more than me.”

The power did.

They heard requests for water and requests for drier sleeping places. Someone wanted timber that would not have to be spliced again. Someone else wanted one season in which no promising block turned out to be supporting a roof. A plasterer wanted to know why one cistern lost water when its neighbor did not. A child wanted the tree to live forever. Another child, more practical, wanted the lizard’s coping stone left where it was.

The answers did not converge into a single wise desire. They quarreled, qualified one another, and occasionally contradicted themselves. The power did not take this as a failure.

In the end, the help accepted was limited enough to be understood and large enough to matter.

“Rain,” the power was told. “But not tonight.”

The upper grate needed replacing. Two baskets blocked a turn that would have to be cleared. A new settling hollow had not yet been tested, and no one could agree whether an old channel beneath it had run east or west.

“How much rain?”

“Enough to show us what we have built. Not enough to bring the plain down on us.”

“And after that?”

“After that, we’ll see.”

The power waited three days.

They did not clear the channels themselves. They did not make the grates self-emptying or teach the ropes never to fray. On the third evening, after the tools had been made safe and the last arguments had narrowed to gestures at particular stones, it began to rain.

The first water crossed the plain almost invisibly. Then it found the cuts.

Dust darkened. Scratches became channels. Someone above a junction called down what the flow was carrying, and below, pale runoff was turned toward the settling hollows. A stick grate caught once, was cleared, and caught again. Water appeared where no one had expected it and failed to appear where an old memory had confidently sent people to wait.

The power did not secretly correct these mistakes. The rain was help, not a demonstration that help could make people infallible.

One threshold was wet before its owner finished complaining. A retaining wall took a small scar of silt. The new hollow proved useful, though not in quite the way its diggers had intended. Later, cleaner water ran under the stone lids. In the deepest cistern, the sound of its arrival changed slowly as the level rose.

By morning, the town knew more about itself.

The power felt the temptation to continue. Here, a little depth could be added to the new sediment. There, a seed could be woken. A root could be guided past stone; a companion to the tree could be made to stand already several years old, as if it had always been coming.

They did none of it.

“After that, we’ll see” had not been indecision. It had been an instruction.

So the power left the next decision where they had found it: distributed among thresholds, work yards, memories, and disputes; among roots and grates and hands; among things noticed and things overlooked.

When they went up the haul ramp, there was no new monument below. The cisterns held more water. One channel would have to be altered. Someone was already insisting that the rain had been heavier than promised, and someone else that it had scarcely reached the lower court at all.

Behind the oldest wall lay a fresh fan of silt, thin enough to sweep away. For the moment, no one swept it. A seed had lodged near its upper edge.

Whether it would live was not something the power settled.

From the plain, the New Cut was once again easy to miss. Below, a shutter moved. Water ticked once into a cistern. Someone on an unseen stair called down a question and waited.

After a moment, someone answered.
