### A Little Time

The power’s first act was to refrain from draining the basin.

From a sufficient height, this might have looked like kindness: firm paths, dry walls, clean foundations, cottages free of damp. But the power saw the reed carts and the brown bales leaving the drying shed. It saw channels kept at their accustomed levels, roots holding banks, thresholds raised without resentment. A dry Sumpwick would not have been Sumpwick improved. It would have been another place, imposed upon people who had not asked for it.

Nor did the power silence the pumps.

Their noise was an affliction, some days. It entered conversations and made everyone shout; it followed residents into other towns, where quiet could feel strangely exposed. But the pumps also spoke through the bridge rails and beneath sleep. Their note was one of the ways people knew where they were, and whether matters were as they had been. To replace all that with perfect, uninformative quiet would have been a very poor miracle.

The power did not make the brick new, clear every gutter, straighten the willows, or fill the channels with fish so that the water would appear more abundantly alive. It did not repair the crack above the pump-house door. It looked at the crack for a long time and saw the years of watching contained in it.

Most of all, it did not make everyone love Sumpwick.

That would have been an efficient blessing. Contented residents would have stayed; skilled hands would have remained available; departures would no longer leave gaps in the works. But it would have served the settlement more neatly than it served the people. Those who were bored would remain bored in their hearts, only mysteriously unable to act on it. Those who had come for wages would be made grateful for a home they had never wanted. The power did not mistake contentment for care.

So, for a while, it did nothing visible.

It listened until it could distinguish the pumps. It learned that black ground was not necessarily neglect, that a humid cottage could be meticulously kept, and that a new tide mark might matter more than a foot of familiar standing water. It saw that Sumpwick was not one being with one wish. The willows were not the works. The works were not the residents. The residents were not a single folk, however often their voices crossed the same channels.

It also saw that stability was not proof that every life within it was well. Its restraint was not a doctrine that suffering must be instructive. Where help was wanted, it would not refuse aid merely to preserve the charm of self-sufficiency. A place could be admirable without being more important than the people in it.

At last the power understood what it wished to give.

Not permanence. Not freedom from maintenance. A Sumpwick that tended itself might continue for a while in the same shapes, but its inhabitants would become dependents of a miracle whose workings they could not learn. The power valued their knowledge too much to make it useless.

Instead, it gave a little more room for response.

Its help, when it came, would look like earliness rather than rescue. A bearing might begin its faint, irregular note before it was beyond repair. The softness in a plank might be discovered beneath a testing boot rather than a loaded cart. A gutter might be noticed while rain was still only a possibility in the heavy air. The interval between the first small indication and the large consequence would sometimes be wide enough for a second look.

No one would be obliged to use it well. Warnings could still be dismissed. Work could still be postponed. A careful person could still be wrong, and waste an afternoon finding that nothing was wrong. The power would not turn attention into wisdom by force.

Nor would it decide that every response must preserve the works. Sometimes the right answer would be to close a shed, abandon a channel, or let an old arrangement end. Sometimes it would be for a person to leave. The power would not hold a cart wheel in the mud, or make a pump falter in reproach, merely because someone’s departure was inconvenient.

One evening, long after the power had begun its watch, a pump keeper paused on the bridge.

There was nothing to alarm him. Water hissed at the sluice; a belt ticked in the mill; the pumps maintained their accustomed weight beneath the other sounds. He might have gone on. Instead he put one hand on the rail and waited.

For a moment he thought he heard a second note inside the lower pump’s running. Then it was gone. He waited longer, heard it again, and chalked a reminder on the doorframe when he came off shift.

The matter was checked the next morning. It was not urgent. A repair was made the following week, between other jobs, and entered in the log without drama. No one regarded the keeper’s pause as miraculous. It was simply the sort of pause on which Sumpwick depended.

Elsewhere, a willow went on lifting its brick. Duckweed returned to a cleared channel. Someone oiled tools that would have to be oiled again. At the end of the season, a resident left and did not come back. The moorhen disappeared after cutting, and its eventual return settled nothing about whether it was the same bird.

The power had not spared Sumpwick time, disagreement, uncertainty, or loss. It had only made it a little more possible for care to arrive before care was useless.

That night, the pumps ran on. Someone crossing the bridge stopped briefly, confirmed that the sound underfoot was the expected one, and continued toward the cottages.

The power did nothing then.

For that night, nothing further was required.
