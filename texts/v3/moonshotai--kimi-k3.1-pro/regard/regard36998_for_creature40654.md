It came as shade.

Not as a shadow across the Cistern—that would have set moisture gathering on the walls and sent forty households to their lids and gates—but as a small darkness beneath an overhang, no larger than a guest standing out of the light.

From there it watched a complete turning before allowing itself to wish for anything.

It saw the signs pass from roof to roof. It saw jars carried indoors, goats resisting on the stairs, a felt turned too late, a child running where an adult could not safely go. It saw the water leave and return by different routes. Afterward it saw repairs begun before the dripping had stopped, and an old quarrel resumed over whose roof had put ceiling-rain into whose bed.

It also saw the dark film close again behind the goats’ muzzles, the eels finding deeper water, the slatewings resettling beneath the eaves. Nothing here was asking to be made still.

Its first thoughts were therefore large, and its second thoughts dismissed them.

To soften the light might diminish the pasture. To make the pools dependable would be to choose, without understanding every choice, which seeps should cease feeding them. To warm the whole room would not rescue the Cistern; it would replace it. Even an abundance of goats would become hunger once the animals had cropped the underwater film too closely. Every sufficiently grand gift would arrive as a new kind of weather, and the inhabitants would have to pay for learning it.

But the power did not make the opposite mistake either. It did not decide that because the people had adapted beautifully, nothing in their lives ought to be eased. There was nothing sacred about bedding that never dried. A child’s white fingers were not a necessary tuition. Patience with hardship was not the same as consent to it.

So, after a time, it entered the town on foot.

It squinted. It kept one hand on the rock. It accepted the lidded cup offered to it and sat where it was told, away from the brightness. When it stopped to admire the view from an exposed stair, someone pulled it impatiently aside.

It asked two questions, indoors and without thunder.

“What would you have a stranger leave alone?”

And later: “What work would you gladly keep, if only it cost you less?”

The people did not answer immediately. A seep-reader said it had seen too few turnings. Someone requested a goat that would always do what it was told, and was mocked by everyone who knew goats. Another person asked that no roof should ever drip again, then began explaining which shelters and returning waters might be lost in the accomplishment. The discussion became several discussions. The power waited without making its waiting into pressure.

Eventually it made a small offer.

Nothing in the open Cistern would change. There would be no new fuel, no fire that lit itself, no endless abundance to be guarded or inherited. But the stones of the communal hearth-pits could be made to waste less of the warmth they were already given. They would return a little of it slowly, after the dung had burned down: enough to keep a seat warm through a long story, to dry bedding laid nearby, to bring color back into dangerous hands with less expenditure of fuel. Their warmth would not pass beyond the old hearth walls or make a new weather among the seeps.

The gift could be refused. It could also be taken away later, without punishment, if the people found that it had cost them something they had not foreseen.

The decision took considerably longer than the miracle.

Then the power did what it had offered, and nothing else.

Winter remained winter. Turnings still came. Felts froze, gates stuck, goats objected to glaze underfoot, and the seep-readers continued to be wrong about small things. There were still damp rooms and visible meanness and people who disliked one another for thirty years.

But the hearth stones stayed warm after the fires had diminished. Less dried dung was needed to keep the communal pits useful. Bedding from the poorer rooms was brought to dry more often. Old people lingered longer beside the warmth, and children discovered that a stone wrapped in felt could be carried a short way into a cold sleeping chamber before someone older objected that they were doing it wrong.

The inhabitants watched the gift as carefully as they watched any unfamiliar seep. They tested it, argued over it, and looked for the household secretly disadvantaged by it. When no hidden leak appeared, they did what they always did with a reliable thing: they built custom around it. The warmest place remained available for white-fingered hands. Banking the fire became a slightly different art. Someone received credit who probably did not deserve it. Someone else had been saying all along that the old stones were better.

The power did not stay to be thanked, or to settle the arguments that followed. To remain available for consultation about every repair would have made it a ruler, however gently people spoke its name.

It had not made the Cistern gentle.

It had made gentleness a little easier to afford.
