### The Unmade Blessing

The power came without arriving.

That was the first courtesy. It did not descend in fire, stand upon a fin, or give itself a body that would require one local down rather than another. Even its attention it kept light, lest attention become a kind of weight and the loaded fins begin humming in answer to it.

It waited through a cloud tide. It felt the mires take water, the joints open beneath them, the temporary runnels resume their old itineraries. At noon it endured the doubled shadows without deciding that one kind was false. Before evening, when every sound ceased to arrive, it did not mistake the silence for distress. Somewhere water was still moving. Somewhere stone was still cooling. The power waited until the routes opened again and the Catchment became crowded with impacts.

It had been told that a being lived here.

It found no concealed mind in the quartzite, no intelligence distributed through the mires, no ancient presence using the sounding stones as an instrument too subtle for ordinary hearing. The mechanisms were not lonely. The echoes were not imprisoned voices. The seven depressions in the shelter’s threshold did not secretly record seven deaths, and the polished bench did not ask to have someone sitting on it again.

The power refused the consolation of a ghost. A ghost would have made the place easier to love and less possible to understand.

Nor did it conclude that its errand had been foolish. If the being was the whole company of recurrences, there was no single place at which it could be addressed. If it was the Catchment itself, there was no wish in it that could be distinguished from the wishes of an observer. Either way, there was no petition to grant. This was not the same as there being nothing to consider.

The power could have made life.

It could have darkened the sheltered cracks with lichen, laid the first soft films beneath the water, seeded the scree with roots small enough to enter the joints. Given time, there might have been green cushions in the lee of the fins, quick translucent things in the mires, creatures whose bodies kept one private downhill while all around them down remained local. It would have been beautiful. That was precisely why the temptation was dangerous.

The blank census was not an accusation. The Catchment was not failing to become a garden.

The power could also have repaired the place. It could have made gravity unanimous, given every pool one outlet and every traveller one honest shadow. It could have caused each cleft to remain the same width from dawn to afternoon, each passage to lead where it had led before. Water would have become easier to direct. The builders’ gutters could have been joined into a single efficient system. The old shelter might have received all three.

Within a few ages, the flutings, lenses, impossible rims, and recurrent adjacencies would have become the remains of an extinct grammar. A clear, serviceable waterworks would stand above the clouds. Almost nothing would be broken.

Almost everything that made the Catchment itself would have been removed.

Most of all, the power did not awaken the mechanisms. Their patience was not waiting. To give the balanced stones minds capable of hoping for their makers would not have honoured the builders. It would have created loneliness and called it continuity.

Still, benevolence did not become indifference.

Nothing in the Catchment could be frightened by erasure. That did not make erasure nothing. A mire could not mourn its next outline, but its margins could be sealed and its cloud tide diverted. A recurrent passage could not miss itself, but the seams that favoured it could be bound into permanent roads for someone else’s convenience. A sounding cairn could not care whether its question remained askable. It could nevertheless be stripped for copper, toppled for amusement, or made to serve a purpose so narrow that no variation in its answer mattered.

A thing need not suffer in order to be violently simplified.

Yet the power made no law of preservation over the summit. It did not promise that every mire would return, every fin remain standing, or every familiar echo find its chamber again. Such a promise would eventually have required it to defend the Catchment against its own weather. In the end it would have preserved an arrangement and prohibited an event.

So it did nothing to the stone.

It left no mark, hid no guardian in the cloud sea, and devised no test by which future visitors must prove themselves worthy. It did not make the region inaccessible. The builders themselves had once altered it, and wisdom did not require that all later hands be prevented from chipping a gutter, setting a basin, or wearing another hollow in a threshold. The Catchment had never been pristine. Compatibility, not untouchedness, was what remained of the builders’ virtue.

The power’s only immediate act was to remember accurately.

Not as a paradise. Not as a ruin. Not as an empty place awaiting use, nor a wounded place awaiting repair. It remembered that persistence here was not survival, that silence was not necessarily peace, that an answer could be completed without being heard, and that the absence of a wish was not permission to substitute one’s own.

If a time ever came when another will sought to give the whole region one owner, one use, one fixed map, or one compulsory downhill, the power would oppose not change but foreclosure. It would not defend any particular noon against evening. It would defend the possibility that water could again disagree with itself, that a dry channel might remain an unoccupied gesture rather than a failed canal, and that no single intelligence—including itself—could declare the argument finished.

Until then, there was nothing to add.

Before withdrawing, the power attended to one brief sequence.

A thread of water left a wall. A lens dimpled, and sediment turned slowly within it. Below, a mire accepted new overflow; its crust tightened with a soft plucking of flakes. The altered load set a fin humming. In a constructed catchment, water gathered until a balanced stone leaned, passed the point of return, and knocked once against another stone.

The note travelled by no visible opening.

Much later, an answer arrived from an apparently dry basin.

The power did not answer back. It did not interpret the exchange aloud, preserve the sound, or carry it away as evidence. Its presence had added nothing to the completion of the event, and it did not regard this as a disappointment.

The High Catchment had not become sacred because a power noticed it. The power had become careful because it noticed the High Catchment.

Then the cloud tide began to turn. Somewhere a channel whispered before its water arrived. Nothing had been given a soul, nothing had been promised freedom from time, and nothing had been required to become alive in order to be worthy of care.

A basin filled. A stone tipped. The note went out.

The power listened, and did not improve the answer.
