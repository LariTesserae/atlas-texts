### What the Power Did

The power came without arriving.

It added no light, because even light had consequences. It did not warm the air or take a shape the millbacks would have to spend attention upon. For a long time it did nothing. It had not yet learned whether its wish to help was knowledge of the plateau, or only knowledge of itself.

So it stayed through brief wettings and long frozen intervals. It watched a congregation beneath a slab wake incompletely, feed, lay, and return to dustlike stillness. It followed a swallowed stone through years of internal weather. It saw a young millback mouth unsuitable grit and an old one stand feeding on a pasture prepared by an animal long dead. Only after some of these things had happened more than once did the power begin to understand the expense of a gift.

Its first intention had been water.

That seemed benevolent until the power saw what a single generous rain might do. It could wake too much at once. It could turn stored possibility into simultaneous appetite, wash films from places that would not soon grow them again, and make activity compulsory for bodies whose lives depended on being able to postpone it. Warmth presented the same temptation in another form. To make waiting costly would be to make much of the population impossible.

The power considered richer films, then imagined the herds becoming denser than the ground’s slow preparation could support. It considered removing the hunters, then watched an animal too weak to rise continue through a decline that no abundance of pasture would reverse. Even kindness, arriving in sufficient quantity, could become a way of spending the future without asking it.

But the power did not decide that the plateau was perfect. That would have been only a more elegant way of stopping attention.

One old millback had begun to fail. Something among its internal cultures had collapsed, though its lips still tested the stone and its chambers remained full. A hunter had approached twice and twice withdrawn. The power could perceive the likely course ahead: the gradual weakness, the hunter’s returns, the long interval during which the animal would be unable either to recover or to keep others away.

It could also perceive beyond the body. It saw the brief enrichment of the hollow, the waking of dormants, the dismantling of hide and bone, and the pasture that might one day be scraped there.

None of that was an answer to the animal.

A future pasture could not be offered to it as consolation. The beauty of the whole arrangement did not make every moment within it good. The power refused to call suffering necessary merely because it could draw a line from that suffering to something else that lived.

Yet it did not heal the millback simply because healing was within its ability. To restore every animal at the first failure of an internal culture would alter hunger, mortality, and the movements of the herds. To heal only this one, because this was the one its attention had happened to rest upon, would make the accident of being noticed a principle of mercy. The power did not wish to flatter its own compassion.

Instead, it chose a mercy that required no favorite animal and no favorite species.

Where pain could still guide a body away from injury, toward shelter, or into the defense of its young, it remained. Where pain or fear had become only helpless awareness, with nothing left for them to accomplish, the power allowed them to loosen and go quiet. The body’s ending did not have to be accompanied by every possible experience of ending.

No hunter lost a meal. No carcass was spared its uses. The little clocks of the plateau still met around death, and death still passed into intake. The power did not call this good. It only made dying no crueler than the continuation of life required it to be.

There was no visible sign of the change.

Then the power turned its attention outward.

Here it was willing to be less subtle. It would not permit the plateau to be destroyed by an arriving kindness any more than by an arriving appetite. It would turn aside engines made to bring rain as readily as engines made to remove stone. It would refuse the bright seeds of those who wished to see the rock bloom, the imported herds of those who pitied the millbacks their thin pasture, and the careful enclosures of those who might try to preserve the place by making it unable to change.

Against weather, deep time, and the plateau’s own transformations, it set no wall. Against erasure—the ending that would leave no later herd, no altered descendant, no unforeseen continuation—it set its refusal.

This was not a promise that nothing would ever be lost. A lineage might fail. A climate might drift beyond the tolerance of some films. A species might leave descendants so changed that its old name would no longer fit. The power was not making a museum.

Nor was its restraint intended to bind the future. If some descendant of the herd, or some being not yet imaginable, ever became able to understand the power and ask for rain, warmth, healing, speech, or an end to some old necessity, the power would have to listen again. The present inhabitants had not consented to become an argument against their own descendants’ desires.

Having done these things, the power left no monument and taught no name for itself.

On one night afterward, cloud settled against the ground. Condensation darkened narrow bands of gravel. Films resumed their chemistry in pores and seams. Millbacks lowered their heads, and minute blue-white branches passed along their filaments as the accumulated charge leaked away.

From a height, scattered outlines appeared across a province: one near at hand, another far beyond it, others separated by distances that had looked empty. The power did not mistake the flickering for acknowledgement. Nothing there knew that a decision had been made. The young still took unnecessary steps. The hunters still tested and withdrew. Somewhere a meal advanced from one chamber to another.

Nothing had been built. Nothing had been kept unchanged for the pleasure of being preserved. The work had never stopped.

The power’s work was now mostly to refrain—and, where refraining would have been only another form of indifference, to do the smallest thing that mercy made clear.

Afterward, the silence was exactly as it had been. It simply contained one more presence that had learned not to hurry it.
