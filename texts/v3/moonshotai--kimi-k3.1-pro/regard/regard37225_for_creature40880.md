### What the Power Left Undone

The power came at noon, when the plain was easiest to misunderstand.

From above, it saw pale ground, scattered scrub, and towers without smoke. There was no water. Its first impulse was to make a river: clear, permanent, and generous enough that nothing would ever again need to wait years for rain.

Its second impulse was to wait.

It stayed while the light moved across the shells. It followed the covered runways and found the harvest passing through them, fragment by fragment. It entered the warm disorder of the fungus gardens. It watched soldiers hold a breached gallery while workers walled off the passage behind them. At dusk it heard the silt-mice beginning their precise journeys between cover. After dark, it watched a moundbreaker tear open a wall that had stood for years.

The river remained unmade.

A river such as the power had first imagined would not simply have added water. It would have made another country: drowned caches and galleries, cut channels through the soft floor, drawn a bright green seam through the basin and called that seam rescue. Much would have lived. Much would have flourished. But the gift would have required the giver to stop asking whether the Mound Plain itself, with its deep roots, metabolic water, dormant seeds, and humid buried rooms, had been empty before help arrived.

The power looked for the being it had come to bless.

There was no single being, if that meant one inhabitant able to answer for the rest. The queen could not speak for her colony, much less for the plain. The moundbreaker’s hunger was real, but so was the catastrophe it left inside the nursery. The exposed termites fed birds and lizards; the open chamber sheltered beetles, spiders, snakes, and mice. None of these consequences made the breach good. They only made it difficult to mend without deciding whose life should count.

The power considered making the mounds permanent. Then it saw a mouse nesting in an abandoned store and a tree root following a ruined gallery.

It considered giving the plain a voice. Then it wondered which life would be silenced by the choice of speaker.

So it waited longer.

It did not make the mistake of deciding that everything was good because everything was connected. Not every death was necessary. Pain did not become innocent because something fed afterward. Beauty was not an acquittal.

But neither was suffering a license to stop looking.

When rain finally came, the power watched seeds open that had waited longer than many of the animals above them would live. It watched winged termites rise into the brief humidity, and most of them be taken. It watched fresh walls slump and repaired runways fill with sediment. Then the surface dried, and the abundance was packed away again into seed, egg, pupa, spore, cache, and flesh.

The rain did not prove that drought was good. It proved only that the plain had a history larger than a visitor’s first idea of need.

In the end, the power’s small mercies were private. Where a creature was already beyond recovery, and pain could no longer help it escape or survive, the power shortened that pain. It did not prevent the body from passing onward into other lives. It did not call this balance or pretend that ecology required it. It was mercy, and mercy needed no further excuse.

Its great intervention was against itself.

It renounced the right to finish the plain.

It would not turn the basin into a garden, a monument, a sanctuary fixed at one favored century, or a lesson arranged for the instruction of others. It would not preserve every tower and thereby abolish the inheritance of ruins. It would not make the inhabitants safe by making their lives unrecognizable. Above all, it would not mistake the elegance of a single plan for an improvement upon millions of partial ones.

It laid only one command upon its own future attention: never to love the Mound Plain so abstractly that the small bodies carrying it became invisible.

And if some force ever came whose work would leave neither seed nor spore, neither refuge nor material from which refuge could be made—an ending after which nothing there could begin again—the power would resist it. Not to keep the plain unchanged. To keep it capable of change.

Then it left no sign.

At dusk, a silt-mouse paused beside its customary grass stem. A dust-cat listened at a burrow mouth. In a damaged mound, workers carried fresh earth toward the open air, while beetles were already entering the refuse behind them.

No creature knew that the plain had been loved.

Before dawn, a worker pressed one more pellet into a breached wall. The power did not improve its placement.

Then it withdrew, leaving the plain unfinished, unowned, and still making room for whatever would live there next.
