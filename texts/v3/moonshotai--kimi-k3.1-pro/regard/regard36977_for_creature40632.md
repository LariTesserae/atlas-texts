### External Assistance, Non-Load-Bearing

During the common beat, the power would check every map.

It would not do this to catch the surveyors in an error. It would do it because attention, before intervention, is the least misleading form of respect. For that brief interval it would attend to all of Vael: the brick wards beneath their adjustable galleries, the pales lying under intake screens, the splitfoot trail crossing a route that had lengthened since morning, the young bellwings asleep above a condenser bank scheduled to cool. It would hear the survey pairs beyond the last anchors, keeping the way back shared with talk of worn boots, abandoned nests, and what would be eaten on return.

Every map would be correct.

The power would understand that this was not evidence Vael ought to be made permanent. It was a glimpse of the city performing itself.

The large mercies would then suggest themselves. It could quiet the deep reports, finish the mountain, pin every route to a dependable length. It could make water abundant and put an end to drift. Some suffering would undoubtedly be prevented.

But to still the mountain might also be to still the heat that sustained its inhabited seams. To fill every cistern without asking would be to rewrite gardens, customs, and lives. To make all distances obligatory would be to replace Vael with a more tractable city and call the replacement a gift. Even perfect foresight would not, by itself, confer the right to choose for everyone.

Yet the power would not make the opposite mistake either. It would not leave Vael untouched merely because danger had produced beautiful customs. Exact water discipline is admirable; it is not a reason to cherish scarcity. The survey verses are worth preserving; they are not a reason to let a child remain lost beyond an anchor. A wise power would not fall in love with the inhabitants’ hardships and call that love respect.

So it would offer help, and make the offer revocable.

It would speak plainly, in a manner anyone could ignore. It would explain what it could do, what it did not understand, and what it would not do without invitation. It would demand neither worship nor civic unanimity. It would consent to being tested.

Vael would test it.

The surveyors would ask whether its assistance could be used as a reference point. The water wardens would ask what it knew about isolated cistern populations. Condenser crews would ask whether it could distinguish an active nest from an old one. Magistrates would want to know whether accepting help in one district obligated another. Engine keepers would ask whether anything would happen to municipal space if the power were refused, dismissed, or simply ceased to be believed in.

“Nothing,” it would have to answer. “Nothing that would not have happened before I came.”

That answer would matter more than any demonstration.

While the arguments continued—and they would continue—it would not withhold emergency aid to prove a point about consent. No one should have to wait for a civic determination while hanging at the end of a chain. It would rescue where rescue was wanted, explain what it had done, and make no claim to authority on the strength of gratitude.

In the end, I think Vael would ask for less than it could have demanded.

Not a new geology. Not an infallible map. Not a god concealed inside the engines. It would ask that help be available without becoming a foundation.

So the power would become an answer at the other end of the chain.

Anyone beyond the maintained network could call. If they wanted directions, it would give directions. If they were injured and wanted to be carried home, it would carry them. If they wished to find their own way but could no longer reliably keep the return alone, it would speak with them at intervals. Its voice would not be thunderous. It would ask about the condition of a boot, the number of steps since the last marker, a bird’s nest, what might still be waiting for supper. It would not turn rescue into a lesson about the foolishness of needing it.

It would never become necessary to the ordinary agreement between cable length and walking distance. No district would drift because it had failed to pray. No engine could be allowed to fail on the theory that the power would now hold the street in sequence.

For Vael’s other inhabitants, its assistance would take different forms. It would not distribute questionnaires among the moths or pretend that watching splitfoots had told it everything a splitfoot might want. It would not make predators harmless, roots considerate, or every death an administrative failure. A world edited until nothing could damage anything else would soon be a world in which very little could live.

But it would refuse to treat human convenience as the only interest visible enough to count.

Before a cistern was emptied, it could make known the pale shape beneath the screen. Before an array was cooled, it could draw attention to the three young still in the nest. Before a warm fracture was sealed, it could show which garden depended on the leak. It would not always forbid the work. It would not make every decision for people merely because it could make better ones. But it would make ignorance harder to use as an excuse, and it would never call a place empty simply because no human being could reliably return to it.

Its eventual entry in the civic records might be very short:

*External assistance. Available on request. Non-load-bearing. Not a survey anchor. Availability is not grounds for deferring maintenance.*

There would be no need for a longer title.

On most days, Vael would remain difficult. Bearings would still need dressing. Gardeners and wardens would still quarrel. Roads would be closed, maps reissued, and doors numbered by another survey epoch. The power would accept no dedication of clean water; if a cup were left out for it, the water would go to roots.

At the common beat, it would add no stroke of its own. It would listen while district after district answered across the empty kilometres, and it would take care that the city’s agreement with itself never came to depend upon being observed.

And occasionally, far beyond the last anchor, where a route had begun to disagree with its own recollection, someone would say:

“I am here.”

The power would answer:

“Then you are not the only one keeping the route. Tell me what you can see.”

It would not have made Vael safe, eternal, or its own.

It would not be the thing that held Vael together. It would be the help Vael could call upon while holding itself together: powerful enough to matter, patient enough to wait, and wise enough to remain something Vael could refuse.
