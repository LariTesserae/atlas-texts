The power would be tempted, at first, to improve the place.

It would want to hold the rim against fracture, keep the ice from tearing at the dams, make the food caches last until spring. It would want to turn the owl aside—and then, having done that, to find the owl something else to eat. It would want to lift the stone from a buried lodge, warm the water around an exhausted animal, keep one particular bank from slumping into the canal.

Each mercy would be intelligible. Together, they could become a government.

Soon nothing could go wrong without the power permitting it. Then, slowly, nothing could happen without its permission. Water level, already the nearest thing to law, would have acquired a lawgiver. The basin might become safer. It would also cease, in some essential way, to belong to those who lived in it.

So the power would not assume that its first idea of kindness was wisdom.

Nor would it make a doctrine of refusal. It would not call a buried family necessary, or a kit’s death good because an owl must eat. It would not look at moss covering a ruin and declare the loss repaired. The basin could go on flourishing, but the basin was never the one who suffered. Wisdom would not allow it to love the pattern so much that it stopped seeing the animal.

It would attend, instead, at the scale of a life.

It would learn the depth at one submerged doorway, the branch that could be gripped in darkness, the route taken when an owl crossed the roofs. It would not offer a beaver the view from the rim as though that were automatically a gift. The animal’s small country of water would not appear to it as a failed map of the whole. It was knowledge fitted to a body, a family, a food cache, and a door that had to be found beneath ice.

Nor would it give one animal the plan and make that animal ruler of the rest. It would not replace thousands of local certainties with a single correct design. It would not make the work unnecessary and call that liberation.

Where help could return a life to itself, perhaps it would help: a current eased for the length of one breath, a passage left open, a trapped body freed. Such acts would leave no doctrine behind them. But it would not undertake to become the answer to every current, every winter, every hunger. Its care would not require the basin to become dependent on being cared for.

Most of its work would lie at the edges.

It would keep poison out of the seep water. It would not let the outlet be casually sealed or the headwaters taken away. It would preserve ways by which young beavers could disperse and survivors return after catastrophe. It would turn aside destruction so complete that nothing could answer it—not teeth, not roots, not current, not time. It would protect the place from being drained, owned, exhibited, or loved into accessibility.

But it would not protect the present arrangement.

A pond could still fill with sedge. A favoured lodge could still become an island, then a root-bound bank, then the foundation of unfamiliar work. Rockfall could erase a district. Ice could open a dam. The power would save no shoreline merely because it was beautiful from above, and no family territory merely because it was old. It would guard conditions rather than appearances: clean water, living connections, material within reach, and the freedom of repair.

Then it would leave no sign.

No stone would bear a promise. No beaver would be made a prophet. No animal would owe gratitude for dangers it never knew had approached the rim. There would be no sacred map, no final water level, no moment when the inhabitants were expected to understand what had been done for them.

On a rainy night, water would still find a gap. A beaver would still feel the changed pull at its lodge and push mud into the leak. Upstream, a fish would move farther into the flooded sedge; a vole would leave a burrow that had begun to drip. By morning, water would already be finding its way around the new work.

The power would feel again the temptation to finish something.

It would not.

Its gift would not be that nothing was ever lost. It would be that loss was not permitted to become the end of every possibility. There would be another thaw, another stem within reach, another small necessity that an animal could answer with its own body.

Nothing would be completed.

The power would simply have kept the continuing possible.
