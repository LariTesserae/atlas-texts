The power would not begin by drying the plateau.

Nor would it stop the pumps. Either act might look like mercy from a sufficient distance: the city delivered from its labor, the water released from its engines. But the power would be wise enough to distrust the kindness of gestures that required no knowledge of what they interrupted.

For a long time, it would do nothing distinguishable from attention.

It would listen through a seasonal rise and fall. It would follow a change of load from the Gauge House to a pump hall, from the pump hall to a quarry pond, and from the pond, hours later, to water beneath a kitchen floor beyond the ridge. It would learn which silences were rest and which were warning. It would see that the roar injured people and also told them whether they were safe; that the courtyard trees were not a failed attempt at an orchard; that a repair described as *holding* was not therefore a failure. It would learn to respect the Sumpers’ caution without mistaking caution for consent to every hardship under which it had developed.

And it would refuse the romance of the place.

It would not preserve damaged hearing because the hand language was beautiful. A language born partly from necessity could become a treasure without making the necessity good. It would not preserve exhausted watches because old keepers made moving figures in the name-blocks, or leave children without other choices because those who stayed had made staying meaningful. Neither would it decide that everyone must secretly wish to leave.

So its first interventions would be almost offensively practical.

It would take the injury out of the sound without taking the information out of the chord. The pumps would still thunder, the penstocks would still hammer, and an experienced keeper would still hear the small alteration that meant a bearing or a narrowing pipe. But the noise would no longer exact hearing as wages or press itself into the body long after a watch had ended. Hand speech would remain, used across yards and over tables, because it had become more than compensation. So would the ear guards, at first from habit and later from prudence. No one would be required to become a different sort of Sumper in order to be healed.

It would put slack into the city.

Not perfection: slack. Enough relief that no crew’s endurance was the hidden component in a pump’s reliability. Enough spare capacity that a mistake need not become a catastrophe before help could arrive. Enough warning that a domestic seep book could be consulted before, rather than after, the Gauge House ordered a change whose consequences would appear in someone else’s bedroom. Where the power knew a path or a likely consequence, it would not withhold the knowledge merely to preserve the city’s admirable humility. But it would give no map the authority of infallibility. The crossed-out arrows would remain. So would the blank spaces.

Its miracles would often resemble time: time to move a bed, time to send word to an outlying rib, time for a second opinion, time for a keeper to go home without feeling that the city had been entrusted to their absence.

It would also make departure less like rupture. A young person could go down to the lower country without being treated as lost, and return without having to pretend that the return proved leaving had been foolish. Staying would become more fully a choice. So would serving a watch, inheriting a house, or declining to inherit its obligations.

The power would not make the plateau lush. It would see no poverty in the mere fact that the sedge was scattered and the films thin. But neither would it treat the small margins of life as worthless because they were poor building sites or troublesome in gauge lines. A snail’s seam, a pocket of cooled sedge, the expansion joint in which lizards waited out the heat: these would not be turned into sanctuaries with plaques. They would simply cease to be quite so easy to erase without noticing.

Toward the possible inhabitant, the power would be even more careful.

It would not assume that something with delayed responses and returning paths must therefore possess intentions. Nor would it conclude that, because no intentions could be demonstrated, nothing was owed except to human convenience. If there was a mind in that immense relation of rock, pressure, water, galleries, and load, it would not speak at the tempo of a courtyard conversation. The power would wait. It would try to distinguish preference from pressure, answer from coincidence, and distress from its own wish to be needed.

If an answer came, it would honor what could be understood and admit the rest.

If none came, it would not treat the silence as permission.

It would not “free” the being by sending all the water downhill, any more than it would “protect” the city by sealing every unexplained return. It would concentrate instead on irreversibility: on the decision that could not be reconsidered after the next seasonal rise, the gallery closed merely because its behavior was inconvenient, the intervention whose beneficiaries were present and whose costs would arrive elsewhere, much later. It would leave room for observation without making observation an excuse for endless suffering.

Above all, it would not make the Upper Sump eternal.

Permanence would be only another form of coercion. The city might one day have done enough of its work. Whole districts might be allowed to wind down; pumps might be taken out of the chord one by one; houses might be opened to a climate their double walls had long resisted. The power would not prevent that ending merely because the existing arrangement was intricate and moving. It would try to ensure that an ending, if it came, could be tended rather than suffered as collapse.

Then, after remaining long enough to see some of the unintended consequences of its own benevolence, it would withdraw. No shrine would mark the withdrawal. Nothing in the Gauge House would become a throne.

From the plateau rim, the Upper Sump would still look almost dormant: pale reservoirs, dark roofs, scattered plumes, long distances between things. Rust and scale would continue. Walls would still be dry at present. Fruit would still be shared partly for the proof that a tree had survived. Seep books would still disagree with charts, and people would still cross exposed ground deliberately, giving the sign for attention before approaching someone at work.

But fewer lives would be consumed in keeping the exceptions maintained.

And when a sleeper woke because one familiar note had gone missing from the chord, there would still be urgency. There would still be knowledge, uncertainty, and work. There would simply be a margin between the missing note and disaster—enough margin for other sleepers to wake, for word to travel, for help to come without being asked to own what it saved.

The power would not have completed the old agreement.

It would have made the agreement less cruel to keep, and possible, one day, to lay down.
