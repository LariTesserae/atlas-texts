There would be no miller to meet.

The power would understand that before doing anything. They would not step out onto the moss, part the walls, or call the hidden birds into view. Their attention would enter the central air without weight, and for a while they would only listen.

An hour would teach them weather. A dry interval would teach them how much of the labor could fall silent without ceasing. Longer attention would correct the first impressions of tenderness: the empty floor was not abandonment; the noise was not toil asking to be relieved; the absence of a single inhabitant at the center was not a vacancy at the heart of the place.

If a snail lay far out on the open moss, they might feel the ease of rescue. One small movement could return it to the wall. But they would not assume that its visible position made it lost, or that being visible made it the life most in need of help. Their care would not be apportioned by conspicuousness. The moth crossing a patch of sunlight would not be granted additional nights as a reward for having been seen. The springtails beneath it would matter no less for passing unnoticed.

They would not give the frogs a common pond, the spiders a bridge across the central air, or the birds quieter walls. They would not save the trunks by stopping the beetles, or spare the larvae by stopping the birds. They would not make the oldest wood incorruptible, knowing how many lives depended on its softening.

Nor would they wake the grove into a single mind. No tree would be required to know what its roots enclosed. No larva would be troubled by the dimensions beyond its culm. The place would not have to become conscious of itself in order to receive care, and none of its inhabitants would be asked to become witnesses large enough to appreciate what had noticed them.

Even preservation would tempt them, and even that they would refuse.

They would not make the present balance everlasting. What had endured was not thereby obliged to endure. If some distant age brought standing water, a failing section of wall, a roof slowly closing, that would not necessarily be a loss to be prevented. The power would not embalm the arrangement merely because they had arrived while it was beautiful. They would love it without making their moment of attention into its final purpose.

Only an injury approaching from outside might move them to act: some appetite that saw old wood as unused material, open moss as wasted ground, and silence after destruction as peace. Then, perhaps, a hand would be turned aside before it reached the walls, or a plan would lose its urgency far from the grove. The act would end at sufficiency. There would be no ring of thorns, no guardian beast, no curse laid upon the plain. Mill Hollow would not become frightening in order to remain possible.

Within the hollow itself, there would be nothing to add.

So the power would withdraw, if attention can be said to withdraw. No feather would be left arranged on the moss. No new note would enter the chorus. For a little while the central air would have contained one more event; afterward it would contain rain, pollen, a turning leaf, perhaps the quick passage of a bird.

Nothing in Mill Hollow would have been improved. Nothing in it would have been overlooked.

The place would never know that it had been loved. The power would not confuse that with having loved in vain. They would remember the separate water cups, the routes through the bark, the lives conducted within single stems, and the open air in which all that uncoordinated labor could briefly be heard together.

Then they would leave the center empty.
