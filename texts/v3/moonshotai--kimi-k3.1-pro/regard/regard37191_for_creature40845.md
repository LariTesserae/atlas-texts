### A Mercy at the Rim

The power came without light, because an unneeded warmth would have altered the wall meadows. It came without a voice, because a voice would have returned hours later from a standpipe or an empty house, and it did not wish to mistake its own echo for an answer.

It stayed through drawdown, through the making of cloud, through the downpour and the opening of the sluices. Then it stayed through them again. Only when it could tell a regularity from a request did it consider acting.

Its first mercies were refusals.

It did not silence the pistons. Their violence travelled through brick and water, but nothing in the basin was asking for quiet, and the engines’ heat was interwoven with too many lives. It did not repair every failed machine. Ferns had entered the dead housings, and some still mechanisms held gardens more various than those beside the working ones. It did not clear the channels of every obstruction. Nor did it let the reeds close them all.

It brought no birds to the roof voids, no fish to the reservoir, nothing to feed or be fed. The emptiness was not a list of omissions.

Nor did it make the whole basin into one organism so that it could conveniently be asked what it wanted. The pumps had no allegiance to the reeds. A garden could be scalded by the same pressure change that opened a passage for seeds elsewhere. To give the Works a single will would have been a way of not listening to its inhabitants.

The nearest thing to one ancient inhabitant lay beneath the flooded courts.

There, under platforms and silt, the old reed rhizomes persisted. Stems above them had been cut, carried away, pressed, and burned. Surges had flattened whole courts. Ash had settled over the roots. Still the buried networks put up growth where cutting and current permitted. Whether the oldest bed was one being or several could not have been settled without tearing it apart. The power did not tear it apart.

It attended to that life in the manner appropriate to roots: by warmth, by water, by air carried through debris, by the slow difference between a viable bud and one that would rot. It found no concealed plea. No wish to be elsewhere. No wish to remain forever. Those were the power’s categories, and it set them down.

The absence of a prayer did not make the life less worthy of care. It only meant that care could not be guided by gratitude.

The power saw, too, what age would do. Redundancy was a long answer, but not an endless one. Routes would fail. A repair would outlast the conditions that had made it useful. Eventually the basin might become quieter, hotter, drier, or more openly a marsh. Some lives would vanish with the machinery that had accidentally nourished them. Others might continue in the ruins.

The power could have made every bearing whole and every boiler new. It could have defended forever the exact green dusk in which it had arrived. But that would have been to preserve its own vision of the Low Works, not necessarily the lives before it.

Equally, it could have caused the last pump to fail and called the result liberation. An ending was not made innocent by being called natural.

Finitude was not, by itself, an injury. But neither was the closing of every possibility something to mistake for peace.

What the power chose was smaller than rescue, and less satisfying than repair.

Beyond the rim, past the collapsed aqueduct, lay an abandoned settling pond. It was cut off from other waters. A little rain remained in its deepest seam, darkened by old sediment. Once, an overflow from the basin had reached it; silt and roots had long since closed the way.

The power did not reopen that way. To do so would have altered the tide, and perhaps drained the very conditions it had come to cherish. Instead it made one slight provision.

If water ever rose above all the pale old marks—not in an ordinary tide, but in some exceptional rain, when the basin had already ceased to be quite itself—a thread would be able to pass through the buried seam. Only a thread. Not enough to save the Works. Not enough to change them now.

That thread might carry nothing but silt. It might carry spores, or a floating seed case, or a fragment torn from a rhizome. Whatever reached the settling pond would find no cistern waiting to lift it, no grille to sort it, no channel prepared for its return. It might rot. It might lie dormant and be buried. It might grow.

The power did not decide.

Its gift was not survival. It was that survival, if it came, would not also have to be a return to the same intakes.

No current inside the basin changed. No being was removed to a better place and expected to flourish there in demonstration of the power’s goodness. The old reed bed was not made eternal. The pumps were not taught allegiance. The six houses were given no keeper.

The power left no commandment in the Low Works, not even *Survive*.

For one more tide it remained and did nothing. Mist lowered. Rain struck the reservoir until its surface seemed to boil. The floats rose; the pumps altered their labor; the sluices opened together. Water surged through the broad streets and returned toward the engines.

Then the power went.

By morning, nothing announced that mercy had visited. A wall meadow was perhaps one frond wider. A seed case had lodged against a different grille. On a new fan of sediment, the first dull film was spreading. At the rim, the old watermarks remained unexceeded.

Below, another gate opened. The fern crowns shuddered, and the whole basin answered with thunder.

It was not gratitude. The power was wise enough to let it be weather.
