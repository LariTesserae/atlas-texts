### A Margin of Time

At first, the power thought of rain.

Not the cold, brief rain that already came to the plateau, but generous rain: dark clouds crossing the tableland in season, water standing in open pools, soil deepening in the fractures, green visible from the rim.

Then it followed that imagined kindness downward.

It saw water entering fissures and freezing beneath old foundations. It saw retaining walls lifted grain by grain, reliable seeps displaced, herd routes altered, cliff colonies thinned by new paths and new hunters. It saw the city growing beyond the pass that fed it, and beyond the range that sustained its herds. A river would not remain a silver ornament laid across the stone. It would become another force the city had to survive.

So the power did not make the plateau gentle. It did not cover the lava with soil, soften the basalt, or fill every cistern and call the result peace.

Then it considered doing nothing.

This was a more respectable temptation, and therefore more dangerous.

The people were ingenious. They were patient. They had made warmth, water, memory, and even laughter out of very little. But admiration can become a way of leaving people in pain because their endurance pleases us. There was nothing sacred in smoke damaging a child’s lungs, or in an isolated elder lying cold beneath a roof she had once helped to maintain. A herder dying within sight of a shelter was not a lesson the plateau required.

The power would not make the people helpless in order to feel benevolent. Neither would it preserve their suffering in order to feel wise.

It began at the seep.

Deep below the city, where water moved along the buried contact between lava flows, it made no abundance. It made change legible.

When ice or a shifting fracture began to trouble the flow, the water would lessen by degrees. A faint mineral taste would reach one settling basin before the others. The echo beneath a cover would alter while there was still time to clear an inlet, ration carefully, send word to range relatives, or prepare a household to move. Keepers would discover these signs for themselves and argue over their meaning. Some would mistake them. Some would improve upon one another’s explanations. Their knowledge would remain theirs.

In the worst years, when the seep would otherwise have failed without warning, the power kept a thread of water moving.

Not enough to end rationing. Not enough to make water rights meaningless, or to save people from having to ask one another for help. Enough for a decision.

To the basalt it gave no new virtue. To those who worked with it, it occasionally gave one more breath of attention.

A mason would set down the maul, turn a slab over, and find the flaw that winter would otherwise have found. A cliff fowler would test a projection twice and move the rope. A keeper reaching to replace a cover would pause, hear the wrongness beneath an ordinary note, and call for help before the lining opened. Far out on the tableland, a traveler about to take the straight gully would remember the whole of its name.

The power was careful. It did not make every maul stroke true or every rope hold. People would have stopped testing them. It did not make skill unnecessary; it made skill less often useless.

Other help it concealed inside ordinary thought.

A woman patching a smoke vent saw how a low baffle and a second narrow opening might draw the smoke away while leaving more warmth in the wall. A young household, tired of maintaining channels whose water they could scarcely claim, imagined a different arrangement of shallow roof gutters—one that could be built without touching an old court’s inlet. Neither design required metal, precious timber, or the power’s continued presence. Both could be copied, criticized, and improved.

Some versions worked badly. Some were resisted for good reasons and some for bad ones. Credit was not always given where it was due. At later channel cleanings, names became attached to work those named people had never done.

The power let all this happen. It had not come to make inspiration infallible, or human memory flattering.

And sometimes, when there was no time for skill, it helped directly.

It held a fractured roof for seven breaths while a court was emptied. It warmed a lost herder until her hands could feel the shelter wall beneath them. It eased the breathing of a smoke-sick child. It sat, unseen, beside an old man whose knowledge had outlived his strength, and kept the cold from taking him before morning brought the neighbor who had meant to come sooner.

It did not ask whether any of them was *inside*.

It did not help only the useful, the agreeable, or those whose survival would benefit the city. It kept no ledger of gratitude. No one was made to suffer longer so that a later generation could inherit a better story.

Yet it would not give the city a ruler, or pronounce from above the correct settlement of its disputes. A just arrangement spoken in its voice would still have been an arrangement imposed. Instead, where it could, it gave those with little bargaining power more means of their own: a catchment that could be repaired without waiting for an older court, a cleaner fire that required less fuel, a little more warning before necessity made any offer impossible to refuse.

It gave no commandment and no name by which one court could silence another. When people began leaving water out for it, it was not offended, but it did not encourage them. Gratitude could become a gate, and they already had enough gates. It would not become another covered water, with keepers entitled to decide who might approach.

Years passed.

The plateau remained sharp. The city remained crowded, argumentative, and unfinished. Herds still vanished beyond weather that had not yet reached the rim. Fuel was still counted. Young households still complained, sometimes justly, about labor and water. Old channels still preserved the mistakes of the dead.

But fewer failures came without any warning. More rooms could be warmed without being filled with smoke. More households had one small work of their own to repair and improve. And beyond the last wall, travelers who would once have been remembered only by an unfinished route recitation sometimes returned instead, bringing with them an account of shelter reached almost too late.

On an evening much like the one on which the power had first begun to listen, a keeper raised a cistern cover with a child beside her.

Below them, water struck water.

The child waited. “Is it failing?”

“No,” the keeper said. Then, after another drop: “Changing. We’ll look at the second basin tomorrow.”

She lowered the cover. The sound continued faintly through the stone.

The power had not saved them from time, or from one another. It had given time back to them, here and there, where silence and bad luck had stolen it. The rest remained in their hands.

It stayed near enough to hear a call for help, and far enough that no one could claim to speak in its name.
