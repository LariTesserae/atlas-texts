### The Interval

The power’s first act was to remain longer than the noise required.

From the rim, it would have been easy to mistake the Drum for a single assault upon the ground, and the crews for its authors. From the pump floor, it would have been just as easy to mistake continuous running for necessity itself. The power listened in both places, and in the shelter at handover, and in the brief water around a moss leaf. It attended to the living sphagnum and to the dead stems beneath it, without confusing one with the other. It followed the fox only as far as the fox went. It learned the raven’s pole, the uses of each lamp, and the entries that one tired person left for another.

It also followed the peat, the cables, and the wages away from the basin. It learned which distant necessities had been fastened to the active face: who would be cold if the fuel stopped coming, who would be blamed if the work ended, whose security depended upon the next cut being opened.

There was no single inhabitant whose wishes could stand for all the rest. The power did not invent one.

It did not call down summer. A general thaw would have been only another abrupt event, and the ground had endured enough of those. It did not command the peat to live again; an archive is not a seed. Nor did it strike the engines silent in mid-note and call the result peace. Pumps, roads, water, buildings, and people were too entangled for that kind of cleanliness.

Instead, the power made stopping possible.

Cold remained cold. Metal still wore. Bad judgement still had consequences. What ended was the old automatic penalty by which rest itself became failure. A pump could be stopped without the night turning it into an emergency. A conveyor could stand unused without demanding to be fed merely so that it might remain usable. An engine could cool, be repaired, be reconsidered.

The crews discovered this as they discovered most things in the Drum: through a disaster that did not happen.

A repair overran its safe interval. The expected race back to the machine did not take place, because there was no longer anything to race against. At first the workers distrusted the reprieve more than they would have distrusted a breakdown. They tested one machine, then another. They left a stopped motor through a cold night and returned with tools whose use they had already rehearsed.

The logbook recorded the result without emphasis:

*Mill stopped 02:10. Restarted 09:40. No fault.*

The power did not require belief. Reliability was enough.

Beyond the rim, it removed the other penalties as carefully as an engineer isolating a pipe. Where the basin’s product had been needed for warmth, another dependable warmth was provided. Where wages had depended upon the next face being opened, they continued until other work was real rather than promised. No riches were distributed, and no lectures were delivered about the value of moss. The crews were not asked to choose between their own people and the ground.

The power did not make them good. It made goodness unnecessary as a form of heroism.

The first deliberate silence lasted eleven minutes.

An inspection had been completed, and there was no reason to delay the restart except that, for the first time, delay was possible. The conveyor remained still. Then one of the drying fans was shut down. The pumps continued below, and the wind continued above them, so the basin was not silent. But the great note had thinned enough to contain spaces.

The workers stood near the shelter door with the slightly bewildered expressions of people who had brought the wrong tools. Their ears still rang. One of them heard ice ticking in a ditch and went to look, suspecting a fracture. Another heard clothing move against his sleeve.

Someone said, “That’s the wind.”

It had been there all along.

No one heard the moss growing. That was not the sort of miracle on offer.

The larger changes took years. No new active face was opened. The old workings were made safe, and drainage was altered by stages. Some pumps continued to run; others ran only when required. Human attention did not cease, but it ceased to be a relay maintained chiefly to prevent a pause. People still came on rounds, still wrote what had frozen and what had begun to thaw, still listened for a note that did not belong. Sometimes the work now consisted of leaving something alone and recording why.

The mill’s warmth was withdrawn gradually. The green margin beside it was not frozen in an instant, nor preserved forever as a shrine. What could enter dormancy was given time to do so; what could move was not suddenly enclosed by ice. Then the little artificial summer was allowed to narrow and disappear.

The power did not mistake every consequence of the works for something that must be retained. A gift dependent upon the wound did not have to be made eternal in order to have mattered.

Nor were the human remains swept away. The rail stayed where moss was finding it. The survey stakes kept their grey paint. The roofless hut wall continued to be a wall for a while, and then less of one. The cuttings remained dark and angular. Water returned as seepage, slush, brown films beneath ice, and occasional shifts in the old drains. There was no flood and no green advance.

The peat did not become innocent. The dwarf birch did not stand up.

Years later, a visitor on the rim might have concluded that the power had done nothing. Basin Four was still scarred, still sparsely lit, still capable of looking newly damaged after decades. There was no settlement and no forest. On some days an engine could be heard filling the cold air out of all proportion to its size.

But when its work was finished, it stopped.

A pump could be silent without anyone interpreting the silence as a failure. A crew could leave without bequeathing uninterrupted noise to the next shift. Decisions no longer had to be made at the speed of freezing grease. The basin had not been restored; it had been released from the necessity of remaining as it was.

On the upper bench, moss closed over another cold centimetre of rail. At the old mill, snow lay in the former halo of warmth just as it lay elsewhere. Somewhere below, a pump started, ran for a while, and was shut down without ceremony.

A raven called from the highest pole.

This time, the sound reached the ground.
