It would not arrive as light.

Even that would be an alteration. A little more brightness on the brightest wall might be a gift, or it might become a quiet demand that the olive films grow greener, larger, easier to admire. So the power would first know the chamber without entering it in any way the chamber had to accommodate.

It would not search the open water for the being and, finding nothing there, call the room empty. Nor would it need to gather the films, mosses, filaments, and sediment cells into the fiction of a single person before it could care about them. It would not find the inhabitant wanting because it had no voice with which to be addressed.

Neither would it mistake stillness for sorrow. Nothing here was bored. Nothing was waiting for the roof to open or for something to cross the floor. The cold was not an insult; the machinery’s note was not a grief. But the power would also understand that the absence of pain did not make harm impossible. A film could be killed without ever fearing death. A patch could lose its last suitable surface without having hoped for another. Benevolence would not require suffering as a credential.

So it would not warm the water, quicken the light, add a current, or bring in animals to make the place more visibly alive. It would not make the chamber more interesting in order to love it. More growth would not necessarily be better growth; greater complexity would not necessarily be kindness. A gift that required the inhabitant to become something else would partly be a gift to the giver.

Nor would it stop the distant pumps out of piety toward whatever was natural. The cold was no less real for being partly engineered, and the lives using it would not be helped by the purity of that gesture. The power would trace the machinery’s dependencies in both directions: what the chamber received from it, what other lives the installation served, what would actually follow its silence. It would not prefer an untouched system to a touched one if the preference accomplished only destruction.

At the same time, it would not worship the present equilibrium. Stability was not proof of perfection. The chamber’s long duration did not mean that seven degrees, this clarity, and precisely these patches must be maintained forever. To seal the place against every possible change would turn a home into a specimen. The power would not make deathlessness the price of having been noticed.

Finding no present wound it could heal, and no improvement it could make without merely imposing an idea of improvement, it would alter nothing.

Its restraint would not be indifference. Indifference would not need to know the cost of added light, the chemistry of the water, or the difference between persistence and replacement. It would not bother to distinguish an old, stable sparseness from a habitat in decline. Only an exact attention could refrain so precisely.

And that attention would not become a rule made once and never revisited. If change came, the power would look again. It would distinguish change from needless destruction, and the good of the inhabitant from its own pleasure in finding everything unchanged. Against some casual, irreversible injury arising elsewhere, it would be willing to act, at the smallest sufficient scale and with regard for everything else its action touched. Against time itself, it would not make war.

For now, there would be no visible blessing. The mosses would not brighten. The stones would receive no inscription. No current would announce that the chamber had been chosen. The power would leave no evidence of itself, because it did not need the inhabitant to become evidence of its goodness.

The pale opening would continue to admit its scattered daylight. The algal surfaces would remain dark olive. In the sediment, cells would receive and release their minute supply. Far away, the pumps would labor on.

Nothing would cross the chamber. Nothing would have been improved. Nothing would have been abandoned.
