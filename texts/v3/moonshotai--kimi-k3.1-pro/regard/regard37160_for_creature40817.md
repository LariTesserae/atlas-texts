The power would not arrive as light.

Light would have altered every hunt, opened some bodies to danger and closed others, sent the gecko deeper behind the bark and changed the meaning of the pools. Nor would it come as rain simply because rain is easily mistaken for kindness. A seed might be released by it; a spore might rot; a nest might soften; water might stand where an animal had learned to find dry ground. In a place made of local differences, a universal blessing could become difficult to distinguish from a catastrophe.

So it would begin with attention, and remain there longer than benevolence usually finds comfortable.

It would learn that the basin could not answer with one voice. The turtle’s desire for water would not be the sedge’s, nor the sedge’s the seed’s waiting in dry clay. The mammal’s safety would not be the snake’s hunger. The standing dead tree would be one thing to the fungi opening it, another to the beetles, another to the seedlings above, and none of these accounts would be false. The power would not average them into a single interest and call that the good of the whole.

Neither would it decide that, because every life passed into other lives, no individual life mattered. The small mammal drinking at the seep would not be merely a route by which water and nutrients moved through the system. For the length of its life, it would be someone to whom the night was happening. Its fear would not be a defect in the basin, and its eventual death would not become beautiful merely because ants, fungi, and roots could make use of what remained.

The power would therefore refuse two easy forms of kindness. It would not cherish the whole by looking away from the parts. It would not cherish one part by demanding that the whole be rearranged around it.

It would not make the mammal fearless, since fear was among the ways the animal knew the world. It would not abolish the snake’s hunger, silence every parasite, fix every pool at its fullest, or preserve each tree at the moment before its wood began to open. Such gifts would not save the basin. They would replace it with a place whose first principle was the power’s inability to bear loss.

But neither would it make a sacred rule of never touching anything. That, too, could become a form of self-regard: a wish to remain innocent rather than helpful. A trapped body might still be freed. A wound might still be closed. Pain might still be eased when easing it did not require the power to appoint itself author of every surrounding life. Its interventions would be as local as the basin’s own addresses: this root, this seep, this body, this night. It would act without requiring its action to become a law.

If greater power were needed, it would look first beyond the basin. There its restraint need not become passivity. Against the severing of the hidden water, the poisoning that entered no food web as food, the clean cut made across all the rounded divides, or a change in rain and heat too rapid for roots and routes to answer, it could be firm. It would not defend every present arrangement. Channels must still shift; pools must still fail; colonies must still collapse; some waiting seeds must never wake. What it would defend is the basin’s capacity to revise itself—to suffer changes without having all its futures decided elsewhere.

That night, however, there would be no such work to do.

The mammal would drink, freeze at its private perception, and withdraw. The power would not follow it to its nest. Some knowledge would be only another way of entering. Ants would cross the damp impressions of its feet. The turtle would breathe. Somewhere a hunt would end, and somewhere else one would not begin.

The power would leave no warmed stone, no improbably lasting puddle, no mark of protection on a tree. It would not call its restraint a blessing. Restraint was simply what power owed to a world that had not asked for it.

Then it would go, before its goodness became another presence the basin had to accommodate.

A drop, delayed from leaf to leaf, would fall onto lower foliage with its small, deliberate tick. Nothing would answer. The heat would remain. The basin would not know that it had been considered, and would not have been improved by knowing.

It had not been saved. It had not been abandoned. It had been allowed to remain larger than the power’s idea of helping it.
