## The Attendance

The power did nothing at first.

It had learned that help offered before attention often makes the helped resemble the helper. So it did not silence the engines, call rain out of season, or fill the aluminium trays with soft soil. It did not enter the central column, whose nature it did not understand. It read the warning beside the throat and kept clear of the opening.

When it took enough form to be present, it sat in the empty chair. The cup shivered against its brass chain. The roar passed through the power without becoming music.

At first, it thought the inhabitants should be taken elsewhere.

The thought was kindly meant. Elsewhere there could be footing that did not tremble, water that did not have to be awaited, air in which a small wet animal could cross more than the width of a leaf. There could be calls and answers. There could be ground in which roots were not required to stitch together rubber dust, old wings, and metal oxide.

But the power stayed.

It saw a fern remain furled through a long dryness while its roots held fast around the edge of a cassette. It felt a cricket’s courtship travel along a pipe, not as a diminution of the engines’ vibration but as a pattern made legible within it. It watched a spider distinguish the struggles of one moth from the tremor that had preceded the moth and would continue after it. Inside a gauge, a snail woke beside the needle and resumed a curve of grazing no wider than the painted division between two pressures.

To carry the inhabitants away one by one would be to preserve specimens and lose the relations that had shaped them. To carry them away together would require carrying the heat, the height, the vibration, the condensation, and the long intervals between water. The power could imagine doing this. It could not honestly call it rescue.

Its wish to give the crickets hearing, the frogs a lake, and the ferns gentle ground gradually left it. The wish had been kind. It had also been about another room.

None of this taught the power that every loss in the Sump was necessary. It did not admire a drain for taking eggs, or suppose that a failed clutch was ennobled by the persistence of the filter beds. A place was not good merely because life had found a way to continue in it. Between the eagerness to remake the Sump and the eagerness to admire it exactly as it was, there remained the possibility of useful work.

The power found some.

High in the old baffle mechanism, where no root or film could make use of it, a small fracture was growing. It would not stop the engines. It would not even stop the warmth. It would only prevent one bank of baffles from completing its next movement. There would be no roof rain at the end of that cycle.

The machinery would eventually be attended to. But the failure would arrive first, and pass downward through gutter, junction box, filter felt, and cup. Moss could wait. Ferns could remain furled. Snails could seal themselves to glass. Not everything could wait long enough.

So the power did not make the baffles incorruptible, or decree that nothing in the Sump should ever again break. It arranged for the maintenance arm, at its next emergence, to replace one part a little before the part broke.

The arm’s additional movement took twelve seconds. No gauge in the Sump changed. The birds did not alter their turns.

That was for the room.

Then the power looked into the tin cup.

The water there had diminished to a shallow, bright lens. The eggs were still translucent, but their centres had darkened. Far above, the baffles were moving as they should; the repaired mechanism would keep its appointment. The cup, however, would be dry before the condenser combs were wet.

The power brought no strange water from elsewhere. It took condensation already gathering on a cold copper eyebrow above the window and let it run, one drop at a time, down the brass chain. It did this only when the surface in the cup fell. There was no taste of miracle in the water, no new property in it. It had simply not gone to the drain.

The power did not hatch the eggs or promise them froglets. It merely kept the ending from arriving before the possibility.

When the rain came, the power was still sitting in the chair.

For eight minutes the Sump became populous in the open. Moths lifted from beneath the leaves. Frogs appeared at the gutter mouths. Snails extended their horns into the wet. The two birds cut through the bright lines of water, taking insects too small for the power to trouble itself to count. One web bowed, held, and then gave way. Guano and dead wings began their slow return to the trays. Water shone on rust, glass, fern, and tin.

The power stayed until the leaves had almost stopped dripping.

Afterward, the tadpoles in the cup kept close to the bottom. They grazed the film on the tin while vibration passed continually through their small bodies. Above them, the roar remained exactly as it had been.

Before leaving, the power added no instruction plate and left no explanation beside the central column. It did not ask to be remembered by creatures whose attention was better spent on wet seams, favourable pipes, and secure anchors.

But thereafter the old maintenance contained a very slight excess of care. Once in a great while, the arm emerged and performed one task not quite required by the last inspection. A part was replaced early. A route for water remained open. Nothing in the Sump became safe in any general sense. Moths still travelled too far from the wall. Some webs failed. Some clutches dried.

The room had not been made new. It had only been kept from ending, unnecessarily, before its next slow adjustment.

Much later, a minute froglet climbed the inside of the cup. It reached the brass chain, then the chair, and rested on the old paint with every toe fastened down.

The power was gone, or at least had no shape there. The chair was empty. The roar pressed at the froglet’s body, and the froglet held on.

Far above, at a speed no frog could perceive, the baffles had begun their next movement.
