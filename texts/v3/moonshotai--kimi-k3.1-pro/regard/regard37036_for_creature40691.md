### The Tenth Listener

The power came to Nine-Spout Hollow without making the water louder.

For a while it only listened. Nothing was drowned out for it: not the thin knocking inside the fourth spout, not roots entering a stone joint, not cord being twisted under cover of a long story. It heard a young person on the lower path hoping never to return, an old person wishing for one dry night, and a trader silently resolving to give short measure. It heard the market frog. It heard the much smaller frogs beneath the leaves. It heard the marsh taking water into itself.

It had intended, perhaps, to give a gift.

An uncomplicated benevolence might have dried the houses, made the timbers proof against rot, cleared the leaves before they reached the grates, and caused all nine spouts to run evenly forever.

The power followed each gift downstream.

It did not conclude that damp was holy, or that cold work was good for the soul. A leaking roof was still a leaking roof. There was nothing virtuous in a sickness, a shirked duty, or a household forced to sleep wet because another household had been careless. But neither could it improve the hollow by loving only its roofs, or only its people, or only the pleasing idea of people and forest living together.

Every blessing had a lower course.

To silence the water would be to take away the messages by which small failures were found. To fix every level forever would be to make a keepsake of a place whose inhabitants still needed to alter it. To make the fallen wood imperishable would be to stand, eventually, in a crowded house wondering where the mushrooms and young trees had gone. And to preserve the Hollowers exactly as they were would have required not listening to those who wanted privacy, dry grain, a different trade, or a life somewhere else.

So its first act was to refrain.

Its second was to stay through a storm.

When the hollow-beam sounded, it went out with the others in a body that could be cold. It carried a pole. It was not immediately useful. Power is not the same as skill. It pulled when it should have lifted, stood where the hooked branch needed to swing, and was told without ceremony to move. When the packed roots came free and the water broke through, it stepped back too slowly and was soaked like everyone else.

Afterward, while capes were being wrung out and someone was explaining at unnecessary length why the blockage had not been their fault, one of the spout listeners sat beside it.

“You’re the one who has been listening,” she said.

The power did not deny it.

“Well,” she said, “don’t stop the rain. I need to know whether the new notch holds.”

She considered the matter further.

“Listen where we can’t. Under the wall. Under the springs. Where the ground begins something before we can hear it. If trouble is coming from there, tell us. Give us time if you can.”

She paused.

“And come plainly. We have enough noises to interpret.”

The power took this as good local advice.

It made no covenant above the intakes and left no object that had to be kept dry. It did not promise an eternal town, obedient water, or a people who would henceforth deserve their own good opinion of themselves. It set its attention beneath the reach of their hands: in the deep fractures of the rock, the buried sources of the springs, the slow weight of the saturated shoulders. Where warning could help, it would warn them in ways they could examine rather than merely obey. Where time could be given, it would give time. If harm ever came that no gathering of households with poles and matting could meet, it would come too.

The rest it left inconveniently unfinished.

People continued to favor their cousins and exaggerate the labor they had done during previous storms. Children continued to release bark boats where bark boats should not have been released. Grates blocked. Pegs lifted. A hated neighbor sometimes arrived first, and a beloved cousin sometimes stayed in bed. The dead were remembered in new treads and sound basin rims. Some young people left. Some returned. The spouts received new use-names, and the power learned them without insisting on the old ones.

Occasionally, on a very wet night, there seemed to be one more worker at a channel than had come out of the nearby houses. This was never established to anyone’s satisfaction. The matter did not become important enough for a shrine.

Most often the power did nothing at all.

When the beam sounded, it listened while a kitchen conversation stopped, a bowl was put down, and feet began to strike the boards. It did not confuse love with taking over. It did not clear a grate merely because it could, or mistake the endurance of the hollow for the only good that mattered to those living in it.

It had not promised that Nine-Spout Hollow would never end. It had promised something narrower and more exact.

If hands were already coming, it let them come.

If none could, it answered.
