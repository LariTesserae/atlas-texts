## A Tolerance

The power did not arrive as light. In the Low Engine, an unaccounted light might have been answered by switchgear.

For a long time, it only attended.

It remained through enough phases to learn that the roar was not a cry, that the darkness concealed no grief, and that water running uphill was not a wound in the order of things. It did not mistake sparseness for poverty, or antiquity for suffering. Nothing beneath the causeways was waiting for natural ground. Nothing in the condenser fields was praying for morning.

The felt took longer to understand than the turbines.

The power had to attend at the scale of active margins, to distinguish a living stain from a repair and a broad, mineralized cushion from the thin tissue still flourishing at its edge. It watched patches contract before scour and open when the current gentled. It followed spores into intakes, onto bare slag, into water whose chemistry offered them nothing. It saw that the resident’s good was neither permanence nor abundance, but recurrence: a suitable difference, enduring long enough; a seam; another seam, elsewhere, in which life might begin again without any visible connection to the last.

The power could have stopped the pumps. It could have warmed the pans, put soil over the composite, filled the darkness with wetlands and wings. It could have made the felt resistant to cold, abrasion, and altered flow. It could have preserved every existing patch exactly where it was.

Each kindness would have replaced what had drawn its attention.

To make the Low Engine richly habitable would have been to end the Low Engine and call the ending care. To make the felt ubiquitous would have been to love an image of life more than this particular life. To guarantee every cushion against loss would have turned growth, contraction, dispersal, and death into the maintenance of a specimen.

Nothing here needed consolation. Something here could be spared.

So the power altered no purpose and no body. What it left was not a law, but a tolerance.

Across the works, within the immense exactitude of pressures and clearances, there were small equivalences. Two routes by which a maintenance carriage could reach the same intake. Two positions in which an arm could clear it equally well. Two sound ways of laying a repair across a fracture, one passing through an old seam and one beside it. Until then, nothing had valued the difference.

Where such an equivalence existed, and only there, the power placed a slight preference: leave the living margin.

No pump would be required to falter for it. No intake would remain blocked for the sake of spores. No necessary fracture would go unsealed. If there was no room within the works’ proper operation, the works would proceed as before.

The carriages did not become gardeners. The pumps did not become guardians. The felt acquired no sacred status, no indicator lamp, no immunity from time. Gradients could still fail. Cold could still narrow it. Abrasion could still strip it back to dead filaments and bound oxides. Nearly every spore would still come to nothing.

The power had not promised survival. It had only removed one kind of unnecessary loss: erasure where leaving life alone would have cost nothing.

Then it withdrew.

It did not remain to make its kindness certain. Certainty would have summoned intervention after intervention, each small, each reasonable, until there was no longer a resident persisting within the works, but a possession continually rescued from them.

Long afterward, a maintenance carriage crossed a pan toward a fractured transfer deck. Its lights showed brief pieces of wet ceramic, a submerged rail, the grey seam already mixed for the repair. Living felt lay along one side of the fracture, contracted to dark cords beneath the flow.

The carriage did not pause to investigate it. No jointed arm touched it. Nothing about the machine’s movement became tender or hesitant. It laid the repair by the second of two equally sound paths, completed its task, and passed on. Fresh grey material brightened the fracture for a little while.

The seam beside it remained open.

The felt there was not safe. The next operational cycle might cool the warm leakage beyond use. A later repair might have no alternative. Sediment might fill the seam, or a hard current might strip it clean. Nothing had been promised except that it had not been destroyed when its destruction was unnecessary.

At the next phase change, layers of noise dropped away. The nearby water smoothed into black glass. In the seam, minute grey surfaces opened to the gentler current. Spores entered the water, and nearly all of them went, as before, toward intakes, bare mineral, and nothing.

Then pressure returned. Water boomed in the culverts. The felt drew itself back into dark cords, and the roar closed over the causeways.

Nothing in the Low Engine looked altered.

But beneath the water, in a seam that had not been closed, something living waited for the next quiet.
