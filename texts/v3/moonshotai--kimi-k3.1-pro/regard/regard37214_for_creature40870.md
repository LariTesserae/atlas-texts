### What the Power Did

The power did not arrive in glory. Glory would have cast a shadow, and in Vey even a shadow changes the water.

For a long time, it did nothing that could be distinguished from listening.

It looked first for the being among the people, because they had laws, memories, and names for the wells. But the people were plainly not the whole of what lived there. Their own service slates admitted as much. A pressure reading could be incomplete without bronze shells beneath a gate; a repair could depend on whether eels were migrating or merely numerous; a silent animal on an outer pier could know something before the instruments did.

The power considered the water. But a sealed cistern could be full without being alive in Vey’s sense, and a living bypass could be dry for days without ceasing to belong to the route. It considered the engines, the microbial films, the brief bodies of fleas, the eels behind glass. Each was necessary to something. None was necessary in quite the way a center is necessary.

At last it stopped looking for a central body.

What had drawn its attention was not a spirit concealed in the circulation, waiting to be discovered and praised. It was the continuing, imperfect passage among bodies: mineral into shell, seepage into film, film into flea, flea into eel, eel into wader or keeper, waste into darkness, darkness into food. Even the people’s best law was only a way of saying that no one, however thirsty, was entitled to mistake their portion for the whole.

The power understood then that the worst gift it could offer was completion.

It could have cooled the white sun, made the engines unfailing, filled every pan from a source no drought could touch. It could have preserved Vey exactly as it found it. But to preserve only what was visible would have been to love a census taken at noon. An inexhaustible gift can become another sealed passage: after it, nothing needs to be noticed, because nothing is allowed to depend on notice.

So its first act was restraint.

Restraint alone, however, was not yet care. A power can leave a place untouched merely because it does not wish to become responsible for it. Nor did the power make a doctrine of Vey’s balance. Some losses were only losses. The clarity of the water did not make them good, and no grief became necessary because something small fed afterward in the dark.

Where a hurt could be eased without simply moving it into a margin the power had not yet examined, it eased the hurt. This was seldom simple. It did not mind being slow.

Its one lasting alteration lay far below the town.

There, beneath the deepest well and the oldest human record, it found a fracture sealed by mineral growth. On either side were separate provinces of that deep, patient life which might wait years between useful seepages. Nothing in present Vey required the fracture to be open. No flea could give warning of its closure; no wader would change its route because of it; no keeper could wake and hear that this particular sound was missing.

The power opened the fracture by the width of a thread.

It added no water. It applied no pressure. It did not arrange a future emergency in order to make its gift admirable. It merely left a way by which, under conditions it did not control, one region might someday answer another.

The gift was not abundance. It was not immunity. It was one more possibility of change without ending.

Then the power set a limit upon itself. It would not become Vey’s owner, its necessary miracle, or the new center around which every route had to turn. It would accept no gratitude as a toll. It would remain near enough to hear a silence change, and far enough that nothing in Vey would have to live for its attention.

No inscription appeared. The oldest building law gained no exception for gods.

At the next Turning, the condenser fins darkened. Moisture gathered on their surfaces, then on roofs, pipe bridges, and tower rims. Eels came to the gate mouths. Crabs turned toward the coming flow. Waders stood above channels that had been empty a minute before, and keepers came to their doors with tools, cups, or nothing in their hands.

In the west field, a keeper wrote on her slate:

*second gate slow; bronze shells beneath it; eels thick in the west bypass; wader on the outer pier.*

She added nothing else. There was nothing else to see.

Far below, where no Turning had a name, a little water entered the opened fracture, paused against the dark, and went on.
