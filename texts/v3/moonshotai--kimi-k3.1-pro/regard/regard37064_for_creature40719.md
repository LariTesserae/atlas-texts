### What the Power Left Undone

The power came without shadow or warmth.

A shadow would have cooled one basin more than another. Warmth would have drawn water toward the sky. Even a footfall, however gently intended, would have entered the deep work as load. So its first act of benevolence was to make no place for itself in the Catchment.

For a long while, it did not know whether there was anything there that benevolence could reach.

Its first impulse was to make the highland live. It could have darkened the joints with moss, seeded the still reservoirs, filled the shafts with blind and delicate animals. It could have made the white escarpments generous to roots. But that would only have made the Catchment hospitable to kinds of life it already knew how to pity. The clear water would have become fertile; the slow mineral routes would have been clogged, acidified, fed upon. The power left the water clear.

Then it considered repair.

It could have straightened the wandering courses and replaced the three thin pieces with one sound block. It could have cleaned the lime from the spillways and opened every seam narrowed by white deposits. The highland would have gleamed with a more durable perfection.

It saw in time that this would have been a curious form of vandalism. A delay might live in those three thin courses. Two distinct keepings might owe their separateness to a line of masonry wandering by a thumb’s width. A deposit that looked like neglect from above might be the last still-active consequence of a rain older than the exposed shell. The power put away its wish to tidy.

Preservation tempted it longest.

It could have held each living arrangement at its proper fullness. It could have kept every necessary constriction from closing and every distinction from merging into flood. Nothing would have been lost.

But nothing next would have depended on the last thing, except by permission. The keepings would have continued as maintained shapes, their water replaced, their routes assured, their histories excused from acting. The power understood that it could preserve them exactly and, in doing so, make their history stop mattering. That was the Catchment’s description of death.

It did not give them awareness, either. It would have been easy to mistake that refusal for coldness. But to give a keeping a knowledge of its own ending, merely so that its continuation could become hope and its destruction grief, would have been to create a need for comfort in order to enjoy providing it. There was no plea in the deep cells. The power did not manufacture one so that it could be answered.

Nor did it choose the oldest keeping and declare that this one, at least, must be spared. Age gave it more ways for the past to act; it did not make the future indebted to it. The power would not turn its own astonishment at endurance into a law against ending.

By then it had done nothing. But its nothing was no longer the nothing of passing by.

It stayed through the brief life of one small keeping above a cold underside. After rain, the basin filled. As it dried, a silver film remained at one corner. Below, at intervals no patient traveler would have connected, a drop swelled and fell. The relation lasted three seasons. During the fourth, a mineral skin altered the shallow floor. The film ceased to linger. Much later, one more drop gathered below, and then that habit also ended.

The power did not rescue it. Neither did it call the little arrangement wasted because it had not become something larger. It attended until there was nothing left to attend to except wet stone, and it did not confuse that stone with a body abandoned by life.

This was all the companionship the place could receive without being changed: to be known without being needed, regarded without being made grateful.

Before withdrawing, the power did one thing. It closed the way by which its attention had come. It left no map, no blessing likely to attract seekers, no account that could become a claim. Its discovery would not become the Catchment’s first use. If others ever found the highland, they would not arrive because it had been loved loudly.

It remembered, but made no archive from which the Catchment could be reconstructed. A reconstruction might reproduce every basin and shaft and still contain none of the same delays. Its remembrance was not a plan for return. It was only a refusal to call the vanished arrangements nothing.

When it went, no rim dried. No dark line paused on a wall. The basins returned the sky with the same excessive precision, and the black shafts gave back nothing.

The power had not made the highland deathless. It had not improved its rain, purified its lime, strengthened its masonry, or appointed a guardian among its residents. It had not even promised that the possibility of keepings would always remain. Nothing had been given forever.

But nothing had been taken away to pay for the power’s love.

Far below, the disturbance of an ordinary afternoon arrived as a slight change of pressure. Moisture moved toward a seam already narrowed by lime. The seam wetted; the pressure eased; the route did not quite close.

The power had not caused this.

That was the shape its mercy had taken.
