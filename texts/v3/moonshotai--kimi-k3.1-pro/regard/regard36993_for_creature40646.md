### The Loose Knot

At first, it would do nothing the town could feel.

No second noon would open above the summit. It would not raise the mountain into air and call that rescue. The quarry would not finish itself. The seeps would not become more generous, nor the mountain more silent. A power able to alter all these things would begin by distrusting its own first idea of kindness.

More light would favor some gardens and starve others. More warmth would make certain districts wealthy and their neighbors dependent. A mountain that never spoke might be safer, or might merely have lost the warning by which masons knew to stop. Even clearing a neglected court could erase the record of why it had been neglected.

Yet it would not make the opposite mistake and worship restraint. The town was not a vessel to be kept unbroken. Hunger, illness, a bad gate, a child trapped beneath a shifted block—these would not become beautiful because the walls were old. The power would not preserve suffering as part of the view.

So it would listen, and know that listening was not yet understanding. It would wait until its understanding could be corrected by the people themselves.

Then, if contact could be refused without fear, it would make itself known at the outer marker. It would bring no gift, no light, nothing living from elsewhere. Its first message would be brief:

*I am here. I will come no closer without leave. I can go.*

Its offer would include its absence.

It would not ask for belief or worship. It would not require the town to decide quickly, or to speak with one voice about matters on which it had never spoken with one voice before. Permission from a gatekeeper would not be permission from every household; one gardener’s desire would not be consent from everyone downstream. Before any act touched shared water, it would name the act, its likely path onward, and the means of stopping it. Where it did not know, it would say so.

If told to go, it would go, without leaving behind a blessing that functioned as a debt.

If asked for help, it would give the help asked for, where it could do so honestly: hold one gate while a felt lineage was moved; carry a sick traveler; mend a crushed hand; explain what it could sense in a new fracture, including the limits of what it sensed. It would not use a crisis to finish the quarry, reconcile old households, or install itself as the final listener. It would be as willing to have its work inspected as any mason.

And if there were no time to ask, it would act. It would catch the falling block or hold killing water out of a court. Then it would explain, answer anger, and accept being asked to repair what it had done, or to go. Wisdom would not become an excuse for watching someone die merely to keep its own hands clean.

The gardener would not be made its prophet simply because they had been the first person it noticed. They would be allowed to remain a gardener: curious or indifferent, willing to speak or unwilling, with private griefs and small preferences the power did not claim to understand. Attention would not confer a destiny.

Its greatest care would be for people not yet born. No continuing gift should be impossible for them to refuse. No gate would open only to its touch. No harvest would depend on its return. No child would enter the world owing it gratitude. It would not make itself indispensable and call that love.

Perhaps, after all this, it would do a great deal for the town. Perhaps almost nothing. It would not measure the encounter by the amount of itself left behind.

In the first court, the gardener leaves the knot slightly loose. The stem will thicken. Whoever comes later should be able to free it without cutting the fiber.

The power would take that as instruction.

It would leave nothing tied so tightly to itself that later hands could be free only by cutting. Whether it stayed for a time or went away forever, the town would remain free to become something the power had not imagined.
