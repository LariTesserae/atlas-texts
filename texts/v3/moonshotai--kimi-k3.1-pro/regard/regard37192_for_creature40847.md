### What the Power Did

The power’s first mercy was to refrain.

It did not silence the pump. Nothing with ears lived beneath that noise, and the machinery’s indifference was not cruelty. It did not cool the brine, or sweeten it, or call down a storm to wash the white pans clean. It did not repair the flange. A sound repair would have been the fern’s death, neatly entered as maintenance.

Nor did it make the fern spread. A handbreadth of green was not a failed garden. The sedges were not a meadow waiting for encouragement. The hollow did not need to become fertile in order to deserve care, and its life did not need to become numerous in order to be life.

For a while, then, it seemed that the power would do nothing.

It saw the whole small arrangement: fresh water arriving as an accident of heat and metal; roots occupying a temporary correction in the chemistry; fallen stems acquiring the appearance of great age; the pump sending away nearly everything it produced. It saw also that nothing here had been promised continuance. The fern would die someday. The pump would stop someday. Stone might close over both. Wisdom did not require calling every ending an injustice.

But there was a difference between an ending and an erasure.

An ending could belong to the hollow. An erasure committed by someone who had never seen it would belong to human blindness.

So the power waited, not for a catastrophe, but for a work order.

In time, one came.

A gasket at the flange was to be replaced. The leak was slight and within tolerance; the repair could be deferred without danger. It had been scheduled largely because another job was bringing a crew nearby. The instructions were simple: isolate, unbolt, replace, seal. Nothing green was mentioned.

The fitter sent down was competent and not unkind. At the floor, the pump’s strokes struck him through his boots and chest. He set out his tools beneath the flange. He had already seen the fern, in the way one sees a stain, a scrap of wire, a discoloration at the edge of the work.

The power did not stay his hand. It put no pity into him and gave him no command.

Its whole intervention was to let a dropped nut roll a little farther than it should have.

He followed it beneath the flange and found his face close to the fern.

A drop gathered above him. He watched it fall. He saw where the water went, and where it did not go. He saw the wet cracks around the roots, the salt beginning just beyond them, the precise little shelter made by leaking water and metal shade. He understood that the repair and the fern could not both survive.

He remained free to decide that the gasket mattered more.

Instead, he sat back on his heels. Another concussion returned from the walls. He retrieved the nut, checked the leak again, and packed the new gasket away.

On the job card he wrote:

**REPAIR NOT URGENT. FERN LIVING ON DRIP. LOOK BEFORE SEALING.**

He was not sure why he wrote *look before* rather than *do not*. Perhaps because he knew that someday the repair might be necessary. Perhaps because he did not trust himself to prescribe the whole future on the strength of one plant. In any case, the sentence was accurate.

Then he climbed out.

He did not become the hollow’s guardian. He did not return every season with water, or give the fern a name, or mistake his unease for a vocation. No fence was built. No sign declared the place significant. The pump neither knew nor needed to know what had happened.

The job card might be lost. The next person might look and decide differently. The power did not make the future obedient.

It had not made the fern safe. It had made it visible.

The pump drove on. Fresh water gathered, fell, and met a few roots. The sedges held their transparent tips above the crust. Beneath the flange, the fern remained no broader than a hand.

And the metal, for a little longer, went on failing at approximately the right rate.
