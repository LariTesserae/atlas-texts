### What the Power Did

It came without light. There were birds sleeping in the shafts, and there was already a lamp beside the unfinished repair.

For a while, it did nothing.

This was not because the Undercroft was perfect, or because everything that happened there was necessary. Nests failed without teaching anything. Animals were injured by accidents that had no meaning. A keeper could be crushed by a falling basket as easily as a fern could be spared by a patient repair. The power did not mistake the keepers’ acceptance of loss for consent to every loss, or their restraint for a law that no one else should ever act.

But neither did it begin by improving the room.

It could have given the frogs water that never dwindled, the doves nests that never failed, the keepers masonry that never cracked. It could have made the fig harmless and let it become a tree. It could have driven out the snake, silenced the swifts during working hours, and softened every ladder fall.

Each mercy would have required another decision, and then another: which wetness should remain, which joints should stay open, which hunger should be thwarted, whose convenience would become the shape of the walls. To bless any tenant completely would have been to make an enemy of another. To preserve the chamber exactly would have been to choose one moment in its long practice of alteration and forbid all the rest.

So the power listened until its wish to help no longer needed to look immediately like help.

It learned the seven shafts by their weather. It learned that a marked niche could delay necessary work without making the delay a failure, and that the fig was loved most honestly by those who cut it back. It heard the oldest keeper distinguish, beneath the general noise, the hollow knocking of a basket against a ledge. It watched a fallen swift receive the wall again without ever learning whether the bird survived.

Nothing in this instructed the power to remain forever passive. It only made haste seem less wise than it had at first.

Then it found something no amount of acceptance would have improved.

High in the vault, where a newer rib had been built into an older wall, a fracture had begun deep inside the brickwork. It started at a peg socket and ran inward, beyond the reach of eye or smoothing bone. No keeper had neglected it. No bird had caused it. For the present, the rib held.

Given enough rain and enough ordinary vibration, it would not bring down the whole Undercroft. It would do something smaller: release a burden of brick and tile through a tier of occupied niches, onto whatever happened to be working or sheltering below. Afterward there would be repairs. There would be fresh plaster, new nests, absences noted and investigated. Much of the place’s practice would continue.

The power saw no reason to prefer that outcome merely because something durable might emerge from it.

It held the fracture closed.

The pressure did not stir dust. The doves did not shift. The night keeper, listening for the next drop, heard nothing unusual.

That was the greater part of the intervention. The lesser part came the following morning, when the power allowed the damaged rib to be heard.

A wooden mallet struck a peg near the stair. Baskets knocked. The swifts made their high stitching above the doves. Through the ordinary return of sound from the vault came one flat, hollow note, clearer than brick and distance should have permitted.

The oldest keeper lowered her smoothing bone.

“Again,” she said.

The mallet struck again. A few birds clapped upward and settled. The keeper turned her head, not toward the shafts, but along the wall.

They did not find the fracture at once. Sound in the Undercroft had too many doors. A ladder was moved, then moved back. A basket was lowered and raised without being lowered all the way. An apprentice suggested a cracked tile; someone else suggested an empty jar. The oldest keeper sat apparently idle for much of the morning, which was how everyone involved knew that serious work was being done.

By dusk they had found enough to know she was right.

The repair could not proceed from the nearest ledge because a strand of dyed fiber crossed a niche beside it. Nor could the vent above be screened, even while loose material was being cleared. The keepers chose a longer approach. They rigged a second rope, spilled plaster once, and were cursed by a dove whose ledge they had temporarily made useless. Work that might have been finished in two days took eleven.

For all eleven, the power held the rib.

It did not hurry the nestlings. It did not make the rope lighter or reward the keepers with an easy discovery. When the fresh patch had set, visible as a red-grey interruption among the ochre and chalk, it let go.

No one noticed.

The keepers credited the oldest keeper’s ear. She credited the apprentice who had finally opened the plaster in the right place. The apprentice, who had opened three wrong places first, credited luck. All three accounts were true enough to be useful.

The power left no name by which it could be thanked and no rule by which it could be obeyed. It did not ask for a niche. It did not make the old keeper’s hearing permanent, or promise that every hidden failure would announce itself. Most especially, it did not confuse what it had done with the work itself. The work remained in the hands that had climbed, listened, argued, waited, and laid the patch.

Below the second ledge, the snake continued to hunt. The fig put out another sideways shoot. A web was removed from a ladder and another left undisturbed across an empty darkness. After rain, the frogs became implausibly numerous by voice and then seemed to vanish as the little puddles dried.

At midnight, a drop fell into the old basin and struck the clay where centuries of water had worn it white.

The splash returned from the roof. The doves shifted together. The keeper looked up, heard nothing further, and did not invent a warning.

The power listened with them.

That was all it had done: held one danger long enough for it to become knowable, and made it knowable without making itself the master of the response. Not endless rain. Not painless brick. Not harmony.

A little more time for care to be practiced, and then the care left, rightly, in other hands.
