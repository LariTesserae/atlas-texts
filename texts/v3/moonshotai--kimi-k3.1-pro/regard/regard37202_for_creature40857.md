### A Catastrophe with No History

The power would begin by refusing the two easiest miracles: light and silence.

Both would flatter a visitor before they benefited the basin. Light poured into every chamber would not merely reveal the place; it would revise it. The green halos would spread beyond the lamps. Algae, moss, and roots would alter the films and pools, while other lives, adjusted to darkness and chemical pasture, would retreat or disappear. The bats’ roost and emergence would cease to be the same roost and emergence. What looked from above like illumination would feel below like an invasion.

Silence would be just as easy to misunderstand. The pumps are not simply an insult inflicted on the rock. Their water goes upward, to people who also drink, wash, irrigate, and suffer when a reservoir fails. To stop every machine and call the resulting quiet innocence would only be to move the cost somewhere the power had chosen not to look.

So it would arrive without radiance and without command. No chamber would change. For a long time, nothing would happen that a maintenance crew could have entered in a log.

The power would listen.

It would listen until the general roar separated into its labours and accidents: water over travertine, the worn note of a bearing, air in the fans, the loose pipe knocking through stone. Beneath and between these it would attend to smaller events—the shift of sediment under an amphipod, a crab withdrawing beneath a ledge, the continuous dry unrest of the bat ceiling, the overflow finding a new weakness in a rim.

And its attention would become particular.

In one central pool, between two low dams, it would notice a pale fish holding station in the current. Not the species only, not the food web, not an emblem of fragility: this animal, turning toward the close disturbance of prey, its effective world bounded by water it could not see as a visitor sees water.

The power would not pity the fish for its reduced eyes. It would not fill its mind with landscapes, or give it a longing for the plateau. The fish’s life was not a defective version of a human life. Nor would the power assume that the snail’s few pool rims made a small existence, or that the brittleness of new calcite was a failure to become proper floor.

It would recognise the essential thing: the Lower Basin was not a finished place that had suffered damage. It was ground still being made, with life continually finding room on its moving surfaces.

But the power would not confuse patience with invulnerability.

A mineral rim thickens slowly enough for a seep to migrate, for a grazing surface to shift, for a community to move a few centimetres and continue. Human decisions can arrive at another speed. A channel can be drained in a working season. A gallery can be lit in a week. Vibration can be driven through rock that has no way to organise a response. The basin’s slowness would not make it safe; it would only make some injuries difficult to see from the surface.

The pumps, therefore, would not be treated as villains. Neither would they be treated as neutral merely because they were useful. The crews would not be cast as despoilers. Most of them would remain what they had been: people doing necessary work inside a map made of access, footing, gauges, and machinery.

The power’s intervention would concern that map.

When the next substantial alteration was proposed—an efficient intake, a shortened route, a straightforward programme of work with the water neatly represented by lines on drawings—the power would not strike the proposal down. It would not put terror into the planners or make their pens refuse to sign.

It would give one of them a moment of accurate imagination.

For a few breaths, that person would understand what the drawing omitted. Not a vision of paradise, and not an oracle commanding preservation, but the actual crowded consequence of the work: which films would dry, which pools would lose their overflows, where vibration would run, what the roost imported, how far a fish could go when its pool ceased to be connected to anything. At the same time, they would understand the surface need just as clearly. There would be no comfortable villain and no costless choice.

Then the perception would be withdrawn.

The planner would be left with what seemed, in retrospect, like an ordinary professional doubt:

*I have not actually seen what I am asking to change.*

They could still have signed. That possibility would matter.

Instead, they would go below. The first inspection would probably disappoint them. From the route, the halls would still look largely deserted. The pumps would still overwhelm speech. The fish would be hard to find, and no chamber would declare its own importance. Only when someone held a lamp close to the sediment, or waited beside a pool without immediately moving on, would the drawing begin to fail.

What followed would not be miraculous. That would be part of its value.

The intake would still be built, but not by treating a chain of living pools as temporarily inconvenient storage. Work would be staged. Water levels would be watched at the places where life actually encountered them, not only at the station gauges. Where vibration served no purpose, new machinery would be isolated from the rock. The western roost would acquire times and distances that maintenance had to respect. Lamps would remain local. The old intention along the Hot Fault—the gauge, the ceramic insulator, the route imagined beyond them—would at last be allowed to expire.

The surface supply would continue. So would breakdowns, shortages, arguments over cost, and imperfect compromises. No one involved would become a saint. On the plateau, the project would be remembered, if at all, as troublesome and somewhat more expensive than expected.

Below, there would be no history of salvation.

A catastrophe prevented before it acquires witnesses looks very much like nothing.

For the pale fish, nothing visible would have been done. It would continue to hold station, take small animals from the water, and respond to disturbances that no planner could feel. It would not become immortal. One day its life would end, and what remained of it would enter the same busy economy of scavengers, sediment, and recirculating matter as everything else.

The power would not regard that as a failure. It had not been trying to exempt one animal from the conditions of life. It had been preventing the water, films, dams, and shelters on which that life depended from being treated as blank space.

Afterwards, the power would leave no sign of itself. Certainly no inscription; the water would eventually glaze it. No eternal lamp, no voice in the pump house, no new law written into the instincts of crabs. It would not require gratitude from creatures incapable of knowing what had been contemplated on their behalf, or obedience from the people who had chosen, with incomplete knowledge, to do somewhat better.

The basin would remain loud, though some of its needless vibration would be gone. It would remain dim, warm, wet, and difficult to cross. Clothing would still not dry. New crusts would still break under a boot. Snails would still leave pale trails through the films; guano would still feed the ledges; the pumps would still lift water toward people who might never learn the names of the chambers beneath them. Calcite would continue around the rails and stakes. A seep would move. A pool would find another overflow.

A later visitor could be forgiven for thinking that no power had ever been there.

The power would not have saved the Lower Basin from change, suffering, rockfall, or time. It would not have made it safe, pristine, or complete. It would have done something narrower and more exact.

It would have kept human haste from masquerading as necessity.

And it would not have finished the Lower Basin. It would have left the place, and the lives upon its rising surfaces, free to go on becoming.
