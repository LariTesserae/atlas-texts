## What the Power Would Do

At first, it would watch long enough for its categories to fail.

It would not arrive with thunder. There are roof gardens that could be startled out of their moorings. It would not arrive in blaze or uncovered flame. It would come, if it came visibly at all, as gently as the gray clarification before dawn, and it would stay through mist-rise, rain, departure, and repair.

Only then would it begin to know where not to put its hands.

It would see the temptations of help. An unfailing spring would not merely fill the jars; it would alter everything that gathered around water, everything that hunted there, and perhaps the careful manners by which people had learned to live among them. Houses made permanently square would be houses whose inhabitants could slowly forget how to open them. Gardens commanded to remain *held* would no longer be invitations. They would be possessions with roots. Even the hunters could not simply be subtracted without declaring human comfort the measure of the cleft.

To preserve the place by making it incapable of becoming anything else would be to keep its outline and lose its life.

But the power would not make a religion of leaving things alone.

The people had learned to endure; that did not mean endurance was all they deserved. An infected cut would not be beautiful because the people treated it pragmatically. A death beneath fresh stone would not become necessary because the houses had always been repairable. A gardener’s ability to lash a new tray would not make the departure of the old garden painless.

The power would not confuse the end of suffering with the end of change. Neither would it call every suffering the necessary price of change.

Eventually, therefore, it would make itself available to be asked—and refused.

Not with a command from the strip of sky. Perhaps only as a visitor sitting at the edge of the common court, still tired from the stairs, willing to be doubted. Its questions would be practical:

*What hurts that need not?*

*What would help?*

*What must I not touch?*

*How will I know when to stop?*

The nineteen roofs would not answer with one voice. They would argue, favor their relations, suspect a trick, ask for incompatible things, and change their minds. The power would stay for the argument. It would not require universal agreement before easing one person’s pain, nor treat the majority’s wish as permission to injure everything else.

Its help would be openable.

To a child with a festering hand, it might offer healing; to those who tended the child, the knowledge and materials to treat the next wound without calling the power back. To a household beneath a shifting terrace, it might offer time—enough time to wake, lift the floor, carry out the sleepers, and choose what could be saved—not an everlasting prohibition against the stone moving. To the water-keepers it might offer a better knot, a safer way to clean a fouled vessel, or simply another pair of hands during rain.

No gift would be hidden where its recipients could not reach it. No blessing would depend upon perpetual gratitude. Nothing would be placed irrevocably in the blood of grandchildren who had never been asked.

Its care would also extend beyond the seventy human lives. A being would not have to be useful, constant, or classifiable before the power resolved not to crush it thoughtlessly. It would not demand that a colony explain itself in terms of harvest, or that a green-backed creature promise to remain either garden or animal. Where it could understand the other lives of the cleft, it would listen. Where it could not, it would not mistake their silence for consent.

It would not fetch back every garden that left. Sometimes its attention would simply follow beyond the rim, not to recover property, but because care need not end where usefulness ends.

And if a day came when the whole cleft could no longer be jacked, lashed, drained, or climbed—when repair had finally met something larger than repair—the power would not demand that the people remain there to prove the purity of its restraint. It would help them go. Somewhere beyond the cloud, it would make room: not a finished second town, not a paradise requiring a new obedience, but a place where a few carried knots, jars, slabs, and hard-won habits might be enough to begin. They could refuse that too. An offer with a punishment for refusal would be only another cliff.

In the cleft itself, there might be almost nothing to show that the power had ever acted. No throne in the court. No twentieth roof. No spring. Perhaps only an ordinary length of cord hanging into the public cistern, replacing one that had rotted unnoticed.

It would not make the cord everlasting.

In time it would fray. Someone would swear softly, cut it away, and tie another in its place, perhaps without ever knowing where the first had come from. The power would not be offended. The replacement would be the gift continuing without it.

And on some later morning, when a person whose garden had departed set out a shallow tray, arranged a little shade, and provided for one dependable drop of water, the power would not fill the tray with something tame and permanent.

It would recognize the gesture.

Its blessing would not be *stay*.

It would be: *You need not remain what you are to remain worthy of care. You may refuse me. You may leave. And if you choose to begin again, there will be room—and you need not begin alone.*
