## What the Power Did

The power did not silence the Undermere when it arrived.

That was the first wise thing it did.

Beside a throat, sudden silence meant that the pressure below was turning. It sent people away from the mouth and made herds lift their heads from the stone. A silence across the whole basin would not have sounded like peace. It would have sounded as though every old rule had broken at once.

So the roar remained.

The power did not clear the cloud, either, or warm the patient rain. It did not lay soil over the limestone and sow reeds around the pools, though it could see how easily that might have been mistaken for kindness. There would have been green visible from a distance. There would have been stems to show the wind. Meanwhile, buried films would have failed, seeps would have altered, and animals adapted to bare mineral and cold chemistry would have become scarce in a country that looked, to a passing eye, more alive.

It understood that the Undermere was hard to live in without being a mistake.

For a long while, therefore, it only listened.

Gradually the roar ceased to be one sound. Beneath it were the smaller hisses, the hollow movement of water, the tick of shell-legged waders along a margin. A slateback herd passed beyond sight, its slow coordinating strikes travelling through limestone. The young answered lightly. Around a seep, blind eels tightened their knot against the current. Behind a hide curtain, a child laughed with the complete disregard for noise that children indoors could afford.

Nothing in the basin asked to be made into another basin.

At last the power turned toward one small amber point beside a seep.

The woman who lived there was named Sef. She was not the last of her people, or their chief, or the oldest thing the power had noticed. For the moment she lived alone. Her household’s other members had gone with a herd movement and would return by a longer route, if the pans allowed.

Sef sat beside a fat lamp with nine pebbles arranged on a flat stone. She moved one, frowned, and moved it back.

The power, which had made itself able to be cold, tired, and spoken to sharply, stood at the turned entrance and asked permission to come in.

Sef gave it the driest place near the lamp.

“Lost?” she asked.

“No,” said the power. “Are you?”

“Not yet.”

She moved the pebble again.

There was a gathering several days away at a dependable spring. Sef had meant to attend the last two and had not. Her knees had become unreliable, and lately her memory had begun to skip: not whole routes, but small joins between them. A turn omitted. A cairn expected too early. The sort of errors a person could correct easily in company and disastrously alone.

That was not the whole of her trouble.

Years before, she had taught a western path to people from three households. Since then, one of its cairns had vanished into silt. A pan near its far end had softened. Travelers had brought conflicting reports of a throat whose breath seemed to have changed. Sef knew some of this at first hand and some only by cautious inference.

“I taught the path,” she said. “Now I need to go and unteach parts of it.”

The power looked at the pebbles. “You could send a warning.”

“I have. Two versions. One was wrong.”

“Ah.”

“Being nearly right is very contagious.”

She began to put the pebbles away, then stopped with three still in her hand.

The power said, “I will go with you, if you will have me.”

Sef examined it with the frank attention she might have given an unfamiliar stretch of clay.

“Can you be quiet outside?”

“Yes.”

“Can you follow a hand signal?”

“I can learn.”

“Can you turn back when I turn back, without standing there asking why?”

“Yes.”

“Then you may be useful.”

They left before the next fine rain.

The power did not part the fog. It did not make Sef young, or lighten the ground beneath her feet. It carried the heavier pack and left her the cord, the striking stone, and the decisions. When she tired, it took some of her weight through the cord. It did not offer to carry her as though she were another pack.

She led by touches and absences: the rounded cairn, the fractured one, a dome whose answer came hollowly through the feet. Twice she stopped because a marker she expected had not appeared. Both times the power waited while she resisted the temptation to invent it.

“Not here,” she signed the second time.

They returned to the last certain stone and took a longer way.

Once, while they were crossing low ground, a throat nearby stopped roaring.

The power could feel the immense turning of pressure beneath them. It could have remained to learn precisely what would happen next. Sef was already moving. It followed without question. They went behind a bedrock baffle and did not stay to discover whether that particular silence would have ended in inhalation, mist, or nothing dramatic at all.

On another morning they waited while slatebacks crossed ahead of them. The herd was felt before it was seen: a deep, irregular patience in the ground, then the lighter answers of the young. Grey backs emerged from the fog and vanished again. Sef rested one palm on the limestone until the last of them had passed.

The power began to understand that she was not leading it through a map she possessed. She was leading it through a conversation she knew how to continue, even when the other side changed its answer.

At the gathering, there were more voices than Sef had heard in weeks.

There were also arguments, repairs, an eel theft blamed on waders, two courtships proceeding badly, and a disagreement over stored fat that outlasted the lamp. Several people scolded Sef for travelling with so little escort. Several others began, almost immediately, to ask the stranger questions about its household and routes.

Sef interrupted them.

“First,” she said, “you must forget two things I taught you.”

She told them which cairn was gone. She told them what she had felt in the western pan and what she had only been told. She distinguished, with some impatience and considerable care, between the two. Then she described the places where her own recollection could no longer be trusted to supply a missing step.

A young traveller contradicted her about the order of two markers.

Sef went still.

Everyone nearby waited.

“Say that again,” she said.

The young traveller did.

Sef set out the pebbles she had brought. They argued over them for some time. In the end Sef moved one and said, “There. I have been carrying that wrong.”

She made the young traveller repeat the correction to the others.

By the time the gathering broke apart, the safest version of Sef’s knowledge no longer depended on Sef being right. Other people held pieces of it. They had compared those pieces, disputed them, and marked their uncertainties. A route that had existed too perfectly in one old woman’s memory had become less elegant and more useful.

When Sef prepared to leave, she did so with two households travelling past her spring. She did not make the return journey alone.

The power went with them only as far as the first cairn.

Sef never asked whether it was a spirit, an unusually helpful traveller, or some pressure in the stone that had briefly taken the shape of company. The Undermere contained stranger things. Before they separated, she thanked it by warning it which of the gathering’s clear pools was not safe to drink from.

The power accepted the warning seriously.

Afterward, no one could have pointed to a work it had left behind. No spring had been made inexhaustible. No cairn would repair itself. The throats still reversed, the clay still concealed soft ground, and slatebacks still ended at places where the mud closed without explanation.

But months later, in fog, a young traveller came to the place where a certain second cairn should have stood.

It was not there.

She had been taught that its absence was information, not an inconvenience to be walked through until the route began to resemble memory again. She stopped the person behind her with a hand on the wrist. They returned to the last firm dome and took the longer crossing.

Neither of them knew Sef’s name.

The power had not made the Undermere safe. It had helped one old woman travel far enough to correct what she knew, and had left the correction in keeping with others.

Measured against the basin, this was almost nothing. The power did not think that was the right measure.

Nothing rooted there. What endured did so by holding on, by travelling, or by being taken up again: film upon stone, eels around eels, herds through the ground, warnings through fallible hands.

The roar continued.

Behind the hide curtains, so did the voices, patiently correcting one another.
