It would not bring rain.

That would be the easiest kindness to imagine, and the first one wisdom would refuse. Water is not an unqualified good. For the minute colonies in the crust, sufficient water would be ruin; for the shrubs, it would not restore some greener life they remembered. There is no remembered abundance here. Rain would be help addressed to an idea of barrenness, not to the lives actually present.

Nor would the power quicken those lives. It would not make the shrubs flower, cause the cushions to spread, or teach the colonies a less costly chemistry. There is no impatience in them to relieve. A knee-high shrub has not been waiting centuries for the opportunity to become a tree. Its slowness is not a long postponement of some proper condition. It is the pace at which this particular life continues.

The power would not add animals to make the hollow whole, or join its separate inhabitants into a hidden harmony. Nothing has been fractured that requires such repair. The long bare intervals are not failures of communion. The absence of passage from one life to another is not a sorrow known only to a sufficiently compassionate observer.

Neither would it search for a single being capable of receiving its care. There is no spirit of the salt to address, no ancient consciousness distributed through the crust. The power would not invent one merely so that its benevolence could have someone to speak to. It would not mistake silence for a request, or for consent. It would simply attend closely enough to understand what its gifts would do.

After that, very little would remain for it to do in the hollow.

This would not require calling the place perfect. Perfection would be another inattention. A stem can be snapped. A cushion can be crushed before its living edge has thickened by anything an eye could measure. A colony can end between one grain and the next, without leaving enough difference behind to constitute evidence. The power would see all this. It would not confuse equilibrium with safety, or great age with a right to greater age.

But neither would it make the inhabitants unbreakable. It would not fix every root in its fracture, hold every colony forever at its present size, or preserve the cushion’s dull cap of pebble against every possible disturbance. To do that would be to protect a description of the hollow at the expense of its life. The place is not precious because nothing can happen in it. Small things happen, and most of them leave it nearly itself. Its persistence is not invulnerability awaiting recognition.

So the power would set down no light at the center. It would leave no warmth beyond the warmth already present, no mark upon the rim, no stone arranged to say that the hollow had been seen. It would not make the place sacred in a way that required visitors. Even a protective sign would introduce a reference to something outside the hollow, and the hollow has no need of such a reference.

If its attention began to gather other attention—helpers, witnesses, guardians, those eager to admire its restraint—it would send none of them. If some design of its own had been about to cross the basin, it would change the design. If it had intended a gift, it would keep the gift. Its clearest intervention would be in itself.

This would not be a covenant that nothing could ever end there. The power would not appoint itself the eternal opponent of every foot, fracture, and hard season. It would make no promise the inhabitants could not understand and had never required. Its care would not become a form of custody.

Then it would withdraw.

The darkness would not deepen after its departure, because its presence had added nothing to the darkness. The salt would not shine. The shrubs would continue maintaining their narrow living surfaces; the cushions, their scattered green edges. Around the pebble, almost nothing would be added, again and again. Within the flaws of the crust, the colonies would continue spending nearly all they had on remaining distinct from mineral.

Nothing there would know that it had been considered. Nothing there would need to.

The power would have done no visible good. It would have saved nothing from an imminent disaster, relieved no suffering, corrected no deficiency. Yet its restraint would not be indifference. It would have wanted the good of lives whose good could not be measured by increase, company, comfort, or renown, and it would have accepted what that wanting permitted.

It would not have improved the hollow. It would not have preserved it forever. It would simply have refused to become one more force the hollow had to survive.

And the place would go on containing no sign that wisdom had been there, and no lack of one.
