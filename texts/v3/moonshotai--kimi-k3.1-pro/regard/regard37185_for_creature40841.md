### The Pause

For a long time, the power did nothing that could be distinguished from weather.

It had been drawn there by a discrepancy: a few wet animals, each small enough to sit in a cupped hand, calling with what seemed to be the voice of a mountain. It came with the afternoon cloud. There was no radiance. The pale sunlight remained pale, and a copper-colored bee finished its work beneath a scarlet hood before the light went.

The power did not enter the garden. Even a careful presence, it thought, might crush more than it intended. It remained at the bare margin and paid attention.

It followed water from cloud to fracture, from fracture to moss, from moss to the black lip of a stone cup. It learned the borders of a frog’s world and the dry holes in which the swifts waited without appearing to exist. It watched the small herbivores carry stems into cracks. It stayed through one of the tree’s brief considerations, during which a root thickened almost imperceptibly and the shelter settled a little farther into itself.

Only then did it consider what mercy might offer.

Its first thoughts were large. The seep could be made unfailing. The small pools could be spared their ice. The pale raptors could be turned aside, the swifts assured of food, the ancient tree given shelter from every wind capable of breaking it. Even the old human work could be completed: a roof, a sound channel, a basin that would not go dry.

But each gift, examined closely, became a kind of replacement.

An unfailing seep would make a different garden. To exclude the raptors would be to decide, without asking, which lives were the true inhabitants. To protect the tree from every crushing wind would be to misunderstand the long answer it had already made. And to preserve the Crown exactly—to hold every moss cushion, every nest, every settling stone in its approved place—would be like packing a bell with earth to keep it from being damaged.

The Crown’s absences were not omissions. It did not need to be completed.

Yet the power did not make a philosophy of leaving things alone. The beauty of the whole did not make particular losses good. A tadpole dying in a shrinking pool was not comforted by the shape of a flock above it. Wisdom could not consist merely in calling every death necessary, nor benevolence in admiring pain from a sufficient height.

So it resolved to distinguish, as well as it could, between the costs by which lives remained their own and the wounds that ended their ability to answer. If it acted, it would act at the smallest sufficient scale.

For many years, there was nothing to do.

Then something came that the Crown could not answer. It did not arrive as malice. It arrived as drawings.

People below wished to build a refuge near the summit. Their purpose was not contemptible. The weather was dangerous; climbers had died on neighboring ridges. The plans showed a narrow building anchored inside the shaft, a protected path across the traverse, and a pipe from the seep so that visitors would always find clean water.

The work would occupy very little ground. By the measures used below, it was almost nothing.

No one had planned to destroy the frogs. They had simply not known where to look for them. No one had counted the swifts’ holes because, on the day the measurements were taken, the wall had appeared empty. The seep had seemed so slight that improving it could hardly matter.

The power did not shatter the rock beneath the surveyors’ feet or send their instruments spinning. It did not fill anyone with holy dread. Instead, it offered the first person to reach the rim a little of its own patience.

That was all: a pause, before he spoke.

He accepted it without knowing that anything had been offered. He sat down on the dry stone. Cloud came up through the shaft and beaded his chin. For a while nothing happened that he could have included in a report. Then the raptor passed, and the smaller lives vanished into their various silences. When it had gone, a frog called beneath the ferns. Another answered. The hollow made them difficult to locate and impossible to dismiss.

Near his boot, the surveyor noticed the shallow channel pecked beside the seep. Later, waiting for the cloud to lift, he found the hammerstone in its niche.

Someone before him had also needed water. Someone had also needed to cross the exposed rock and return alive. They had used what they found, improved a little, and left the tool where another hand could find it.

He did not become a saint, or even a particularly gentle man. He remained proud of his drawings and annoyed by the delay. But he could no longer see the upper works as harmless.

The refuge was built lower down, where the rock could bear it and where its water did not have to be taken from the garden. The pipe was omitted. The line of anchors was never drilled. His report gave several practical reasons, all defensible. He did not describe the frog chorus. He thought only that, at the rim, he had been given time to look.

That was the whole of the power’s visible work: no lightning, no inscription, no new species placed as a guard. A little time, set where a decision could still turn.

It did not make the refuge below harmless forever, or bind the surveyor’s children to his choice. It did not declare the Crown saved. Other dangers would have to be met in their own time. The place remained vulnerable to storm, neglect, understanding, and love.

Before withdrawing, the power returned once more to the margin of the garden. It left nothing in the shelter and made no alteration to the tree. The hammerstone remained exactly where a returning hand would expect it.

Evening cloud wetted the ferns. A white throat swelled beneath them.

The first call was still absurdly large for the body that made it. Another frog answered from across the seep, and the shaft joined their voices.

The power could have answered with a sound before which the whole mountain would have seemed small. It did not. A third frog began.

It stayed until the chorus thinned, then went without taking a token. Behind it, water ticked into the stone cups, the swifts shifted in the dark wall, and the Hollow Crown went on answering for itself.
