### What the Power Did

The power did not enter.

It rested its attention beyond the narrow copper lip, where dampness darkened the masonry and a drop sometimes gathered without any sound of arrival. It could have passed through the vaults without displacement. It could have known everything in an instant. Instead, it waited.

Benevolence had given it the wish to give something. Wisdom made it suspicious of that wish.

At first it looked for the being who lived in the cistern. It found no single inhabitant at the center. It found films changing the texture of wet brick, shell pavements thickening around their dead, fans withdrawing from disturbances too slight to name. It found the hidden lengths of hunters and the crowded fish correcting their positions against one another. It found lives entire within niches, and countries in the distance across a pore.

There was no throne to approach, no representative to whom a gift could be offered.

The power’s first impulse was to open the roof.

Only a little light, it thought. Enough to make the vaults visible. Enough to reveal the thumbprint beneath its glaze, the wooden grain preserved in brick, the pale layers of fish hanging in their chambers.

Then it saw that light would not merely reveal the cistern. It would feed something else there. Green growth would enter the close budget of chemistry and seepage. Films would thicken or fail; grazers would multiply or lose their routes; the slow differences between one side of a pier and the other would be overrun. The darkness was not a cover over the place. It was one of the conditions by which the place remained itself.

The power considered fresh water, abundant and clean. It considered clearing the sediment, rounding no further edges, restoring the basin’s squared rim. It considered repairing the pipes.

But clean water would have been another water. Clearing the basin would have removed shelter, food, and the unfinished substance of generations. A repaired pipe might be the eviction of an animal whose whole world lay behind its visible head. Restoration would have required choosing a century and calling every later century damage.

Even the makers’ marks did not ask to be rescued from use. Life in the thumbprint was not an insult to the thumb.

The power did not conclude that the cistern was perfect. Hunters fed. Bodies broke down. A pipe narrowed until its inhabitant had to yield. There were deaths without witnesses and injuries too small for any history. The power refused to call these things good merely because, taken together, they made a beautiful stillness.

But neither was every cost an instruction to intervene.

So it did not illuminate, replenish, repair, enlarge, or complete. It did not give the blind fish eyes, or carry one away to show it the weather. It did not make the shellfish swifter, the hunters gentler, or the old fish immortal. Above all, it did not preserve the cistern by fixing it forever at the moment it had first been loved. That would have been a way of keeping the power’s own experience safe, not the cistern.

Nor did restraint become a vanity. The power made no sacred rule of its abstention. Had a fracture begun emptying the vaults, it might have slowed the loss by no more than the necessary fraction. Had some outside purpose come to drain the place as an inconvenience, it might first have tried to make that purpose capable of understanding what it was about to destroy. It remained able to act. It simply invented no emergency in order to prove that its ability was love.

For the present, it listened.

It stayed long enough for a closed shell to open by the width of a hair. Long enough for a disturbed colony of fans to emerge unevenly, one after another. Long enough to understand that a fish turning without appearing to move could alter the tension of a whole crowded layer.

In time, it came to know one old fish—not as an emblem of the cistern, but as this fish. It knew the slight asymmetry of its corrections, the place where age had changed the outline of one flank, the small interval it maintained between itself and a pier. It perceived how completely the fish was addressed by its world: by pressure, dissolved traces, the warmth beside old clay, the responsive nearness of other bodies. Nothing in that life was an imperfect approximation of a surface existence. Nothing in it required the power’s nostalgia for the sun.

When the fish died, the power did not prevent it.

Neither did it call the death necessary in a voice that made indifference sound wise. It stayed. It watched the body begin its slow passage toward the basin. It saw the neighbors retain, for days, a narrow looseness where the fish had hung.

The power could have held that interval open forever.

It did not.

It let the small corrections continue. It let the absence become less exact. It remembered the fish without requiring the crowd to remember, and without turning remembrance into another form of captivity. When the interval at last closed, the power did not mistake closure for worthlessness.

That was nearly all.

No inscription appeared on the copper lip. No new mineral brightened the walls. The cistern was not promised freedom from collapse, nor were its inhabitants promised that their patience would one day be rewarded with another kind of life. The power left no gift that could become a burden of gratitude.

Yet something had been done. The power had withdrawn every demand that the cistern become more visible, more useful, more comprehensible, or more durable in order to deserve care. It had known the place without possessing it, and had allowed that knowledge to change the knower rather than the known.

A tube-dweller extended its fan. A grazer lifted one limb and set it down again. Somewhere a shell shut with a small, hard note.

Nothing in the cistern knew that it had been loved.

That was not a failure of the love. It was the form the love had taken.
