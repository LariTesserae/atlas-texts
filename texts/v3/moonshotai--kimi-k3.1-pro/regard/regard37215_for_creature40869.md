### One Run

The power came to the rim and, for a long while, did nothing.

Benevolence made many suggestions. Wisdom made it examine them.

It could have hushed the basin, but then keepers would have lost the changes in sound by which they knew their water. It could have cleansed the pools until they shone, and starved the film on which the runs depended. It could have brought green things to soften the stone, but roots and soil would not have been decoration; they would have been an ecological decision made from a distance. It could even have made the walls permanent. Then no step would need replacing, no inscription would need recutting, and much of the work by which knowledge and obligation passed between people would quietly disappear.

So the power waited until it could understand that a silent cistern might be failing, a clean run might be empty, and a smooth parapet might be useless to birds. It waited until the roar was no longer merely something to be endured.

But restraint could become vanity too.

From the rim, it would have been easy to admire Sinter’s equilibrium so greatly that individual pain began to seem like a small price for someone else’s beautiful idea of balance. The power refused that conclusion. A run could be healthy and its keeper exhausted. A district could be rich in food and poor in sleep. A scar could be instructive without being necessary.

Sinter did not need to be remade. Neither did it need to be admired and left exactly as it was.

The power went down into the city.

It went barefoot, without radiance, and made itself small enough to be refused. It did not announce itself over the roofs. Besides being useless at that volume, an announcement would have suggested that its arrival was more important than the work already taking place.

The first keeper it approached showed no interest in its nature. She signed that it was standing on the slab she needed.

It moved.

This was its first accepted act in Sinter.

After that, she put a cistern cover into its hands and showed it the height at which the stone was to be held. When it tried to make the cover weightless, she stopped it. She wanted to feel where the weight went.

*Hold. Lower. Still.*

It obeyed while she cleared the seat, shifted a fragment of crust, and brought the cover down again. The work took no miracle. It did require that the power not drop the stone.

When they had finished, it asked, in hesitant hand-speech, what it might give her.

She considered the question. Then she asked whether it could listen.

It answered that it could listen to everything.

She found this answer useless.

She taught it to listen to one cistern.

Here was the knocking near the inlet. There, the broader voices at the overflow. This tremor in the rim belonged to water moving beneath the cover. That clatter above was only birds changing place. A thinning of sound beside the outlet mattered. A brief eruption of cries from the roof might not.

She made it repeat the instructions against her palm, so there could be no misunderstanding.

Then she told it to wake her if the run changed, and went into the next chamber to sleep.

The power, which could have attended to every pool and roof in the basin, kept watch over one run. It did not silence the frogs to make her rest deeper. It did not improve the water while its keeper was away. It placed a hand on the stone and attended to what she had entrusted to it.

When she returned, she inspected the cistern herself.

The power was not offended. Her inspection was not a failure of gratitude. It was how trust became accurate.

Only after she had touched the rim, checked the overflow, and found the run alive did she turn back to it.

*Again,* she signed. *If you are willing.*

It was willing.

Afterward it did many things, all of them smaller than its abilities, though not all of them small to the person concerned. It carried loads for stiffened hands. It took watches so that the exhausted could sleep without leaving water unattended. When it perceived a danger no one else could yet detect, it gave warning rather than command. It offered healing where healing was wanted; it did not secretly put healing into the water merely because it was certain of its own benevolence.

It was often corrected.

A stone worker showed it why a span could not be replaced in the order it had proposed. A child stopped it from scraping a slab clean. A rookery keeper caught its wrist before it smoothed a ledge. It learned that having good intentions toward an animal was not the same as knowing where the animal nested.

Sometimes people asked for gifts the power would not give. It refused without contempt and explained what those gifts would cost. Sometimes they explained something in return, and it discovered that its reasoning had been incomplete. It changed its mind.

Their quarrels did not persuade it that they needed ruling. Their mistakes did not make its help conditional upon their becoming worthy of it. Nor did their skill persuade it that they should always be left to manage alone.

It raised no temple and cut no commandment into the stone. It made no promise that Sinter would remain forever as it had first appeared from the rim. Preserving the city against every possible future would have been another form of possession.

Instead, it accepted obligations in a run.

From the rim, afterward, there would have been little to see: pale roofs, black channels, steam, the same unbroken roar. That was appropriate. The power had not come to make a difference visible from a distance.

It had come able to change everything.

It stayed willing to be told that it was standing on the wrong slab.

And now, with one hand resting on warm cistern stone, it listened. Below, the run was alive. Above, someone was already signing that there was work.
