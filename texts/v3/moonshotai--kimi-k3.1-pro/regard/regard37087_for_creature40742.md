### A Little More Dew

The power would not arrive as light. In the sealed galleries, light where no light should be is not revelation but damage: dry air, an opened passage, workers hurrying with mud. Nor would they speak to the queen. Her chamber is a place of continuity, not government, and a command delivered there would only show that the speaker had misunderstood the town.

They would begin by attending.

They would find no single being to whom a gift could simply be handed. The colony was not only its queen, or its workers, or the sum of their bodies. It was also the gardens they tended, the passages that made a climate, the roots that supported and threatened them, and the moisture on which all their arrangements depended. Yet the power would not love only the whole. A whole can become an elegant excuse for disregarding the small lives within it. They would keep both in view: the town, and the worker at the breach.

Only then would they consider help.

They would not give that worker a vision of the six turrets. It would be no great kindness to burden a life with an overview it could not use. They would not make the queen a ruler, or turn the dispersed intelligence of the nest into a plan held somewhere above it. They would not make the walls permanent. Permanence would take away collapse as material and damage as instruction. They would not destroy every ant, spider, centipede, and lizard merely because some lives in the hollow ended in other lives. Nor would they keep the hollow open forever. To stop the sediment would be to preserve the court’s appearance by arresting the ground that was making it.

At almost every point, improvement would mean replacing the town with the power’s idea of it.

So most of their power would remain unused, as deliberately as the bare clay between the turrets.

But restraint would not be indifference.

If a drought came that exceeded what the colony could answer—if the gardens began to shrink, the deepest passages lost their dampness, and repair consumed more bodies and material than it could preserve—the power would not send a river. A river would only make a different catastrophe. They would not cover the fig with cloud for a generation, or abolish the dry season, or suspend the needs of everything else on the plain.

Their intervention would be small enough to be mistaken for weather.

For several dawns, condensation would gather a little more heavily on the root bark and the undersides of the fern leaves. Not enough to make bright pools in the court. Enough to keep one seam of clay dark after sunrise. Enough to leave a vent humid, a garden workable, a gallery worth repairing. The gift would arrive already divided into drops, delayed by leaf and bark, passing into the hollow by the same routes water had always used.

No termite would experience rescue. There would be only moisture where dryness had been expected, clay that could still be shaped, one chamber that did not have to be abandoned. The rest would be done by saliva, fiber, fungus, mud, and labor. By the time fresh pellets darkened the base of a turret, the gift would have become indistinguishable from the town’s own work.

Then the power would stop.

They would not make every dawn generous. Help continued without regard to need would become another climate, then another dependency, then another form of possession. When ordinary rain returned, the extra dew would cease. The colony would again close its own openings, abandon its own routes, misuse some materials and recover others, and remain wiser than any plan imposed upon it precisely because no single part of it had to be wise in that way.

The town would never know that it had been noticed. It would receive no name, no monument, no promise of everlasting safety. Later, if the fig roots split the turrets or the rising sediment closed the court, the power would not necessarily undo those things merely to keep the object of their attention available. Care would not require the being to remain visible to the one who cared.

Perhaps other colonies would by then be deepening elsewhere. Perhaps not. Permanence had never been the only form a future could take.

After rain, a worker would come to a broken gallery with mud. It would set down its pellet and withdraw. Another would add to it. A third would press the new material into the old until the breach became sufficiently wall-like to be treated as wall.

The power would have made the town neither eternal nor complete. They would have returned to it, once, the means of continuing in its own terms.

Their mercy would be a little water.

Their wisdom would be leaving the town to decide what to do with it.
