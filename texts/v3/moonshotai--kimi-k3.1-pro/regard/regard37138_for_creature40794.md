The power would begin by not entering.

Even gentle attention can become an alteration. A shadow held too long above one court might cool the flowers. A foot placed carelessly on the nacre would trouble work laid down by ten thousand generations. A voice, however soft, would be the loudest event in Sere’s history.

So it would remain beyond the outermost root, where the chemistry failed and the bare mountain resumed, and it would learn the difference between being needed and wanting to help.

Its first impulses would all be generous. It would see the dry air and think of rain. It would see a hunter’s eye changing from ivory to bronze and think of a world without fear. It would see the browser’s dry body disappearing beneath the sheaths and want to preserve it. It would see the arch that had been closing for two hundred years and feel how easy it would be, with the smallest exercise of power, to bring the living tips together.

Each kindness would require correction.

Rain was not necessarily refreshment to leaves whose chemistry had never required it. Safety imposed from above could unmake one reciprocity after another. To preserve every body would be to interrupt the long distribution by which a life became floors, roots, oil, and meal. And the arch was not an unfinished object awaiting a more capable builder. It was something a colony was doing with its body. To finish it in an instant would not fulfill two centuries of growth. It would merely bring them to an end.

So the power would wait.

It would watch a noon, then a night. It would remain for a season, until the scavengers’ labor became visible. It would stay longer, until a masonback colony revealed itself not as a multitude of negligible animals but as a slow resurfacing of the world. Longer still, and the browsers would become brief passages of warmth through lanes maintained by appetites older than their lineages. The veilwings would seem quick as sparks. The vault-stems would come forward, green at their crowns and stone below, until the whole town appeared to move.

Only then would the power begin to understand how little its first idea of Sere had contained.

It might search for a single being within the city: a sleeper in the deepest vault, a mind distributed through the roots, a civic soul whose slow thoughts were the closing of doors and the opening of courts. Finding none, it would not invent one. Sere would not have to be secretly a person in order to deserve care. Nor would the power awaken it into speech merely so that benevolence could have someone to question, and someone to thank it.

There would be losses in Sere, and the power would not call them good simply because they belonged to an old and balanced life. But neither would it mistake its own sorrow for proof that Sere had been badly made. It would learn to be sorrowful without immediately becoming busy.

This would not harden into a proud rule against touching. Rules, too, can become refusals to pay attention. If the young mountain broke beneath the basin, or some alien chemistry entered the roots and spread without answer, the power might act then: narrowly, at the cause, without redesigning everything that survived. It would not promise never to interfere. It would promise never to use benevolence as an excuse to stop learning.

Against intrusion, however, its decision might come sooner.

If curiosity came with containers, if admiration arrived carrying knives, if some other greatness wished to improve Sere, exhibit it, reproduce it, or keep it safely unchanged forever, the power would refuse. There would be no radiant wall around the summit and no guardian terror placed upon the rim. Nothing in Sere would be recruited into its own defense. The protection would simply hold, beyond the last root, without becoming a new condition that the town had to accommodate.

No citizen would know. There would be no altar, because there would be no sign; no covenant, because nothing had been asked to consent; no tribute of pollen or oil. The power would not require Sere to become grateful in order to remain unmolested.

Its care, in the end, would be less like a gift bestowed than like an appetite restrained.

At noon, sunlight would cross the open court. Pollen would hang nearly motionless in the warm air. A veilwing would pass beneath an arch whose living edges had drawn almost imperceptibly nearer since the power first arrived.

The power would know how to complete it.

It would not.

Instead it would leave the small remaining space open, and the silence intact, and keep watch at the pace of Sere.
