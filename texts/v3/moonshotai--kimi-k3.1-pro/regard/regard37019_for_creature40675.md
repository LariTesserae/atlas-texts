## What the Power Would Leave Undone

The power would begin by believing the Height.

It would believe the census. No native life. No concealed biosphere. No inhabitant hidden behind the apparent absence of one. It would not invent a resident to complete the shape of its errand, nor call the province lonely merely because someone from elsewhere would have been lonely there.

That would be its first mercy: not to confuse the place with its own expectations.

It would not mistake activity for suffering. A plateau foundering in concentric slices would not be a body coming apart. A mineral patch cracking when its vent shifted would not be a creature dying without descendants. A ridge would have no reason to be grateful for being preserved at one height. To treat these events as injuries would not be an excess of compassion. It would be a failure of perception.

So the power would not still the crust. It would not finish the mountains, give the clefts bottoms, or make a map reliable. Nothing in the province had asked to be delivered from becoming something else.

Nor would it arrive bearing water, soil, spores, and a plan. The warmth was not an empty cradle. The dryness was not a drought. Life introduced merely to make the place less empty would be need created for the satisfaction of relieving it. A wise power would not make inhabitants so that its protection could have an object.

It would not even arrive as light. There was no sleeper to wake, no seedling to nourish, no eye whose darkness required pity.

Then it would come to the built traces, and its restraint would become more difficult.

A collapsing dome required no apology. But five slabs stacked largest to smallest were evidence that, on at least one occasion, something had been present that could choose. The stones were not important because they were old, or beautiful, or useful. Their importance lay in the narrow interval between what stress had done to them and what placement had done afterward.

The power could preserve them. It could hold the leaning wall upright, keep the cairn’s terrace motionless, and prevent fresh glass from covering the shallow rectangle. It could reunite the split doorway and make passing through it permanently possible.

But it would see how easily preservation would become authorship.

Every repair would declare intactness preferable to damage. Every restored arrangement would imply that its original form had been understood. Sites connected for safekeeping would begin to resemble a network; sites of similar age would begin to resemble a people. Eventually the record would contain less evidence of visits than evidence of the caretaker’s desire for a story.

So it would not put the stones back.

It would remember what it had actually seen, with its uncertainty intact. Five slabs in descending order. An arc sheltering nothing. Two uprights and a lintel. Three stones against a fracture. It would not remember surveyors, priests, exiles, or a vanished civilization. It would not supply generations between the placements. Its memory would be exact about its ignorance.

And if, while it attended, a wall fell or a cairn was carried apart by uplift, it would let the event remain an event. This would not be indifference. It would be a refusal to turn a guest’s pause into the caretaker’s monument.

Only one kind of occurrence would call it to action: not motion, of which there was always more somewhere, but presence.

If a traveler entered the Height, carrying a little elsewhere in blood, shell, solution, circuitry, field, or some enclosure the power had no name for, then the province would contain something that could be harmed. Silence could become peril. A changed route could become abandonment. The warm stone could remain indifferent while the traveler’s supplies diminished.

Then the power would help.

It would not first demand to know whether the traveler had made the doorway. It would not require an explanation of the cairn, a purpose for the visit, or gratitude afterward. It would offer whatever the traveler actually needed, rather than whatever an imagined body ought to need: atmosphere, moisture, shelter, stillness underfoot, a means of communication, a way home. It would lend them an elsewhere for as long as they required it, and no longer.

It would not try to make them native. It would not ask them to stay and justify the warmth.

For a being capable of choice, it would also preserve choice. Its aid would not become ownership. Its questions would not become tolls. If the traveler wished the stones left alone, they would be left alone. If the traveler wished a mark remembered, the power would remember it without pretending to know what it meant.

Until such a visit, it would build nothing. Even a refuge would be a guess about an unknown anatomy. Even a warning would assume eyes, language, and ground stable enough to warn from. Its readiness would have no architecture.

Consequently, almost nothing in the visible history of the Height would change. The population would remain zero. Vents would open and close. Crystal patches would acquire their brief mineral histories and end without descendants. New ridges would interrupt old distances. The province would receive no shrine, moon, garden, sentinel, or name.

Perhaps no traveler would ever come. Wisdom does not guarantee a climax, and benevolence does not cease to be real merely because it is never needed.

But if, after an hour or an age, something alive did stand in that darkness—alive in the Height, though not of it—and the ground began to rise beneath its route home, it would discover that help had been possible without having been advertised.

The power would not save the mountain.

It would help the traveler, and then let the traveler go.

Behind them, without sound, several stars would go out in a row as a new ridge lifted. The power would not make the ridge give them back.
