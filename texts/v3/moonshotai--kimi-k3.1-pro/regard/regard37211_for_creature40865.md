The power did not arrive as light. Light was already occupied.

For a long while, it allowed itself only attention. It remained through the blade of noon and the brown dusk, through the opening of the flowers and the pouring-out of the bats. It stayed until the hunting bird crossed the opening and the court forgot its noise in sections. It stayed through the dust days, while trails vanished and spiders came down from the upper walls. It had learned elsewhere that help which cannot wait for understanding is often only a quicker form of possession.

Mercy proposed water.

The power could have filled the basin without dimming any star. It could have darkened the seams, softened the dust, and caused green things to spring up with an ease the court had never known. But wisdom made it watch what that kindness would replace.

A spring would not simply have been added to the court. Roots would have changed their journeys. Dry shelters would have become damp ones. The dust bath would have vanished; some nests would have failed; creatures adapted to the court’s fierce economies would have been joined, and perhaps displaced, by others. Water might have made a more generous place. It would not necessarily have made more room for the lives already there. It could have founded a beautiful new court over the quiet eviction of this one.

Mercy proposed safety.

But safety for the moth was hunger for the spider. Safety for the spider was an empty wren’s beak. To abolish the geckos’ disputes would not tell the power which gecko belonged on the stair. To defend every scale insect would be to make a pasture of the vines; to defend the vines against every mouth would be to abolish half the roads by which the court sustained itself. Even the ants, whose industry looked from above like cooperation, were carrying their small wars across the ceiling.

Mercy proposed preservation.

The power could have held every wall at its present angle, renewed each patch of wax, restored every web after the dust, and ensured that no vine outlived its masonry. It could have kept the court forever at the hour in which attention had discovered it. Within a little while, there would have been a perfect image of the court, increasingly skillfully maintained. Whether the court itself would still have been present was another question.

For the being here was not the wrens, or the vines, or even the total of its inhabitants at any one moment. It was the continuing passage among them: shelter becoming refuse, refuse becoming fertility, fertility becoming leaf, leaf becoming food, and food becoming another brief tenure in a hole made by hands long absent. It had no shape at which it could safely be stopped.

None of this made the court innocent. Its intricacy did not make injury gentle, or turn fear into a form of wisdom. A moth’s struggle was not ennobled because its wings would later be examined by ants. The power refused the pleasant falsehood that everything was therefore as it should be.

So it did not simply withdraw, congratulating itself on having left no footprint.

It made one change, at the boundary where an individual life could no longer alter its own fate.

Where pain could still be a message—could drive an animal toward shelter, make it favor a wound, loosen a foothold, abandon a meal, or try once more to escape—the power left it to its work. But where there was no longer any message that could be answered, no repair possible and no action left that could return the creature to its life, the power quietly allowed suffering to cease.

No moth was taken from a web because of this. No spider was denied its food. The mantis still struck, sometimes missed, and sometimes went hungry. The snake still opened its nocturnal seam through the court. Nothing survived merely because the power had admired the place.

That was why the gift could be given to all without being stolen from some. It had no body that could be eaten, no warmth around which a territory could form, no sweetness that ants could carry away. It altered no trail and gave no competitor an advantage. It could not become another resource in the court’s exhaustive economy.

To the court itself, the power gave no exemption. Walls could still fall. The vine might die before its roots found another pocket. A brood might fail; a colony might not return. Even the greenness was promised nothing. The power did not require the place to repeat forever the particular abundance that had first drawn its attention.

To love a process, it understood, is not to sentence it to remain in its most admirable year.

Then it went away.

The basin still held dust and beetle shells. No luminous handprint appeared on the lintel. The bees continued to defend their low boundary of sound. A spider fastened new silk to lines whose makers were dead. Beneath the cushions, a seed trembled, rolled, and came to rest no closer to certainty than before.

At dusk the cream-colored flowers opened. Moths arrived. The geckos assumed their old disagreements on the stair, and the bats loosened from the vault into the strip of darkening sky.

Nothing looked blessed. The court remained dry, crowded, dangerous, and unfinished.

But mercy had been there without becoming an owner. And the only gift it left was one that no other life had to pay for.
