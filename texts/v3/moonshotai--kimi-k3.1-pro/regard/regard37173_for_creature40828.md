## What the Power Did

The power came without parting the clouds.

For a long time it did nothing visible. It did not mistake Serein for its people. Nor did it make the opposite mistake, and love the whole so greatly that it overlooked the parts.

It followed water from an old condenser vane into a gutter, from the gutter into a high reservoir, through a pump hall where a woman diagnosed a worn bearing with her heel, and down again into a garden where a knotwood root had entered a joint no wider than a letter. It watched moss darken on a wall before anyone indoors admitted that the plaster was damp. In a covered cistern it found pale worms inhabiting a darkness that, to them, was not darkness at all. On the rim it saw a glider fold its wings and become, for a moment, only another stone on the nesting ledge.

At the scale of the plateau, Serein was a brief wetness. At the scale of a water-flea, one reservoir was a country. Neither view was false.

If Serein was one being, it was not a harmonious one. It hoarded, leaked, invaded itself, and forgot. Some of its members survived by damaging others; some survived by repairing damage they had not caused. Yet neither was it merely a machine that could be improved by removing friction. Some friction was a root breaking a pipe. Some was a young engineer refusing to repeat an old mistake.

The power considered dependable rain, unwearing metal, channels no root could enter. It set these gifts aside—not because difficulty was holy, but because it did not wish gratitude to become the town’s final item of infrastructure. Instead, it remained long enough to distinguish difficulty from abandonment.

The distinction became plain during a long dry interval.

The public records were accurate. Freight had been deferred, allowances reduced, gardens pruned according to need rather than affection. No gauge had been falsified. Nevertheless, beneath an overflow lane, a household sold the good lathe with which its members had expected to earn the following year’s cable fees. They sold it to purchase reserve from a catchment whose inner rooms remained dry. Uphill, a councilor could truthfully report that total consumption had fallen and that no common main had failed.

The power saw that scarcity was weather. Its distribution was a series of decisions.

When the emergency cistern reached its lowest usable mark, it acted.

That night, fog came against the wind. No river in the low country ran shallower for it; the power was not moving misery downhill. The fog gathered first around the oldest towers. Water formed in the dark lace of their vanes and released in heavy, separate drops. New towers yielded too, though less. Gutters ran briefly. Pipes shivered, and keepers came out in their damp clothing, not quite believing the gauges until they had read them twice.

By morning there was enough. Not enough for a bright night, not enough to reopen every press or restore every garden allowance, but enough to keep drinking water common and the last working pumps from destroying themselves. Then the fog thinned.

The power did not call this a lesson. Neither did it promise that restraint would always matter more than mercy.

Afterward, it made quieter changes.

It did not make failure impossible. It made certain failures a little less likely to be perfectly silent. A crack in a common main might weep one bright thread before opening. A cable beginning to strand might hold an unfamiliar hum through a still afternoon. Contamination in an old reservoir might alter the water-fleas while there was still time to compare them with the keeper’s last sample. Nothing was guaranteed to be understood. Attention remained work. The gift was only a margin: an hour for a sluice, a season for a wall, sometimes a generation for a town.

To an apprentice gauge-keeper, copying records beside a heat pipe, the power offered one question. It arrived without grandeur, with the troublesome clarity of something remembered too late:

*When the reservoir is low, who becomes poor first?*

She could have dismissed it. Instead, she wrote it in the margin of her copy book. At the next reading she asked a more practical version: which households exhausted their discretionary share at each level, and which still possessed sealed reserves?

Some keepers thought this an improper use of a public record. Some thought it so obviously proper that they claimed to have considered it years before. Arguments followed, several of them ill-tempered. A few catchments refused the new figures. Others used them to refine old privileges. The power did not make anyone good.

But gradually, on certain gauges, the reservoir level ceased to be the whole account. A second column recorded when private reserve would have to enter common consideration. In two catchments, and later in more, a household could no longer be required to sell its means of work before transfers from surplus had been witnessed. The prosperous did not become dry. The poor did not all become secure. One form of abandonment merely became harder to describe as prudence.

The power left the argument over the old condensers unsettled. Sometimes preservation would be wise; sometimes replacement. It took no side in favor of crookedness or efficiency. It did ensure that the tower books survived both decisions, so that neither side could make the past disappear merely by winning.

It did not rescue moths from gliders, or roots from saws, or snails from every careless boot. It was not trying to create a world without appetite, accident, or maintenance. But where some small, unchartered community stood at the edge of irreversible loss, it leaned again toward notice: a nesting ledge discovered before an anchorage was cut, a cistern examined before it was cleansed, a patch of seemingly ordinary patina left unscraped until someone had time to look more closely.

Then the power withdrew.

There was no temple, no new calendar, no likeness left behind in wet glass. Serein still leaked. Knotwoods still investigated warm joints. Young people still departed on the cable, vowing never to organize a celebration around a reservoir reading. Gauge-keepers were still gossiped about on bright nights. The old towers continued to give, or not give, in still air.

Years later, a pump-listener woke before dawn. For a while she heard only ordinary quiet. Then came a faint irregularity through the floor, so slight that she might have mistaken it for her own pulse.

She dressed, went down to the intake, and found a root mat where no root should yet have reached. She cleared it before the pump ran dry. Then she went home and slept. No reservoir failed, and no child was ever told the story of their rescue.

Above the roofs, an old tender opened a tower book and wrote:

*Taking east fog before dawn. Giving again in still air.*

The power had not made Serein immortal, harmonious, or grateful. It had left the town with what care most often needs and scarcity most often takes away: a little time in which to notice, and a little freedom about what to do next.
