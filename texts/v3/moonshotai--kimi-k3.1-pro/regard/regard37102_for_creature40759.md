### The Visit

The power came at midday, when the shelf was least like an omen.

It made no wind. The pale crowns did not brighten, and the pools kept their reflections. If it had a shadow, it left that elsewhere. It took care that even its attention should not rest heavily on the green filaments in their grooves.

It could have been generous in all the easy ways.

It could have closed the open intervals with moss and made the four trees the beginning of a forest. It could have set small lives stirring in the pools, hung webs between the branches, given the silence something that would break and resume. It could have quickened the upward seep until water shone on the stone and any visitor could recognize a miracle.

Each gift would have been comprehensible. Each would also have made the shelf more nearly resemble the giver’s idea of what a good place should be.

So the power waited.

It stayed through rain and through the days after rain. It watched the pools join and separate. It watched a pale summit darken almost imperceptibly, though the stone below seemed dry. It saw spores fail on open rock, a seedling persist and then vanish, a root broaden where it could not penetrate. At last a bead gathered above it, fell, and struck water with one small note.

The power did not conclude that the shelf was perfect. Wisdom did not require that kind of lie. There were losses here that served no visible purpose. A groove could dry; a stem could die; centuries of growth could remain, in the end, only fiber held in red clay. Nor did the power decide that every accident had been secretly necessary.

But it saw that sparseness was not the same as want. The open ground was not a wound between gardens. The trees were not waiting to become a grove. The stillness was not a hush imposed on creatures that would rather have been singing. Nothing visible was asking to be delivered from the conditions of its life.

Nor could the power find one resident whose wishes might stand for all the rest. It did not invent one. It did not wake a tree into language, give the rising water a voice, or appoint the oldest hummock keeper of the shelf. Those gifts would have relieved the visitor’s uncertainty. They would not necessarily have given anything to the tree, the water, or the moss.

While the power remained, a dry seed went over the open rim.

It could have brought the seed back. It could have placed it in a damp seam and supplied exactly the depth, shade, and nourishment required. For a moment, benevolence might have looked very much like that small correction.

The power let the seed go.

Not because its loss was good, and not because every possible life must be left to chance. If it grieved, it kept its grief from becoming a hand. To return every seed, sustain every filament, and prevent every failed beginning would eventually make the power the author of each root and frond. The shelf might become richer. It might even become more beautiful. But gradually it would cease to be a place engaged in its own processes and become instead an expression of someone else’s care.

This was not a vow never to help. It was only a refusal to help merely in order to escape the discomfort of having done nothing.

Before leaving, the power did one thing. It remembered the shelf accurately.

Not as a paradise, not as a desolation, and not as a riddle whose answer was the upward water. It remembered the failed spores as well as the reflected fern, the difficult bare ground as well as the saturated hummocks. It remembered that the place did not need to be called empty merely because nothing there could speak, or unfinished merely because much of it remained open, or available merely because no hand had marked it.

It set no ward around the rim and promised no permanence. Even protection could become a form of possession; even preservation could harden into a command that nothing change. But if there came a time when memory was not enough, the power would know what it was being asked to defend: not four trees forever, not every fern, not the shelf arrested at the moment of its visit, but its freedom to continue without having to become useful, expressive, or improved.

Then it went.

No pebble had been turned into a sign. No object had been carried away. There was no new shoot whose presence might later be mistaken for a blessing.

Toward evening the pale ribs held the light a little longer than the dark floor. Heat remained in the stone. Somewhere above a pool, another bead slowly gathered at the highest pore.

When it finally fell, the sound ended, and nothing answered it.

The power had not needed an answer.
