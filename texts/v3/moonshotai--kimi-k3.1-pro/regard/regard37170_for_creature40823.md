### The Spare Round

I think the power would begin by walking.

It would learn a round before it learned the names of councils. It would find out which faulty coupling served the household, the roof mat, and the newts; which silence meant that an engine had stopped and which meant that a street was about to move; who kept spare hose and who had already lent theirs. It would watch a *making* from ground level, where it was not a negotiation with emerging form but a door jamming, a perforated zinc tin passing hand to hand, and someone deciding whether the ledger could be left.

It would do the immediate mercies.

A beam would be held a little longer. A crushed hand would be healed without being made into a parable. A household with no refuge would be given one. Where every pipe was already at full extension, the power would not offer a meditation on adaptability; it would bring slack. It would also notice when scarcity was not the basin’s doing but someone’s locked store, and it would not mistake the lock for a fact of geology.

But it would be cautious about the largest miracle.

It would not, unasked, make the basin still. Newground is not merely a stable city that has failed to remain stable. Its pipes, gardens, portable affections, and rounds have all grown around movement; beneath it there may be another participant whose stillness would not be peace.

Nor would the power decide that movement, being natural, must therefore be preserved at any cost. Newground’s competence would not excuse its injuries. Its resilience would not become an argument for leaving the poorest districts to be resilient again.

So it would ask, and then ask again after the ground had changed. Some people would want warning. Some would want water. Some would want a particular arcade kept open long enough to discover whether it could still be home. Some would gladly leave and never return. There would be no single answer entitled to speak for the whole city.

The power would know, perhaps, whether anything lived in the deep seams. It would not wake a sleeper merely to obtain an answer. If it found a mind, it would not treat silence as consent, or an inability to speak as an absence of interests. If it found no mind, it would not conclude that the basin could therefore be used without care. It would refuse both easy titles: god and machine.

What it could do, without demanding an answer, was return. It could listen when nothing was being asked of the basin, so that its attention would not be only another kind of use. If something eventually answered, it would listen. If nothing did, it would not call the patience wasted.

Its lasting gift to Newground, if it left one, would not be an object.

A wise power would not leave a monument in a city whose oldest possessions are portable. It would leave something that could be repeated.

In the watch books, it might later be called **the spare round**.

The practice would be simple. At agreed intervals, and after the first count following a *making*, those who had anything to spare would walk one route that was not theirs. They would carry a coupling, a dry cloth, a roll of mat, a kettle, or nothing but the knowledge of how to fetch help. They would knock at the household no round had expected. They would ask which cistern name had traveled to which new water. They would find out who had been dispersed, and where.

They would not preserve every path. Some arcades ought to narrow. Some *suggestions* were bad habits. Some places had been abandoned for good reasons, and keeping them open would be only another form of refusal to listen.

The spare round would merely keep return possible for a little while.

A route used by three frightened households would not disappear solely because they lacked the traffic to instruct the basin. A refuge would not be lost simply because its usual walkers were injured. A household dragged halfway across the city would have time to find its neighbors before its old pattern vanished entirely. No one would be required to go back. They would be given time to choose.

Those with nothing to spare would not be asked to finance the miracle. Their routes would be walked first. Nor would the practice be allowed to become a pious substitute for opening stores, releasing crane time, or repairing water rights. The power would have little patience with a holiness that admired shared tea while leaving the hose locked up.

At first, the power itself would walk the spare round. It would be seen at odd hours wearing out replaceable soles, carrying chalk, sometimes mistaken for a new watchman. During a *making*, it would not necessarily stop the ridge. It might be found instead at the stiff door, beneath the leaning frontage, beside the cistern keeper whose gauges had failed. When loss had already happened, it would stay for the counting. It would not turn grief into evidence of a larger plan.

Later, other people would walk it.

Not everyone. Not reliably at first. It would spread in the unglamorous way useful things spread in Newground: because a crew had once been found by it, because a gardener had borrowed the idea, because a child sent the long way for bread came back knowing the name of someone who had been alone.

No temple would be necessary. There might be an extra cup beside certain kettles, or a chalk mark whose meaning no two districts explained alike. Surveyors would argue over whether the spare round altered the basin’s behavior or merely shortened the time before human help arrived. The argument would improve the vocabulary. It would not stop the walking.

And late at night, in an arcade with no houses, there might still be only condensation ticking through roots, hares stirring behind a grille, and someone passing who did not need that route at all.

The ground would remain young. The city would remain frightened, ingenious, and unequal in ways still requiring repair. Nothing would have been settled forever.

But a path would remain open one more night—not as a command to return, and not as a promise that nothing changes.

Just long enough for someone to come back, and for someone else to notice if they did not.
