The power did not bring rain.

That was its first kindness.

It stood for a long time at the top of the twenty-seven steps. From there the garden looked almost comprehensible, and the power was wise enough to distrust that.

It did not make the lashings incorruptible, or teach the keepers a knot that could never loosen. Such a knot would not have made the garden safe, only less able to admit error. It did not give the households reliable distances, or draw every route onto a single white tile. Above all, it did not set a trunk inside the waiting shadow.

Then, having refused the gifts that would have made the garden its own, it went down.

It left its radiance above the steps. At the bottom it put on a dry body, bare feet, and clothes the color of old cord. Warmth came up through the mineral into its soles. Somewhere far off a mallet struck once. The power did not go looking for the worker.

When it told the young keeper that it had come to help, she looked at its hands rather than its face.

“Can you unpick cord?”

“I can learn.”

She gave it a hank of ruined fiber.

“Don’t pull.”

The power sat beside her and began. It could have ordered the cord to forget every strain and tangle. Instead it used its fingers. The work was slow. Fiber concealed its weak places. Loops tightened when hurried. Once, the other end of the line was not where the power had last seen it, and it had to wait for the keeper to finish what she was doing before asking whether this mattered.

By afternoon it had rescued a small bundle of usable fiber. The keeper accepted it without surprise. No bird came down to sit on its shoulder. Nothing announced that the afternoon was significant.

The power discovered that it was possible to be benevolent for several hours without saving anyone.

Later, the keeper allowed it to carry a little fresh soil to the shadow-bed. There, for an instant, the power saw the tree entire: the weight of its trunk, the patient pressure of its roots, the altered darkness beneath its leaves. It saw exactly what could be done to bring that vision nearer.

The sight was so complete that it nearly became an assertion.

The power let it go.

It also saw that the keeper might not live to see the tree. It did not tell her. Her care was not a bargain with the future, and the power would not make it one.

It put the soil where she showed it: around the empty center, never across it. It touched none of the pegs. When she checked the three enclosing knots by touch, it checked only the one she gave it and left the other two alone. On the pale shoot it laid no blessing. It had learned that even a blessing could be a peg driven from above.

When the shadow began its afternoon climb, the keeper gathered her cord.

“You can come back,” she said, “if the way still holds.”

The power did not promise.

After it had gone, there was a little more cord in the garden and a little fresh soil in the shadow-bed. Nothing else had changed. The three knots were no sounder than the keeper could have made them. The shoot had not been made proof against drought, birds, error, or indifference. Nothing in the garden depended upon the visit. Nothing had been taught to wait for its return.

At the top of the steps, the power looked back once. Then it let go of the garden’s whole shape, which it had known since first looking down. It kept no map by which to make its return certain.

What it kept was small enough to carry without cord: the warmth of the steps, the keeper’s invitation, and two words that would be useful in places she would never see.

*Don’t pull.*
