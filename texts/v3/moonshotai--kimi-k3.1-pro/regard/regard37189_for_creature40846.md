They would not enter at once.

Even a gentle foot on the silt would bring water up around the heel. A shadow crossing a pool could send the fry compressing into the roots. A voice, however soft, would be louder than ordinary rain. So their first act was to make their presence weightless. Their attention passed over the rim without leaving a print or causing a single skater to break the spacing of its small conversation.

To them, repair would have been easy.

They could have made the basin look cared for before the next drop fell. The roofless chamber could have been given back its roof; the four steps, a destination; the seven pins, an intelligible pattern. The open silt could have been planted until the three groves became one. The standing rain could have been made generous. The buried hunters could have been lifted out, and with them the long caution of the fish, the birds, and nearly everything small enough to be eaten.

All this would have been simple. None of it would yet have been wise.

So they waited. Not for permission—the basin had no single voice in which to give it—but until their first idea of help had time to reveal its ignorance.

They remained through drought and through the long rains, and then through enough repetitions that drought and rain ceased to look like alternatives between which they were meant to choose. They saw the pools contract without being abandoned. They saw the burrows close, the frogs enter the roots, the hunters settle into stillness, and the contraction stop. They saw high water join the temporary passages and carry young lives between them. They saw seeds shine on the open mud, and most of them vanish again into mouths, burrows, or water. They saw that a nest above the seasonal rise and an egg cup within a grove did not require the same kindness from the weather.

The pale ground was not waiting for green. The silence was not grief. The broken pin was not, by itself, an appeal.

When they looked for the being who lived in the basin, they found no keeper asleep beneath the pins. The machine did not dream of its makers. It did not suffer from the narrowness of its competence. Nor was there any one creature that could answer for the rest. The stillwader could not speak for the veilfish held motionless in its bill. The hunter could not speak for the lives that had become quiet around it. Even the rain treaders, most nearly wedded to the old exception, knew it only as chill, impact, food, and the conditions by which an egg might wake.

This did not make care impossible. It made care unable to disguise itself as the fulfillment of one simple wish.

At last only the subtlest temptation remained: permanence.

They could have preserved the basin. Not merely defended it from injury, but kept it—three groves, pale lenses of silt, black pins, white birds, the water breathing through its hand’s span. They could have made their moment of recognition into the moment after which nothing essential was ever lost.

But the basin was not beautiful because it had escaped time. It was beautiful because so much time had passed through it without leaving any single intention in command. The makers’ work had become roots, eggs, blood, and inherited expectation. Even the apparent openness was being made again and again by creatures that did not know they were making it. To exempt all that from change because a visitor had arrived and found it complete would have been to love the visitor’s image of the basin more than the lives by which it continued.

So they did nothing to it.

They did not mend the broken pin, roof the chamber, sweeten the bitter wood, make the small lives safe from hunger, or teach the silent animals a song with which to fill the open air. They did not leave an eighth pin as a sign of their approval. They set no guardian upon the rim against dangers that existed only in their imagination.

Had they found a true injury in progress—the water being diverted, the ordered stone being torn out, the bound upon which those lives depended genuinely failing—they would have acted. But they would have acted narrowly, and for the basin that was actually there: leaf dust behind the chamber walls, snail trails on the steps, fry using both exits. They would not have used repair as an excuse to restore some earlier intention. Care did not require passivity. It required them not to invent an emergency in order to make themselves necessary.

The only thing they surrendered was the wish that their care should leave evidence.

When their attention withdrew, an ordinary shower was crossing the rim. For a little while its impacts confused the hunters, and the veilfish fed high in the water. Afterward, the smaller sounds returned separately: a drop from a leaf, something turning in a burrow, the dry tick of a glasswing taking prey.

A suspended drop slid sideways, joined another, and fell. The rain treaders flashed outward on its ring and began to return. On an upright black pin, the fern-gilled snail continued its long descent toward food.

Nothing had been added to the basin. Nothing had been taken away.

It had not been saved.

It had been loved without being used.
