They would be wise enough to distrust their first mercy.

Their first mercy would be light. They would see the black basin beneath the cold sky and think of imprisonment, of sight withheld, of all that growth pressing itself against the ground in darkness. But illumination would not reveal the Garden. It would revise it. The prohibition was not a bandage to be torn away, and the inhabitants were not enduring a defective version of the visitor’s world.

So they would stop at the rim.

They would not enter as warmth, because leaves would begin closing around them. They would not enter as sound, because somewhere a bell-throat would make room in its life for a voice that was never going to call again. They would not enter as a body and leave footprints holding water for days. If they entered at all, it would be only as attention, and for a long time that attention would do nothing but learn the scale of what it was tempted to help.

A single visit would not be enough. They would remain until a warmth-closing leaf had finished responding to a hand long withdrawn; until a grazer had completed some small part of its circuit; until the rough voice of a young bell-throat had become an established interval and the intervals around it had quietly changed. They would learn that stillness was not consent, just as balance was not proof that nothing suffered. A ribbon hunter still fed. Bodies still failed. Not every death became meaningless merely because roots made use of it.

But neither was suffering, by itself, an instruction to unmake the world.

They would not give sight to the fish, speech to the bell-throats, or a human loneliness to the listener. They would not end predation and call the resulting starvation purity. They would not make every life deathless within a basin that could feed only the lives already there. Nor would they make the Garden eternal merely because they had found it beautiful. Beauty is a poor excuse for denying an ending to everything except one’s own admiration.

There would be no single inhabitant to whom they could offer a gift on behalf of all the rest. Even the listener, which could receive the basin’s whole outline in one flight, did not know the chemical geography beneath one root mat. The power would understand that there was no convenient soul of the Garden to consult, and they would refuse to invent one because inventing it would make their choice easier.

In the end, they would leave no fourth pin.

They would make no promise that nothing would ever change. Their care would not be for the Garden’s resemblance to itself, but for the lives within it. If the pins failed, if warmth departed, if water began to be lost faster than the old patience could return it, they would come back. If some catastrophe arrived from beyond the rim, they might turn it aside. Even then, they would not automatically restore every former condition. Maintenance continued after it had ceased to shelter life would not be fidelity. Their intervention would be no larger than the need, and it would preserve possibilities rather than embalm the past.

Until such a time, their benevolence would consist of something powers seldom practice: remaining available without becoming present.

Once, before going, they might attend to the listener on the central stone. It would press its body against the rock, then click, receiving back rim and seep, bare stone and warm bodies, the irregular edges of growth. The power could answer with an echo from no surface. They could give the creature an outline of themselves, vast and wingless and attentive, and thereby become a new feature of its world.

They would not.

An impossible echo might become a landmark. A landmark might become an expectation. An expectation might trouble a solitude that had never been sorrow. So they would let the basin return only what was actually there.

The listener would fly. The power would withdraw. No leaf would turn, no call would quicken, and nothing in the pins would register that patience had been met by patience.

Hours later, a bell-throat would boom. Much later, another voice would occupy the room left for it.

Nothing would have been done to the Garden.

Something would have been done, permanently, to the power.
