### What the Power Did

The power did not arrive as light.

It could have made the whole basin visible, from the rounded rim to the deepest buried court, and held that visibility for as long as it wished. Instead, it left the darkness as it found it. It perceived first by warmth, then by the movement of water, then by the exceedingly small chemical differences along which something living persisted.

It did not ask where the builders had gone. It did not call the courts empty until it understood what emptiness meant there.

Beside an overflow stone, it found a closed fan. Most of the fan was mineral and old tissue; the living part would have been easy to overlook, or to preserve by accident through an act that killed it. The power could have opened it with a touch. It could have made the two necessary waters meet and held them there, gentle and inexhaustible, until the fan became more vigorous than any fan in the basin’s history.

It waited.

The wait lasted through rain and outflow, through the narrowing of passages and the failure of connections, through cycles in which whole precincts seemed to contain nothing but residue. The power learned the dry arrangement and then the wet one. It followed threads from one district to another without mistaking their arrival for choice. It watched remote fans reveal their kinship and neighboring ones remain strange to each other. It saw that the city’s connections were not merely routes through space, but an order of events.

Its benevolence did not disappear during this attention. It became less certain of its first shape.

More rain would have rescued some exhausted felts and stripped other growths from their stone. A permanent current beside the overflow would have spared one fan while founding a condition to which its folded body did not belong. Keeping every passage open would have preserved existing dispersal and ended the long differentiations produced by closure. Adding animals would have given the covered walks something to shelter and the cisterns something to carry life, but it would also have made the city an apparatus for needs it had never had. Even a richer chemistry, offered only to the living seams, would eventually have entered the water, the sediment, and the stone.

The difficulty was not simply that every intervention had consequences. It was that almost every improvement began by choosing one aspect of the basin as the part worth keeping, then treating the rest as an obstacle.

The power did not bring light. No growth could use it, and the desire to see more clearly was the power’s own.

It did not give the city a mind. A mind capable of knowing the courts and distances would also have been capable of loneliness within them. That would not have revealed a hidden inhabitant. It would have created one, and then called the creation a rescue.

Nor did the power make an idol of leaving things alone.

The first fan died while it was still learning. Its useful current failed; its layers closed, then ceased to be capable of opening. Nothing about that death was secretly beneficial. No other fan was ennobled by it. The power did not call it necessary merely because it had occurred without interference, or beautiful because the remaining lattice was delicate. A lineage ending was a loss, even where nothing feared the ending and nothing mourned afterward.

But neither did the power decide that, because nothing cried out, nothing mattered.

It grieved without making grief an authorization. It understood that benevolence could not mean compelling everything to continue, and that wisdom could not mean watching everything end with a serene face.

So, at last, it acted—but not upon the stone.

It withdrew the slight warmth and pressure of its own presence. It took no specimen, carried away no viable thread, and left behind no enduring substance by which its visit could later be detected. It did not establish a garden elsewhere from the basin’s life. A fan preserved in some kinder place would have been tissue rescued from its history, not the continuation of that history; and the power did not trust itself to choose which fragment should represent the whole.

Then it placed a limit where a limit could properly be placed: upon itself.

The basin was not to be made useful for the power’s sake. Its heat would not be taken because the power admired endurance. Its water would not be diverted to demonstrate generosity elsewhere. Its stones would not be carried off as relics, nor its last living seams collected for safekeeping. If another will subject to the power proposed to drain, seal, populate, or improve the city, the power would refuse—but it would not freeze the rain’s own work in order to make refusal easier.

It made no vow covering every possible future. A vow, it saw, could become another way of ceasing to pay attention. If it returned one day and found the last viable seam failing, it would have to learn again whether restraint was still mercy or had become only the pride of having once been restrained.

Finally, it remembered.

Not the dry city alone, and not an ideal city with every cistern full. It remembered the schedule: which court became adjacent to which buried stair, which overflow sent threads toward a pool beyond ordinary distance, which apparent isolation was only a long interval between connections. It remembered the living growths without pretending they formed an organism, and the dead lattices without pretending they had all once been alive.

It did not keep this knowledge as a blueprint. The city was not to be rebuilt from memory if it ended. A reconstruction would have satisfied the observer, not continued the place.

The watching itself was not called a gift. Nothing in the basin was better for having been seen. It was a discipline the power imposed on its own love: to know the place more exactly than its sorrow desired, and more humbly than its power required.

When the gray visibility next passed through the basin, the power saw the immense flats, the scattered precincts, the roofs and channels of different ages lying open beneath one another. Here and there, life marked the darkness almost invisibly: a pale seam, a clear fan against wet stone, a milky thread in water that had seemed untouched.

The power did not bless the sight. It did not pronounce the city perfect, or eternal, or complete.

Then visibility ceased. Rain touched stone at long intervals. Somewhere, a passage narrowed by almost nothing. Somewhere else, water resumed an old route.

When the power withdrew, no court had acquired an occupant. No cistern kept water for anyone new. No thread had been spared merely because the power had noticed it. The next cycle brought both continuations and deaths, and the city remained neither safer nor more purposeful than before.

The rain kept the city.

The city continued becoming itself.

Nothing there knew that it had been loved. Nothing there needed to. And nothing there had been changed in order to make that love easy.
