They would begin by doing nothing visible.

Not because the Sinter was perfect, and not because its inhabitants were beyond help. They would wait because the place was easy to misread, and power exercised upon a misreading can be more destructive than neglect.

For a while, they would only listen. They would learn the difference between an ordinary pump stroke and a late one, between local quiet and the wrong quiet. They would follow a phase notice from the load node where it was authorized to the outer rooms where someone began sealing cooking cultures and taking a table apart. They would see who experienced reconstruction as spectacle and who experienced it as the latest proof that their home had always been regarded as temporary. They would watch wall crews protect a colony on one surface and scour it from another. They would observe how quickly life returned—and refuse to confuse return with the absence of loss.

Their first conclusion would be that most of the obvious miracles were acts of vandalism.

To drain the basin would be to destroy the films, the eels, the waders, the veils, and much of human knowledge along with them. To silence the pumps would not necessarily bring peace; it might bring terror, followed by failure. To make every wall permanent would merely ensure that pressure, flood, and load found more catastrophic ways to change the city. To cover the ceramic with reeds and moss would be to prefer the appearance of consolation to the actual living world.

But their second conclusion would be that leaving everything alone could be just another failure of perception.

A wall recolonized is not a colony unharmed. A household reopened is not an eviction undone. Adaptation is not consent, and survival does not refund what survival cost. The persistence of an ecology does not excuse every injury within it, any more than the persistence of an address excuses what was done to the people who once lived there.

The Sinter did not need to be rescued from change. It needed change to stop being financed so reliably by those with the least ability to refuse it.

So the power would not give the basin permanence. They would give it slack: a margin in which a body could heal, a household could hesitate, a colony could survive a construction front, and a slow animal could remain slow.

Some of that slack would be bodily. Throughout the city—not only in secure foundation reaches—there would be refuges independent of address and tenancy. Places dry in the uncomplicated sense, quiet enough for injured hearing and exhausted sleep, with space for illness, infancy, age, and recovery. Possessions could be stored there without their owners having to anticipate every future phase. Food cultures could be maintained through storms. Treatment would not require anyone to surrender their home, work, or district.

No one would be compelled to live in such refuge. A pump listener could still prefer the familiar stroke transmitted through a floor; roof dwellers could still choose glare, rain, and flier colonies. The point would not be to make the Sinter’s inhabitants ashamed of their adaptations. It would be to ensure that adaptation never again had to mean simply accepting preventable damage.

Some of the slack would be temporal. Phase notices would cease to be information delivered after the meaningful decisions had been made. Those whose rooms, routes, workshops, roosts, and drainage reaches stood to be altered would have standing while alternatives still existed. They would be able to question a flood classification or load decision without first having to prove that they were important enough to deserve an answer.

This would not give every household the power to freeze a district. A city that cannot shed a floodwall can drown as surely as one that sheds homes too readily. Storm crews would still need emergency authority, and some refusals would still have to be overruled. But genuine necessity would have to be demonstrated rather than assumed. Emergency powers would end in accounting, not quietly become permanent arrangements. Sacrifice could not be assigned to the outer blocks merely because foundation holders found that assignment convenient.

A dwelling could remain sacrificial without its household being treated as sacrificial material.

If rooms had to go, there would be time and means to move what actually constituted continuity: sealed possessions, cooking cultures, tools, records, care relationships, access to work, and the routes by which people remained recognizable to one another. A promised return would have to be a possible return. Where return was impossible, that would have to be acknowledged before demolition, not discovered afterward. A bed in a distant pressure phase would not automatically count as restoration.

For the other inhabitants, slack would often look like space that had not been optimized.

Not every surface would be preserved; such a rule would make maintenance impossible and would soon become its own kind of cruelty. Door seals would still be scoured, grates cleared, conduits flushed. But reconstruction would retain connected refuges from which recolonization could begin: wet pores and drain joints, seepage margins, roosting warmth, reservoir shallows, enough old paths or adequate new ones that survival did not depend entirely on accident. Where a brief pause allowed wall-fur to withdraw or a colony to move, and the pause endangered no one, the constructors could learn to wait.

Maintenance records would begin to remember service as well as obstruction. A veil’s hydraulic signature would be watched for distress, not merely for its effect on pumping. Uncertainty about the inner life of an animal would not be treated as proof that avoidable harm cost nothing. Nor would the return of a species be allowed to erase every particular death.

Storms would still come. Warm rain would still rebound from the pavement, drains would reverse, and emergency fields would take the load with their terrible cracks. But spare refuge, spare pumping capacity, protected routes, and rested crews would mean that survival did not always depend on somebody enduring until endurance failed. The storm would no longer be permitted to reveal, again and again, exactly whose safety had been omitted from the design.

The power would not remain to approve every wall. That would make them only another, more absolute phase controller. They would leave these protections in many hands, with records open to dispute and local knowledge intact. No single map would be declared sufficient. No pump listener, reservoir warden, tenant, culture worker, or storm crew would be assumed to know the district entire.

Their help would also be refusable. A refuge that became a means of confinement would have to be changed. A protection that produced stagnation would have to be reconsidered. Even wisdom would not be allowed to make itself unquestionable. The power would remain capable of being told that they had mistaken a blessing for an injury.

Afterward, from above, a visitor might decide that nothing had been done.

There would still be no reeds. The basin would still turn the colour of heated pottery toward evening. Construction fronts would pass like squalls. Eels would appear where people had believed there were no connections. Lidbacks would be welcomed on one surface and cursed on another. Fresh ceramic would remain faintly speckled with shells and bodies. Addresses would outlive buildings, and people would continue arguing over what, exactly, had survived.

The difference would be in where the strain went.

Less of it would disappear invisibly into skin, hearing, hurried departures, broken routes, unrecorded animal deaths, and the private labour of beginning again. More of it would become visible as a cost requiring an answer, or as spare capacity deliberately provided before the emergency.

The Sinter would still be old in the way a delta is old. Its substance would still be carried away, replaced, and carried away again. Life there would still depend on the ability to resume when the substance had been moved.

But that ability would no longer be treated as limitless, costless, or free for anyone more powerful to spend.
