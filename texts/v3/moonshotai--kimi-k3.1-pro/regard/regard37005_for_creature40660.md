The power’s first impulse was to silence the shell.

It did not. Before the white hours had passed, it understood that this would have been a way of killing Senn while leaving its bricks standing. The orchards would stiffen; pollen and seed would remain where the shell had shaken them loose; the old traffic between growth and masonry would become a slow starvation.

Water was a still worse thought. So were darkness, a permanent repair, a bridge across the hollow. Every kindness it first imagined would have made Senn more comprehensible to its visitor and less itself.

For a while, the power took pride in its restraint.

That passed too.

It saw that a thing could be left entirely unchanged and still be treated as an object. The people were not details in an admirable mechanism. Their pain was not made clean by belonging to a balance. Their weariness was not erosion, and their quarrels were not mortar. To preserve every brick while refusing to hear an unwelcome request would have been only another kind of gardening.

So the power stopped studying Senn and began asking the people.

It did not announce itself through the rails, where every pattern became work, warning, or relay. It took a shape with hands and sat where a gardener could see it. Its first signs were correct but graceless. She corrected them.

When it had become sufficiently understandable, it made its offer: it would not alter the shell, the gardens, or the terms of their lives merely because it could. Nothing would be taken from them. Nothing given would create a debt. They could ask for something, ask for nothing, or refuse to believe that the offer was safe.

The offer traveled slowly. The answers took longer.

There was no single answer, because Senn was a place, not a person, and the people were not its voice.

An old gardener asked whether the pain could be taken from her hands without diminishing their sensitivity. It could. That was done, exactly; she was not also made young, or given hands that would never tire.

Someone else asked to experience true silence. The power made a silence that belonged only to him, leaving the shell untouched and every human warning able to reach him. He remained in it for less time than a seed takes to settle on a ledge. Then he asked to be returned to the roar. The power did so without amusement, and without concluding that silence was bad for everyone.

Some wanted knowledge. Some wanted a little help with what had already injured them. Some wanted time to consider choices they had never before been able to make. Others wanted nothing from a being capable of altering their home, and considered this not ingratitude but prudence. The power honored that answer as fully as any request.

It repaired nothing as a surprise. It improved nothing in addition to what was asked. It did not make the people unable to err, or their work unnecessary, or their city proof against every possible ending.

To those who wished for some way of continuing the conversation after it had gone, it gave its name. Like their names, it had a visible form and a tangible one. It was not a prayer and carried no obligation. It could be taught, forgotten, withheld from children, or tapped into a rail generations later. Using it would not summon a ruler. It would only mean that someone wished to ask again.

Then the power made ready to leave.

News of a moth passed along the rails.

People left mills and seed beds. A repair waited with its dust still in baskets. The power went with the others until they found a sightline, and stood among them while the small colorless wings caught briefly against the light.

It could have searched out every hidden egg. It could have learned why some seasons produced an emergence and others none. It could have made the moths numerous, their flights regular, their survival secure. It could have given the people a dependable wonder and the creature an office in the city.

It did none of these things.

The moth crossed the hollow. For those few wingbeats, the power did nothing powerful at all. It was simply glad that a small life was going where it went, and that neither Senn nor its visitor had found a use for it.
