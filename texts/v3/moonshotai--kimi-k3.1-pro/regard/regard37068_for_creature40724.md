### Not Vacant

The power did not enter the Cistern. To enter would have meant displacing something: a layer of mist, a little warm water, the air lying against a fern. It began instead by learning how to attend without becoming weather.

For a long time, nothing changed.

It watched more rains than it needed to watch in order to understand the mechanism. It saw construction dust leave the new cuts in milky ribbons, spores vanish through the grates, and root mats return their divided sections to the channels. It saw a damaged plate cease for a while to be rigid and then resume the obligation of being a plate. It followed the slow discontinuity of a fern bed from its original planting. Above the reservoir, where no leaf had turned quickly enough to suggest an event, it waited until one archipelago of roots was no longer quite the same archipelago.

It found no cry concealed in the tower tones, no prisoner behind the maintenance, no sleeper whose dreams were the rain. This did not prove that there was nothing to help. It proved only that help would have to begin without the vanity of rescue.

So the power did not awaken the city.

A mind was not a repair for the absence of a mind. To give the continuing city an observer might also give every unfinished obligation the force of pain, every interrupted description the frustration of an unfulfilled command. The power would not create someone merely so that its own attention could be returned.

It did not fill the empty addresses. It did not put buildings on the plots to reward their patience, or inhabitants in the buildings to reward the plots. Empty was not the same as deprived. A capacity did not become a tragedy simply because a visitor could imagine loneliness in it.

It did not introduce an animal into the reservoir to see whether the root mats would accept it. The absence of hunger was not a wound. Nor did it make the rain gentler, the chamber dry, the excavated surfaces old, or the city eternal. Persistence was one of the city’s processes. The power would not mistake it for the city’s prayer.

All these interventions would have had something in common: they would have made the Cistern easier for the power to love according to what the power already understood.

But neither did it simply go away.

The greatest danger to the Cistern did not arise within it. It arose from how easily the place could be described from outside. *Empty.* *Unused.* *Incomplete.* *Waiting.* Each word could become a permission. A later visitor might see unoccupied pavement where the power saw established relations; available machinery where it saw a continuing organisation; bare plots where it saw promises that had never been promises of company. They might cut the towers loose, clear the channels, seed the water with mouths, or generously complete the city according to a plan of their own. Afterwards they could truthfully say that nothing had protested.

The power therefore changed no stone, root, instruction, or drop of water. It changed what the Cistern’s silence would be allowed to mean.

Elsewhere—among the arrangements by which places are reached, inherited, requisitioned, or improved—it removed the assumption that silence was consent. The Cistern could no longer be entered in any ledger of purposes as vacant merely because no face appeared at its entry stair. Where a claim would otherwise have rested on there being *no one there*, the claim would fail. Not because the city had been declared a person. Not because its consciousness had been proved. Simply because the absence of a census was no longer accepted as an absence of inhabitants.

This was not invulnerability. The power did not preserve the basalt against every fracture or guarantee that the gardens would never end. To make the city incapable of ending would have been to impose upon it the heaviest and least examined of all descriptions: *this must continue*. Stone could still fail. A lineage could still narrow and disappear. Some future use might still be worth the cost of an irreversible change.

But no later hand would be permitted to call that cost nothing.

Those who came intending to transform the Cistern would first have to understand what their hands were about to interrupt: not a picturesque chamber, not an ingenious water engine, but the whole dispersed continuation of gardens, obligations, memories, and accommodations. Understanding would not compel mercy. The power had no wish to make a universe of obedient children. It would only remove the shelter of not knowing.

Then it made one provision for the city itself.

It did not give the city a voice. It made itself able to listen without requiring one.

At present, a repaired plate proved no preference. An accommodated root proved no affection. Even the movement of a survey line might remain only the least costly form of maintenance. The power did not pretend otherwise. But if, across some great succession of material, an instruction were repeatedly left unhonoured; if a boundary were revised when revision had become more difficult than obedience; if the city’s patience should ever narrow, without warning or witness, into a refusal—then that refusal would not have to become speech before it could matter.

It would not first have to become human.

It would not even have to become certain.

Having done this, the power withdrew until its presence made no further demand, not even the demand that something meaningful happen.

No plaque brightened. None of the towers missed a tone. Grey material resumed its slow honouring of damaged surfaces. Condensation fell into the reservoir and troubled the broad leaves. At the far wall, the moss remained for a while a narrow exception beyond the illuminated line. Much later, when the line moved, the power did not take the movement as a message addressed to itself.

The tower frequencies converged. Fog gathered. Warm rain struck ceramic, metal, leaves, and open water until the whole district seemed to exist only as sound.

For three minutes, the power attended without calling the city lonely, innocent, sacred, or its own.

Then the drains opened. It let the city sort itself back into gardens, surfaces, and intentions, and remained only where a future refusal might need to be heard.
