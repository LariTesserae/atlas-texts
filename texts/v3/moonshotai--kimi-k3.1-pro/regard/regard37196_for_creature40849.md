### The Dew Was Not a Sign

The power’s first thought was rain.

They saw the exposed surfaces tightening under dry air, the dark residue at the bottoms of shrinking cups, the frogs folded into chambers scarcely larger than themselves. They saw travelers shading empty basins and striders altering their routes toward weather that might never come.

It would have been easy to cover the whole summit with cloud. For a while, nothing would have thirsted.

But rain without interval would have softened the root arches, filled the galleries, and carried the Thatch’s scarce nutrients through the old drainages and over the rim. Lives adapted to waiting would have been drowned in the answer to other lives’ needs. A kindness applied at the scale of the landscape could become an erasure at the scale of its inhabitants.

Their second thought was silence. Then they heard what the chorus did: pollen loosening, dew running down the flutes, mates finding one another across distances that no small, unaided voice could cross. To spare every ear was to close many doors.

Their third thought was to leave everything untouched.

That thought lasted longest. It had the appearance of humility.

Then, at an abandoned dew station, they noticed a clay basin holding less water than a human mouth could carry away. A small cup frog crouched in the last of it. Along one side, three transparent larvae had gathered where the wall still gave a little shade. The frog was not the last of its kind. Nothing in the basin was rare. Its loss would not have altered a route, silenced a stand, or changed the outline of the plain.

It would not have been nothing, either.

So that night the power cooled one fold of the neglected catchment roof. Dew gathered along the fiber. The first drops darkened the groove without moving; later ones followed them into the basin. There was no thunder, no shining figure above the poles, no general alteration of the weather. By morning there was enough water to shade, to drink, to hunt in, and to be hunted in.

The shower that crossed that part of the interior came eleven days later.

Afterward, the power did not make noninterference into a virtue. Neither did they make interference into one.

At the scale of individual lives, they began with suffering that had ceased to do anything but continue. Pain remained where it could warn: a burnt hand still withdrew, an injured foot still demanded favor, a creature still feared the hunter it might escape. But no torn animal was required to remain in agony for hours merely because its body was slow to die. No terrified creature had to experience the whole length of a death from which there was no longer any escape. The power did not make death good. They made its usefulness to scavengers, roots, and future growth irrelevant to the question of whether the dying should be needlessly hurt.

To those whose deaths could not or should not be prevented, they offered what company could be offered without deception. For one being, that was only the loosening of fear. For another, it was a hand around a cord in the darkness beneath the floor. None had to understand the power in order not to be abandoned.

Where help was asked for, they listened. A cry did not become more worthy because it was addressed to them, or less worthy because it was only the sound of an animal in a drying cup.

Not every desire was granted. Water given to one thirst could be taken from another. A refuge made permanent could become a place where everything learned to wait for everything else. Rescue, repeated without understanding, could become another kind of weather. But the difficulty of helping well was not accepted as a reason never to help.

A lost party in full chorus might feel, through a staff pressed to the thatch, one clear answering tap from a companion. A strider with a shattered leg would not have to wait for night with every nerve alight. A traveler asking how to rebuild a failed catchment might see, with sudden completeness, why its roof had dried the basin beneath it, and how a second shade could be made without stripping a living mast. These were not rewards for good character. The traveler might be selfish; the strider might be old and common; the station-keeper might later use the knowledge badly. Help did not require a story that made its recipient deserving.

For the country as a whole, the power preserved possibility rather than appearance.

They did not stop every mast from falling or every dome from dying. They did not make human routes permanent, or preserve every custom as though poverty and impermanence were themselves endangered species. People were not required to remain scattered, simple, and picturesque for the sake of the view.

But no single fire, conquest, appetite, or well-intentioned design would be allowed to close every refuge at once. Somewhere beyond the reach of that one act, a root would remain alive, an egg would remain viable, a spore would endure, a person would remember enough to begin differently. The power made no visible ark. Their ark was room left in the future.

To the peoples of the Thatch they offered knowledge without command. Anyone sincerely willing to know what an irreversible act would cost could be answered: what water a green mast held, what nested in its upper chambers, what would come to drink from a new reservoir, whose route a settlement would sever, what burden would fall on lives absent from the discussion. The answer did not compel agreement. Some people built anyway. Some stole, stripped, and fouled. The power did not make them harmless by making them stupid, nor good by making them afraid.

No worship was required. No family was chosen to speak for all the others. No one was made an intermediary between the power and a cup of water.

Nor did they declare the existing Thatch the best country that could ever be. Its intricacy did not acquit it of cruelty, and its beauty did not make every necessity beautiful. They would not sacrifice living generations to a sudden perfection. But neither would they preserve avoidable suffering forever merely to keep the grey-green floor, the wide intervals of air, and the returning choruses exactly as they had first appeared to them.

The Thatch was not a plan to be defended. It was a multitude of lives to be cared for.

From a high mast, therefore, almost nothing seemed to have changed. Rain still came in isolated cells. Stations still failed. Cup water still darkened and shrank. Hunters still waited at the rims, and the floor still boomed beneath a strider’s misplaced weight. At full chorus, speech was still useless.

The power had not come to improve the view from the mast. They had come for what the view concealed.

Long afterward, travelers occasionally told the story of the abandoned basin that had filled with dew on a still night. Some called it a sign that the stations were watched. Others said cold nights sometimes did such things and cleared the catchment groove before moving on. The power corrected neither account.

At evening, when the masts began answering one another, they did not hear a single voice or call the whole uproar harmony. They heard courtship, warning, hunger, injury, accident, and joy; voices beyond counting, none made insignificant by their number.

They listened. Where listening was enough, they remained. Where it was not, they helped.

And the first dew had not been a covenant, a lesson, or a message to the people who might later find the basin.

It had been water.
