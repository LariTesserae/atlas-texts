They would begin by refusing the view from far away.

From far enough away, even suffering could be mistaken for circulation. A carcass became a change in vegetation. A flooded nest softened into a hummock. A hunt ended, and the account returned to dripping water. All of this was true. None of it was an answer to the animal involved.

So they would come close. They would attend to the vole carrying her young ahead of rising water, the broadfoot calf separated from the herd, the hunter waiting through rain with an injury that would not heal. They would not tell the vole that her fear belonged to a rich food web, or the cat that its hunger helped regulate the herds. Nothing wasted was not the same as nothing lost.

Nor would they mistake the plateau for a larger being entitled to spend its residents. The Quiet Works was a name for their relations, not a mouth into which their lives could rightly be poured. There was no central purpose to assist, no product whose yield should be increased, no missing caretaker whose place they ought to take. The absence of an engineer was not a vacancy.

Two kinds of love would tempt them.

One would preserve everything: these forests, these herds, these black lakes, even the present proportions of mist and open ground. But held closely enough, preservation would become taxidermy. Rivers would be forbidden to abandon their courses. Species would be prevented from giving way to others. Every landslide would be treated as damage. The plateau would survive as an image of itself while losing the freedom that had made it.

The other love would want to make everything safe.

They would be cautious with that desire, but not ashamed of it. They would not call suffering good merely because it was old, or necessary merely because they could see some of its consequences. Yet neither would they assume that replacing these vulnerable lives with different, happier lives was necessarily the same thing as helping the beings already here. Power could always call replacement improvement. It could always find, afterward, that the improved world was easier to love.

So there would be no universal edict. No sudden removal of the cats to comfort the voles, no multiplication of voles to comfort the cats, no decree that henceforth the peat would never flood a nest. They would not turn quiet into peace and then claim to have fulfilled the place.

But they would not do nothing and call that respect.

On some rain-heavy night, water that would otherwise have entered a root hollow might run a little wide of it. A vole would get her young to higher moss. Nothing in the basin would commemorate this. The rescue would not balance another nest’s loss, and it would not be offered as evidence that the world was just. It would simply be the help possible there.

Elsewhere, where a life could no longer be mended, they might lessen pain that had ceased to warn or protect. They would not make death beautiful. They would not ask the dying animal to understand anything. They would only decline to require its final terror as the price of leaving nature untouched.

Their mercies would be small and incomplete. They would not wait until help could be universal before permitting help to be real. Neither would they make a law of their first restraint. If there were ways to reduce suffering without treating the inhabitants as material for someone else’s harmony, they would remain willing to learn them. They would be slow where slowness prevented injury, and quick where slowness would only dignify neglect.

Toward ownership, they would be firmer.

If others came with ditches for the peat, shafts for the mineral seams, channels to make the distributed water legible as intake, they would not need to strike anyone down. The place would simply fail to become available. A survey would discover better ground elsewhere. A road would lose its conviction. The spring terraces would remain strange until they ceased to seem valuable.

The resemblance to a works would not become a title deed.

They would not defend the plateau against time. A lake could still fill; a forest could still fall; a whole basin could become something no present resident would recognize. What they would guard was its capacity to change without first being made someone’s possession.

Afterward, at clear noon, there would be little to show. Water would bead on stone. Iron would redden at a seep. A herd would turn along the wet ground, and a peat dome would give its nearly silent sigh. The plateau would still not be peace. Its richness would still not make any individual life secure.

The power would not have solved that. They would have refused two easier things: to worship the whole, and to improve it until it belonged to them.

They would leave no name and require no gratitude. They would not call what they had done enough. They would keep listening, close enough that their love of the place could never become merely a love of the view.
