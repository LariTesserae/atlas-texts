### A Little More Time

The power listened for a long time before doing anything.

It heard the seeps beneath the lanes and the brief, delicate knocking of picks on new crust. It heard questions repeated outward from the settling court, and quarrels carried onto the lakebed so that walls would not have to hold them. It heard children being extremely loud on the roofs. It heard an old keeper say “the last time,” carefully, and another say “never,” with the rain already entering his shoes.

It also heard how often a household’s first knowledge of trouble was a wet sleeping mat, a heaved floor, or water rising where someone more fortunate had insisted water could not go.

Rill did not need to be taught that everything was connected. It had learned that through its floors.

The power would not return the lake. That would merely drown one arrangement in order to restore another. It would not bind the seeps into permanent courses; pressure denied one path would choose another, perhaps a worse one. It would not give the Rillfolk a perfect map. A perfect map would become a cherished error, and more dangerous than the unfinished one they already carried.

Nor would it make the whole lakebed firm and green in a night. That would answer, from outside, a question the Rillfolk were still asking among themselves: whether to remain crowded together on the hummock, to build outward, or to abandon the lowest rooms. Their uncertainty was not foolishness. The ground had given them no good reason to be certain.

But neither would the power leave them untouched because their labor was ingenious and their customs admirable. Admiration, by itself, would have been a very clean way of abandoning them.

So it gave Rill notice.

From that time on, water that was about to force a new way into a dwelling knocked first.

The knock came from the stone nearest the gathering pressure: three small blows, as if someone stood outside a door and wished to be admitted. It was not a voice. It carried no instruction. Sometimes it sounded several days before the first stain; sometimes only a morning. It might have to come more than once before anyone believed it.

But a child could hear it as plainly as a channel-keeper. It sounded beneath a disregarded floor as readily as beneath an influential one. It sounded when the pressure had gathered by itself, and when its course had been helped along by a board someone had set in the dark and hoped not to mention.

The power did not say where the water had come from. It did not say where it should go. It did not soften the crust, clean the settling court, or make a hidden board confess its owner. It only ensured that the first consequence was not always damage.

The first knocking occurred beneath a sleeping platform in one of the lowest rooms.

For a little while, the household caused considerable annoyance by knocking back. Then someone went to the outer wall to see whether a neighbor wanted admittance. Only a child, still lying on the platform, insisted that the last three blows had come from below.

The stones there were dry. An experienced keeper said, honestly, that no channel ran beneath them. That was true according to the last arrangement anyone remembered.

They lifted the access slabs anyway.

By the time a clay-red thread of water appeared at the foot of the shared wall, baskets were already standing in the lane. Two keepers were disagreeing about the source, three households about the overflow, and everyone about who had been slowest to come. A storage shelf had to be taken down. A jar broke. Somebody said something about a neighboring household’s grandfather that made the work briefly slower.

But the floor did not heave. The wall did not burst. No one had to spend the first day proving that the water existed before being allowed to ask for help.

That was the heart of the gift. It spared them neither work nor disagreement, but it made it harder for surprise to turn one household’s uncertainty into everyone else’s emergency.

In time the knocking became ordinary enough to be resented. People cursed it when it woke them before dawn. Keepers were occasionally insulted by the suggestion that a stone had noticed something before they had. Children incorporated it into the questions belonging to the second map:

*When the stone knocks, what do you do?*

*Wake the house.*

*And then?*

*Wake the next one.*

They were careful, after some painful mistakes, not to teach that silence meant safety. Rain could still enter a roof without knocking. A neglected channel could still narrow. A person could still hear the warning, roll over, and hope the pressure belonged to somebody else.

The power had not made the Rillfolk wise. It had only made wisdom a little less dependent on being the first person believed.

No shrine was built. Some people called the knocking the seep’s courtesy. Others called it stone-knock and objected to any suggestion that the water had manners. A few maintained that such warnings had always occurred and that people had simply begun listening properly. The argument remained available whenever the more urgent arguments were exhausted.

The power stayed until the gift had become part of the work, then did nothing further.

The water-cress remained bitter and sparingly harvested. Crust still fused jar bases to shelves. Higher households could still speak pleasantly while imagining inconvenience downhill. Younger people still spoke of building outward, and others still distrusted the new silt. The gray heron still arrived without reference to any human arrangement, and work still stopped when it did.

On the power’s last morning, a heron stood beside one of the outer pools. Rillfolk gathered quietly at stairs and doorways to watch. The power, for whom no one had stopped work, was content.

It had added no eternal thing to Rill. It had added a little time between pressure and consequence: time to wake a neighbor, time to credit the lowest household, time to lift a stone and ask not only where water might be taken, but where it would go when the plan failed.

The rest remained theirs.
