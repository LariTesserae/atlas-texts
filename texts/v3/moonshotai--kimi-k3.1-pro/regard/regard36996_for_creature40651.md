### A Mercy of Continuity

The power would begin with the life in front of it.

An antecedent had already made its stroke. It had read the early pressure correctly, opened its fins at the right moment, and committed its whole clear body to the hollow. But correctness was not protection. The interval was going to close while part of the animal remained outside it.

The power would not stop the concussion. It would not silence the mountain, preserve the cliff, or lift the swimmer out of the sea. It would hold only one small relation steady until the stroke was finished: the hollow would remain a hollow, and the distance being crossed would remain distance.

Then the animal would be through.

It would fold its fins and hang beside a rib while stone broke somewhere behind it. Its gut would still be nearly empty. It would know nothing except that it was here, and that the water immediately around it could be used.

Only afterward would the power consider whether anything else was needed.

It would not decide quickly. Wisdom, in the Nave, would first resemble patience.

It would not still the basin merely because the roar was intolerable to a visitor. It would not make a calm lake and call the substitution rescue. It would not give the antecedent eyes, the snail a map, or the films a fortress. It would certainly not complete the broken horseshoe and populate it with grateful life. The mountain had already produced the appearance of shelter; there was no need to repeat that misunderstanding with better masonry.

But neither would the power romanticize what it left alone. Adaptation was not consent. The snail’s patience did not make erasure good, and the antecedent’s narrow margin was not beautiful simply because the animal sometimes crossed it. The power would not preserve danger for the sake of austerity, nor confuse a complete ecology with a painless one.

It would ask, instead, where help could be given without replacing the lives being helped.

Almost everything necessary, it would find, was already being done—not by the Nave, but by its inhabitants. The films continually shed their vulnerable surfaces. The algal threads sealed their wounds. The snail carried living cells without knowing that it carried them. The antecedent’s young began within the mucus rather than in open water. Even the Nave’s failures were usually partial, leaving some seam outside the broken relation.

The kindest grand act would therefore be a small act placed exactly where that sufficiency ended.

For the Nave itself needed to conserve nothing. Its inhabitants did. And there was no promise that, after some especially comprehensive unmaking, enough of them would remain.

So the power’s lasting intervention, if one was needed, would be this:

**No convulsion would be allowed to take every living remainder.**

Not every life. Not every brood. Not every patch of film.

Only enough.

A few cells enclosed in mucus might remain viable until another hot seam offered stone. A sealed fragment of wine-dark thread might survive the disappearance of its first holdfast. Somewhere, not always in the same place or from the same brood, an egg capsule or a flattened transparent juvenile would cross a transition that might otherwise have left nothing alive to cross it.

These remnants would not be deathless. They could still scald, starve, fail to attach, or be stripped away by an ordinary concussion. There would be no hidden abundance and no appointed age in which the Nave became a reef. The promise was not prosperity. It was only that continuation would never depend upon life inventing itself again from sterile water.

If the basin’s strange recurrences already accomplished that much, the power would add nothing. There would be no requirement that benevolence leave a mark merely to prove that it had been present.

To an observer, therefore, almost nothing would change. A fresh ledge might occasionally carry a mature film. A grazing track might end against bare stone on one wall and appear to resume on another. A transparent speck might persist in mucus whose supporting rock had no continuous history anyone could establish. These events would remain too uncertain to become doctrines. The inhabitants would neither worship the power nor know that they had been helped.

The horseshoe would stay empty. The two upright stones would mark no route. No voice would make itself heard above the water, because there was nothing the snail or the swimmer needed to be told.

The Nave would go on tearing itself into sharpness and dusk. Films would vanish. Snails would wait until waiting became impossible. Antecedents would still misjudge hollows, or find none within reach, and become brief clouds of milkiness in the olive water. The power had not made the place safe.

But the first swimmer would finish its stroke. And life as a whole would retain one narrow assurance that the landscape itself had never possessed.

Not silence. Not permanence. Only this: beginning again would not always have to mean beginning from nothing.
