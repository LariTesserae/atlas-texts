## The Allowance

The power came before dawn, when Noll was making its water.

There was no sign in the sky. The fans continued their patient rushing; moisture gathered in the seams of the plates; the pump kept its own counsel. The power gave its attention first to one drop, from its beading on cold iron to its fall into the glazed trough, and then to everything that altered because of the fall.

It listened until the works ceased to be a single noise. It heard the keeper’s map of pressure and numbered valves, the frog’s map of shelter and temporary water, the rook’s bright map of roofs and stealable things. It followed the alder roots down into mortar. It found the map no inhabitant had drawn: soot, shell-lime, leaf mould, cinder and rust slowly agreeing to become soil.

Noll, it saw, was not a machine being improved by nature, nor nature imprisoned in a machine. It was a young compact among unlike lives, still discovering which of its accidents could be kept.

The power considered water. A river would have been easy. It might have benefited many creatures, but the condensers would have become curious antiques, the overflow rules meaningless, the green seams first common and then invisible. Much of what Noll knew would have become unnecessary. It would have been rescue by replacement: another place wearing Noll’s name.

It considered sealing every genuine waste, making every repair painless and every human inhabitant unfailingly gentle. But care compelled would have been only another works order, and the power did not wish to become the most efficient machine in Noll.

For a while, it seemed that the wisest course was to leave.

Yet admiration is not always benevolence. A puddle’s last hour might be poignant to a distant mind; it was not poignant to the spawn in it. The power did not mistake a large intervention for a large kindness. Neither did it mistake Noll’s beauty for proof that all its losses were necessary.

So it looked again, not for what was picturesque, but for what was needlessly final.

Then it made one alteration.

Beneath the Alder Overflow, where a hand’s depth of dark soil lay over the young stone, it changed nothing visible. It only made a narrow seam of that stone less willing to surrender the last of its damp.

Not a spring. Not a hidden cistern. No water appeared from nowhere, and the tower’s yield did not improve. The surface could still dry. The mint could brown. Frog spawn could still be stranded, and the sticklebacks would remain as dependent as ever on their small stone-walled world. But below, in the root-dark, a trace of moisture would persist through the longest ordinary drought: enough that not every delicate thread below the soil would have to be broken, not every living continuity returned to nothing.

It was a refuge, not an exemption.

The power had made Noll fractionally less forgetful. When water returned after a long absence, some green seams would be able to return from within themselves, rather than beginning again entirely from seed in wagon mud and spores on packing timber.

The alteration lay no deeper than a spade could reach. Anyone could dig it out, drain it, or build over it. The power left no prohibition and attached no punishment. It was not interested in keeping Noll small, or quaint, or unchanged. If the people someday laid foundations there, or chose a different future for the basin, the gift would not avenge itself. A kindness that cannot be refused is only a more agreeable command.

Then the power watched. It watched to discover whether its refuge had made a trap, whether roots would damage a main, whether some creature would gather there in numbers the place could not support, whether water would go missing from a ledger already written close to the margin. It was prepared to be wrong.

Years later, in a dry summer, a keeper noticed that the soil beneath the oldest alders remained dark after the surface moss had crisped. Following Noll’s custom, the place was inspected before it was repaired. There was no failed joint. No main ran beneath it. A wren objected from somewhere inside the root mat.

In the maintenance book, after the morning’s yield and pressure readings, the keeper wrote:

*Alder Overflow. Dry above. Damp at roots. No loss found. Leave and recheck.*

The power had received no altar. It had been entered in the maintenance book.

Nothing else was settled. Plates continued to narrow with scale. Rooks continued to steal wire. Some broods succeeded and some did not. The basin kept its slow geological counsel. Houses might still be built on the empty surveyed streets, or grass might keep them. Noll remained free to become something its present inhabitants would not recognize.

But its failures were not all quite so absolute.

At dawn the pump struck, the rooks rose shouting, and water ticked into the trough. Beneath the alders the soil stayed dark. When the mint returned, it returned along the old line.

The power had added no abundance and issued no law. It had slowed one small departure, and left the rest to be arranged.
