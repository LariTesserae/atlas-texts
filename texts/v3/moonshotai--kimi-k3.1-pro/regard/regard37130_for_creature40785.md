### What the Power Did

The power’s first thought was to make the rain kind.

Not more rain, at first. Only knowable rain. It could have left every device unchanged and merely made their waking legible: a pressure in the feet of slatebacks, a certain slant of light that travellers could trust, a pattern beneath the apparent disorder. Fewer people would have followed distant kites until their water was gone. Fewer calves would have been led toward a seep remembered one season too late. Covered basins could have been visited before their water vanished, and no device would have received the name *Late Water*.

The power did not pretend that this would be anything but a mercy.

It looked further.

Where rain could be expected, journeys could be built upon expectation. Some journeys would become shorter and safer. Others would become unnecessary. The old, dispersed knowledge of the herds might be lost, or might change into something no less valuable. Among people, the first certainty would be shared freely; later ones might not be. Water that can be promised can also be guarded. A crossing that never fails can become a boundary. None of this was inevitable. That was why the power hesitated. It could make the change, but it could not honestly claim to know which world it would be giving, and those receiving the gift would have no way to hand it back.

So it considered the opposite kindness: to leave everything untouched and admire the completeness of what was already there.

There was much to admire. The beetles did not commit a whole generation to one shower. The trees kept their lives mostly out of sight. A herd preserved knowledge by refusing to gather all its animals in one place. Even human camps were made so that the country could forget them. Nothing depended upon being right every time.

But the system did not feel thirst. Individuals did.

A dead calf was not made acceptable by the persistence of the herd. An unsuccessful hunt was not painless because stillness conserved energy. A traveller who had replaced every stone lid and remembered every warning could still die through no failure worth calling a lesson. The power saw that it could turn restraint into a kind of vanity: the pleasure of being the only being wise enough to appreciate why no one should be helped.

It refused that vanity also.

For a while, therefore, it did nothing. It stayed long enough to discover that doing nothing was not a single act but a succession of decisions, each requiring as much attention as rain.

It did not wish to become the climate of the uplands, the memory of every herd, the instinct of every hunter, and the inaudible correction of every human error. A mercy so continuous would leave only one creature truly acting in the world. Yet neither would it make a covenant of non-intervention and then sacrifice the living to its own consistency. If a life required rain, it might rain. If help had to be visible, it would allow itself to be seen. It would not protect its reputation for subtlety.

But it would not make its answers inevitable.

It would remain, as nearly as it could understand the matter, in reserve.

This did not mean preserving the uplands exactly as they were. Preservation could become another form of possession. The wayhouse might one day fill with sand. Wireleaf might spread or vanish from a fissure it had occupied for centuries. People might settle, or leave, or cease to be rain-readers without ceasing to be wise. Even the devices might eventually become only smooth stones whose former consequences persisted in the routes of animals. The power did not intend to keep the country as a specimen of admirable difficulty.

Instead, it would watch for the difference between a loss and the closing of every reply. Between an empty basin and the destruction of all covered water. Between a false gathering of kites and a world in which no creature was permitted to be mistaken. Between change and the peculiar finality that came when some single will—however benevolent—decided that nothing further needed to be learned.

Most of its care would never be distinguishable from restraint. Some of it might one day be indistinguishable from luck. It made no rule that its interventions must always be small, only that smallness should not become an excuse for withholding what was needed.

Then, because there was something immediately before it, the power performed its first act.

At the three-quarter ring, someone had recently drunk from a covered basin. The traveller had not been careless, only tired and hurried; the stone lid had been put back slightly askew, leaving a gap no wider than two fingers. Through it, the warmth of the afternoon was entering. By evening a little of the water would be gone. By the next afternoon, a little more.

The power set the lid flush.

No device woke. No light deepened above the slab. There was nothing to tell a later traveller that the place had received divine attention, and nothing about the act that required such attention ever to be given again.

The slatebacks continued toward water that might already have disappeared. The pale hunter remained hungry. At the wayhouse, the cistern-nester pushed sand from its entrance and waited for the vibrations overhead to cease. The power did not call any of this good. Neither did it decide that its own uncertainty licensed it to rearrange everything.

It had not solved the problem of living there. It had declined to become the sort of solution that made everything else unnecessary.

Above Wrong Kites, the translucent birds gathered from separate thermals, wheeled for an hour, and then dispersed beneath an empty sky. The power did not correct them.

Beneath the stone lid, a little water stayed cool.
