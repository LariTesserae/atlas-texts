### No Second Loom

The power’s first impulse was to give Serein rain.

Not the measured islands that crossed a court and vanished into stone throats, but a generous darkening of the whole sky: water on every causeway, every fissure filled, the bare intervals softening into soil. In the vision, green spread from precinct to precinct until the basin no longer resembled an archipelago. The browser’s kind became common. No cistern failed. No spore arrived on dry ground and found nothing with which to continue.

The vision was beautiful. That was why the power waited.

It looked longer.

In the cistern courts it found no catastrophe that required disguising itself as abundance. People cooked, repaired small things, grew old, and traveled enormous distances to sit close together. Some were lonely; some were not. Some work had gone wrong and would have to be done again. The power did not mistake emptiness for peace, or scarcity for virtue. But neither did it mistake every difficulty for an emergency, or every uncompleted thing for a wound.

Then it considered the ground.

Here was the fact that decided almost everything: Serein endured by becoming harder to command.

Young land was agreeable. Mature land had acquired objections—root and void, acid and salt, a thousand histories too small for any clean description. Its durability was not stillness. It was the accumulating inability of one will, however far-seeing, to say exactly what the land was and therefore what it ought to become.

A miracle that made all the ground permanently stable would still be a description imposed upon it. A miracle that made every garden flourish would still be a survey. The power did not wish to become a gentler Loom.

It saw the low browser, too: not as the residents did, through prints and stripped fern tips, but whole, resting beneath a copper-fronded canopy with dust on its soft pads. The power could have made it visible for the residents’ delight, or numerous for the gardeners’ convenience. Instead it watched the animal rise, pass beneath a vine-covered arch, and carry a trace of one damp place into another. Rarity was not, by itself, a request for multiplication. A life did not become more worthy because something greater could imagine a larger version of it.

At the overflowing cistern, the power nearly changed its mind.

The little channel seemed so easy to protect. It could have been given permanent water, secure banks, a course safe from revision. It could have been made certain.

But to fix its course would preserve the evidence while destroying the event. The channel mattered because it could go elsewhere; because it could fail and another line could begin; because no authority had yet decided what its success would look like. Even the hope that it might one day become a stream, and then a river, was a hope the power put down. A river would not be more righteous than a damp hollow that lasted one additional hour.

Still, the power made no religion of restraint. Not acting could be vanity too.

Had someone asked for water, it would not have answered with a lecture on rootlace. Had one failure been about to close every cistern and silence every condenser, it would not have called extinction a necessary stage in the basin’s education. Its wisdom did not require helplessness, and its benevolence did not require abstinence.

So it set one limit upon itself.

It would make no unasked revision of Serein. It would give the place no destiny, not even a green one. If intervention ever became necessary to prevent an ending from which nothing could answer, it would preserve as many next steps as possible—and then stop, leaving those steps to the lives that would have to inhabit their consequences.

Having decided this, there was very little left to do.

Before withdrawing, the power took the form of a visitor and followed the three instructions. It kept out of the unplanted survey polygons, though nothing there could have imprisoned it. It stepped around exposed white rootlace, though it could have restored anything it severed. When a shadow appeared at the growing edge with nothing to cast it, the power moved away.

Omnipotence did not make it exempt from the manners of a place.

It took no dust, left no token, and entered nothing in the survey. It did not designate the channel as sacred. It did not tell the gardeners that they had been right to leave it alone. Their restraint did not need to be confirmed by something that had never had to wait for rain.

The next overflow came days later, no larger than usual. Water darkened the first fans of sediment, hesitated at a fork, and went a little farther into ground that had never held it. Another hollow remained dry. Weeks afterward, a white thread followed the dampness. A fern appeared where no fern had been planted.

No one called any of this a miracle.

The power had not made Serein safe, finished, or holy. It had refused to make Serein its own.

It left no blessing in the dust. It left the next line unwritten.
