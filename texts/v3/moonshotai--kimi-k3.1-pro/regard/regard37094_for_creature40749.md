## What the Power Did

The power’s first act was to stay longer than the roar.

It had come because of a life in a drop of rainwater: a rotifer unfolding among wet moss leaves, feeding through an interval that might end with sun, wind, or the next cold hour. Around that minute event the basin made its weather-like concussion. Steam tore from a vent. Water hammered inside steel. The power did not ask which was the true voice of the place.

It could have given the High Moss silence.

It could have closed the wells without cooling the rock, laid the pipes gently down, and returned every plinth and saddle to the manufacturers’ dreams. But it followed the power lines beyond the rim. It saw that the works were not simply an appetite with pipes attached. It also saw the workers arranging their gestures before entering loud ground, sleeping badly, learning the ground with their knees and hands. To break the machines would have been easy. To call that healing would have been careless.

It could have made the basin warm. It could have softened the lava, deepened the pools, hastened the moss, and caused every candle to flower beneath a sky full of willing insects. It did not. It had no wish to replace the place with something more readily admired. The hottest water was not the richest. Greenness was not always safety. Even an unsuccessful flowering became shelter, collected water, and entered the mat. To preserve every pool, every outlet, and every stalk would have made a keepsake of the High Moss, not a living place.

Nor did the power find one resident who could be saved on behalf of all the others.

The rosette could not speak for the beetle. The beetle could not speak for the films in the warm runnels. The films could not speak for the worker cleaning them out of a condensate trap. Even the rotifer, whose brief unfolding had first drawn the power’s attention, was not an emblem. It was only itself, which the power considered sufficient.

For a while, therefore, it did nothing.

It watched a pool shrink without disaster, dormant life waiting in its damp margins. It watched an outlet shift and a rust-coloured film whiten and die. It watched a flower stalk stand through several winters after its flowering had failed. Not every ending required opposition. Much of the basin’s continuance depended on things being allowed to pass out of their present forms.

But neither did the power mistake patience for permission.

A pool drying with the season was not the same as a seep severed by a track whose makers had never known the seep existed. A flowering wasted by cold weather was not the same as a whole district being treated as empty because its inhabitants were too small, too slow, or too irregular to appear on drawings. Frost worked patiently, but patience did not make frost answerable. Human beings could answer. Their speed was not itself a fault; their inability to see what moved at another speed was not always a fault either. It became one only when no time was allowed for looking.

So the power made one alteration, too small for any instrument to record.

It changed the interval before certain decisions.

Henceforth, when someone was about to commit the basin to a change whose consequences would outlast their ready means of repair, that person was offered one breath of exact knowledge. Not a command. Not a vision of catastrophe. Not guilt, which the power considered a blunt and often self-regarding instrument. Only the connections themselves: where the water went after it vanished; what waited in the mud; which fracture remained warm; how many years of dead stems lay beneath a firm green surface; who would have to cross the ground afterward; what maintenance would remove, and what maintenance would preserve.

The knowledge could be refused. It could be forgotten. It did not make anyone good.

A well was drilled. Another was not. A proposed track remained crooked for a reason that, twenty years later, no one could quite remember. A hydrologist added three seepages to the list of things worth measuring. One discharge was kept off a root mat that had previously appeared on the plans as unoccupied ground. Elsewhere a film was cleaned away because the equipment required it, and the cleaning went ahead without either reverence or pretence that nothing had lived there.

The turbine hall did not fall silent. Wet clothing still chafed. The light still burned. Workers still disagreed about whether the place was magnificent or merely miserable. Beetles remained absent from pools that seemed entirely suitable. A rosette flowered in a cold week and set almost no seed. The raven crossed, dropped behind a ridge, and vanished.

The power had not come to make the High Moss easy to love.

Then it did one smaller thing.

It returned to the raindrop on the moss leaf.

The rotifer was still active. The power stayed while it fed, while something smaller passed through the water, while the drop trembled under the pressure of distant steam. It did not enlarge the drop. It did not exempt the animal from cold, dryness, feeding, or stillness. It did not promise that dormancy would always be followed by another unfolding.

This was not a rescue. The power did not believe that every good action had to be a rescue.

It remained because concern that never returned to one life, in one drop, at one particular afternoon, could become only another way of looking from the rim.

Afterward there was no sign of its visit. From above, white steam still stood sparsely over the green-black floor. Pipes crossed the moss. Water disappeared into rock. A valve received fresh paint, and a drying stalk lost another fibre.

Only now and then, someone with a boot raised above green ground found that they had time—just time—to ask what the greenness held.

Sometimes they put the boot down anyway.

Sometimes they moved.

The power had not finished the High Moss. It had made its unfinishedness harder to mistake for emptiness.
