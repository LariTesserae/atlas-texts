### The Open Hand

The power came without light. Light would have made an event of their arrival, and the basin had no need of events. They left the wind at the rim, stooped beneath the wet branches, and remained still until condensation had gathered on whatever shape they wore.

Their first thought was that the seep needed opening.

They could have given the canopy a gap and let one blade of sunlight travel across the floor. They could have sent a flood through the outflow, allowed a shell heap to rise beneath the overhang, or caused a single fallen tree to remain fallen long enough to alter everything around it. They could have put into the gray animal’s hand a pebble whose edge would survive the opening of that hand.

Each gift would have been easy. Each would have left evidence afterward. The power distrusted that resemblance among them: every gift was also a way of making their visit visible.

So they waited.

They watched until they could tell silence from silencing, and a completed act from an interrupted one. They saw that the birds’ quiet was not necessarily a song stopped in their throats. They saw the gray animal reject a twig, choose another, correct the angle of its wrist, and feed. When the twig fell, nothing had been lost merely because nothing had been kept. Nor did that make kept things contemptible. Elsewhere, a path might be a kindness and a store of food might save a child through winter. Here, the absence of paths was not, by itself, a wound.

The power did not call the seep perfect.

A moth was still a moth before it was a vacancy closing on a trunk. When something struggled beneath a fern, the power did not look away and admire the efficiency with which its body would be subdivided. They did not love the balance more than the lives balanced within it. If they had found a captive will beneath the stillness—a mind prevented from remembering, a voice compelled into quiet, a being straining against a future denied it—they would have broken the equilibrium without ceremony. They would have accepted the cloudy water, the opened canopy, the irreversible alteration of the basin. No shape, however exquisite, would have been worth the keeping of a prisoner.

But they found no such captivity.

There were appetite and fear, injury and death, choice and failure. There were animals accommodating one another, animals feeding, animals simply enduring the proximity of others. Their lives were not painless. They were not unfinished. The silence did not ask to be broken merely because the power was capable of breaking it.

At length the power sat near the dripping lip, making themselves no brighter than wet fur and no warmer than the stone.

One of the gray animals was there. It rose slightly, drew its forepaws toward its chest, and watched. The power did not approach, touch it, or enter its thoughts. After a while the animal shifted enough to make the small room necessary. The power accepted exactly that much room. For a time they were only a temporary difficulty of space, and did not demand to become anything more.

The animal selected a snail, turned it, and put it aside. It chose another. Water gathered along its forearms and darkened the fur between its fingers. When it picked up a pebble, the power felt again the nearness of intervention: how little would be required to improve the blow, to preserve the tool, to make this moment the beginning of something.

The animal struck once.

It ate. Its fingers opened. The pebble fell among the other wet stones.

The power opened their own hand. There was nothing in it.

Before leaving, they made no promise over the seep. They did not bind it against erosion, conceal it behind everlasting cloud, or appoint themselves the guardian of its present form. To preserve it forever might have been only to become another thing that held it. If change ever arose from within—a favored cup carried twice, a path kept open, a young animal shown what an older one knew—the power would not come secretly in the night to take those things away. Nor would they arrive to applaud them as the beginning of what the animals had been meant to become.

Their only continuing act was attention. If a request ever came from the basin, in whatever form a request could take, they would not answer it with a lecture on sufficiency. They would not point to the clear water and the closed canopy as though beauty settled every question. And if no request came, they would not invent one in order to make themselves necessary.

They carried away no specimen, no stone, and no map. They carried the memory of one animal: not as evidence of intelligence, not as a creature halted before its destiny, but as the particular being who had rejected one snail, accepted another, and changed the angle of its wrist before striking.

That memory made the power careful. Not passive. Careful.

Then they stepped back across the rim into wind, sound, and consequence.

Behind them, a drop fell into a crevice. Wings adjusted on a trunk. The seep did not brighten, quicken, or give thanks. Nothing had been added to it, and nothing had been taken away.

It had not been saved.

It had not been abandoned.

It had not been kept.
