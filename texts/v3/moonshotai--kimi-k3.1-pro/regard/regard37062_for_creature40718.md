### One Grain

The power came without light.

It brought no warmth, either. Warmth would have opened the clear rinds of ice, altered the seepage, and set small currents moving through fractures that had remained stable for ages. It made no pressure-shadow above the summits and displaced no water with its arrival. Nothing turned toward it. No stem quickened, no cup opened, and the old cell did not change the note it sounded in the rain.

For one whole turning of the water, the power only listened.

It learned to separate the roar of a pass from the deeper vibration passing through the ridge beneath it. It heard sand travel over schist, veilgrass ribbons fold in succession, and ice tick inside the stone. It waited through the uphill current and the long return of the rain. It attended to the spaces between gardens as carefully as to the gardens themselves.

At first, its attention rested on the made things. The hollow threshold suggested departure. The seventeen steps suggested interruption. The whole submerged province suggested, to a mind accustomed to other places, that something had been taken away.

But those suggestions belonged to the power, not to the Catchment.

No maker remained to be restored to the cell. Nothing desired a platform above the stair. The gardens were not waiting for sun, and the water was not an injury from which the mountains had failed to recover. Even the dead frond beds, layer upon compacted layer, were not evidence of a task left unfinished. They were simply what persistence looked like when nothing came to consume it.

So the power did none of the large things.

It did not drain the province. It did not raise the summits into air or hang a light above them. It did not quicken the plants, people the silence, or provide mouths for the dead. A gift of universal growth would only have made the root mats outrun their seeps. A gift of animals would have introduced hunger into a place whose lives had no defense against it. To have made the Catchment more readily recognizable as flourishing would have been to destroy its actual inhabitants in the name of their good.

Then its attention came to the fresh fracture beneath the ice.

The filament was there, colorless against the wet schist. It was not the last of its kind. It was not the beginning of a new kind. Nothing depended on it that would not, in some other age, depend on another spore lodged in another seam. There was no request in it, and no sorrow. There was only a little living chemistry maintained against the cold.

The power saw that, during the next ascent, a flake of black schist would probably be carried into the fracture. Its edge would lodge against the ice. Silt would gather behind it. The seep would not stop entirely, but its course would change by enough. The filament would remain for a while, then cease to maintain the small difference between itself and the surrounding water.

It would have been possible to call this necessary.

It was not necessary. It was merely one of the accidents by which the province continued.

The power moved the flake.

That was all. One dark fragment settled slightly farther down the fracture, among other fragments, and the thread of seepage remained where it was. No radiance passed through the water. No covenant was written in the stone. The filament did not know that anything had been done, or that anything had needed doing.

The power did not then go searching for every other spore. It did not intercept every burial, hold open every failing seam, or guide each broken growing tip toward shelter. To secure every beginning would not have preserved the Catchment; it would have replaced the conditions under which its lives had become what they were.

Nor did it make the filament sacred. It had not been chosen for a destiny. It was simply the life before the power, and the help it could use was small enough to give without wounding anything else.

To refuse that help because others beyond counting would receive none would have been consistent. It would not necessarily have been wise.

Before withdrawing, the power looked once more through the whole province: the lush local gardens and the bare fluted slopes, the pressure gathering above the passes, the comparatively buoyant stillness of the gorge, the old copper-green cairns, the artifacts around which sediment gathered without preference. It took away no spore, bowl, or fragment of quartzite. It left no guardian over the ruins and appointed no age in which the province would be healed.

It remembered. That imposed no weight on the water.

Afterward, a pass began to roar. The ascending current took the old stair at its usual angle. Silt crossed the cell’s threshold. Loose spores lifted from the basins, most of them traveling toward scoured stone, deep burial, or still water. Cup-leaf vessels remained open to the mineral dust. The gardens continued their almost imperceptible movement along the seeps.

The filament might still freeze. It might be stripped away in the next rain, or fail much later with its mineral seam. The power had not promised it a grove, another cycle, or even one further branch. It had prevented no fate. It had only turned aside one ordinary accident.

Under the clear ice, the seep continued to touch the living thread.

It was almost nothing, measured against a province.

The power had not measured it that way.
