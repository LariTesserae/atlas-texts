### Exact Mercy

The power listened until it no longer wanted the chord to change.

It did not enter. There was no need. Without casting a shadow or warming the stone, it could know every continuous path by which leaf became conductor, conductor became root, and root became part of the engine’s hard anatomy. Nothing in the chamber was hidden from it.

At first, something like pity arose in the power. This was not foolishness. It had known many lives in which stillness meant exhaustion, dryness meant desolation, and an unvarying note meant that some voice had been worn down until only repetition remained. Its benevolence had been formed among perishable things. It knew how often mercy must arrive quickly, carrying water.

So it looked for the injury.

There was none. No leaf had been prevented from opening. No seed had failed to form. No prisoner waited inside the engine, and no sleeper dreamed beneath the pavement. The garden’s lack of alternatives was not a deprivation inflicted upon it. There was no neglected wish, no suppressed development, no suffering concealed by perfection. The power was too wise to mistake the absence of everything it usually healed for evidence of a wound it had not yet found.

Then it considered gifts.

The smallest seemed to be a bud.

Only one: a little folded possibility beneath a translucent scale, so slight that the chord need not falter. The power could have made it without disturbing anything else. For an instant, the gesture had all the innocence of generosity.

But a bud would not have been merely a shape. It would have been a direction. Around it would gather *before* and *after*, readiness and delay, the possibility of opening and the possibility of failing to open. The garden would acquire something to await. Then there would be something to protect, something whose loss could matter to the protector. The power could easily have spent eternity defending the garden from the incompleteness it had just bestowed.

It did not call that life. The garden was already alive.

Nor did it give the garden a mind with which to appreciate its own accordance. Appreciation would have pleased the giver, perhaps, but the garden would have borne the cost: an interior in which duration might become boredom, attention might become loneliness, and the unchanging note might at last become a question no one could answer.

The power put away both gifts.

Its next desire was better, but still not wholly wise. It wished to preserve the chamber forever—to turn aside every falling stone, every careless hand, every slow astronomical accident. It could have made destruction impossible without touching so much as a filament of moss.

Yet there was no destruction approaching.

The power would not invent an emergency so that its goodness could remain necessary. Had harm been near, it would have turned that harm aside and gone without leaving a debt. But it would not surround the garden with an eternity of guardianship merely to spare itself the knowledge that continuation need not mean permanence. Even continuation, it saw, should not be made compulsory by love.

So it did nothing to the garden.

It left no blessing in the roots and set no sentinel on the needle. It did not hide the seam, mark the way, repair the enamel tag, or take one dry leaf to keep elsewhere. It did not make the chamber a sanctuary. That word, too, can sometimes mean only that someone powerful has decided a place must never again be allowed to change without permission.

For a while longer, the power simply attended.

It did not require its attention to be useful. It did not pretend that being known enriched the garden, or that its own admiration had become an offering merely by being sincere. If there was joy in knowing such a life existed, that joy belonged to the power. It made no claim in return.

Then it withdrew.

No watchfulness remained behind. No thread of memory was fastened to the rock. Nothing in the chamber had to survive the visit, because nothing in the chamber had experienced one.

Only in the power did anything remain: a more exact understanding of where help ends. This was not a lesson the garden had been made to teach. It was simply what attention had done to the one attending.

The power turned toward other places, where water could still save something, where a voice might be going unheard, where an added hand would not be an intrusion but an answer.

Behind it, the narrow shadow remained on the insulator. The broad leaves received the low note. The fern-work held the high one exact. Root, crystal, iron, and charge continued their work without having acquired a witness, a future, or a meaning.

Nothing happened in the chamber.

The power’s mercy had been exact.
