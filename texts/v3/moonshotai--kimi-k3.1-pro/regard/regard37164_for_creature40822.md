### What the Power Did

The power did not enter. There was no room, and wisdom did not begin by making room for itself.

Their attention rested on the nodule without weight. They heard the choir, and then—because it is easy to hear a choir and miss everyone in it—they attended to the lives one by one. The clear mite was not merely a point in the radiance. The worm was not merely the choir’s depth. The snail beside the spiral was not an emblem of sleep, nor the springtail an emblem of useless longing. The power did not let the beauty of the whole decide, in advance, what was owed to its parts.

Their first impulse was the old benevolent one: to help.

They imagined letting the ninth bead fall.

It would have been such a small thing. A shiver through a fissure; a darkening of clay; the first wet sound in a world where every other sound came from shell, wing, muscle, and stone. One action would have an after.

But after that, the power could not honestly pretend to know where their gift would end. A foot might find purchase or lose it. A note might cease. A mouth might diminish the greenness beneath it. Hunger might become something that could deepen. Courtship might become something that could fail. Hearts that had kept their garnet-red patience might learn to tire.

The power was not afraid of time. They had loved gardens, watched young animals leave their birthplaces, and knew that an ending was not always an injury. A world with consequences could be good.

But a gift was not made innocent by being good.

They imagined space instead: a little withdrawal of the walls, an opening among the bodies, room for the folded springs and half-held wings. Yet much of what had first appeared to them as crowding was also relation. The snail’s shell was another creature’s instrument. The worm’s flank was another creature’s shelter. Remove the pressure, and some lives might be eased; remove the contacts, and the choir would become a collection of separate noises. The power could not tell in advance which loss would be the greater one.

They imagined opening the chert only wide enough to ask.

But there was no one who could answer for all the rest, and no question could be asked without changing the conditions under which an answer might arise. To give the multitude time enough to consider time would already be to have chosen for them.

The absence of a request was not consent. Neither, the power saw, was it a request too faint to hear.

So they did not finish the bead’s fall. They did not wake the snail, release the ceiling grain, complete an egg, or let one green scale be eaten. They did not make a second nodule in which to try the experiment and call the first untouched. Nor did they name the hollow perfect. Perfection would have been another way of stopping their attention.

They stayed until their listening no longer contained the wish to be answered.

Then they made one promise, and it required no alteration within the stone. If harm came against the nodule from the changing world above, that harm would be turned aside. If a cry ever came from within—if some next moment, beyond all their understanding, found a voice—it would not find the power elsewhere.

No seal appeared on the chert. No light marked the pavement. The power did not take the stone into a sanctuary or set it apart from other stones. They would not make it safe by making it theirs.

When they went, they carried away no sound they could use and no image they could improve upon. They carried only a caution: never again to confuse a life without outcome with a life without value. In other places, where silence meant despair and doors had been closed by cruelty, that caution would become intervention. Here it became restraint.

Above, rain darkened the grit and passed. Wind moved dust from one shallow hollow to another. Within, the mite tended its point of light, the blind worm held its grain, and every note was taken up by another note.

Nothing had been finished.

Nothing had been abandoned.

The black stone stayed cool.
