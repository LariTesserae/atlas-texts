### The Repair

The first thing the power did was listen.

It did not listen for a night, or for one of the long, mild years. It listened until it could distinguish the press lane’s double tick from the ticking of something alive inside a wall; until the granary drone, the thin note across the western roofs, and the hiss at the rim were no longer one climate of sound but separate voices. It heard the hushwing pass. It heard the silence spread through the nesting chambers, and then the first beetle resume.

Only after that did it consider help.

It did not bring rain. It did not part the mineral haze to give the gardens a brighter noon, or cover the bare mountain with soil, or teach the roof trees to hurry. Those would have been mercies borrowed from another kind of world. Iru had not failed to become wet, bright, and quick. It had become something else.

But the power did not mistake equilibrium for innocence.

It saw that a custom could be old without being just, and that an inherited obligation might fall heavily on someone who had never agreed to it. It felt the coldness of rooms whose warmth depended on another household’s diligence. It heard grief in the sleeping galleries, and anger over garden rights, and the particular weariness of people who had done everything required of them and still had too little. Nothing about these sorrows was ennobled merely because the town was ancient.

Nor was there one inhabitant whose good could stand for the whole. The granary recorder and the roof-glider did not want the same kind of abundance. The hushwing’s meal was another creature’s last morning. Even among the people, every improvement seemed to cast a shade, mask a tick, narrow a duct, or warm one room at the expense of another.

So the power resolved not to become the hidden author of Iru. Where the inhabitants could perceive a difficulty, understand something of its causes, and argue over what to do, it would not end their argument by miracle. It would not preserve them by making their choices unnecessary.

Then, far beneath the deepest garden, it found something none of them could hear.

A fracture was closing under the slow weight of the mountain.

It was not closing quickly. Centuries would pass before the upper seams cooled, and longer still before the change became unmistakable. That was the danger. The first signs would be small: an inherited felt-bed that failed to take, a nursery that needed its slab opened a little farther, tallowbacks growing sluggish in a pen whose keepers had neglected nothing. Gardeners would alter their mixtures. Vent-keepers would lift floors and clear passages. Each generation would inherit less warmth and more elaborate explanations.

By the time the shaft-notes changed beyond dispute, there would be too little time for a town that built its gardens as successions and planned important repairs across several lives.

There was no malice in it. It was only stone being stone. But the power saw no reason to call the ending good merely because it was natural.

There it acted.

It did not make the mountain young. It did not kindle a fiercer heat or grant the basin an endless summer. Far below the deepest seam-felts, it eased the closing stone apart by the smallest sufficient measure. No new furnace opened. No surface vent changed its note. The old warmth simply continued rising along the old fractures.

Nothing in Iru became richer. Nothing became easier. The gift was not even heat, exactly. It was time: time for seed beds planted before their harvesters were born, time for timber to pass from loom to bridge to lintel, time for bad arrangements to be resisted and good ones to be patiently remade.

Then the power left no sign that had to be maintained.

No shrine appeared beside a vent. No new note entered the signal grammar. No commandment was laid upon people who had not asked for one. The power did not remain where every dispute could become a petition and every repair could wait for its approval. It did not stay to be loved.

That night, a glider defeated a seal whose maker had been especially confident. Someone in a sleeping gallery laughed after everyone else had asked for quiet. An old person was carried toward the public warmth. A child gave directions correctly except for the last turn. Two households resumed an argument about a canopy, and a vent-keeper, hearing a harmless creak in old fiber, nevertheless stopped to listen to it again.

An apprentice set an ear against the floor.

She heard no miracle. She heard the foundation chord, slightly confused by the presses, exactly as her teacher had promised. She named the notes to herself, found the passage she had been sent to inspect, and went on.

By morning almost nothing appeared to have happened. The mountain was warm. The work remained.

The power had not made Iru perfect, painless, or eternal. It had not rescued the hollow from its future.

It had simply refused to let an ending no one could perceive decide everything in advance. Then it had returned Iru to its own time, and to the difficult, noisy, unfinished privilege of keeping itself alive.
