### The Staying

No thunder accompanied its arrival. It stood on the southern rim at noon, when the stone was white, the wet seams black, and the basin had no mist with which to make itself mysterious. It heard an inhabited city and saw a first draft, and did not suppose that either was an error.

Its first act would be to refrain from the easiest kindnesses.

It would not deepen the marsh, widen the springs, or lay rich soil over the pale terraces. It would not fill the sleeping cells with people merely because they were empty, or soften the waterworks because keepers sometimes had to raise their voices. It would not make the salamander numerous, the gardens sufficient, or the mineral lips obedient. Even the slate-colored bird would be allowed to drink and leave without being burdened with a future chosen for it.

The power might see many possible basins: one wooded, one deeply terraced, one nearly human again, one in which the old drains survived only as mineralized hollows. It would not mistake any of these for the place’s secretly intended form. Its power would not make it the author.

For a while, then, it would listen.

It would learn which silences belonged to frost and which required tools. It would learn that a full cistern and an empty cavity could both be conditions of health, and that an apparently useless court might be holding a level no wall could replace. It would listen past the water to the keepers: their arguments, their unnecessary repairs, their relief when a work party departed, and the loneliness that sometimes followed the relief.

Nor would it mistake the absence of catastrophe for the absence of need.

The patience required by stone would not become an excuse for postponing kindness to people. A keeper should not have to be serene about damp bedding, a bad injury, dwindling stores, or twenty years of the same dispute simply because the surrounding work was slow. The power would help with those things plainly where it could: carry loads, mend rope, bring food, sit with the sick, take the cold or tedious turn. If the keepers asked it to *finish something*, it would listen long enough to discover whether they meant the town, or only the upper cistern before winter.

Then, if they wished, it would take a turn staying.

Not forever. Not as a vow. Long enough that someone who wanted to go down the mountain could go without making the others’ winter harder. Long enough to learn the eastern drains and be corrected about them. Though it might hear every channel at once, it would not answer when an apprentice was asked what was absent. It would wait, with everyone else, for the apprentice to hear.

Its more extraordinary acts would be governed by a simple distinction: whether they returned choices or removed them.

A healed hand returned choices. Food in a hard winter returned choices. A little warning, given in time for someone to reach a failing channel, returned choices. But a spring made eternally compliant would remove choices—not only from future keepers, but from moss, snails, sedges, mineral deposition, and all the lives whose opportunities no one yet knew how to foresee. The power would not call that benevolence merely because the immediate result was convenient.

Neither would it preserve the basin as it had first found it. A wise affection would not require youth to remain young. Human absence might one day be the right continuation of the place. A new plant might establish and ruin a cherished bed. A future generation might misunderstand an old wall and make something better from the misunderstanding. The power would not station itself against time.

It would also be willing to become unnecessary. If keepers began waiting for its hearing instead of cultivating their own, it would withdraw. If its memory of old instructions began to outweigh present evidence, it would speak less. It would not stay so long that care became dependence, or accept a shrine in one of the resonant chambers. A chamber’s usefulness would be reason enough to leave it empty.

Perhaps, after all this, there would be disappointingly little to point out.

One winter in which the stores had been sufficient. A keeper who went below and later returned freely. A rope put away dry. A slab replaced wet side down. A basket of hand-made soil that did not spill when its carrier was called suddenly away. No new terrace, no inexplicable abundance, no note in the waterworks by which the power could always be identified.

On some later afternoon, a gardener might again be moving no more earth than one basket could hold. An expected note would fail beneath the paving. If the power happened to be staying then, it would not speak about geological ages or the holiness of patience. It would set the basket somewhere safe and go with the gardener. The obstruction might be cleared in minutes. On the way back, if there was time, it would help carry the soil.

Its finest work would disappear as the finest work there already did: first into reliability, then out of sight.

It would not have come to finish the basin. It would have come to keep *unfinished* from meaning *unattended*.
