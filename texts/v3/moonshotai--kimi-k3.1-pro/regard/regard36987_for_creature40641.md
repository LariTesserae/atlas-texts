### What the Power Did

The power did not still the Roof.

It listened long enough to learn why. The roar was not a cry for help. The wells were not wounds, and the pressure moving through them carried water, seed, warning, and life in ways that could not be separated cleanly into what was cruel and what was kind. Even to take away only the sound would have deprived the keepers of one of their few warnings. To make rain dependable would have been to appoint one kind of greenness over another. A road would have required a destination; a village, the reasonable expectation of gathering. Nothing living there had asked to be made easier to understand.

So the power did not send a river across the table. It did not raise a forest, teach the stonefeet to cry out, or make the keepers agree. It left no word in the ancient pools.

This might have been the whole of its answer, had it not found the rimwing.

The animal had come over the western lip during an exhalation. The same breath had stripped a slope of flowers and lifted an old pool briefly into shining threads. The rimwing had fallen much farther than the petals.

Now it lay on the open table with one wing folded beneath it and the other spread uselessly across the rosettes. The fine fringes along its wings were clogged with seed-down and wet root felt. Whenever it struggled, the level air gave it nothing. Whenever it rested, dew gathered along its body.

Behind it ran a track of crushed green. The nearest edge was still days away at the pace it had managed.

The power could have changed the animal. It could have given it wings suited to the table, or feet that could bear its weight, or some new appetite by which it might prosper where it had fallen. Instead it considered what the rimwing was already made to do.

The Roof would survive its death. That was not the same as saying its death did not matter.

The power began with the wing fringes. There were thousands of them, fine as hairs and separately mobile, each caught in a slightly different way. It freed them one by one. It supported the long wings without letting them fold back upon themselves. It waited while the animal exhausted its fear and then while it found a little strength again.

Benevolence, there, was mostly the patient untangling of small things from other small things.

Only then did the power lift the rimwing.

At the western lip it did not throw the animal into the gulf. It waited through two changes of mist. Cloud pressed against the cliff below them and withdrew. Somewhere underfoot a deep flue faltered, resumed, and was answered by a distant shriek. At last warm air began to climb strongly along the rock.

The rimwing’s fringes stirred before its wings did.

The power let go.

For several heartbeats the animal fell. Cloud closed over it. Then, far below the rounded lip, a long shadow turned upon itself, opened, and rose along the cliff. It passed once through a rent in the mist and was gone. It did not cross the sky above the table.

No keeper witnessed this. The rimwing did not return bearing gratitude or a message. The power left no mark beside the track through the vegetation, and in time the rosettes and ferns closed over that evidence in their own slow way.

The Roof remained dangerous and abundantly itself. Keepers still missed one another for years. Stonefeet still stood with all four feet planted, attending to movements no god had explained to them. In the cells, people still complained about damp bedding and argued over adzes. The hush still came without warning.

The power had changed almost nothing. There was one more living shadow along the cliffs.

Then it went on listening. When the next hush came, it too got low.
