The power came without light.

Light would have been useful. So would warmth, rain, or a body set down upon the felt. It did not wish its first gift to be merely something the basin could use, so for a long time there was only attention.

It did not watch from the rim alone. From there, the waves resembled weather, and the whole valley seemed always to be approaching some decision. The power went closer. It had time enough to distinguish a wave from a wound.

It watched a column give up its sap before its crown paled. It saw that the final collapse was not a life interrupted, but the completion of a transfer. It followed a pulse through cords that never joined into a single command. It found failed unions, drying boundaries, one tract absorbing another. There was conflict here, and hunger, and continual loss. But in the great circulation itself it found no suffering coextensive with the valley.

No imprisoned intelligence waited beneath the felt. No voice had been trying to speak through the pressure sacs. The black pools were not eyes. The motion was not a convulsion. The basin was not a person who had been cruelly deprived of a body; nor was it a body vainly waiting to be given a person.

The power considered stilling the waves. But that would have meant stilling the circulation, and calling the result peace.

It considered waking the basin. But to give it a mind would not have healed a wound. It would have created someone capable of experiencing every subsequent wound: a witness for every collapsing column, every tract sealed off from another, every rich body received by the felt. The power did not regard self-knowledge as so invariably good that it could be imposed without need and called mercy.

It considered preserving the basin forever. Yet continuation was what the basin did; that did not make endless continuation what the basin, or wisdom, must desire. Survival could become a sentence like anything else.

None of this meant that the power would never have interfered. Had it found a trapped awareness asking for release, it would have answered. Had a traveler been lying exhausted on the mat, it would have carried the traveler beyond the pass. It would not have demanded that the felt feel remorse afterward. Restraint, too, could become vanity if it were valued more highly than help.

But there was no traveler that day, and no plea.

So the power performed none of the great acts by which benevolence often makes itself visible.

Then it turned its attention again to the pools.

It did not decide that they were sacred. It did not mistake habit for longing, or age for wisdom. Their openness might have been necessary to the buried circulation. It might have meant nothing beyond the repeated turning-aside of roots. Still, these were the places where the basin already made room without immediately filling it. If the power was going to touch anything, it would touch there, where its touch could follow an old tendency rather than impose a new form.

It chose a covered pool in one of the oldest hollows. Above it, columns stood close together, and the roof beneath them was layered with root, moss, and dead fronds already being drawn back into use. The water below received almost no light.

The power made an opening in the roof.

It was no wider than a bird’s wings. It killed nothing to make the space and altered no deep vessel. A few living surfaces drew aside; thin gray daylight passed between them and lay on the water.

The pool did not become radiant. Nothing stirred in its depths. It simply became possible to see, from above, that there was water there.

The power placed no enchantment on the opening. It did not command the roots to preserve it, or punish the felt if, in time, pale filaments crossed the gap and began their patient work. A gift that the recipient must maintain forever is only another obligation.

Perhaps the opening would close before winter. Perhaps the old habit of the hollow would keep it clear for centuries. Perhaps rain would sometimes fall directly into the pool. Perhaps the dark bird that crossed the basin would one day descend, stand on the root-bound margin, drink, and fly away. Perhaps nothing would come at all.

The power did not require an outcome.

Almost everything offered to the basin became supply. Almost everything the basin made became the condition for making something else. This was not greed, and it was not a fault. But the power did not wish merely to add more material to that relentless sufficiency. It wished to make possible an event that could be useless without being a failure.

A bird might drink and leave without becoming necessary.

A shadow might cross the water without anything beneath it responding.

For a little while, daylight might lie on a black surface and nourish nothing.

Even if the felt closed the opening again, the gift would not have been refused. It would simply have been finished.

Then the power left. It did not stay to see whether anything used what it had made. It left no name in the valley, no promise of return, and no mark by which later visitors could distinguish the opened pool from any other hollow.

From the nearest pass, the basin appeared unchanged. A distant shoulder subsided without sound. Darker growth rose behind it, following the old slope exactly. The mountain remained where it had always been. Everything covering it became something else.

Nothing essential had been altered.

But for a time—perhaps a season, perhaps much longer—a little water at the bottom of an ancient hollow held the ordinary sky. No cord drew the reflection downward. No crown opened in answer. It was not a message, and the basin was not required to understand it.

The power had not given the being a purpose.

It had made one small space in which nothing had to have one.
