The power would begin by not arriving.

There would be no second radiance in the court, no warmth moving up the pebble walls, no voice accomplishing what voices cannot do there. It would not cause the cistern to shine or the standing stone to reveal an inscription. Such signs would be comprehensible to visitors, but the power would not have come primarily to be understood by visitors.

For a long time, it would simply attend.

It would stay long enough to see that the people were not maintaining an ideal town; that the clear paths and exact margins of moss were not acts of devotion; that the equilibrium was real, but not the whole truth. It would notice the badly mended basket and the laughter that made the old quarrel difficult to sustain. It would learn which seat had once been avoided. It would see someone sit beside the black water and decide, for reasons no observer could extract, not to draw from it.

Most of all, it would learn not to confuse the people with the place.

That would rule out the grand interventions. It would not warm the hollow, because the warmth would alter water, growth, sleep, and the delicate negotiations by which the whole community lived. It would not uncover a spring merely because an outsider had calculated the visible supply in buckets. It would not widen the passages, straighten the stairs, restore sound as though the hand-language were a damaged substitute for speech, or fill the granary niches until their smallness ceased to trouble an observer. It would not appoint a keeper. It would not make the inhabitants gentle, farsighted, grateful, or permanently satisfied. It would not preserve the town forever so that something rare would remain available to be admired.

Nor would it call the place perfect. Perfection is often the word an observer uses when other people’s costs have become too small to see.

But reverence alone would not be enough. A benevolence that could watch moss rise behind a footstep while overlooking cracked lips and clumsy fingers would have learned the shape of the hollow and failed its inhabitants. Leaving everything untouched might be wise in a garden. Here there were people who could want something, refuse something, or change their minds.

So, after refraining, the power would ask.

It would make itself small enough to be refused. No gift would arrive first, making acceptance feel like repayment. It would not seek out the oldest person, the most eloquent hands, or the figure seated nearest the standing stone and mistake that person for a representative. It would learn enough of the language to ask plainly, and badly enough at first to be laughed at. It would not interpret stillness as consent.

Its questions would not be: *How may I improve this? What does the hollow require? What must be preserved?*

They would be closer to: *Is anything hurting you that you would like eased? Is there something you want that need not change anyone else? Would you prefer that I do nothing?*

Then it would believe the answers.

For someone who wished it, warmth might gather in cloth and flesh without entering the air. The sleeping places would remain cold stone; the sleeper would simply stop waking with aching hands. An old injury might cease to decide whether a person could use a particular stair, though the stair itself would remain narrow and uneven. Someone hungry might receive enough without the roofs bursting into unnatural abundance. Someone who wished to leave might be accompanied onto the plain, not commanded to go and not promised that return would be required.

Another person would refuse. The refusal would remain whole. The power would not conceal help in their food or wait until sleep made refusal impossible. It would not love them more cleverly than they wished to be loved.

Some requests would not result in anything an observer could call a miracle. Someone might want to tell the unnecessarily long story of a person who had died. The power would listen without shortening the story, correcting its repetitions, or treating grief as another imbalance to be restored. It would not offer the town’s endurance as consolation.

A child, knowing the old sign for *bell*, might ask to hear one.

The power would not break the silence of the hollow. It would go with the child up the three worn steps and out onto the gravel. There, if the child still wished it, a bell might sound once: small, bright, and absurdly violent after the life beneath the seam. The child might laugh, cover their ears, or immediately ask to go back. Any of these would be a satisfactory outcome. The gift would not be the bell. It would be the discovery that a wanted thing could be tried without becoming a destiny.

Nothing given would establish an office. No one would have to tend the warmth, interpret the healing, or remember the correct day for gratitude. The power would leave behind no object important enough to fight over. Its attention would not become a star above the plain, guiding pilgrims, kings, or excavators to the entrance. It would not protect the hollow by turning it into a prison, nor protect the people by deciding that no harm, choice, departure, or ending should ever reach them again.

When it withdrew, the town would be almost exactly as difficult to describe as before.

Water would still gather slowly in the bends of pipes. The fish with the damaged fin would still be looked for by one person before the others. The white light would neither gold nor dim. The basket would remain badly mended. People would still hoard a good handful of seed, remember one another’s stiff knees, sulk without adequate reason, and invent signs their elders considered excessive.

But someone would have slept warmly. Someone would have climbed without pain. Someone would have been allowed to refuse. Someone’s grief would have been accompanied without being repaired. Perhaps, out on the plain, the gravel would retain the brief impression of a child jumping at the sound of a bell.

Within the hand-language there might eventually appear a new expression, something like *help that does not stay to be thanked*. In time, the children would probably find a way to make it rude.

That would be all.

The power would have done almost nothing to the hollow, and it would not have made the people more worthy of it. It would not have enlarged their continuation or guaranteed that continuation must leave a greater shape behind.

It would simply have made their lives, where they wished, gentler.
