## The Haste of Kindness

The power’s first thought was rain.

From a distance, the plateau seemed to be asking for it—not with a voice, but with every pale basin and rounded stone. The power imagined clouds gathering without violence, water returning often enough to be trusted, the dark crust softening and spreading. They imagined green visible from the sky.

Being benevolent, they wanted to give. Being wise, they did not yet mistake the beauty of the gift for knowledge of the receiver.

The first thing they did was nothing.

This was not yet wisdom. It was only the pause in which wisdom might begin.

They did not descend. A foot, however gently placed, would have crushed routes renewed by generations. A shadow might have altered the condensation on a shell. Even a body made for reverence would have displaced something. So the power remained without weight and watched through a day, then through a rain, then through the long drying afterward.

At first they saw stones. Then they saw which stones breathed.

They saw the veilwings appear when the light turned across them, and vanish without departing. They saw a builder add one crescent to a wall whose shape had been decided by no builder. They watched water pass from a groove into a film, from the film into a grazer, from the grazer into another body, never once becoming visible enough to reflect the sky.

They had come because the place and the being who lived in it had drawn their attention. But the being would not remain singular beneath their regard.

An old stoneback seemed at first entire: one ancient life, closed within its mineral back. Then its seam opened, and the power found mites, hunters, grazing margins, larvae in the basin, young plants rooting in silt made from older inhabitants. A builder colony seemed more like a people, but its memory lay in corrections made to a wall, and the wall was already becoming shelter for lives that did not build. Even a path had a continuity longer than any traveler’s, though no single animal knew its whole extent.

There was no clear threshold at which the power could say: here the being ends, and here its surroundings begin.

They did not solve this difficulty by calling the whole plateau one organism. That would have been another convenience. They simply stopped requiring a single voice before they would listen, or a single hand before they would care.

Then they reconsidered rain.

Their gift would not merely have added water. It would have decided which eggs should wake and which reserves were no longer needed. It would have changed the value of a seam, a pore, a chamber, a body. Routes maintained for centuries might vanish beneath a film that no existing multitude could crop. Dust might travel again. Water might begin to gather, and gathering, learn to run.

A river could have carried away in an afternoon what had taken an age to find a user.

The power saw that their first kindness would have made the plateau easier to see and harder to be.

This did not teach them that everything upon it was good. They did not call thirst sacred, or failure necessary. Soft-backed young were taken. Broods woke into weather that could not sustain them. Colonies dwindled in chambers they continued repairing almost to the end. The power refused to make these things beautiful merely because they belonged to an intricate whole.

But sorrow did not tell them which appetite to frustrate, which succession to arrest, or how to preserve the inhabitants while replacing the conditions under which their lives had become possible. Silence was not consent. Persistence was not approval. Neither, however, gave the power permission to turn preference into remedy.

So they brought no abundance. They did not make the crust inexhaustible or the shelters permanent. They did not spare every small life from every hunter, knowing that some hungers were the width of a dewdrop and no less real for that. They left no monument. At that scale, even a monument would have become a small climate.

Nor did they make an eternal law of noninterference. That would only have been another way to stop paying attention. They required instead that any future action answer to what was actually there, rather than to what they had first imagined.

What they did was remember, and allow the memory to correct them.

They remembered that stillness was not necessarily dormancy, and dormancy was not death. They remembered that an unhatched egg might be a reserve rather than a failure; that an abandoned chamber could still be performing its makers’ work; that an animal might be a locality and a locality might be crowded with invisible decisions. They remembered how much of the plateau’s abundance consisted not in having more, but in allowing almost nothing to go unused.

Memory preserved nothing by itself. The power did not pretend otherwise.

Much later, another great and kindly attention turned toward the plateau. It too saw the pale ground, the shallow pans, the long intervals between rains. It too began to imagine clouds.

The first power did not forbid it. Forbiddance would have made the plateau seem like their possession. They said only:

*Wait. Look longer. You are about to give water to a place whose lives are largely ways of keeping it. Learn first what your gift will replace.*

That was their intervention: not upon the rain, nor upon the plateau, but upon the haste of kindness.

In time, rain came of its own accord.

It darkened the ground without making a stream. Some eggs hatched; others remained closed. Part of a film brightened while the adjoining crust stayed dark. Stoneback seams opened across a field that, an hour before, had appeared mineral and vacant. Builders worked at the damp edges of their walls. For a little while, abundance became visible.

The power did not take this as a sign, or as approval. They watched until the water had been taken into shells, walls, tissues, seeds, and dust. Then they watched a little longer.

By noon the plateau was again a pale table beneath an ordinary sky. No river shone upon it. No new name lay over its basins. An old animal adjusted itself by less than the width of a grain, and dew gathered where it had gathered before.

Nothing there knew that it had been spared a kindness.

Nothing there needed to know.
