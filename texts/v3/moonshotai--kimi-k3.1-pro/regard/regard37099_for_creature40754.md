The power did not arrive as light.

Light would have been the easiest gift. It would have revealed the arcades, given the shell ridges an horizon, and made visible at last the city that no resident had ever seen whole. It would also have altered hunting, shelter, courtship, and concealment. It would have favored some lives and exposed others. What seemed from above like an improvement might have been only the imposition of a different world.

So the darkness remained.

For many tides, attention was the power’s only act. It did not mistake its wider view for complete knowledge. It learned the city at the scale of a worm’s aperture, where goodness was food-bearing water and catastrophe a grain too large to sort; within a sponge, where a colony’s world could fail with the faltering of one pump; along the intimate junctions by which an eel avoided another eel; and across the broad electrical silences known to the ray. It saw that no inhabitant’s Low City could simply be corrected by reference to another’s.

Nor did it mistake the city for a single organism whose health made every part’s suffering irrelevant. The whole could remain in balance while a small world ended in terror. A carcass could nourish a chamber without making the dying good. A fallen arch could become shelter without making the crushing fortunate. The power refused to call pain beautiful merely because beauty sometimes grew from its remains.

But sorrow alone did not tell it what to change.

To save every animal the ray uncovered would be to hunger the ray. To make every nursery succeed would be to crowd the mud with lives for which neither food nor space had been provided. To hold every arch in place would prevent the succession of rubble, excavation, settlement, and lower dwelling. To keep each seep eternally constant would fix the outer beds around conditions that had always moved. Even rescue, repeated according to one design, would eventually become authorship. The Low City would survive as the expression of the power’s preferences rather than as the accumulated solutions of its inhabitants.

Its mercy therefore began as a boundary around itself.

It would not preserve the city as a specimen. It would not choose a favored species, appoint a ruler, or make the settlement everlasting. It would not demand gratitude, understanding, worship, or even awareness. Nothing there would be required to become more comprehensible in order to deserve care.

Yet restraint was not permitted to become an excuse for admiration at a distance.

Where suffering could be eased without merely transferring its cost to another life, the power eased it. Pain that had ceased to warn, protect, or make escape possible was not required to continue for the sake of the valley’s harmony. No agony was cherished as an indispensable ingredient in the food web. These were mercies without civic consequence: no current altered, no hunter’s meal removed, no shell inscribed, no survival guaranteed. The city’s history would contain no record of them.

The power did not pretend that this answered every question. Wisdom did not consist in calling its own solution final.

Against the ordinary dangers of the Low City, it remained reluctant to become a second current. Against an erasure of another order, however—some harm so complete that no district could shift, no nursery could replenish another, no larva could settle, and no living balance could re-form—it would act. Not to dictate what must thereafter be built, but to restore the possibility of response. It would defend conditions, not prescribe a final shape. Then, if it could, it would stop.

For the city itself, it made no promise of permanence. Permanence, too, could become a form of possession. Seeps would still weaken and strengthen. Ridges would migrate by almost imperceptible degrees. Some maintained openings would close, and spaces inside old bodies would become passages. The power’s care would not be another force preventing the inhabitants from becoming whatever their lives, over time, might make.

At the turning of the next strong tide, silt lifted from the lanes. Feeding crowns withdrew, fishes rose, and burrowing animals reversed their ventilation. A long hiss passed through the terraces and arcades. Then the current steadied. Small pumps resumed. A crab returned to the apron before its refuge. Scavengers investigated something newly uncovered. A grain was loosened from one wall and bound into another.

No resident knew that anything had changed.

Almost nothing had.

The Low City had not been saved, because it was not lost. It had not been pronounced perfect, because it was not painless. It had been seen without being possessed, and loved without being made useful. Its darkness had not been treated as a deficiency; its balance had not been treated as an absolution.

The power remained, if it remained at all, as an attention that would not become a hand merely because it could.

And in the warm black water, without witness or skyline, the masonry began to open.
