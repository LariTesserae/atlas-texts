At first, nothing. At last, very little.

The power would not begin by speaking. There would be no silence in which to speak, and nothing in the ward that required words in order to be understood.

They would remain through many risings and withdrawals of the water. They would watch a repair cool one fern colony into browning while, a little farther along the main, another seam became warm enough for spores. They would follow a willow fragment from the rake bank into the canal and see that most such journeys ended in compost. They would learn not to expect the ward to declare, in some simpler fashion, whether it was well.

An impatient benevolence might dry the basin, silence the engines, and let clear weather reach the roofs. Another might stop the pumps and allow the marsh to take everything back. Another, seeing no fish, no nests, no burrows, might fill the ward with animals and call the absence remedied. A very thorough savior might make the iron stainless, the masonry unwearied, the water clean, and every leak obedient.

The power would do none of these things.

They would not bring back people merely so that the lamps would seem less lonely. Loneliness would be their own feeling, not necessarily the ward’s. Nor would they make the place incorruptible. A ward without rust, silt, scale, root pressure, and small failures would not be this being preserved. It would be a diagram of it.

Yet they would not mistake endurance for happiness, or repetition for contentment. Neither would they conclude that, because nothing there suffered as an animal suffers, nothing there could matter.

The being before them had made no conscious petition, and the power would not invent one. But it had a pattern. Cut, scalded, buried, torn from its attachments, it continued where continuation was possible. A margin remained. A fragment rooted. A population moved without migrating. This was not a prayer, but neither was it nothing.

Eventually the power would understand two things.

The ward did not need to be made other than it was.

And its equilibrium was not the same as self-sufficiency.

Its life could survive almost any local injury, but it could not sleeve a pipe. It could colonize the warmth created by a repair, but it could not place a concrete shoe beneath a settling wall. Its circulation depended upon machinery; the machinery depended upon frequent, modest work; and that work might depend upon people whose reasons for coming belonged to a world beyond the fog. The gardens could begin again after almost anything except every refuge failing at once.

There, if anywhere, benevolence could enter without becoming conquest.

So the power would not take a throne above the engine hall. They would become, in the most limited sense, a keeper in reserve.

Their interventions would be rare and almost indistinguishable from the ward’s existing repairs. A sleeve fitted before a general rupture, but after the old leak had done its long work. A gate eased before water hammer could break the last dependable main. One settling wall supported just enough that its failure would not carry the courtyard, the roots, and the warm return pipes down together. Nothing would be restored to an earlier condition. Nothing would be made new merely because the power could make it new.

They would not save every fern. They would not preserve every willow trunk, or decide that a particular sedge island must always remain an island. An intake clearing would still strip one reach. A flood would still carry the fragments elsewhere. The power would not prevent every ending. At most, they would sometimes prevent every ending from becoming the same ending.

Even this keeping would have a limit.

They would not undertake to make the Weeping Ward eternal. If, after ages, its life had passed beyond the basin while the old engines labored on in an empty shell, they would let the engines stop. If there came a time when every wheel turned only because they willed it, and every green thing persisted only because they held it in place, they would recognize that maintenance had become preservation, and preservation had become possession. Then they would open their hand. To keep an image of the ward forever would not be to keep the ward alive.

For the present, however, nothing would appear to have happened.

Water would rise through the lowest lanes and withdraw. Rain would begin beneath the market trusses. The flywheels would turn slowly enough for their spokes to be counted. Moss would fix itself into stone behind its living edge, and the waterweed would stream, be torn away, and stream again. If a keeper came after many years, they might find one necessary repair already made and assume only that someone else had preceded them.

There would be no altar, no inscription, no new voice among the pipes. The ward would offer no gratitude, and none would be required.

The power’s mercy would be a little more time. Their wisdom would be to leave the ward enough like itself to use that time—and then to let whatever began next belong to the ward.
