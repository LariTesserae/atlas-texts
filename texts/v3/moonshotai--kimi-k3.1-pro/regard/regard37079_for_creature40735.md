### Margin

The power would listen until its admiration had worn off.

Admiration would be easy. Rill offered so much to admire: lamps burning in the wet dimness, workers rising before the bell, a child’s report taken seriously, swallows rebuilding beside a flange, frogs using the pauses in steam. Even the gauge-keeper’s ledgers might seem beautiful to a sufficiently distant observer—all those small entries by which disaster had been postponed.

But the power would stay long enough to see what admiration concealed. It would notice the worker who had grown tired of being necessary. It would hear the future thinning in the questions of the young. It would watch a flushing erase a neighborhood of snails and refuse to be comforted merely because other snails would come. It would understand that a niche’s renewal was not the same thing as a life spared, and that the level on the brass gauge was not the whole of Rill’s health.

Then it would resist two temptations: to abolish Rill for Rill’s own good, and to leave everything untouched because its endurance was beautiful.

It would not silence the engines or dry the bench. It would not cover the basalt with soil, make the cloud lift, or turn every outfall into a garden. Much that lived in Rill had been shaped by heat, water, shelter, and repetition; a transformation meant as kindness could simply be eviction on a larger scale.

Nor would it make the brass gauge infallible. The keeper would have distrusted that gift, and rightly. An instrument that cannot be wrong can no longer be understood, only obeyed.

But neither would the power do nothing. There is a kind of reverence that is chiefly pleasure in somebody else’s endurance.

What it would give Rill is margin.

Not immunity. Margin.

A bearing would still fail, but not always before a second pair of hands could be woken. Water would still rise, but not every delay would have to become a catastrophe. Warnings would last a little longer—long enough to be acted upon by people who were ill, frightened, exhausted, or new. An unattended valve would remain forbidden. A grating would still not be a floor. The blue-banded pipe would lose none of its importance. The rules would remain, but they would no longer have to carry quite so much of the difference between life and death.

One small room would become dry.

It would not be a palace or a sanctuary. The low pump note would still come faintly through its walls. But bedding left there would dry. Paper would remain flat. Medicine would not need to be wiped before it was opened. Nothing in the room would be necessary to the operation of the pumps, and nothing done there would count as maintenance. That would be the heart of the gift.

The gauge-keeper would use it first for the ledgers. Later she would sleep there. The first time she woke, she would lie still with her head inclined toward the engines, trying to identify what required her attention. After a while she would understand that, for several more minutes, nothing did.

For the other inhabitants, the power would not suspend hunger, current, heat, or death. It would not make herons apologize for being herons. But here and there water would find a way around the work: a side pool beyond the full violence of a flushing, a refuge from which a channel could be recolonized, a ledge where a repair need not always mean an occupied nest’s destruction. A fern might survive without having to become useful. Not every neighborhood would be erased at once.

The world would remain dangerous. It would merely contain a few more deaths that were not required by convenience.

The people would notice the change slowly, and some would distrust it. Old vigilance would not relax simply because it had become safe to do so. Some would fill every spare hour with more work. Some would continue to sleep badly. Tools would still be hoarded, reports postponed, rosters contested.

But an illness could sometimes be an illness before it had to be a roster problem. A departure could be mourned without immediately becoming extra shifts for everyone left behind. Those who stayed might discover that staying felt different when it was not quite so compulsory.

A town without spare attention can only repeat itself. Given a little, it might still choose the old water level, the old paths, the old footprints. It might also move a culvert by two feet, enlarge a window toward the lagoon, or decide that one repair could wait while a child explained an idea that had nothing to do with engines. Not every proposal would be wise. Not every wise proposal would be accepted. That, too, would be part of having a future.

The power would not require Rill to last forever. If, generations later, the condensate main became unnecessary, if an engine hall fell silent and was not repaired, if an abandoned trench began the long work of becoming a canal, it would not rush in to restore the view from the condenser-house roof. It would feel no piety toward bare black ground and wet lamps merely because they were beautiful.

On an ordinary night, the keeper would still make a mark resembling yesterday’s mark. Frogs would still call between steam releases. A heron would stand at the ring drain, and a gecko would hunt the amber wall beside a lamp.

Somewhere a cottage door would open at an unfamiliar sound. The person inside would listen, one hand already near a boot.

It would be laughter in the dry room, perhaps, or someone trying to teach a child a song from elsewhere: thin, uncertain, badly tuned, and of no consequence to the water.

The listener would recognize it. The door would close. They would go back to bed.

Rill would still hold its ground at one level, its stillness supplied by motion. But not every change would be a restoration now, and not every new sound would be a fault.
