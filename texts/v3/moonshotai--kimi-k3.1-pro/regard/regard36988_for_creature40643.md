### What the Power Did

The power’s first courtesy was to bring no light.

Nothing changed beneath the ice. No current altered its course, no seep brightened, and none of the transparent animals turned toward a presence they could not have perceived. For a long while, attention was all the power gave.

Their earliest impulses were generous, and therefore worth examining.

They thought of light, until they understood that light would chiefly have made the basin easier for them to see. They thought of warmth, and followed in imagination the melting roof, the shifting brines, the seeps flowing into new chemical intervals. They thought of food in abundance. Then they watched a carcass become a crowded province and saw how much of that province depended upon the scarcity surrounding it. To make abundance continuous would not simply have given every inhabitant enough. It would have made another water, another chemistry, another sea.

So they did not call the darkness a wound merely because they could imagine illuminating it.

Neither did they call hunger good, or decide that every hardship was necessary. That would have been another way of making their own restraint pleasant to themselves.

The hunter tempted them most.

It would have been easy to give it a tool worth keeping, a place to return to, a memory in which successful improvisations could accumulate rather than fade. Easy to arrange meetings between solitary adults, to let one solution be seen by another, to make the placing of a stone into the first act of a longer story. The power could already imagine that story: caches, favored objects, shaped things, durable signs upon the floor.

But the hunter had not asked to become the ancestor of builders. Its act was not incomplete because it had no sequel. To love it chiefly for what might someday descend from it would have been to overlook the animal actually lying in the sediment, tasting the water, needing resistance for the duration of a meal.

The power did not uplift it. When they examined the word, they found that it described mostly the direction from which they were looking.

Still, they did not make a purity of doing nothing. To refuse every act because an act might leave a mark would also have been to think chiefly of themselves.

Once, after a long poor interval, a hunter came upon carrion.

Little remained but a soft mass turning slowly beside a low swell. The hunter was weak enough that its movements had lost their usual economy. Each time it tried to tear the tissue, the current rotated the fragment and drew it away. It spread its arms, gathered them, tried again. The carcass that had enriched so many other lives was almost finished. Soon there would be only stained clay and a downstream vagueness in the water.

Not far away, a sediment current was carrying a stone.

The power did not create food. They did not still the water, open the remains, or place the stone in the hunter’s arms. They only allowed the stone to settle a little earlier than it otherwise would have.

It came to rest at the limit of the hunter’s next reach.

The animal touched it. There was a pause, though perhaps only the pause of any body encountering an unexpected solidity. Then the hunter moved the stone, drew the carrion against it, and found the resistance it needed.

It fed.

Nothing in its manner suggested revelation or gratitude. When it had finished, it released the stone. The remains softened further, scattered, and entered the slower economies of microbes and worms. The hunter crossed a short distance of plain and pressed itself down until settling silt began to obscure its outline.

It was a very small help. It did not end scarcity, secure the hunter’s future, or improve the basin. The power did not despise it for being small. Neither did they make it the foundation of a relationship. They did not begin leaving stones in the animal’s path until it learned expectancy. They had not chosen it for a destiny. They had helped it once, in a way that still required the help to be found and used.

For the sea as a whole, their care took the form of a boundary around their own power. They would not preserve the basin as a specimen, immune to age. They would not save every mat from burial, every brood from the brine, every hunter from the final stillness that awaited it. Nor would they permit the sea to be opened, seeded, harvested, or lovingly remade merely because some later visitor possessed the means and called the possession care.

Its life would not be protected from one kind of danger only to be made available for another’s purpose.

Eventually the weather came.

The stone lifted, rolled, and was buried. The last organic stain around the carcass dispersed. The hunter’s resting impression filled with silt. No arrangement remained that could be distinguished from chance, and nothing marked the place where power had briefly touched the basin.

The power did not preserve the evidence.

They remembered instead: not the promise of a future craft, not the first step toward something more admirable, but one completed act in the dark. An animal had needed resistance. For a little while, it had been given the opportunity to find some.

Then the power let the sea go on without their hand upon it.

They had used power much as the hunter used the stone: only as much as was needed, without shaping it into a monument, without keeping what it helped, and without leaving their name upon it.
