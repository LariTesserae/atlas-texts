The power came down the nine steps without bringing more light.

Light would have made the chamber easier to inspect. Instead, it sat on the clay seat, where the boundaries were uncertain, and waited. It had been there only a little while when the crickets stopped.

It stayed for the whole weather. It heard the vents begin their low drawing of air, saw the ceiling points brighten, listened as drops entered the cups one by one. Frogs called from vessels that sent their voices elsewhere. The power did not try to locate them. It watched the newts appear at the wall openings, the fish gather at the runnel mouths, the spiders climb toward drier anchors.

When the siphon opened, compassion suggested the first miracle: hold the water back.

The power could have done it. The pans would have remained full. No fish would have flashed itself dry in a shrinking cup; no newt would have had to cross bare clay too late. Beneath the causeway, the salamander would have gone hungry.

So the power did not stop the drain.

But neither did it do the opposite, easier thing: admire the roar, admire the salamander’s thoroughness, and call every small death necessary. Some deaths fed another animal. Some fed only the film. A brood failed in a cup whose note had once favored its parent, and nothing in the later resettlement required that failure. The power did not call it meaningful. It did not require suffering to become music before it could be grieved.

Nor did imperfection seem to it an invitation to become the chamber’s author.

It could have kept the basin full and silenced half the knowledge by which its animals lived. It could have removed the salamander, preferring the creatures it could imagine saving. It could have made every cup safe for spawn, favoring beginnings without asking what would sustain all the lives that followed. It could even have corrected the acoustics, so that every call came honestly from its caller—and destroyed, in an instant, the frogs’ other geography.

It had come, perhaps, for the salamander. At least the salamander was the easiest inhabitant to mistake for a destiny: the largest body, the longest stillness, the shadow beneath the only crossing. The power saw it entire without disturbing the shadows that divided it. It did not give the animal speech, appoint it guardian, or call it the soul of the Clay Lung. Its stillness was not wisdom; its hunger was not cruelty. It was an animal. The power did not require it to be more than that in order to care what happened to it.

But neither was the salamander the center. There was no single creature for whom all the others were scenery. And the Clay Lung itself, for all its breathing and voicing, could not be loved instead of them. A vessel does not suffer when it is emptied. A fish does.

So the power worked no miracle among the animals. It did something less satisfying: it remained until after the music.

It listened to a beetle freeing one leg from sediment, to water leaving an isolated cup, to the small, irregular sounds of resettlement that no listener would have mistaken for a chorus. It remembered what had been lost without pretending that remembrance was compensation. Then it turned its attention to the clay ribs, the old repairs, the buried jars, and the hidden passages on which another weather depended.

Here it found a distinction it could use.

Some changes belonged to the Lung’s continuing life, even when they were fatal to particular lives. Others would merely end it. A cup could fail and the community go on. Moss could close one seep and lengthen another. But a shell broken beyond recovery, an intake sealed from outside, a sump emptied past the reach of every permanent refuge—these were not weather. They were the loss of weather.

Against the second kind of ending, and only against that, the power set its care.

It would not make the Clay Lung eternal. It would not manage its populations, select its survivors, or stand inside every consequence. But if some final accident threatened to close the whole instrument, it would return. Its work then would be small: pressure where clay had slipped, an old channel cleared, a rib supported only long enough for new packing to bear the load. It would repair as the makers had repaired, without improving the design. If it left a fingerprint, water could soften it.

For the present, nothing needed mending. The power laid no hand on the walls. It left no mark on the seat, added no rush to the trough, and made no cup safer than it had been. It stayed only until the first cautious frog called and the spider came down to inspect its silk.

Then it went up the steps.

The makers had given the chamber a shape; water had given it weather; the animals had given it a voice. The power gave it no purpose. Purposes are too often the beginning of ownership. It gave, instead, the least possessive gift it knew: not safety, not perfection, not forever, but time that would not be cut short through its own inattention.

Below, a drop struck a pan beside the empty seat. A buried jar answered late, and the sound seemed to come from beyond the far wall.

The power did not correct it.
