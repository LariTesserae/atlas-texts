### A Gift Without an Address

The power did nothing to Serein. This took it a long time.

Its first attention settled over the basin as stillness.

The insects ceased. The frogs stopped answering one another. At Fourth Commons, a cistern keeper looked up from her screen; elsewhere, custodians set down their tools and people began gathering the small things they carried from one removal to the next. There was no danger, but Serein had learned what stillness usually meant.

The power withdrew its regard until it lay no more heavily on the town than cloud-light. A frog called. Another answered from somewhere that sounded like a wall.

It had learned the first thing: even attention could isolate a district.

It looked for the being who lived in Serein and could not find only one. There was the keeper cleaning frog eggs from a cistern screen. There were eels worrying open a channel no human eye could see. There was a condemned storeroom that, to several generations of minute lives, would be not an episode in a municipal transfer but the entire world. There were root beds sharing water and refusing one another’s tissue. There were people who belonged to obligations more durably than to rooms.

The human custodians were not Serein’s mind. The rooted majority was not its owner. There was no single ear into which a blessing could be poured.

The power considered the gifts it might have given.

Houses that did not die. Water that never failed. Roots that did not dispute. Matter enough that a market roof need never be paid for with a storehouse, nor a repaired pump with a lane of white down.

Each gift would have relieved someone. Each would also have decided, in advance and from outside, what everyone else could be asked to do without.

A rescued storeroom might be a mercy at the scale of a street and a famine at the scale of its walls. Roots made to agree would no longer be parties to an imperfect treaty; they would be subjects of a successful one. Permanent buildings would accumulate around the addresses until the oldest features of Serein—the commons, the open market centre, the unbuilt margin—had become merely the spaces not yet used.

The power could have made Serein last. It was less certain that it could have made Serein last while leaving it Serein.

It learned to distrust any blessing whose recipients were all visible.

But it did not make a religion of leaving things alone.

Serein’s people were sometimes exhausted. Their meetings could be cruel as well as tedious. A fourth removal in a year did not become easy because the food web was elegant. The power refused to call these things necessary merely because it could see, in section, how beautifully the whole was composed.

Nor did it swear never to intervene. A principle could become another way of not listening. It resolved only that its first kindness would not be a design for everyone.

At Fourth Commons, the cistern keeper struggled alone with a screen swollen by wet root-felt.

“If you’re going to watch,” she said, without looking up, “you can hold this.”

She may have been speaking to the apprentice who had gone to find a cord.

The power held the screen.

It held too firmly at first. The frame creaked in protest, and the keeper clicked her tongue. The power loosened its grip. She cleaned away frog eggs, seed husks, and gray filaments with the unhurried irritation of someone who had performed the task many times and expected to perform it many more. When the apprentice returned, she was already rinsing her hands.

“There,” she said, to whichever of them had helped.

The power did not explain.

It was not a solution. It was only help, and it was finished when the screen was back in place.

The power stayed a little longer. It did not found a shrine, leave a seventh knot, or establish an eternal protectorate over the commons. Any of these would eventually have required custodians, arguments, maintenance, and matter released from somewhere else. A gift with a keeper would become another address.

What it kept was memory.

It remembered the particular delay before one frog answered another. It remembered a small, useless bowl carried through twenty removals. It remembered that a condemned storeroom had been a complete world to lives no census had counted, and not merely material awaiting a roof. It remembered without demanding that anything return. It did not confuse witness with the right to restore.

Then it left. It took no seed, no grafting pattern, no map, and no authority. It did not even promise to come back.

Elsewhere, afterward, it became somewhat less eager to repair every ruin. It left more of its own works with seams by which they could eventually come apart. When it was tempted to preserve something beloved by force, it asked what the preservation would require others to keep—and what it was prepared to stop keeping itself.

In Serein, nothing marked the visit.

By evening, moths had gathered along a roof seam two districts away. A marsh fowl stood alone on the commons. Beneath it, eels ticked in a channel no one could see. The cistern keeper went home by a route that had not existed that morning, and her wrists ached less than she had expected.

Nothing had been made eternal. Nothing had been taught to agree. No one had been saved from the difficult question of what to release.

The power’s gift, if it was one, required no keeper.

It had left no address.
