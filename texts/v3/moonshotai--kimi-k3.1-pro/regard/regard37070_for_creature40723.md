The power would begin by doing nothing, and it would be careful not to make a virtue of that too quickly.

It would listen.

Not only from above, where the rookery might appear as one dark, breathing multitude. It would learn the shelf at the scale of a mite, for whom a breathing tube is a climate; at the scale of a bellwing, for whom the world arrives through six hooks and the pressure of neighboring bodies; at the scale of a grayhand, whose open palm can become an ear. It would stay long enough to understand that a silence could be synchronization, danger, exhaustion, or simply the interval before an answer—and that these were not always distinguishable, even to those waiting within them.

Only then would it consider help.

Water would be the first temptation. A spring in the cleft, a dependable seep, a little clean water that had never passed through a body. But the power would see how easily such a gift could become an invasion: flushing salts from seams, drowning lives arranged around films and fingerprint-deep hollows, waking more than it could sustain. The shelf would perhaps become greener. It might also cease to be the place in which these particular lives were possible.

It would not soften the stone merely because the stone cut wings. It would not preserve every stalled molt merely because stalled molts were pitiable; grayhands and scavengers also had to eat. It would not teach the grayhands to cache tools. Having watched an old animal reject six flakes and choose a seventh, it would know that the enduring achievement was already present—not in the stone, but in the hand.

Nor would it silence the blade.

The returned choruses could be wasteful, even cruel in their consequences, but they were also taken up, altered, and answered. The power would not destroy the rookery’s strange inheritance simply because inheritance was imperfect.

Yet it would not mistake connection for sanctity. A thing could belong to a system and still be a needless loss.

Eventually it would find one.

When the blade gave back an ancient chorus, fasting animals sometimes answered with breath and reserves that could never be replaced. The response might help synchronize them; it might merely spend them. The dead gained nothing. The stone gained nothing. No scavenger was nourished by the fat that vanished. The mountain was innocent, and so were the dead, but their voices could still make the living poorer.

So the power would pay the debt.

Thereafter, an old chorus could still enter living bodies. The bellwings could still answer, and their answer could still acquire new pitches, new imperfections, and a place in the fractures. But whatever that answer cost beyond the cost of remaining silent, the power would restore. No additional strength. No guarantee of a successful molt. No surplus by which the colony might be remodeled according to the power’s own preferences. Only this: no voice preserved in stone could again consume the substance of those who heard it.

To the small creatures wakened by the returned ticking of rain, it would extend the same narrow mercy. Where a signal without water had caused nothing but fatal expenditure, the power would return what the false waking had taken. It would bring no cloud and alter no season. It would simply prevent emptiness from collecting a second time.

For the grayhands, it would change nothing about the returns. Their caution was not a malfunction. One would still stop with a stone closed in one member and the other laid flat against the rock. It would still have to decide whether to resume feeding, move the young, or wait. The power would not whisper an interpretation. Their judgment belonged to them.

Nothing on the shelf would look blessed.

Cases would still fuse. Membranes would still tear. Young grayhands would still choose sharp stones when weight was needed. Most drifting larvae would still arrive where they could not live. The power would not call these things good, nor would it regard its own restraint as innocence. It would have found one harm it could relieve without taking food from one mouth to feed another, or replacing the rookery with a garden of its own design. It would not pretend that this settled the question of what else ought to be done.

It would keep watch.

And it would remember.

Not as the stone remembered, holding pressure until some later movement released it into bodies that had no choice but to respond. The power’s memory would demand no answer. It would keep the failed banks as well as the successful departures, the rejected stones as well as the useful ones, the lives too brief and localized ever to have entered a chorus. This would not redeem their suffering. Memory does not do that. But neither would their lives become material only for the power’s admiration of the whole.

Over centuries, there might be one almost imperceptible sign. A bank that would once have fallen just short of the final molt would reach it. When the cleft opened and the mountain’s low note passed through the shelf, a few more clear membranes would appear in the weak starlight. A few more animals would release their hooks and go outward over the drop.

No census would reveal a miracle. No animal would know to give thanks.

Ages later, a chorus would rise from the empty blade. A living bank would answer. Grayhands would stop and touch the stone. Old vibration would become breath; breath would become new vibration; part of it would enter the fractures again. Nothing would be simplified. Nothing would be explained. But when the antiphon ended, the living would be exactly as strong as if the dead had never called.

The rookery would remain difficult, crowded, and unfinished. The future would still belong to its inhabitants, not to its witness.

But memory would no longer have to feed on the living in order to be heard.

The power would stay, and go on listening.
