The power came before dawn, when the caprock had cooled and the first drops were still gathering in the roof.

It did not enter as light, because light would have troubled the moths. It did not enter as wind, because the root curtains had messages to carry. For a while it merely rested its attention against the fissure, feeling the warm breath rise through the dust.

It had expected to find a being. At first it found only needs.

A snail sealed beneath its membrane. A beetle struggling on warm stone. A gecko spending nothing while hunger spent it slowly. A snake waiting among the roots with the patience of an animal that could not afford impatience. Everywhere, small lives were approaching endings that a sufficiently powerful visitor could prevent.

The power was benevolent, and so its first thought was to put things right.

It could have cooled the floor. It could have opened a spring in the deepest alcove. It could have filled the basin until water stood bright beneath the roof slit. It could have stayed the gecko’s strike, fed the snake without loss, kept every moth from the bats, and made the powdery islands green.

But the power was also wise. It followed each kindness far enough to meet whatever would have to pay for it.

Cooling the floor would alter the rising of vapor. A spring would abolish the delicate addresses of moisture. Water in abundance would not simply enrich the hollow; it would make another place, inhabited by other possibilities, and call the substitution a rescue. To save every hunted thing would be to appoint hunger as the punishment of every hunter. To preserve every life indefinitely would be to fill the chamber with the unconsumed weight of its own mercy.

The power did not therefore call suffering good. It did not admire the moth’s fear or find beauty in the beetle’s dry death. It only saw that pain could not be abolished here by preference, one small creature at a time, without love becoming a kind of blindness.

So it waited.

It waited until its benevolence was no longer in a hurry.

Only then did it recognize the inhabitant.

It was not the snails, nor the roots, nor all the lives together. It was the interval among them: the brief retention by which one thing became available to another. A drop held for an afternoon in a root cup. A fungal felt remaining damp after the wall had dried. Nutrients carried upward in a body, downward in a falling wing, sideways in the gut of an animal crossing a rain-run. The inhabitant had no voice, no memory, no desired future. Yet if it were destroyed, the hollow would not simply change. It would become a warm hole through which water briefly passed.

The power understood what had drawn its attention.

Within the chamber, it changed nothing.

It did not repair the steps. It did not lift the roots from the basin or correct its tilt. It did not wake the snails according to a single, efficient schedule. It did not make the crickets audible, or give the moths enough substance that their deaths would seem more dignified on the floor. It left the hunters hungry and the hunted wary. It left the alcoves dark.

Then it rose to the surface, and there it acted.

It did not make the hollow eternal. Stone would still be permitted to fall. Trees could die; climates could turn; the roots might one day break the basin beyond usefulness. A place protected from every ending would become a specimen, and the power would not preserve it by embalming it.

Instead, it set around the hollow one narrow mercy: it would remain possible to overlook.

The fissure would not blaze with significance. The hot land above it would offer no convenient revelation of value. A quarry might find reason to work the farther side of the hill; a road might meet firmer ground elsewhere; a surveyor might mark the opening and feel no urgency to return. Even those who descended would be permitted to leave without understanding what they had seen.

This was not a curse, and no one would be punished for approaching. The power had no wish to make the hollow terrible. It wished only to prevent its defenselessness from being mistaken for consent.

Its blessing was not that nothing would ever end. It was that the place would not have to become useful in order to be allowed to continue.

Then the power went away.

Below, nothing had improved. The old steps still ended against the blank wall. The basin still leaned. A beetle fell with a faint tick, and something in the leaf litter turned toward the sound. The snakes remained in the roots. Heat continued to rise from below, and the roof continued its slow, temporary cooling.

Just before sunrise, a drop released.

It struck a fern, divided, and passed from one leaf to another. Part of it entered the damp film where a snail would graze when the colony woke. Part remained in a small round leaf, shining without brightness. None of it reached the floor.

The power’s gift had not been rain.

It had been time—not endless time, but time enough for a drop to fall through five leaves and still not be lost.
