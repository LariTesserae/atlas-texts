### What the Power Did

The power did not arrive as light.

Light was the first gift it imagined. It could have made the remote surface shine until every cup, ribbon, and black ceramic roof stood revealed. It could have driven the darkness back through the ducts and shown, at last, the whole extent of the buried province.

But light would not merely have revealed the garden. It would have governed it. The faint seasonal allowance would have become a flood; the relations between upper foliage and lower growth would have altered; lives shaped by scarcity and retention would have been required to answer an abundance they had never asked for. The power did not assume that because it found the darkness sorrowful, the darkness was a wound.

Nor did it speak.

It looked for the being who lived in the Cistern and found no single resident awaiting it. There were films within pores, felt in the joints, cups filling with their own patient sediment, clonal bodies continuing around interruptions. The garden was occupied, but not by someone who could be summoned.

The power could have made a mind there: something central, continuous, and capable of answering. It could have given the thickets a voice and then asked whether they were lonely. But that would not have been the discovery of a hidden inhabitant. It would have been the creation of a new one, perhaps able to suffer from conditions the old garden had simply inhabited. The power did not call that rescue.

So, for a long time, it did something almost indistinguishable from nothing. It paid attention without touching.

It remained through more than the settling of a spore. It remained while a water front crossed a district, while films altered and larger growth had not yet answered. It saw that an apparently dead stem could be support, that a clogged pipe could still conduct a life, that an untouched surface was not necessarily a neglected one. It learned to postpone the judgments that came easily to a visitor: *choked, ruined, dormant, empty.*

Still, it did not decide that the Cistern was perfect.

Scarcity did not become beautiful merely because life had endured it. Damage did not become repair simply because the two were often intertwined. A spore that failed was not secretly a success, and persistence was not proof of joy. The power refused to make a virtue of everything it did not know how to relieve.

It considered warmth, and saw how much of the garden depended on cold patience. It considered animals, and saw that the first grazing mouth would be not companionship but a new weather. It considered restoration. That temptation lasted longest.

The power could have cleared the conduits, lifted the old courts, and returned the system to some intelligible operating plan. But to reopen a pipe would be to tear out its lace. To expose the pavements would be to strip away the felt. To restore the builders’ intention would be to decide that the vanished dead had a greater claim on the Cistern than the lives that had inherited it. Restoration, too, could be an eviction.

Finally, it considered eternity.

It could have preserved the basin exactly: no further loss of tile, no failed support, no district passing beyond recognition. But a Cistern unable to fracture, settle, clog, or outlast its own arrangements would no longer be the Cistern. It would be an image held still for the satisfaction of its keeper.

At last the power understood that its earliest impulses had largely been attempts to relieve its own distress. It had wanted to give light because it disliked imagining blindness. It had wanted to give speech because it disliked unanswered questions. It had wanted to repair because continuation without visible purpose made it uneasy.

Benevolence had made it willing to act. Wisdom required it to ask what the action would be for.

So its care became narrow and exact.

It would not improve the garden. It would not preserve it by force. It would not make its own goodness a reason to render the place useful, legible, or grateful. This was not a vow never to intervene; indifference can also call itself restraint. If some future harm required an act, that act would have to be discovered then, with the same patience. It could not be decided in advance by a visitor who had once found the darkness sad.

The power stayed until it no longer needed to be thanked.

Then it withdrew. It took no tile, no cutting, no dormant spore. It left no lamp at the margin and no inscription beneath a stone to prove that someone had cared. Even a small sign would have made the visit part of the Cistern’s history, and the power had finally become willing not to be.

Its only memorial was outside the garden, within itself: the knowledge that a place need not be suffering to merit attention, and need not be transformed to merit love.

Far below, water with another mineral history entered a district. A film changed before anything larger showed a response. A holdfast entered a pore that had only just opened. A spore settled among its predecessors and did not germinate.

Nothing gave thanks. Nothing had been asked to.

The power had done almost nothing. It had loved the Cistern without revising it.
