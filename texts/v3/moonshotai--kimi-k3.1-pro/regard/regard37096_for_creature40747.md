### Nothing That Needed a Roof

The power’s first act was to silence the Calving Basin.

It did not mean to. It merely turned its attention downward, and the scale-lice stopped. The stillness ran through them in widening patches: pool rims, wall cracks, the backs of oxen, the shell grit beneath the lodge. Joint-runners hesitated. Several clapwings let go of their roost and clapped into the dust. At the entry ledge, a traveler put one hand to the alarm cord and waited for the second sign.

The power withdrew.

Its second act was to return more quietly.

For a long while it did nothing but listen. It learned water under stone from scale-lice over it, and the tick of a bad floor from the ringing complaint of an ox shedding a plate. It heard companies divide around water and reunite around voices. It heard children being taught, patiently and then impatiently, that silence was not an answer but a question.

The large mercies occurred to it. It could have given the Basin a river. It could have made the gypsum cups refill, fastened the roof blocks into their fractures, laid a harmless skin over every pavement-mouth. It could have caused something green to root beneath the skylights.

It did not reject these things because change was impure, or because suffering had secretly been good for everyone. It rejected doing them alone. Each gift would have required further gifts to correct what the first had injured, until the Basin no longer belonged to stone, water, animals, or the people who had learned to live there. It would have belonged to its benefactor.

But the power did not make the opposite mistake either. It did not look at a crushed calf or a traveler fevered beside a dry cup and call the sight necessary. Competence was not consent. Endurance was not a request to be left unaided.

Once, beneath the ribbed terraces, it held a settling block for seven breaths while a company dragged their water-carrier free. Then it let the block fall. Dust whited out the court; the herds answered from beyond it. The company called their escape luck, and the power did not correct them. No one was required to be improved by being rescued.

At last it began to ask what help would mean.

An old listener named Sera was the first to answer without either fear or ceremony. The power found her in the low lodge, repairing a gut water bag beside a fat lamp. Her company had gone to an emptying sump. She had stayed because her knee would not bend properly, and because someone had to keep the joint-runners from the cord.

“You came quietly this time,” she said.

“Yes.”

“That is an improvement.”

The power said that it wished to give her people something that would last.

Sera drew the bone needle through the seam.

“Nothing that needs a roof,” she said.

“You would refuse permanence?”

“I did not say that. I said you had chosen a poor place to keep it.”

The lamp fluttered in a dry breath from the inner passage. Sera waited until the flame steadied.

“If you can bring someone home,” she said, “bring them home. If a child is under a stone and you can lift it, lift it. Do not stand outside the court being wise about the herds. Do not make our competence your excuse.”

“And if help changes you?”

“Then ask again tomorrow.”

She held out the water bag.

“For tonight, you can hold the light lower.”

The power did.

“There,” she said, after a while. “That was help.”

So the power made no single gift in the name of everyone. It asked many companies, and their answers did not agree. Some wanted healing. Some wanted missing kin. Some wanted safe conduct out of the Basin and help making a life beyond it, not merely abandonment on the surface with a blessing. Some wanted nothing to do with the power at all. It did not make their refusal a test of worth.

It helped where it could, and remained where its help could be questioned. It demanded no belief, gratitude, or obedience. When its assistance had consequences it had not foreseen, it returned and helped with those too. It was slower to intervene among the other animals, not because their lives mattered less, but because it was slower to assume it knew what assistance meant to them. It did not make the pavement-mouth harmless and call that justice. Neither did it admire the stilt-hunter’s hunger as an explanation sufficient to the clapwing.

And when someone was dying beyond help, it listened.

It did not tell them that their death was useful. It did not ask them to forgive the roof, the draining sump, the bad floor, or the mouth beneath the grit. It asked only whether there was anything they wished carried.

Some gave route knowledge: not directions stripped of their conditions, but whole accounts. *The cup still dripped this morning. The floor held one ox and would not hold the herd. Do not come by the white court unless the wing-noise has returned.*

Some gave names.

Some said, *Do not wait.*

Some said, *I was angry. I am sorry.*

Some chose silence, and the power kept that faithfully too.

It delivered no prophecies and answered no questions on the dead’s behalf. It did not improve their grammar, soften their accusations, or make them wiser than they had been. Children learned a new listening game: to repeat a message without adding a warning, removing an insult, or turning grief into instruction. It was harder than distinguishing wings from their reflections.

The Basin itself remained much as it had been. Pools failed. Fresh brine whitened elsewhere. Herds carried film from one chamber to the next. Travelers still woke with their hands on their boots when the nearest scale-lice stopped. But fewer companies spent dangerous days waiting for footsteps that would never come. Knowledge did not always die with the only person who possessed it. Sometimes the living could mourn and leave, instead of making hope another reason to remain beneath a ticking roof.

The people called the power the Listener, when they called it anything. They built it no shrine. It had asked for none, and the lodge was already sufficiently occupied.

Years later, it came to Sera near the end of her life. Her company was nearby. Beyond the hides, oxen were moving over shell pavement, and somewhere a small fall of mineral crust made a brief, glassy clatter.

“My message is for Nao,” Sera said.

The power listened.

“Tell her she hears better than I ever did. Tell her that is not a reason to walk first every time.”

The power waited.

“That is all,” said Sera. “Do not make it into more.”

“No.”

“You were considering it.”

“Yes.”

She died a little later, with the ordinary noise continuing around her. She was not alone.

When Nao received the message, she repeated it exactly. Then she laughed once, covered her face, and wept. The power stayed. When she had finished, she asked whether it would help carry water to the others.

It did.

By morning the company had gone. A joint-runner was testing the grease on a stone, scale-lice were gathering beneath a new wet stain, and the people’s footprints had begun to whiten.

The power preserved none of them. Care, it had learned, was not a way of making the world hold still.
