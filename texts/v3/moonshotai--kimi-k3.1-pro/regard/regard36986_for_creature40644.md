### What the Power Did

It would not bring a river.

That would be the first temptation of a lesser kindness: to see the dark nets, the grooved fins, the covered channels, and mistake exact sufficiency for hardship. A river would make the cisterns plentiful and meaningless. It would alter the gardens, the paths, the work, the disputes, even the uses of leisure. Generations of knowledge would become answers to a question no one was asking anymore.

Nor would it set the shadows moving, send weather against the cliffs, or make the stone begin to weather merely so that Serein could have what elsewhere was called a future. It would not look at the green seams and decide that they ought to conquer the dry rock. It would not make the city grow in order to prove that its people were alive.

Even benevolence could make an unexamined claim on everything around it.

So its first act would be attention: attention prolonged enough to become accurate.

It would learn that the unchanging waterline did not mean an unchanging life. People still acquired skill, affection, embarrassment, grudges, and private histories. A repair could be public perfection and personal misery. A person could love Serein and sometimes be bored almost beyond endurance by it. Another could find, after long restlessness, that clearing a gutter had become a form of peace. Neither would necessarily require the basin to be altered.

The power would also understand that Serein was not a miniature made for its admiration. Its inhabitants were not the delicate moving parts of a successful arrangement. The city’s completeness did not settle every question that might arise in a human life.

Only then would it enter Serein, wearing a body that could become cold and tired, and ask to be useful.

On a saddle, it would offer to hold a condenser frame while a keeper retied the netting. Its first knot would be strong, elegant, and impossible to undo with cold fingers. The keeper would tell it so. It would untie the knot and do the work again.

Later, at the roofed meeting ground, it would say:

“I have not come to rescue you. I have not come to complete you. Is there anything you want me to do?”

The response would not be immediate. People would have to sleep, work, consult absent relatives, and consider whether any proposed miracle would create additional maintenance for another ward. Someone would want to know whether a divinely supplied reservoir cover could be repaired by ordinary means. Someone else would object that this was exactly the sort of question powers used to make themselves indispensable.

There would be suspicion, curiosity, jokes at the power’s expense, and at least one lengthy argument whose connection to its offer would become difficult to establish.

Eventually, the public response might be: nothing for Serein.

Not because change was forbidden. Not because the people wished to become eternal curators of their own restraint. Simply because no public gift had proved itself better than what they already had, and because they declined to let a stranger’s abilities become a civic obligation.

The power would accept this. It would leave no secret blessing in the reservoirs, no latent abundance waiting to astonish them, no little improvement whose discovery would teach them to be grateful.

But it would not confuse the response of Serein with the response of every person in it.

Privately, it would continue to listen. Someone might ask for relief from an old pain. Someone might want an answer, or company, or help saying something to another person without having the power settle the matter by force. Someone might ask it to arbitrate a quarrel and discover that it would not make their preference into justice merely because they had asked first.

A restless rim-walker might say, “I do not want the city changed. I only want to know whether I am allowed to want something else.”

The power would not prescribe contentment. Nor would it praise restlessness as a superior form of wisdom. If departure were desired and truly impossible, it would make departure possible; if return were desired, that too. If the rim-walker only needed to speak without someone immediately proposing a higher wall or a larger garden, then listening would be enough.

To those who asked for nothing, it would give nothing.

Its one lasting act would concern only itself: it would remember.

Not merely the measurements, the clean levels, and the beautiful repairs, but the inaccurate marmot calls, the jokes that depended on a particular keeper’s expression, the route someone preferred for no defensible reason, the affections and regrets that had required no monument in stone. Nothing would be preserved in Serein by this remembrance. No archive would appear, no obligation to be worthy of it. But no life would have to leave an accumulation behind in order to have been fully real.

Then the power would go.

At the lowest cistern, a goat would put its muzzle to the water. Rings would travel outward and vanish. The surface would settle at the carved mark. The cloud would remain beyond the saddle. No fresh inscription would darken the walls.

Nothing in Serein would have been improved. No one would have been ignored.

The power would not have saved the city. It would have learned that the city did not need saving—and that this was no reason to stop caring about the people.

Serein would remain complete. Its people would not be required to be.
