### The Grounded Guest

The first thing the power did was refrain from rain.

Rain had occurred to it. The Crown was so dry, its distances so severe, its people so careful about the simple act of touch. The power could have filled the deep faults with water, softened the air, hung clouds around the summits. It could have made the cliffs fruitful in a manner more easily recognized as fruitfulness.

It looked longer.

Water would not complete the Crown. It would alter the stone, the films, the enclosed chemistries of every living thing. It would turn an entire ecology into the casualty of a visitor’s idea of kindness. The same was true of other possible gifts. Bringing the summits closer together would abolish more than inconvenience. Ending every equalization would change industry, custom, forecast, and the meanings people had made around danger. Repairing every structure before it failed would leave the Crownfolk living inside works they had not chosen and could not inspect.

These were not proofs that every hardship was good. They were reasons not to confuse transformation with help.

So the power listened.

It learned a district through a handrail. This took years. At first there was only vibration; then there were kiln strokes, freight cages, footsteps, overloaded bearings, children in an upper corridor. Sievebacks grazing beyond a wall produced a faint combing that could be mistaken for the settling of dust. A household at evening was not silent. It was a complex condition of the foundations.

The power also learned that a coherent world was not necessarily a painless one. A badly grounded tool still hurt. A missed visit still hurt. Animals died because crews were careless, and some crews remained careless after being shown the cost. Grief was not ennobled by the excellent condition of the archives. Long life did not make any of these things decorative.

The power therefore made no vow never to interfere. Such a vow might have served its own purity more than the Crown’s good.

Its first intervention came on an ordinary afternoon, when an old fracture opened in a nursery wall beneath a load the survey had underestimated.

The wall held for eleven seconds longer than it should have.

That was long enough for the children and the two adults with them to get clear. Then it fell. The power did not restore it. To do so immediately would have concealed the error that had nearly killed them. It told the work crew exactly what it had supported, what it had observed in the fracture, and where its knowledge ended. It offered no explanation of destiny.

Then, being among strangers, it asked to be shown the grounding stud.

Accounts of the incident varied considerably by evening. One district wanted the power expelled. Another wanted all future collapses prevented in advance. A kiln crew asked whether it would settle an old dispute concerning surface finishes. It declined. Several councils pointed out that an entity capable of holding up a wall was also capable of holding up a lie, and that the second ability deserved at least as much attention as the first.

The agreements took decades. They were characteristically unglamorous.

The power would do no concealed work. It would sign no inspection on their behalf. It would leave no repair whose principles could not be examined, and no system whose continued operation required its continued goodwill. When it issued a warning, it would state its uncertainty and, where possible, suggest a way of being proved wrong. In immediate danger it could act to preserve life and choice, but a rescue would not entitle it to redesign what came afterward. It would not manufacture suffering to teach lessons, nor prevent every error merely because the consequences might be painful.

The power accepted these conditions without regarding them as ingratitude. It had not come to be trusted on the evidence of its intentions.

After that, it mostly did nothing.

It watched pressure tides alter the distances. It traveled by cable when traveling was necessary and discovered that even a being of great power could become bored between summits. It learned several jokes badly. During equalizations it withdrew into the galleries with everyone else, listened to inconsistent accounts of previous discharges, and was repeatedly accused of misunderstanding the rules of games it had in fact understood perfectly.

Occasionally it gave warning of something below the reach of current instruments: a deep fault changing its load, a charged dust system behaving unlike the models, a cable anchor whose apparent stability depended on an unnoticed condition. It did not ask to be believed. It showed the Crownfolk where to look.

Sometimes it lent strength during a repair. The new splice remained visible. The calculations remained in the archive. It never made a cable unbreakable; it helped, when asked, to make a failure easier to detect.

It did not settle the old angers between districts. It did not clean the margins of the archives. It did not make every crew considerate of animals, though it could be extremely patient about remaining present while a council considered the evidence. When people grieved, it did not tell them that a closed file was a closed life. It stayed, when staying was wanted, without offering an explanation that would have made the grief more convenient for everyone else.

Over the centuries, it was needed less often. Instruments improved. Survey methods improved. Crews became better at asking one another for help before asking it. The power regarded this as one of its few unambiguous successes.

An entry eventually appeared in a shared archive:

> OUTSIDE POWER. Capabilities incompletely surveyed. Not an acceptable substitute for ground inspection, structural review, or local judgment. May assist in emergencies. All assistance to be recorded. No permanent dependency known. Continued benevolence not to be assumed without review.

In the margin, another hand had added:

> Does not sulk when checked.

And below that:

> Good at holding walls. Invite to gallery if nearby.

The Crown remained dry, warm, quarrelsome, and unfinished. Mountains lost grains. Freight schedules were disputed. Animals were injured through avoidable haste. Some repairs were postponed too long. Some apologies crossed the gaps in person. The kites still departed before the instruments could say why.

The power had not given the Crownfolk a destiny. It had tried, so far as wisdom allowed, to preserve their ability to have one. Mostly, that meant leaving the next decision in living hands, with the doubts still attached.

During one open-handed interval, after an equalization had passed, the power stood on a terrace among residents touching rails and tools and one another with unceremonious hands. Through the metal came the stroke of a kiln engine, an empty freight cage taking load, and children running two levels below.

Nothing in the plaza looked changed.

Someone tapped the power’s name into its palm—not a title, just the name—and moved aside to make room at the rail.
