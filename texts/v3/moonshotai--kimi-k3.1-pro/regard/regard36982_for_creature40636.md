## A Way Around

The power did not arrive in light.

Light would have been an imposition. It waited in the central oval, where the Ward kept its emptiness, and listened until it could distinguish the common cistern’s note from the smaller, quicker dripping at Three Drips. When the gutter went still, it stopped too. When the pressure pulse came, it felt the far seal tighten, the walls draw inward, the bridges lift, and the gates change hands.

It saw that safety here was not a condition but a repeated act. A cord had to have been checked earlier. A child had to remember stillness. Someone at the far seal had to trust work done in darkness by someone else. Much of the Ward was maintenance. Much of the maintenance was affection. Some of it was fear. None of this, by itself, told the power what ought to be done.

Its first thoughts were large, and most of them were vain.

To make the root loving would be to make another being and call the substitution rescue. To fix the lanes forever would be to preserve the Ward’s shape at the expense of its life. To carry everyone above would be easy, and would make refusal look like ingratitude.

But there was also a vanity in doing nothing: admiring the gate order, the knotted maps, the beautiful economy of a repaired wrapping, and calling that admiration respect. A threshold could be beautifully made and a hand could still be infected. The Ward’s meaning did not make its pain useful.

So it asked.

At the next gray inspection, it made itself visible only as a little more grayness near the plug and spoke no louder than the warders spoke to one another.

“Are you speaking for the root?” someone asked.

“No.”

“Good,” said the pore-keeper. “Then we needn’t argue about that first.”

The power said that it might be able to help. It did not say that it knew what help was. It made no demand for belief and permitted distrust.

The answers did not agree. It did not require them to.

A woman with an infected hand asked for healing. A work party wanted help through a failed hatch. Several people wished to leave but had never found a way to say so without seeming to condemn everyone who stayed. Others wanted the power gone before it became another presence whose moods had to be managed. Some asked it to settle disputes between maps; it declined to become an infallible surveyor, suspecting how quickly infallibility would become a chief.

It healed where healing was wanted. It brought food through scarcity when asked, without attaching to the gift a plan for how the recipients should live thereafter. It helped those who wished to leave find more than a place outside: shelter, work, and people before whom they would not have to spend the rest of their lives being grateful. Those who refused it were not followed by secret improvements.

When it would not grant a request, it gave reasons and allowed itself to be argued with. Sometimes the arguments changed its mind.

One thing it left for everyone, whether they had asked or not.

Just within the outer plug, where a searching thumb could reach it, it set a loop that resembled cord only until it was touched. Then it felt simply like slack.

However the root grew, that slack would remain. A pull would open the plug from within. It required no password, no strength beyond a child’s, no consent from a bridge crew. It did not make the bog above kind, and the power did not pretend otherwise. But no one using it would be met only by weather.

Those who went out could return, if return was what they wanted. No decision made at the plug had to be made forever.

The warders treated this with more suspicion than wonder. They tested it, dirtied it, greased it, asked whether eels could use it, and argued about whether it belonged in the gate order. Backflow House refused for two thaws to put it on its map, on the grounds that anything requiring no maintenance was probably concealing its cost. The power did not take offense. This was not an unreasonable thing to believe about gifts.

They called it the Long Hinge, though there was no hinge and nothing about it was long.

Some people left. A few returned. Most never used the loop except to check, now and then, that it was there—as they checked emergency cords they hoped never to need.

The power built nothing in the central oval. It accepted no household name and answered no prayers addressed to the root on its behalf. It remained near enough to be asked again, and far enough away that a bad argument did not require its opinion.

At the next pressure pulse, the springtails climbed. Beetles unhooked themselves from the walls. The gutter lifted and hesitated, and somewhere beneath it an eel turned into a side pore. Bridges rose. Gates changed. Vulnerable goods passed hand to hand.

Afterward, someone whispered that a threshold had narrowed, and someone else insisted it had always been narrow. The maps still disagreed. The food still had to be inspected. The dead still went to the midden-lock. The root had not learned to care.

But at the outer plug, in complete darkness, there was one small slackness that growth could not take away.

Not safety. Not an end to work. Not a reason to stop helping.

A way around.
