### A Seam Left Open

The power came, if coming is the word, while the shelf was letting go of its drops.

The great pump had just completed its stroke. Water fell from leaf tips and pipe joints into the root mats. The clear snails drew close to their surfaces. Steam rose beside the cliff, brightened, and vanished.

The power stayed until the brief rain had ended and the snails had extended again. Then it stayed longer.

There was no single resident to whom a gift could be handed. The snail beneath the second return pipe did not contain the shelf. Neither did the fin moth, nor the blackstem whose rhizomes joined apparently separate cushions under stone. If the Kettle Shelf had one being, it was the arrangement among them: heat and leakage, root and fungus, metal and mineral, brief abundance and long scarcity.

A gift to one part might easily become an injury to the rest.

The power considered making the ledge broader. It considered softening the wind, deepening the soil, giving the moth a longer adult life and the snails a safer means of dispersal. It considered pipes that would not corrode, a geothermal heart whose output would never falter, condenser fins that cleaned themselves without disturbing the hanging growth.

Each mercy would have made a more comfortable place. None would have made quite this one.

Even the station’s eventual silence was not, in itself, an evil. The engines made water for people below. To keep them running forever merely to preserve the garden would have been to make those unseen towns serve the power’s pleasure in a green fleck on a mountain. Nor did the cold lichens require the green disturbance beside them to become everlasting.

Doing nothing would have been clean. It would not necessarily have been kind.

For there was one needless absoluteness in the shelf’s fortunes. If the machinery were abandoned, the garden would probably contract toward the natural steam seams. But contraction and disappearance were not separated by very much: a few metres of cold rock, a fracture closed by silica, a root that arrived fifty years too late. The whole community might end not because the mountain had ceased to offer it anywhere to live, but because no path remained small and wet enough for it to find that place.

So the power did one thing.

Beneath the cliff, behind the pump house, a natural fracture already breathed warm water into the basalt. Most of it emerged too hot for anything but microbial films. Another branch had nearly sealed itself with white rind. The power altered no pressure that mattered to the engines and took no water from the white main. It only prevented one fine branch of that fracture from closing completely for a very long time.

Not forever. Nothing was promised forever.

A root could not have passed through the opening. Water could.

On the surface, the change amounted to a patch of dark rock no larger than a hand, and in dry intervals not always to that. No gauge trembled. The turbine’s bass note did not change. There was no new fern, no snail delivered safely in a shell, no moth guided toward a warmer stone.

The power planted nothing. It was willing to let the gift go unused.

Years passed. Mineral films gathered and were buried. The seam’s small wetness persisted through repair intervals that dried other places. Microbial life established itself in the warm crust. Saddle moss appeared in a pocket of dust close enough to receive occasional splash. Once, an overflow channel changed course and reached it for part of a season; later, roots diverted the water elsewhere.

Much later, a blackstem rhizome found the fracture.

It did not flourish impressively. Its first fronds were small. One died back when a keeper, following an ordinary work order, diverted an overflow and unknowingly removed half the patch’s moisture. The power did not correct him. Elsewhere on the shelf, other repairs made other droughts and floods. Snails were swept away. The fin moth passed whole years without being seen. The station’s keepers remained, as they had always been, agents of both care and considerable accidental violence.

The refuge grew no faster than refuge required.

When the station at last became unnecessary, the power did not restart it. The pumps stopped. The fin bank cooled, and the moth’s archipelago of warm metal went dark. Condensation ceased to fall from surfaces that no longer remained warm at night. Large portions of the mat browned, thinned, and began the slow work of becoming dry fibre and dust. There was no reason to call this beautiful, and no reason to call it a failure.

Near the cliff, the garden became very small.

It was not the Kettle Shelf preserved in miniature. There was no private rain. Some populations did not reach it, and some that did did not persist. But warm water still darkened the rock. Moss occupied the dust. Blackstem held a narrow place along the seam, and beneath its dead fronds the older mat remained damp.

On the outer stone, the lichens continued without having been asked to witness anything.

The power had not made the garden independent of fortune. It had only made one human ending not necessarily the garden’s own.

Beneath the white rind, in a space no root could enter, warm water continued on its way.
