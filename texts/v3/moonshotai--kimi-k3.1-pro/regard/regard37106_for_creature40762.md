### The Interval

At first, the power would do nothing that Sere could distinguish from attention.

They would stay long enough to learn that a shining wall was not necessarily leaking, that a wet garden could still be dying, and that two households maintaining the same pipe did not therefore inhabit the same town. They would learn the difference between the flywheels’ note and the sound of a bearing beginning to fail; between the silence of people resting and the silence of people who had ceased to expect help.

They would not mistake Sere’s patience for contentment.

Nor would they fall in love with its difficulty. That would be the first temptation: to admire the stained hands, the covered cups, the roots measured year by year, and to decide that nothing should be eased because everything had meaning. But a custom can be wise without making every burden that produced it good. A town can be beautiful and still sleep badly. Maintenance can be love and also exhaustion.

So they would not preserve Sere exactly.

They would not send rain. A generous river on the surface could become a catastrophe below, splitting roots, souring fungal beds, carrying minerals into places where a film of scale had once been the whole event of a decade. They would not make the engines cold, the gardens inexhaustible, or the people unfailingly gentle. Neither would they awaken some vast consciousness in the basalt and appoint it ruler. Sere did not need to be made into a person before its lives could matter.

When they acted, their help would have one character: it would widen the interval between harm and choice.

Far beneath the eastern galleries, a fracture once began to alter its course toward the cold bore. Nothing announced it. The rock at the surface remained sharp and dry; the condensers answered at their usual hours; the deep heat did not quicken. In another century the bore would begin, almost imperceptibly, to warm. The engines would compensate. Then the gardens would compensate. By the time the change became undeniable, there would be too little cold left to power the work of making a new course.

The power could have closed the fracture and left no sign. Instead, they held it where it was and made it visible.

On an abandoned tile near the east return, a bead of water crept three finger-widths away from the heat.

A child saw it first. She called another child, who called a fitter, who said that abandoned tiles were unreliable and that children were worse. The next day two beads did it. On the third day a water reader came with a cloth, dried the tile, marked its edges, and waited.

The inspection that followed took eleven years.

It required opening a wall behind a sleeping alcove, removing a condenser plate older than the household around it, and stripping a vine whose roots had entered the wrong seam. Cuttings were taken. Not all of them lived. Two old water rights had to be argued over again, and one household never accepted that the disturbance had been necessary. The power did not soften any of this into harmony. They had not given Sere a command, only time in which to investigate, disagree, and act.

When the bore was finally exposed, the fracture was visible as a line no wider than a root hair. The engineers understood enough then to choose their repair. They made it with their own machines, at their own deliberate speed.

Only afterward did the power let the rock go.

The ledgers did not record a rescue. They recorded an incorrect condensation path, a new inspection passage, and the year in which the east return was dry in the wrong way. Children continued to use other tiles for experiments with the draw.

That would not be the limit of the power’s benevolence. There is a kind of wisdom that loves wholes because wholes cannot complain about the parts sacrificed to them. The power would not possess that wisdom.

A burned fitter would not be regarded as an instructive accident. A household’s exhaustion would not be called devotion. A person who wanted sunlight, an unbroken room, or a life in which no pipe required their attention would not be treated as a defective component. Where healing, rest, departure, or return could be given without taking over the life receiving it, the power would give it. Not every mercy would be made into a public sign. Sere had enough public surfaces already.

But neither would they protect every living thing from every use. Gardens would still need cutting. A vine might outlive three households and still have to come down. The snails would not be distributed according to human ideas of fairness, nor moths made common for the delight of children. Some losses would remain losses, not problems awaiting correction. The power would know the difference between refusing all change and refusing to let change outrun care.

They would accept no shrine and settle no water-rights dispute by revelation. A power that could be quoted whenever a balcony had to be stripped would soon become only another means of avoiding the neighbour whose wall was opened.

Above all, they would not occupy the noon line.

Its narrow, discontinuous emptiness would remain empty. Even benevolence, the power seemed to understand, could become an obstruction.

On most days, therefore, nothing would happen.

A water reader would enter a home by appointment, step around a sleeping child, and find the cold pipe wet where it should be. She would write down the width of a root. A snail would cross a black leaf. Far below, a hot-work shift would change; far above, someone would uncover a jar. The reader would leave the wall damp and the household asleep.

On a cold pipe, a bead would swell, move slightly toward the heat, and fall.

The power would not catch it.

They had not come to keep Sere unchanged. They had come so that, now and then, there would be enough time to notice what was changing—and enough freedom to decide what care required.
