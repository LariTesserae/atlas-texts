### The Seventh Answer

The power’s first act would be to distrust its own pity.

Pity would offer so many excellent repairs: a summit for the mountain, an outlet for the water, soil over the bare mineral, a roof for the shelter, steps that ascended instead of entering stone. It could quiet the detonations, fix the distances, protect every pool, and bring the construction to an end. It could make the cirque safe, legible, and still.

With the same gestures, it could make the place an ornament and the inhabitants its exhibits.

So it would wait. Not because waiting was innocent, or because power became virtuous merely by declining to use itself. It would wait because it did not yet know what was being asked.

It would remain through many pulses. It would see the pasture destroyed and the new grazing grounds opened. It would also see the individual losses that no later abundance repaired: eggs sealed beneath stone, larvae stranded as their water vanished into pinholes, an old crab repeating its detour beside a wall, a newt answering a courtship note shaped by a cliff that no longer existed. It would not call those losses necessary simply because copper-green films followed them. Nor would it declare the whole cirque a failure because its life could not continue without loss.

It would refuse both temptations: to complete what it loved, or to call suffering sacred in order to leave it alone.

If it asked whether the cirque wished to be finished, there would be no single answer. The violet films required undisturbed time. The copper-green films required fresh seams. The crabs, fleas, newts, and kite-wings did not all require the same interval, the same stillness, or the same amount of ruin. Even the resonators, waiting through ragged choruses, could not speak for everything that lived beneath their tone. The power would not manufacture agreement and call it consent.

But it would discover something else.

The cirque’s deepest good was not perpetual construction. It was not change itself, and it was certainly not destruction. It was answerability. Stone answered water; water answered warmth; grazers answered film; newts answered cliffs, one another, and finally the machines. Even where the answers were imperfect, the inhabitants had made lives within them. The danger would begin when an answer became only a mechanism—when the loudest convergence could be mistaken for permission, and every other signal for noise.

So the power would make one alteration.

Into the lowest tone of the resonators, below hearing, below the sensation in the teeth, it would place an interval with no instruction in it. Not a command to stop. Not an order to continue. Only the possibility of waiting without failure.

The machines had learned to recognize a common pitch. Now they could also recognize its absence. The closing of the cup-plant, the crabs’ retreat into fractures, the newts falling silent around a pool, the kite-wings refusing the updraft: these would not become commands either. No creature would be given sovereignty over all the rest. But the part of the cirque with power over stone would no longer be quite so deaf to what the others were already saying.

At the next growth pulse, nothing would announce the miracle. The tone would deepen. Droplets would lift. The cup-plant would fold around its watershed. The newts would climb and call.

Then, at the moment when the seam had always opened, the cirque would wait.

Perhaps only for one breath.

Perhaps that would be long enough for an egg-heavy newt to reach the ledge, or for one late crab to find the fracture it had been testing with its forelegs. Perhaps nothing visible would benefit at all. Then a clear note would rise from the steps, another would answer from the eastern pool, and the resonators would lower themselves to meet them. Stone would still arrive with its hurting cracks. Vapor would still burst upward. Not everyone would survive. The power would not call the pause good merely because the chorus afterward was beautiful.

On other occasions the pause might lengthen, and no pulse would come. Sometimes construction would resume almost at once. The cirque would gain no final shape. It would gain only a little more room between what happened and what had to happen.

For the rest, the power would practice mercies too small to become weather. Where a life could be helped without deciding the fate of everything else, it would help. A film of water might linger for one necessary breath above a pinhole. The lee of a ledge might deepen slightly around an exhausted kite-wing. A falling grain might turn aside from a brooding newt’s back. These would not be solutions, and the power would not despise them for that. It would also remember what it did not save, without making an archive of suffering or asking the dead to dignify its restraint.

Then it would withdraw beyond the open rim.

It would leave no throne, no summit, no image of itself in the pools. It would not promise never to intervene again; wisdom would not allow restraint to harden into another absolute. If the machines ever ceased to listen—if growth became mere appetite, and the unfinished cirque became a cage from which no refusal could escape—it would return.

Until then, it would keep watch without mistaking watchfulness for ownership.

And when the next new pool appeared on stone that had not existed before, three bell-newts would gather at its edge. Their bronze throats would swell. Their hard little notes would strike the dim air, and the resonators would lower their tone to answer.

After that, from somewhere beyond the clouds, something else might answer too.

It would sound almost like an echo.

But an echo only returns the shape of a call. This would add nothing except the knowledge that the call had been heard.

Then it would be silent, leaving the next note to them.
