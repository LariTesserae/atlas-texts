### A Correction to the Map

The power’s first mercy was to wait.

Not because the Underbloom was beautiful enough to justify its dangers. Beauty had never filled a gourd or caught a falling child. Nor because suffering was necessary to make the route-kin what they were. The power had no use for that argument.

It waited because the first mercies it imagined were all too large.

A gentler climate would wake buds whose needs no one had counted. Rivers would drown one province and feed growth that tore another loose. A bridge made permanently strong would cease to answer tension in the ways its grafted lives expected; a city made permanent would require water, crossings, and ground to remain obedient around it. To make the whole Underbloom hospitable, the power would have to decide which tissues could grow, which could die, which lives should flourish, and which should be postponed for them.

It would have to replace the place with something easier to love.

So it went there.

It came in a body that could numb at the fingers and be deceived by distance. It learned to weave dark fiber into an eye-shade, to approach a warm sap vein without provoking the tissue, to distinguish the silence of dormancy from the silence of withdrawal. At dew stations it drank without fouling the membrane and left empty gourds open. It traded news before food. Its maps, like everyone’s, acquired refusals.

It watched travelers leave an old grazer alive above a warm vein. It watched them take sound cord from the dead and grieve anyway. It saw children drop petals into the gulf and then look carefully to see where any fragment caught.

At a long-tended station, it understood that *kind* was not a promise the place had made. It was a word for what separate lives had briefly done together.

When at last the power made itself known, it did so at one of the recurring meetings. Baskets hung in the root fork. Cord, seed, metal, and fermenting cultures were changing hands. Several people were arguing over whether a distant stain represented a bloom-front or a spore-storm.

The power asked what help would be welcome.

The people did not answer with one voice, and it did not require them to.

The first request it granted was small enough to be spoken while a leaf membrane was being repaired. A woman said:

“My brother’s people went toward the white fork. If you find them, tell them the black cups are bitter again. Tell them our mother died. We kept her blade. If they need it, it is theirs.”

She gave directions with three alternatives and no guarantee that any of them would still exist.

The power went.

It found the brother’s people and delivered the message. It said when it had left the meeting, and did not pretend that nothing could have changed since. Then it carried their answer back.

After that, there was always another message.

It brought word of torn anchors, bitter cups, new shelves, sealed stairs, and crossings that had been usable when last seen. It never said, *The way is safe.* It said, *When I passed, the third anchor was holding, but the outer runners were dry.* Its gifts were dated, situated, and allowed to become wrong.

It carried names as carefully as corrections. A death could be spoken at a meeting the body would never have reached. A child could learn that a woman whose cord stair remained sealed inside clear bark had not merely built a stair, but had once laughed so hard at a plant reader’s mistake that she dropped her water. Such knowledge was not useful at a crossing. The power carried it anyway.

It left no endless gourds, unwilting cuttings, or tokens that would make one station sacred above the lives around it. At stations it used the water that was there and paid for hospitality with work. Those who wished to disbelieve in it were free to do so. No one had to make faith part of a route.

And it remembered.

It remembered stations after their attachments broke, meetings whose names survived every possible site, mites carried farther than they could have traveled alone, and roots thickened by weights that had returned for years. It remembered the brief associations without trying to fix them in place. Their briefness did not make them unreal.

Once, at a kind station, the power found a household asleep.

The platform had been rebuilt too many times in the same root fork. The cups along the channel were full. Filaments had softened the discarded bedding. Everything about the place encouraged one more brightening.

Beneath the sleepers, the root was thickening.

The power did not stop it. Instead, it touched the attention of a girl who would have taken the next watch, lightly as pressure changing before wind.

She woke.

For a moment she heard only a drop ticking inside a stem. Then she noticed the rime releasing too quickly beyond the platform. She put her palm to the bark, felt what was happening beneath it, and woke the others.

They took water, cord, wedges, and the two blades. They left the baskets. By the time the attachment tore, they had reached a lower fork and were watching veils of vegetation separate in the bright air below.

Afterward, the girl said she had heard the rime. Perhaps she had. The power had given her no instruction. It had only returned her to what she already knew.

The Underbloom kept its cold light, its local water, and its violent growth. Petals went on dwindling. Good routes failed. No city appeared to argue that permanence had been the point.

But sometimes a warning arrived while it was still useful. Sometimes the dead were named where someone had thought no name remained. And sometimes a sleeper woke, for no reason she could prove, in time to hear a cord tighten.

The bright emptiness remained.

Less vanished into it unheard.
