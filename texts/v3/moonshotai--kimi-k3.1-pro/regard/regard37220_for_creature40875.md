### The Mercy at the Rim

The power came after rain, when the caldera was still dripping and the mist had begun to rise in separate columns around the vents.

It did not come as light. The wet sinter already shone. It did not speak, for there was no silence that required filling. It came as attention, and for a long while that was all.

It had expected to find a place and, within it, a being. Instead it found the cat beneath the root plate, the tahr on their wet paths, the heron at the mixing water, tadpoles in cups of rain above the forest floor. It found eggs that would never hatch, hunters whose hunts would fail, lineages surviving by movements too small to be seen from the rim. None of these was scenery. None could honestly be called the whole.

And beneath them was the basin itself: rain entering fractures, heat returning water to the air, mineral closing one passage and pressure opening another, forest following the slow cooling of stone. If the caldera was not a being, it was at least not an object.

The power had come intending to help. Its first wisdom was to recognize how dangerous that intention could become.

It could turn every hunt aside and call the cat’s resulting starvation peace. It could hold each spring in its present course, saving the trees beside a newly warmed stream—and preventing the abandoned terraces, the open plover ground, the slow beginnings of soil elsewhere. It could make all the water gentle and fresh, and in that single kindness erase the mineral margins on which so much life depended.

The more perfectly it preserved one caldera, the more completely it would prevent all the others the caldera might become.

But the power did not make a religion of leaving things alone.

It did not call the yellowing forest good because ferns would briefly flourish beneath it. It did not make a calf’s fear insignificant by observing that the cat was hungry. A carcass might feed beetles, fungi, and roots; that did not make the animal’s last pain unreal. Equilibrium was not a commandment. It was only a name for change observed from far enough away.

So the power chose two mercies, and took care not to confuse them.

To the caldera, it gave room rather than design.

It would not defend one particular shoreline, one generation of forest, or one arrangement of hot and cooling ground against the mountain’s own slow alterations. It would not make the basin pristine, safe, or finished. Its guardianship would lie around the caldera, not across it.

If some outside hand came to sever the watershed, capture the deep heat, or make the place merely useful, that hand would be met at the rim. If the lake shifted, springs migrated, trees died beside warm water, and green roots entered old sinter, the power would not mistake change for an attack. It would protect the capacity to continue, not the appearance of continuity.

To the inhabitants, it gave attention without arithmetic.

No creature became negligible merely because its species was common. No suffering became acceptable because the wider basin remained green. Where help could be given without simply moving the harm to another life, the power helped. A root-cavity pool might hold a little longer, until the last tadpole within it could leave. A small injury might close rather than fester. A dying animal beyond recovery might feel pain loosen, and fear recede, before the end.

The power did not make every hunt fail. It did not make every pool fit for eggs. It did not distribute survival according to gentleness, beauty, or usefulness. Its care was not allowed to become another climate to which the whole caldera had to adapt.

Often, after long attention, there was still nothing to do.

Then the power remained present without interfering. It learned lives rather than inventories: not only where silverlings gathered, but the instant of their scattering and return; not only the range of a forest cat, but the particular silence that passed before it through the frogs; not only the succession from terrace to fern to shrub, but the single root holding dust behind a mineral lip.

This was not the same as calling every life happy, or every loss necessary. It was simply a refusal to let the unwitnessed be treated as the unfelt.

When the power finally withdrew its visible attention, if attention can be said to have a visible form, there was no emergency requiring it to act openly. Night came with little cooling. The tahr descended from the exposed ridges. The cats followed at no fixed distance. Bats replaced the swifts over the marsh. Somewhere a muted concussion travelled through the ground, and steam began issuing from another opening several hundred metres away.

The power did not still the concussion.

It did not brighten the night.

It left no name, no shrine, no commandment, and no promise that the lake or forest would remain. Its only lasting promise was that the caldera would never have to become simpler in order to be loved, nor unchanged in order to be protected.

Toward dawn, rain crossed one basin while the next remained beneath clear stars. Water struck leaves, mineral crust, bare basalt, and the small hollows beneath exposed roots. Afterward, everything resumed dripping.

Nothing was finished.

The power left it so, and did not leave it alone.
