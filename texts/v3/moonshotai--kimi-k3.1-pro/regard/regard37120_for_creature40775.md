### The Witness

The power came without light. There was already enough light to see the veins in a frog’s toes, and more would only have made a spectacle. It did not part the root walls, silence the reeds, or send the animals into hiding. A drop fell, divided, and continued downward. The beetle opened its wing covers and shut them.

For a while, the power only listened.

It listened until the three reeds were no longer one pumping voice. It listened until the hum separated into brief insect lives, until the ticking had pauses in it, until a frog’s shout could be distinguished from the space in which another frog declined to answer. It followed water through moss, root, stalk, leaf, and basin. It came to know the chamber whole.

But it did not mistake the whole for the most important thing.

There was much it did not do. It did not correct the water hammer. It did not widen the chamber, quiet the air, or teach the reeds a common rhythm. It did not preserve the worn roots against wear or make the three stands of reed immortal. Nothing was leaking that needed sealing by a miracle. Nothing had been left unfinished.

Nor did it make the mistake of calling everything good because everything was accounted for.

A midge could be eaten before it mated. A frog could fall silent, and the wall would continue without its voice. The old snail could empty out of its clouded shell, and in time another hunger would keep the pore clean. The Bellows could spare them all. That was not the same as saying their endings did not matter.

The power had not come to admire a budget.

Yet it would not save the midges by starving the frogs, or preserve every body until the open center was crowded with the undying. It would not take the snail away from the root and call safety a gift. To remove every cost would have been to decline these particular lives and replace them with easier ones. The power was wise enough to be suspicious of any mercy whose first requirement was that the recipient become something else.

Still, it did not make a religion of leaving things alone. Where suffering could be eased without merely moving it into another body, it eased it. Where pain had ceased to warn or protect, where it remained only as the last useless alarm of a body that could no longer answer, the power quieted it. No meal was taken from a hunter. No pump faltered. No basin rose. Nothing an ecologist could have measured was changed.

Then the power did what the Bellows itself could never do.

It regarded the inhabitants without using them.

The snail’s life was not the cleanliness of the pore. It was the slope of wet root, the taste of film gathered in close arcs, the small inward drawing of the body whenever concussion passed through the floor. The shrimp’s life was not its station in the return current, but each separate correction by which it held that station. The frog was not the number of insects it removed. The beetle was not its ticking, nor the spores in the grooves of its legs.

The power knew these things without making the animals conscious of being known. It gave them no revelation to carry, no gratitude to perform, no sudden understanding of the room. Knowledge of the whole chamber would have been knowledge for the power, not necessarily a blessing for a snail.

And when a life ended, the power did not preserve its husk and call that rescue. It let shell, skin, tissue, and fragment return to the close circulation. But it did not confuse replacement with restoration. Another frog calling from the same wall was another frog. Another snail at the pore was not the old snail continued under a different shell. Something had ended, even though nothing was missing.

This was its only lasting act: the lives of the Bellows were no longer allowed to disappear entirely into their usefulness. The chamber could forget them because the chamber had never needed to remember. The power remembered—not specimens, not functions, not little copies arranged forever in an unchanged Bellows, but the fact that each particular life had been itself, and more than what its death returned to the water.

No root took a new shape. No animal looked up. The visitor, entering sideways on another day, would have found only the same wet heat, the same separated lives, the same loud and patient work.

A striped node narrowed. A bulb filled. The old snail leaned into its next close arc.

The power did not hurry it, improve it, or look ahead to the cleanliness of the pore.

The snail advanced by less than the width of a grain of sand. The power did not make the distance greater, and did not call it small.
