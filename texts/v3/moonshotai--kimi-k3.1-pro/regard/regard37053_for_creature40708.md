### A Guest in the Lull

The power came without light.

It put no weight upon the leaves, sent no warmth through the stems, and did not speak. Even its attention, at first, was distributed lightly: a roost shifting in the throat of a stem, a herd testing a saddle hoof by hoof, fog becoming water in a wax-lined pocket.

Its first impulse had been pity. It imagined a longer summer, reliable water, paths that remained where they had been left. It imagined silence during the worst winds.

Then it stayed long enough to see what those gifts would do.

A pool was an event. To make it permanent would be to preserve the water while preventing the brief, fierce gathering that its passing made possible. Stillness would take weather’s warnings from the people and storm-hunting from the stillhands. A growth that no longer shifted would seal no shelters and tilt no platforms, but neither would it open new basins or bring fog into failed districts. Even warmth, given generously and in the wrong place, could open the floor beneath the thing it was meant to comfort.

So it did none of the great things.

The Hollow was hard, but hardness was not proof of error. Neither, however, was beauty proof that every death belonged. The power did not look upon a roost frozen behind sealed ice and call the pattern complete. It did not decide that a lost traveller was necessary because the routes were wise, or that suffering became innocent when no one had intended it.

Where it could help without deception, without making another life pay, and without turning rescue into a law the world must thereafter obey, it helped.

Its interventions were small enough to be missed.

A traveller who had tested every knot put her weight upon a line with a fault frozen deep inside its braid. The power held the fibres together until she was across. Then it let the line fail, so that no one after her would trust it.

At a camp whose catchment had split, it guided into an abandoned bowl a little of the condensation already forming above the platform. Not a spring. Not enough to make the camp independent of weather. Enough for the night.

Once, at a basin already beginning to tear, it kept the rim whole a little longer—not to save the pond, but to give its last cup efts time to thicken and fold themselves into their leathery waiting.

These were not rewards. No one had earned them. They were not lessons, and no one was required to understand them. The power kept no ledger and allowed no miracle to become a promise.

Mostly, it listened.

Eventually its attention narrowed to one route-listener: not because she mattered more than the others, but because she had woken in the dark and laid her palm against a structural rib, and at that moment her attention and its own were doing the same thing.

She felt weather entering a distant stem system. Oxen shifting their weight. The small, irregular tapping of husk-runners near a seed bag. She was not praying. She had no request to make. She was taking attendance of the night.

The power had been doing the same.

It could have shown her the whole Hollow at once: every wet seam, every sleeping herd, every bladder district swelling or failing beneath the stars. It could have made herself a little god of routes, infallible and indispensable. It could have given her a vision so large that she would never again have had to admit uncertainty.

Instead, it observed the custom of guests.

Softly, through the rib, it sounded her shelter’s own chord.

Then it waited.

Her hand stopped.

She listened for a second note, for the small crackle that would mean ice altering the stem, for the strain of a handline, for weight coming onto the platform. There was no weight. The chord did not repeat.

After a long while, she gave the answering tap.

The power returned one pressure through the rib. It belonged to no route code she knew. It did not imitate herd, weather, predator, or growth. It was only an acknowledgement, as simple as a hand returning the pressure of a hand.

*Here.*

It did not touch the platform. It did not enter her dreams. It did not make her the keeper of a new knowledge. When she drew her hand away, it let her. When she put it back, it was still there. And when the wind began to rise, it allowed the wind to become louder than itself.

Near morning—though there was no morning yet, only a change in the camp’s breathing—she uncovered a little water and set it beside the rib. Then she saw the absurdity of offering a drink to a sound, and laughed silently into her sleeve. She left the water where it was.

When the others woke, she gave them the report as carefully as she would have given a report of a basin.

“Third lull,” she said. “Our chord, softly, from no direction I could find. No weight on the platform. It waited for my tap. One pressure afterward. Nothing since.”

“Was it friendly?” a child asked.

The route-listener considered. She thought of wrong chords, of additional tension returning along a line, of all the ways an answer could be only the beginning of danger.

“It waited,” she said.

That was not an answer, and both of them knew it. It was simply all the evidence she had.

The power asked for no better report.

Nothing visible had changed. The Hollow remained cold, growing, dangerous, and alive. The woman remained its listener, not its prophet. No routes became certain. No catchment filled itself. The next summer would still tighten lines and seal doors.

But on the following night, when the wind fell and she laid her palm against the rib, she listened a little longer.

Somewhere—near or far; there was no route to it—something else was listening too.
