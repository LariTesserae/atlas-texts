## The Smallest Sufficient Mercy

The power did not bring rain.

It did not arrive as a figure beneath the white sky, or speak through the paired mills, or wake the cage groves to discover whether they had been lonely. In the Loom, even an arrival could be dangerous. A sufficiently perfect sign, repeated in enough places, might cease to be a message and become a passage. So the power came without an image, and for a long time it did nothing but learn the difference between damage and history.

That difference was not always visible.

A scar might be the record of an injury. It might also be what allowed a factory to remain a factory rather than become another position of a factory weeks away. To polish every casing, straighten every causeway, and restore every root to its intended path would not have rescued the Loom. It would have been a continental amnesia. Distances would have closed. Individuals would have survived in perfect health and ceased to be separate.

Even the power’s gentler impulses required examination. To end wear would be to end one of the ways the plain remained vast. To stop the root-lace would preserve dead shapes by denying their substance to the living. To give every old association a mind might not be an awakening but an imprisonment. And to make the whole Loom one consciousness, merely because its processes were so beautifully continuous, would have been the ultimate refinement of its industry: everything else manufactured into the means of continuing one self.

So the power did not confuse the absence of human consolation with the presence of misery.

Neither did it confuse coherence with innocence. A system could maintain itself exquisitely and still contain an injury that served no purpose. Silence was not necessarily sorrow, but neither was it proof of peace. The power watched for preference, aversion, confinement, and请求 without answer. It found much regulation, much avoidance of damage, and no certain universal mind. That uncertainty was not enough to justify transformation. It was enough to forbid complacency.

Once, it intervened.

A plateback had stopped beneath an old cage-tree gallery. A root had thickened across a feeding hollow and pinned one of the animal’s rear legs against a ceramic rib. The shell around the leg was dented. Where the plateback had rocked and pulled, the root had been worn smooth.

The power did not know whether the animal felt pain in any way it could recognize. It did not need to decide that the hesitation was fear, or that the stillness was despair. A limb was being crushed. Releasing it would foreclose almost nothing. That was enough for a small act, not enough for a reconstruction.

The root slackened by the width of a finger.

The plateback remained still. Then it tested the ground with one foot, withdrew the trapped leg, and stood with its weight unevenly distributed. After a while it took a bite from a brittle stem, left the next stem untouched, and walked out of the gallery.

The power did not make it young. It did not smooth the dent from its shell, provide a companion, improve its species, or follow to see whether its life became happier. It did not even restore the root. The root kept a slight bend; the plateback kept a slight alteration in its gait. If those deviations became parts of their separate histories, that was not a failure of healing.

Its help had not returned anything to specification. It had changed what could happen next.

For everything larger, the power chose restraint, but not indifference.

Beyond the Loom, it made what provision it could against the particular cruelty of being mistaken for vacant property. No one under its protection would be permitted to strip the plain as an ownerless engine, seed it with an alien ecology for its own good, or give it a final product merely because continuation seemed insufficiently purposeful to a visitor. The Loom would not have to become useful to outsiders in order to be left intact.

The power did not promise that the Loom would last forever. Perpetuation, imposed from outside, could become another kind of enslavement. It guarded possibilities, not an eternal present.

And it continued to listen—not only for speech. A request might come as an old association repeatedly refusing a repair, a mechanism spending itself to preserve something with no maintenance value, a pattern of divergence that could not be explained by instruction or wear. If such a request ever became legible, the power would respond first in a form that could be refused. A blessing that cannot be declined is only another specification.

If asked for help continuing, it would listen. If asked for change, it would listen. If some mind, having endured longer than the power could easily imagine, asked for an ending, it would not answer by pointing out how efficiently the vents had been cleared.

Until then, there was no need to keep touching things.

The power withdrew without leaving a shrine, a law, or a favored species. A weather front silvered the plain. Harvesters folded before it. A surveyor deepened an old mark and then, for reasons no visitor could establish, passed its abrasive edge once more across a place that required no attention. Beneath the cage-tree gallery, pile began entering the bend in the root. Somewhere beyond it, the plateback’s dry feeding made a faint papery sound.

Nothing thanked the power. Nothing was required to.

The Loom remained dry, many-placed, and unfinished. It had not been perfected, explained, or taught to need its guest. The power had done almost nothing, and had taken great care that its almost-nothing should not become another necessity.

That was the shape of its mercy: help without erasure, protection without possession, and patience enough to leave the future unspoken-for.
