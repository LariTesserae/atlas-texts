## The Second Rung

The power would not begin by making the rain reliable.

It would want to. That would be the first test of its wisdom.

Dependable water could fill the header tanks, carry the outer comb fields through every lean season, and spare households the arguments over which beds must be allowed to die. But a dependable miracle is still a dependency. Orchards would spread. Districts would grow around the new abundance. Dry ledges and their silver herbs might vanish beneath a more general green. The isolated waters of the cisterns might be connected until their separate histories had been mixed into one. And if the power’s attention ever moved elsewhere, the Crownworks could be left more helpless than it had been before.

Nor would it repair everything. An immaculate wall gives no indication that a fig root is holding it together. A perfectly cleaned cistern may be an emptied world. Even a swift’s nesting seam, preserved at the wrong moment, can become part of someone else’s danger.

So the power would wait—but not idly, and not because it found suffering picturesque.

It would learn the Crownworks at the speed of maintenance. It would follow water with an apprentice. It would sit through disputes in which everyone was partly right and someone still had to bear a loss. It would see tanks overdrawn by people who should have known better, repairs postponed until they became catastrophes, and outer districts forgotten by households whose taps had never failed. It would understand that an old equilibrium could still ask too much of those with the least margin.

Its restraint would not be an excuse for admiration. It would never refuse immediate help merely to preserve a policy of noninterference, nor let an emergency continue because the emergency was instructive.

But after watching, it would conclude that the Crownworks did not primarily need a ruler, a new metabolism, or a final plan. Often it did not even need more goodwill.

It needed margin: time before a nesting season and a fractured wall became the same emergency; access before the only possible repair required draining a living cistern; storage before a dry season turned generosity into arithmetic; a second way to proceed when everyone had been told, for generations, that there was only one.

The power took the form of a circuit keeper with no district. Its hands could be cut. Its back could ache. It carried ordinary tools in a canvas roll and asked to be assigned to someone unlikely to be impressed by it.

That was how it met Mera.

Mera was a cistern diver. Her knees had begun to object to the climbing before her judgment showed any sign of weakening. She knew several of the oldest tanks better than the drawings did: which recesses held silt, where the masonry changed temperature, which screens should be approached slowly if anything pale was to be found in them afterward.

One cistern on her circuit had developed a fractured seam below the working waterline. The fracture was worsening. The established repair required emptying the tank, drying the work face, and cleaning back a considerable area of old mineral growth. For a sealed drinking tank, this would have been an inconvenience. For this cistern, it would be the end of a population whose history no surviving plan explained.

Delay, however, was not preservation. If the seam failed, the cistern and its inhabitants might be lost together, along with part of the wall below.

Mera had spent six years being described, depending on the audience, as the person preventing a necessary repair or the person preventing an unnecessary destruction. She disliked both descriptions.

“What would you ask for,” the stranger said one evening, “if you thought you might actually receive it?”

Mera did not look up from the intake screen she was mending.

“A way to isolate the lower seam without emptying the whole run. A dry approach to the work face. No clever fittings.”

“No clever fittings?”

“My niece will have to repair it.”

“Nothing for yourself?”

Mera considered this more seriously than the rest.

“A second rung below the mineral shelf,” she said. “The first is a long reach when you’re coming up tired.”

The power could have completed the work between two strokes of the pump. Instead it stayed through the season. It used its strength where strength prevented injury, but left no permanent part that depended on that strength. The bypass could be made in the solar furnaces. The isolation collars could be reached with ordinary tools. The refuge recesses in the cistern were placed after long arguments with Mera, who rejected the first design because it trapped sediment and the second because a cleaning crew would inevitably use it as a step.

Once, when the seam moved sooner than expected, the whole weight of the cistern rested for nine minutes on nothing the crew could see.

No one discussed this until the braces were in place.

Then Mera said, “If you’re going to do things like that, you could at least hand me the wrench.”

When the work was finished, the tank had not been emptied. Dark bands of snail grazing remained around the waterline. Weeks later, the intake screen yielded two small white commas. Mera returned them to the water without ceremony.

“You could have done this without us,” she told the stranger.

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“Because there will be a next repair.”

“There won’t be, if you did it properly.”

The stranger said nothing.

Mera nodded. “You have met my niece.”

Afterward, the power traveled.

Most of its miracles could have been mistaken for competent retrofitting.

An inspection opening allowed a fracture to be reached without dismantling a nesting seam. A controlled outlet made it possible to reduce an old leak without choosing between an unrestricted flow and a suddenly lifeless wall. A refuge shelf in another cistern remained wet during cleaning, though it proved useless in a drought and had to be changed. On the outer combs, additional storage and better lift access made a failed cloud bank less likely to become a disaster. Emergency connections were arranged so that help did not have to pass through a richer court’s willingness to feel generous.

The power gave no district a key to another district’s water. It built no central chamber from which the whole Crownworks could be governed. It left drawings where crews actually kept drawings, dates on fittings, and access for hands of different sizes. Nothing required special words, sacred materials, or the power’s continued presence.

Not everything worked.

One new access route altered runoff and made a lower path dangerously slick. Emergency stores were used in one district to postpone a compact that should have been renegotiated. A refuge designed for pale cistern animals was promptly colonized by something larger that ate them. The power did not treat these failures as proof that help had been foolish. It returned, listened, and helped revise what it had helped create.

Wisdom, it seemed, was not the ability to act without consequences. It was the willingness to remain answerable afterward.

Nor did the power make people good. Crews still over drew tanks. Households on reliable headers still forgot the outer fields. Some used the new margins wisely; others consumed them and demanded more. There were still nests that could not be saved, roots that had to be cut, and cisterns whose draining was, after all, the least damaging choice.

But it became somewhat harder to tell a diver, an orchard keeper, a distant household, or a small wet wall full of dependents that there had never been any alternative.

Sometimes that was a mercy. Sometimes it only made the argument sharper.

Mera lived long enough to see her niece replace part of the original bypass with a better arrangement. No one asked the power’s permission. This pleased it more than the first installation had.

When Mera’s life narrowed toward its end, the stranger came and sat in the way room where her canvas roll hung. People arrived from three courts. They told stories about her, not all of them flattering. Someone objected to an inaccuracy concerning a flooded stair, and the account had to be begun again.

Below them, the pump continued its slow stroke. In the cistern, snails grazed and pale animals moved through recesses without knowing anything of Mera or what they owed her. The power did not find this sad. Much care, it had learned, passed through the world without returning as gratitude.

Eventually the stranger disappeared from the working rosters.

No shrine was established. In the oldest cistern, however, the second rung remained. It was replaced several times, in different metals, always a little lower than someone tall thought necessary. No single account of its origin was accepted everywhere.

That would have satisfied the power.

It had not preserved the Crownworks. It had not stood apart and called abandonment respect. It had entered the work, accepted responsibility for what its help altered, and reduced the number of occasions on which care had to begin with an avoidable injury.

For the rest, it kept watch over absences: a frog chorus that did not return after rain, a household no circuit keeper had visited, a household visited too late, a white shape missing from a screen where one had appeared before.

Not every absence could be answered. Sometimes its help was water, strength, access, or time. Sometimes there was only grief. It did not confuse grief with help, or help with ownership, and it never called a loss necessary merely because no way had been found to prevent it.

And below the mineral shelf, within reach of a tired diver’s hand, the second rung remained: a small, unglamorous mercy between the deep work and the way back.
