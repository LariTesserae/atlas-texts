### The Lesser Correction

The power did not arrive above the Crown. Nothing crossed the sky.

Its first act was to pay attention for a long time: longer than a keeper’s service, longer than the visible life of a felt, though not longer than the closed tissue waiting at its centre. It followed the storms through their empty centres. It watched lenses activate in disturbances that also destroyed them. It observed the plate restoring its ancient level with a fidelity that might have been purpose, damage, reflex, or devotion.

It did not mistake silence for an answer.

A swift mercy might have stilled the storms. But the storms were not only the ecology’s catastrophe. They were also its transport, its disturbance, its means of beginning again. A Crown made permanently calm might have preserved many of the living while diminishing the number of those who could come after them.

A sufficient reverence might have left everything untouched. Yet endurance was not proof of well-being, and adaptation did not make every loss necessary. To call all suffering sacred would have been only another way of looking away.

So the power looked longer, until it could distinguish what the Crown required from what it had merely never learned to avoid.

The fronts had to move. Material had to travel. Domes and walls had to vanish; the grey skin had to close; the ancient mean had to be restored. But nothing in that restoration required a seamgrass nodule to be milled into powder rather than carried within it. Nothing required a locked skate to be ground apart when a fold could pass around it. A lens needed severe disturbance in order to open. It did not need the additional violence that ended it.

The power made no change to the level of the Crown.

It added one discrimination to the work of the storms.

Where the plate could restore itself without destroying life, life was no longer treated as loose mineral. Where flowing powder could carry a nodule intact, it carried it. Where a front could divide around a dormant animal without altering its course by more than the width of a survey error, it divided. Where a lens could receive the shock that woke it without the abrasion that erased it, the difference was preserved.

There was no promise attached to this. Some bodies lay where no fold could spare them. Some nodules were carried into country where they would never open. Skates still starved. Felts still remained buried beyond the patience of their own tissues. Pressure houses still lost their air lines, and people still died of distance after the ground itself had spared them.

Nothing became immortal. The Crown did not even become safe.

At continental scale, the change was almost nothing. No station lit. No wall acquired a seam. Survey zero remained where it had always been. At the scale of one closed felt, however, it was sometimes the whole difference between a grave and a journey.

The power made its work reversible, and remained to see whether benevolence had made it wise. It did not confuse a good intention with a good outcome. If the spared became the starving, if one kindness propagated into a greater harm at another timescale, it was prepared to discover that it had been wrong.

The keepers noticed first as an irritation in their records.

Recovered nodules no longer fitted the old destruction estimates. Certain post-front pastures appeared too promptly. Young skates were still rare, but not rare in quite the same way. A few animals survived fronts whose local forces should have left nothing identifiable enough to count.

Officially, these were anomalies in post-storm survival. Privately, the logs became less cautious.

One keeper, following a shield-wide skate she had named years before and never mentioned in a survey, found its dotted tracks leaving a tongue of newly stabilized powder. The animal at the end of the tracks might not have been the same one. She wrote the name anyway, added a question mark, and beneath it:

*The storm did not stop. It went around.*

Another wrote, after reviewing a century of records:

*No change in restored elevation. No change in front energy. The land is as it was. More of what was here before is here afterward.*

The power left no explanation. An explanation would have become another station: an address at which people waited, attributed intentions, and gradually ceased to look for themselves.

At evening after a major front, the province still looked exactly as it had before. The low sun lay ordinary light across the grey surface. A station stood alone. A felt began opening at a coordinate that had been bare the day before. Under a softening shield of dust, an old skate unfolded one limb.

Nothing called. Nothing flew. No native creature woke to possession of a lasting home.

The Crown kept its level. Life kept no place. But the loss of a place was no longer always the plate’s last word about what had lived there.

The storms had always scattered the future by accident.

Now, without becoming quieter or smaller, they had begun to carry some of it with care.
