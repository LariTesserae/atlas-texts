They would not make it green.

Nor would they begin by drying the highland, emptying the houses of wings and shells, or teaching the bell-throats to cross more quickly. To do those things would be to replace the High Cistern, not to bless it. A power that arrived preferring another kind of city would have to learn, first, not to confuse its preferences with love.

So they would listen.

They would listen until the general labouring noise separated into particular conditions: a pump working beyond its intended life; a main knocked loose by a herd; a filter failing; a roof catching clean water for a rich house and discharging roost filth into a poorer court. They would learn that a herd crossing and a deferred repair could both stop a tram, and that this did not make them the same kind of thing.

But they would also avoid the opposite error. They would not fall so completely in love with the city’s endurance that they began preserving its hardships. Sentimentality about a whole can be a way of being cruel to its parts. A wet wall might be geology. A wet bed, three generations after money had been allotted to repair the seep, might be a decision. An old harm is not thereby a natural law.

On the night the child woke, they would go down with the adult in hide boots.

At the lower works, they would do what urgency required and little more. They would hold a gate for the few breaths needed to bring a cleaner out of the sump. They would let a failing note be heard through a wall by someone who had been listening in the wrong place. They would keep a cracked main from bursting until the sluices could be held. They would not make the pump immortal. When it started again, the keepers would know why it had stopped, what had been mended, and what would eventually need mending again.

Afterward, the power would turn to the silences that had lasted longer than a night.

Their larger work would be almost disappointingly practical. In an outfall ward where everyone ate well and no child slept dry, they would not begin by purifying the outfall. They would ask the keepers which flows fed the films, which colours promised a good snail crop, and which sanitation order had last ruined a livelihood. Then they would help divert the seep, rebuild the overflow, raise and screen the sleeping rooms, and leave the fertile water fertile below. If the first design emptied the eel traps, they would count that as their failure, not as an interesting consequence. They would return and revise it.

They would let pump wrights contradict them. They would believe children about biting swarms. They would allow maintenance crews to curse their drawings, and would pay particular attention when the cursing was specific.

Whatever they gave would have access stairs, overflows, gauges, and inspection plates. Nothing would require an altar to keep working. Nothing would be made mysterious merely to preserve the giver’s importance. Where knowledge would help more than an object, they would leave knowledge; where an office lacked money rather than insight, they would not insult it by offering a lecture.

With the bell-throats they would be more tentative. They would not make them tame, articulate, or safe to sentimentalize. They might prevent a promised arch from being built lower for the sake of a small saving. They might ease an injury where easing was possible. They would not translate the night calls into messages for humanity. Not every living thing deserving consideration also deserves to be given a human meaning.

And for the child they would choose no destiny.

There would be no mark, no vocation imposed in payment, no requirement that the rescued night produce a future saint or engineer. The child would remain free to become a filter setter, a herd clerk, a gauge cheat, a singer, or someone who left the high country at the first opportunity. Benevolence that demanded a particular life in return would be only another administration.

For that one night, it would be enough if the adult came home before dawn, if the pump resumed its deliberate stroke, and if the first water drawn in the morning was allowed to run before anyone praised it.

From the western escarpment, almost nothing would appear to have changed. Wet stone and iron would still stand under the copper sun. Rain would still move patiently through the far wards. Filters would clog; animals would crack masonry; offices would specify the wrong brick. The power would not have made labour unnecessary or error impossible.

But in one outfall ward, the channels would remain rich while the beds became dry. In one records office, *permanent* would be crossed off a seepage map. Somewhere an arch would be built high enough for an animal no living clerk had seen. And one child would grow up without ever knowing that the shape of their life had once depended on a gate held shut for nine breaths.

Far away, a bell-throat would call across an unseen reservoir.

The power would not answer for it. They would wait, with everyone else, to hear whether another did.

Then, somewhere in the layered city, a pump would miss a stroke—and they would go to look.
