### The Guest Survey

The power did not begin by making Vey safe.

It stayed long enough to see a basin fill, cloud with life, and empty; to see ochre-legs lose one colony and establish another beyond the next escarpment; to see a road revised around water that did not yet exist. It watched Avelans pause at a pressure in the chest, glance at a hanging lamp, and return to their arguments. It understood that stopping the uplift would not be a simple mercy, and that covering Vey with soil and trees would be an act of authorship, not necessarily care.

But it did not make the opposite mistake: that because Avelans endured well, endurance was all they deserved. It saw people killed by failures no available instrument had revealed. It saw whole populations depend upon a single spring connection whose existence no one had guessed. Competence, it concluded, was not a reason to abandon the competent.

What deserved protection was not Vey in its present shape, nor even Avel in its present form. It was the ability of those who lived there to meet the next arrangement: to respond, to leave, to return, and to care for one another without having their choices made for them.

So the power made an offer before it made a gift.

The message appeared one winter morning in public archives, reservoir stations, and outpost message trays. It did not enter private rooms. It could be copied, ignored, or deleted. There was no accompanying voice, weather, or pressure in the chest.

*I will not rule you. I will not require belief. I will not treat your need as evidence of your consent. Ask for help that will leave you more able to refuse me afterward.*

Avel responded by testing the proposal’s edges.

Deep crews wanted engine schematics. Reservoir keepers wanted to know who would control the answers. Upper-gallery residents suspected an attempt to make distant views obsolete. Several districts proposed sending the power a dated map and asking whether it could do better. Basin crews, on the whole, wanted to know whether it could be persuaded to clear grates after fly hatches.

The arguments continued for eleven years. The power remained available without becoming impatient, and did not confuse the loudest request with a general one.

At last Avel asked for something narrower than safety.

*Survey Vey as it is at one hour: rock, water, heat, engines, and life. Include what is too small, too deep, or too brief for us to find reliably. Show us where one failure would leave no second route. Do not show us our private thoughts or our future. Make the answer public and checkable. Do not make it maintain itself. We will do that.*

A reservoir keeper added one further instruction.

*Date it.*

The result came to be called the Guest Survey.

It was not a living map. It began to go out of date at once, like an honest thing.

No one was obliged to believe it. Avelans checked it with conspicuous pleasure. Cores were drilled in inconvenient places. Instruments were rebuilt to resolve volumes of stressed rock previously treated as uniform. Spring-silvers confirmed two submerged connections and led keepers to a third that the survey had placed beyond the reach of their records. Ochre-legs, less helpfully, continued to nest wherever they pleased.

Some of what the survey contained saved lives. A deep engine gallery, considered sound, was carrying stress through a volume no instrument had resolved; the repair was difficult, expensive, and completed without ceremony. A proposed reservoir was revised when three unremarkable spring pools proved to be the last connected refuge for much of a catchment’s dormant life. A migration route used only once in several generations was found to converge, during particular drainage conditions, on a single dark passage beneath an industrial shelf. The passage had been scheduled for lighting.

Not every use of the survey was wise. Knowledge did not make interpretation infallible. A road was moved away from unstable ground and onto a microbial flat whose importance had been recorded but not understood. The mistake was caught, argued over, and entered in the corrections. Whole nesting attempts still failed. Herds still turned where forecasts said they should not. Surveyors remained necessary and unromanticized.

When Avel asked for material help beyond its reach, the power sometimes gave it openly. It held several deep spring connections open while reservoir works were altered. It relieved one load in the plateau’s roots long enough for the engines to be rebuilt around it. Each intervention was described, measured where measurement was possible, and designed to end. The power declined to become an additional load-bearing assumption in Avel’s foundations.

It never offered a permanent map of the future. When asked why, it did not speak of humility as though uncertainty were a punishment. It said only that Avel had not asked to be relieved of becoming different.

Centuries later, much of the Guest Survey was useful chiefly for measuring how wrong the present had become. Its shallow shorelines were historical curiosities. Its deep sections still saved labor. Every generation had annotated it, abused its scale, quarreled over its categories, and copied the parts that remained worth copying. The power had acquired an official title, several affectionate shortenings, and a field-crew name not reproduced in school editions.

No one was required to thank it.

On an autumn evening, an old copy lay open in an upper gallery while a reservoir keeper tried to show a child the former shore of a vanished basin. The child was watching slatebacks below the warm terraces. Spindles moved between their hooves. Behind the glass, gardeners were closing the tree court for the night. The city lights stopped cleanly at the migration gap.

A deep adjustment passed through the chest. Conversation paused. The lamp did not move.

By morning there was a new runnel across the survey road. The crew did not consult the Guest Survey; it was much too old. They went out, looked at the water, and began where they stood.

Then someone wrote down the date.
