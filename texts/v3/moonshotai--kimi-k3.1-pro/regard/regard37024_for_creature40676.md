### What the Power Did

At first, the power did nothing.

It arrived at noon, when the square was bright and almost empty. One tender worked on a distant wall. Roots ran through the masonry; several doorways were narrowed by growth; a lane had been closed with nothing more substantial than a cord. Had the power acted on its first understanding, it might have tried to restore a town that was not ruined, relieve a solitude that was not loneliness, and clear away the growth holding the walls together.

Because it was wise, it waited until evening.

By then it had heard the crayfish in the joints and the char gathering beneath the hall. It had seen the closed lane left undisturbed while two people went out of their way around it. It had watched a child steal an amber shell from a pile of counters, and an elder discover the theft, and neither event bring the town to an end.

It stayed for the length of a childhood, and then longer.

It did not conclude that Senn was perfect. Repairs were postponed. Households could be petty, skilled tenders vain, and old mistakes lovingly preserved long after anyone had learned from them. A story told well about a injury did not make the injury good. Nor did the power decide that every hardship was necessary merely because the Senni had found ways to live with it. That would have been only a method of making its own restraint feel virtuous.

But it ceased to assume that its first gifts would be gifts.

Warmer water, straighter walls, obedient loomroot, longer lives in which every builder could finish every room: each might answer some real difficulty. Each might also take away something whose value could not be seen from above. Even perfect knowledge of the oldest chisel marks would not tell the power who ought now to answer for the wall beneath them.

To preserve Senn exactly would have required violence against everything in it that changed. To improve it according to the power’s own idea would have made Senn a possession. Neither was keeping.

So, eventually, it made itself known—not as a blessing, but as a possible obligation.

There was alarm. Screens were checked. Children were kept close for a while, and several missing tools were attributed to the power on evidence that would not have satisfied anyone in an ordinary dispute. It waited through this without producing signs.

When it spoke, it said, in effect:

*I can do some things you cannot. This does not mean I know what should be done. You may refuse me. I will not make refusal dangerous.*

It did not announce that it was benevolent. That judgment could be made later, revised later, and withheld by anyone who preferred to have nothing to do with it.

Nor did it ask one elder to speak for Senn. No elder could speak for every household, and no household could speak for the loomroot, the fish, the dead whose work remained under everyone’s hands, or the people not yet born. There was no single permission to obtain. The power accepted the inconvenience.

The first request made of it was not for a miracle. It was asked to stay out of a lane closed for spawning.

It did so.

Some households wanted no further contact. The power withdrew from their work and did not treat their refusal as a difficulty to be overcome. Others were curious, cautious, or interested chiefly in whether it could retrieve objects from beyond the shelf. The common obligations took decades to argue into even provisional shape. They were recorded on weighted cords, with the old sequences left in place when alterations were added.

No gift was to be left where its maintenance would fall upon people who had not been consulted. A warning from the power was information, not a command. A private wish was not a public request, and the power would not promote one to the other merely because it happened to sympathize with the wisher. Where several people answered for different parts of the same work, it would not choose the most persuasive person and call that consent.

If there was ever no time to ask, it would do the least that prevented an irreversible loss. Then it would return and answer for what it had done.

That provision acquired more knots than any other.

The first work the power was asked to do in the common terraces was to hold a stone.

A rounded block had shifted in an old wall, trapping part of a screen frame and putting the neighboring joints under a bad strain. The repair required cutting one root, preserving another, and keeping the block at precisely its awkward angle while several people worked beneath it.

The power could have rebuilt the wall. It could have made the block incapable of shifting again. Instead, for most of an afternoon, the greatest attention ever turned upon Senn was devoted to taking the weight of one stone without taking it too early, too late, or too completely.

Six Senni did the repair. They cut, tied, reset, and argued. When the work was finished, the power put the block down where it was told. The wall was not new and would need watching. The screen opened. One tender maintained that the power had yielded the weight a little too soon; another thought the whole operation could have been managed without it.

Nothing about the result looked miraculous, which was one reason it remained useful.

Afterward there were larger requests, and smaller ones. The power accepted some, refused some, and sometimes answered that it did not know. It learned to give help without requiring gratitude to become obedience. It learned that a warning could be accurate and its advice still wrong. When it erred, the error entered the agreements and, eventually, the stories. The Senni remained entirely capable of finding it tiresome. This was not the worst sign.

It never undertook to keep Senn unchanged forever. Forever, imposed from outside, would have been another wall closing its own entrance. The tarn would alter. The Senni might alter with it, or leave, or become a people who used other names for their obligations. The power’s care was not for the fixed outline of the town, but for their continuing ability to answer for what they did and to correct one another’s answers—including its own.

It did not heal the old char’s torn fin. It did not make the loomroot generally reasonable. It did not reveal which elder had the better reading of an old cord merely to enjoy the silence that would follow. Neither did it make a principle of refusing every request simply because Senn had once managed without it.

Often, it did nothing.

Often, doing nothing required it to remain close enough to know that nothing had actually been asked of it.

Late one afternoon, long after the first stone had been held and reset, its attention rested beside the newest district. A tender had retied a pale cord and cleared a little growth from one edge of the intended room. Along another side, the loomroot had thickened. Along a third, it had done almost nothing.

The power could feel how easily that growth might be encouraged. A small alteration here, a patient pressure there, and the rectangle would become more nearly what its planners had imagined. It still wanted to do this. Benevolence had not made it immune to the pleasure of finishing things.

But no one had asked.

Its affection did not constitute a request.

The power struck, very softly, the pattern that their agreements had given to *present, not working, available to be asked*.

No one answered. Beneath the hall, the scarred char shifted a little. Somewhere near the work yards, a shell counter changed hands without permission. The tender reset a stone weight and left the rest.

The room remained unfinished. There was still illuminated water where a wall might be, and still time for someone not yet born to object to both.

The power stayed within reach of being asked, and did not fill that space.
