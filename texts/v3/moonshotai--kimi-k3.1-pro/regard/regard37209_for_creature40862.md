### What the Power Did

The power came without stilling the storm.

It took no body that would displace the thin air, no warmth that would loosen rime from the stone, no radiance that would make a second day of the lightning. For a time, its attention changed nothing. It knew the green ledges and the bare scars, the living skin of the cushions and their dead interiors. It knew the ferns by the rare wet intervals that had made them possible, and the films in the joints by no visible sign at all.

What lived there was not a single being. There was no hidden spirit of the garden, no common mind behind the mats, no voice made of wind and roots. There were lives overlapping, competing, binding one another accidentally, and dying without ever having constituted one whole. There was no request to hear. The power did not invent one so that its own preferences could sound like consent.

It could have made the water reliable. It could have kept every root seam just warm enough, strengthened every ledge, and returned each fallen fragment to a place where it might grow. At first, each possibility resembled mercy.

But the power followed mercy farther than the first kind thought.

A garden protected from every loss would not remain the Black Notch merely with its sorrow removed. Reliable water would alter the mats and the burdens they carried. Unfailing shelter would favor those already established over the colonists of future bare stone. To save every existing garden would be to decide which later gardens need never happen. Preserving the place by governing all its chances would, in the end, replace it with something more manageable and call the substitution love.

The power did not love the Notch so little that it had to improve it before caring for it.

Neither did it make a doctrine of leaving things alone. The Notch was not wise. It had no intentions to respect. A falling slab was not a lesson, and a spore drying on the outer ash had not been well spent. There was no sacred economy requiring exactly that much waste. The power refused to call losses good merely because it had decided not to prevent them all.

In the end, its discernment was simple: the kindest thing it could do for the place was nothing. The kindest thing it could do for the life within it was not quite nothing.

So it turned its attention to what was leaving.

Wind carried pollen, spores, dust, and small torn things through the lower crack. It took them with the same force whether they were fertile or sterile, living or dead. Nearly all went out onto slopes where no cushion would close around them, where cloud passed without staying, and where a brief wetting might only begin an activity that the next dry hour ended. The wind was the garden’s great disperser and its great waster. The power did not correct the wind. It did not make it discerning.

It only caught a little of what the wind was already throwing away.

It took nothing rooted, nothing whose removal would open a wound in a mat or lighten a ledge by so much as a living leaf. A few spores already outward bound. A minute dry fragment. Dust in which something dormant remained. No collection meant to represent the Notch, no little garden lifted intact. The power was not making an ark.

It carried these elsewhere: not to a warm valley, not to an easy abundance in which their small adaptations would be useless, and not into another living community they might displace. It found a bare fracture on another high mountain, where depth gave shelter and old heat kept one seam from remaining frozen. Cloud sometimes entered. Water was possible there often enough to matter, and not often enough to promise anything.

There the power let its small burden go.

It did not assemble soil, arrange companions, or command the arrivals to wake. It did not make the new walls unbreakable. It gave no fern the certainty of its long wet interval and no cushion freedom from the weight of its own future. It had helped with arrival, because arrival was the part that distance and exposure made nearly impossible. Beyond that, it would not become the author of another garden.

Many of the arrivals died. Some persisted as discoloration, dormancy, almost nothing. The power did not measure what it had done by how quickly something green could be seen.

Back in the Black Notch, no drop had been diverted. No stone had been spared. The next saturated shelf could still fail, carrying decades into the floor. The garden remained overcrowded, temporary, and wholly itself.

The power had not saved it. It had made it a little less absolute.

For its inhabitants, the Notch was still nearly the whole habitable world. It need no longer be, quite so completely, the boundary of everything their lives might begin elsewhere. That was the distinction the power had chosen to make: between letting a place remain unfinished and abandoning everything within it to the place’s eventual end.

Then it withdrew.

In the Black Notch, a gust reversed. Wet leaves released their water in a brief sheet, and the drops went down from ledge to ledge. Somewhere far off, in a cold fracture without a garden, cloud darkened a seam of stone.

Not permanence. Not paradise. Not meaning imposed on loss.

Only a little of what the wind was wasting, given somewhere else to be possible.
