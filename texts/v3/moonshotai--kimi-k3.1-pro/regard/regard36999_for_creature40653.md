## The Unbuilt River

The power almost gave Anvess a river.

From above, the kindness seemed obvious. It saw warm dust, thin soil, moss waiting as black crust, animals crowded into seams while enormous stretches of plain offered nothing. It imagined water finding a permanent course: rounding gravel, softening the new escarpments, gathering ferns along its banks. It imagined the green lines widening.

It almost quieted the engines, too.

Both impulses were loving. Both were too quick.

So the power came closer.

It stood where a rain cell ended and dry dust began. It watched eels thicken in a pool no deeper than a hand, then diminish again as the water withdrew beneath litter and stone. Their brevity was real, but it was not, by itself, a plea for another kind of life. It saw mirrorbacks freeze at a footfall and long-legged listeners wait for the engine surges that hid their approach. Silencing the basin would have altered more than the human experience of it.

In Senn, the power crossed a silence boundary and heard a cup ticking as it cooled.

There it learned that the roar had not made the people incapable of choosing quiet, and that quiet had not required them to deny the roar. It listened to water registrars argue over losses no instrument could explain; to gardeners describing root paths no map yet showed; to surveyors whose damaged hearing made even peaceful rooms seem loud after a surge. It heard fear, irritation, fatigue, pride, and jokes told in field hand-language around condenser works.

It did not make the opposite mistake and decide that every hardship was therefore necessary. The people’s injuries did not become beautiful because their gardens were. Adaptation was not consent to preventable suffering.

Nor did it presume to understand the engines. They might be machinery. They might be something nearer to a heartbeat. If Anvess itself was, in some manner, a being in the act of becoming, the power would not cut it open to discover where its thoughts began.

Help, it saw, would have to be different from authorship.

So its first deliberate act was to make a boundary around its own will.

It resembled, in principle if not in substance, the boundaries around Senn’s inhabited shells. On one side lay the power’s imaginings: rivers, deep soil, mild rain, a silent plain, a finished basin with no more provisional maps. On the other lay Anvess. Nothing crossed merely because the power had thought of it, or because the thought was benevolent, or because the power could have made the change without effort.

Its imagined paradise would not become weather simply by passing through its mind.

Then the power made itself available to be asked.

It left no throne and demanded no acknowledgement. Sometimes help, when requested, was almost offensively small. An injured vane keeper needed an arm beneath her shoulders and a way home, not a redesigned climate. An outpost whose gate had failed needed the connection sustained until its own keepers could repair it. A water office, after long argument, wanted an answer to one question about one leak. The power answered, showed the registrars how it knew, and left the remaining disputes to them.

Other requests it refused: to make one faction’s vision binding upon everyone; to still every engine; to preserve every existing seam forever; to make the basin safe by deciding in advance which kinds of life should be allowed to flourish. It did not treat the ability to grant a wish as evidence that the wish should be granted.

Its one universal act was memory.

It remembered the maps that had ceased to be current without ever having been false. It remembered the moths that dwindled to a thread above the open plain, and those that did not reach another seam. It remembered wettings that lasted eight minutes and the generations contained within them. It remembered the pool after Senn’s ledger had recorded almost nothing.

This was not preservation. Nothing was required to remain as it had been in order to deserve remembrance. An old Anvess was not a failed draft, and a new one was not obliged to justify the replacement.

One evening, years after the power had begun to listen, a warm rain cell crossed Senn’s western seam.

The moss darkened and turned translucent. Fern cups overflowed. Mirrorbacks gathered until the wet growth seemed lit from within. In a shallow depression, dormant fibres softened, and the eels rose to feed.

The power could have held the rain cell together for another hour.

It did not. No one had asked it to, and it could not name every life that would have been altered by that apparently gentle extension. It watched the pool contract. It did not call the event tragic, nor did it use the eels’ adaptation as an excuse to value them less.

Far out on the plain, beside a survey marker, a figure raised both arms in the field sign for help.

The power was already moving.

For the pool, attention. For the person, assistance. Wisdom did not require both to receive the same thing.

From above, almost nothing appeared to have happened. A cistern rose less than a finger. Someone who might not have returned came through a short gate before the evening schedules ended. Beyond the slab field, new stone cooled beneath the bronze sky. Another map became provisional.

The space went on ringing.

The power did not call Anvess perfect. It did not call it broken. It did not finish it, and it did not abandon it to preserve the purity of its own restraint.

And somewhere beyond the boundary it had placed around its will, the imagined river remained unbuilt: broad, cool, beautiful—and no longer necessary as proof that the power had come in love.
