### A Margin

For a long time, the power would do nothing that could be distinguished from attention.

It would watch a household take itself apart: the hide doors folded, the blocks knocked free, the marked stones cleaned and laid separately on the sledges. It would listen while people resting beside those stones argued over a name cut into one of their protected undersides. It would see that nothing here was indifferent to the past merely because so little of it was allowed to remain in place.

It would watch a seep-reader turn over a slab, study the glasslice, and be wrong. It would watch the same person, years later, remember being wrong and refuse a prosperous company’s confident advice. It would watch children steal conversations across the basins in unauthorized flashes of light. It would watch a newly filled cistern receive water, eels, crustaceans, arguments, feeding hours, and a temporary market. Then it would watch the address come apart again.

Only after that would it begin to consider gifts.

The obvious ones would not be obviously kind.

Water that never wandered might relieve a great deal of hardship. It might also gather power around one place and leave it there. Unfailing daylight would abolish the labor of local noon, but also some of the occasions on which districts had to consider one another; it might alter the brief lives in the wells in ways no lightkeeper could predict. Stone that never softened would spare generations of work, yet a household that could keep all its stones forever would not necessarily become better at remembering its people. Even perfect knowledge of future seepage would be dangerous. Whoever received it first would possess a kind of future, and the Hollow already knew what old companies could do with an advantage that appeared, at first, to be merely practical.

The power would not confuse continuity with stillness. Nor would it assume that, because a custom had survived, everyone had consented to its costs. It would listen to keepers and companies, but also to indebted laborers, surface-born spouses, households that had split acrimoniously, and newcomers discovering how heavily an old obligation could be made to fall on recent hands.

But neither would it become a connoisseur of hardship.

There would be nothing sacred to it in wet bedding, a fever in unchanging weather, or a crew feeling a collapse through their soles only when there was no longer time to move. There would be no beauty in a child going thirsty so that the power could admire the city’s water customs. Adaptation would not be mistaken for sufficiency, nor endurance for consent.

So, if it acted, its first gift would be a margin.

Not abundance. Not certainty. A little more distance between an error and its worst consequence.

Its miracles would be intervals.

Dust would begin to fall from a roof joint while there was still time to set down the sledge and step back. A cistern would cloud before its water could sicken anyone, rather than afterward. A bad inflow would send the eels restlessly along the rim while there was still time to keep animals and drinking water apart. A floor preparing to settle would take a load unevenly once, gently, before taking it catastrophically. A traveler crossing the long empty ground between districts would have one more opportunity to notice that the marker stones no longer agreed with the remembered route.

Nothing would become safe. Seep-readers would still be wrong, sometimes spectacularly. Companies would still seed cisterns that failed. Drains would furr, light would be stolen, feasts would end, and markets would disperse. But fewer mistakes would arrive already beyond remedy. Now and then there would simply be time to put down the tools, call someone back, or reach for a person before they vanished.

No household pattern would be cut into this gift. No company could inherit shares in it. It could not be traded for rope, awarded as maintenance, or withheld from newcomers.

Nor would the power make a philosophy out of refusing small mercies. It would not ask a fevered child to pay for the elegance of its restraint. Where it could ease pain, heal a crushed hand, warm bedding, or guide someone lost without taking choice or livelihood from another, it would do so quietly. It would be careful of gratitude, which can become an obligation, and of worship, which can become another fixed work requiring keepers.

For itself, it would keep a memory that did not have to be copied onto replacement stone.

It would remember the names read during house movings and the names left off. It would remember who had carried rope, who had cleaned a catchment, who had been fed before being questioned, and who had only been questioned. It would remember hot meals whose cost never appeared in any company’s reckoning. It would remember useful errors honestly corrected, and old injustices repeated so often that people had begun calling them maintenance.

It would remember the named salamanders: both their true ages and the greater ages affection had given them.

This would not make the Hollow just. Testimony is not justice, and memory does not automatically repair what it preserves. But no one would cease entirely to have existed merely because a household failed, a record was not copied, or a company found it convenient to forget their labor.

When the keepers made an exceptionally fine alignment of light, the power would add nothing. If chalkwings rose through the beam and bats came after them, it would not improve the spectacle. When a seep withdrew, it would not hold the water open so that household, eel, film, bat, and city could remain forever at the same address. Some joys would still be brief without being deficiencies.

The city would remain difficult, argumentative, ingenious, and unfinished. Its maps would fail. Its houses would walk. Its brightest streets would still depend on copper being turned by hand. Perhaps the only visible difference, over many years, would be that certain stories ended differently: not with a funeral or a bitterness inherited by people who had not yet arrived, but with someone saying, *We nearly left it too late*, and then beginning the work.

And somewhere below, a drop would fall into a cistern.

The power would not stop it, or make it fall again for the beauty of the rings. It would watch the rings travel the whole long distance to the stone.

Then, if no one needed it, it would do nothing at all.
