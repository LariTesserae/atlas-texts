### A Little Continuity

The power came during one of the long, mild rains.

There was no hush. The upper leaves went on ticking, the marsh frogs knocked their wooden notes into the water, and a white fisher missed its strike, shook once, and resumed stillness. Rain stood on the exposed anchor without touching it. Beside the gray seam, an old listener was drying a notebook in the roofless air.

The power did not begin by looking down from a saddle. A whole province can be a way of avoiding its inhabitants.

It attended first to the green thread inside a cistern shrimp; to a hand-newt flattened against a leaf, waiting for dusk; to the painful breathing of an old splayfoot whose ancestral path had begun to lead toward colder air. It noticed the mud inside the listener’s bedding, the stiffness in her hands, and the three different opinions she had held during her life about whether the anchors were alive. She had copied all three into the book, because none of them altered the tone or the date.

Only then did the power allow itself to perceive the Crown entire.

It did not ask what the Crown wanted. There was no single Crown in that sense, and the question would have flattered the person asking it. The tree basins were not rooms in one house. The marshes did not agree to represent one another. The anchors, as far as the power could discover, contained no imprisoned mind waiting to be released and no neglected purpose demanding restoration. They were old machinery, still making certain accommodations possible.

The power could have made the warm air universal. It could have carried the province over every cold pinnacle until the markers became meaningless and the margins ceased to kill. People would eventually have built roads. Roads were not evil, but neither were they an answer to anything the inhabitants had asked.

It could have fixed every marsh in place. That would have saved the fish before it and prevented the marshes in which other fish might someday live.

It could have joined every cauldron basin to every other, making one generous, well-mixed canopy out of thousands of small differences. It could have restored the anchors to their original specifications, if original specifications could be recovered, and discovered too late that fidelity to the beginning was a way of exterminating everything that had happened since.

So it kept its hands off the whole.

That was not the same as keeping its hands off every life.

The power did not believe that because the forest was patient, everything within it had time. Centuries of peat were no comfort to a nest failing in an afternoon. Nor was there any special virtue in allowing an ancient adjustment to become an avoidable extinction merely because the adjustment was old. Admiration could become indifference without ever changing its expression.

It began, therefore, to give time where time was the difference between change and erasure.

Not endless time. Not immunity.

At one cauldron tree, a rain continued for seventeen minutes after the cloud above it had thinned. Water entered a gutter that had not overflowed in years. A raft of leaf fragments carried shrimplets and two clutches of eggs across the narrow gap into a tenant tree’s new cup. Most of what arrived there would be eaten before the season ended. The power had not preserved a collection. It had preserved a possibility.

At a marsh whose seep was failing, the water went down over weeks rather than in a single warm night. The bowl still became sedge and then wet peat. Its small fish still vanished from it. But the two young fishers in the low white nest were flying before the ground beneath them was dry, and one of the departing birds reached the new marsh with mud still adhering to its feet. In the mud were eggs.

The old splayfoot was not given a map. She would not have known what to do with one. The power let the low call of another animal travel through wet rock from a plateau where forage remained. She stood for a long time with her toes spread on the cold path. Then she turned, not because she understood the margin, but because something living had answered from elsewhere.

These acts were so small that, afterward, they could all be described as luck. The power preferred that. Help which required the helped to identify their helper would soon become another kind of path: easy to follow, difficult to leave.

For the old listener, it did less.

She had sometimes wished to hear an anchor change its tone. At other times she had feared that wish, suspecting she only wanted her years of attention to be justified by an event. The power gave her no vision of past cycles and no knowledge of the engines’ makers. It did not make her young enough to finish every correction in the book.

When the change came before dawn, it simply stayed with her.

For three breaths, nothing in the forest became quieter. Water still hissed through the basalt flutes; frogs called; her own blood moved in her ears. But her attention was, for once, sufficient. She heard the deep tone alter beneath everything else.

There was no greeting hidden in it. There was no warning addressed to humanity. It was not a voice.

Her listening had not been foolish.

Those two facts arrived together.

Afterward the power withdrew almost entirely from the weather, the water, and the paths. It did not promise that nothing more would be lost. Such a promise would have been either a lie or a prison.

It remained watchful for failure rather than change. If the anchors one day began to die, it would not let the whole province perish merely to demonstrate the purity of its restraint. It would slow what could be slowed, warn those who could understand warnings, and buy time for movement, refusal, rescue, and grief. It would try to prevent the old machines from turning an accident into everybody’s fate. It would not pretend that buying time was the same as saving everything.

No shrine appeared beside the gray seam. Moss continued to fringe the exposed disk according to light and moisture. Birds stood in the dry air to preen. Travelers dried notebooks there. The listener completed her entry, checked the eastern seep, and left the ceramic shelter ready for the next wet person.

Much later, another reader found her account of that dawn. Beneath the measurements she had written:

> Tone changed before the third rain. Not a voice. Eastern seep already lower; check before the next nest count. The old path should not be trusted.  
>   
> I did not hear it alone. I have put this here because it is true, not because it is evidence.

Below that, in a younger hand, were only four words:

> Seep checked. Path moved. Continuing.

The power had not been forgotten, exactly. It had simply not become the most important thing.
