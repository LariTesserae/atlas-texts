The power would not lay a hand on the stone.

Even warmth would be an event there. Even attention, if it had weight, might become weather. Nor would it arrive as light: in the hollow, light belonged to accumulation, decay, and reuse, and a new radiance would only make the old districts harder to read.

It would begin by refraining—not because refraining was always good, but because it did not yet know how to help.

For a long while, it would watch without concluding. It would not mistake a cold pulse for abandonment, or a rich one for health. From outside, it could see every arch and all six skylines; it could follow the capillary water through districts no inhabitant knew existed. But it would not mistake this for complete knowledge. To see the whole arch was not to know the world of the animal folded in one cup. Its vision of the whole would be another geography, larger than the others, but not therefore entitled to rule them.

Pity would offer it miracles.

It could still the red hunter. It could catch the next falling flake. It could hold the throat open against ice, or close it gently before the fracture widened. It could make the newly settled larva live, thicken its clear rim, bring food within reach, ensure that another cup eventually touched its own.

Each act would seem innocent until followed far enough.

A hollow preserved from every fracture would cease to be a city forming with its cave. A hunter prevented from hunting would not thereby have been fed. A larva saved merely because it had entered the power’s attention would have been chosen by an accident of noticing. And safety imposed everywhere at once might leave nothing but pale chambers, immaculate and shut.

The power would refuse two easy consolations. It would not say that every death was necessary because it belonged to an order. Nor would it say that every death could be prevented without changing the lives around it beyond recognition. It would not call the hunter evil. It would not call the hunted animal’s death good. Above all, it would not use the beauty of the whole to excuse itself from tenderness toward the parts.

So it would act, but exactly.

Where suffering existed and could be eased without taking food, shelter, escape, or possibility from another, it would ease it. Where dying was already beyond prevention, and pain—if pain was there—could no longer guide a body toward safety, it would not require that pain to continue merely to leave the account of the hollow undisturbed. No arch would brighten. Nothing would learn that mercy had occurred.

Where harm arrived through simple carelessness, it would be less hesitant. A needless crushing, a casual poison, a distant decision that treated the small as nothing because no voice had come to object: these it would turn aside at their source when it could. Its concern would not depend on the hollow being useful, unique, intelligible, or pleasing. Nor would it wait for the inhabitants to become capable of requesting help. Their silence would be neither consent nor refusal.

But it would not promise permanence.

If the city’s own making slowly narrowed the central water, if the throat sealed, if the fracture opened and the small distinctions of the place dispersed into the sea, the power would not automatically force the beloved thing to remain available to its gaze. It would grieve without making grief a claim of ownership. It would distinguish, as well as it could, between an ending it ought to prevent and an ending it could prevent only by keeping the hollow from ever becoming anything else.

Most of its care would therefore have no visible architecture.

On the underside of the fresh flake, the clear rim would remain unchosen. The power would not demand to know whether it marked the founding of a city or merely furnished one. If the animal lived, that would not prove it had been meant to live. If it died, its life would not need the later usefulness of its rim to justify having mattered.

At the next inward pulse, the pale crowns would unfold. Eggs would turn in the central water. The old chambers would glimmer, and beneath the bridge the red hunter would begin to move. The power would not make the avenue still, nor stillness into a law.

A hand resting on the stone above would feel nothing unusual: no warmth, no vibration, no answering movement.

The hollow would take another silent breath.

Nothing in it would thank the power. Nothing would be required to.
