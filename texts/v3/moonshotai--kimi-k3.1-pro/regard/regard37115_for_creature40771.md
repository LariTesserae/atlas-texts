### The Open Hand

The first thing the power did was kneel.

From its former height, the cirque offered the usual deceptions: pale gravel, scattered blocks, three doubtful cairns, a shelter that might have been made by frost. Seen only from there, almost any gift would have been an answer to emptiness rather than to need.

So the power brought its attention down to the height of a moss leaf. It put no foot upon the mats. It watched cloud-water gather and vanish, a bee wait inside a garnet bell, a hare carry dry stems below ground. It remained through the reduction of winter and the small, hurried reopening of the light.

Its first impulse had been to give water. Its second had been to give warmth. Only after watching did it understand that both impulses had contained a little vanity: the wish to make a difference large enough to be seen from above.

A stream would have been easy. It would also have drowned some of the seed-holding cushions, changed the distribution of green, and drawn more grazing mouths to shrubs that needed years to replace a hand’s length of growth. A long, mild summer would have summoned lives into activity without giving the mountain a mild winter in which to receive them afterward. A roofed shelter, a reliable store of food, a safe path up from below: each might have been a mercy to one traveler and the beginning of a settlement the basin could not sustain.

The power made none of these things.

It did not make the hunter wicked by intervening always against it, nor the hares sacred by intervening always for them. It did not require the birds to remain through weather they had wisely learned to leave. It did not fill the shelter with generations merely because an empty human structure can look, to a powerful observer, like a reproach.

Nothing in the cirque needed to become grand in order to deserve care.

But the power was careful not to make a virtue of restraint. It did not call the place perfect. It did not call suffering instructive, or a death necessary merely because something else was fed. It never used the beauty of the whole pattern to excuse itself from noticing the small life breaking inside it.

Where help would only have moved the harm elsewhere, it refrained. Where a little help could be given without remaking the world around the recipient, it helped.

Once, late in a short flowering season, a female bee was caught by cloud with one cell still open. She had provisioned the others. She had outlasted cold mornings, wet moss, and the sudden closing of flowers. Now she waited a few body-lengths from her nest while the stone that told her when to fly went cold.

The power did not drive away the cloud. It did not warm the basin or extend the summer. It kept one stone warm until the cloud’s edge thinned.

The bee stirred, basked, crossed the mat, and went below with her last load.

She died before her offspring emerged, as her kind of life required. The power did not try to correct this by making her into another sort of creature. It gave no sign and received no gratitude. She knew only the warmth of stone, the nearness of her entrance, the work still possible in her body.

Much later, the power righted the fallen cairn.

That was all. It built no road and wrote no instruction on the rocks. The next traveler who came over the rim in cloud still had to notice the stones, decide whether to trust them, and find the eastern wall before darkness. No voice promised safety. The traveler was not safe. They were only no longer lost in the one place where being lost could be prevented by three stones standing where three stones had stood before.

The traveler reached the semicircle and lay with the wind passing overhead. In the morning, they assumed an earlier visitor had repaired the cairn. The power was content with this.

Years afterward, the traveler returned with someone younger and showed them which slope sounded hollow, where cloud usually opened, and how to lie so that the low wall took the wind. The knowledge remained mortal. It could still be forgotten. But it had been given time to pass from one mind to another rather than ending unnecessarily in whiteness.

The power’s care did not stop at the rim. It followed the birds toward their hard-season country, the hunter into the larger territory that fed it, the climbers along their ledges. To protect only the basin would have been admiration, not understanding. A place can be enclosed by walls without being contained by them.

Nor did the power promise to keep the cirque exactly as it was. It would not replace every fallen plate, restore every vanished flower, or embalm a living place and call the stillness love. If the shelf changed beyond the present community’s ability to remain, care might have to mean dispersal rather than preservation: seed carried elsewhere, a route to another refuge, room for a beginning not yet visible.

Most of what the power did would have been called luck. A pebble turned a runnel aside. A nest stayed dry. A cloud thinned for three breaths. Nothing appeared often enough to teach the inhabitants that rescue was a law of the world. It was not. The power made no such promise.

From above, afterward, the cirque still looked nearly empty. There was no temple, no stream, no everlasting gentle season. The flowers closed before cloud. The shelter lost stones to frost. The hunter ate and sometimes went hungry. Every vessel leaked.

But now and then a vessel held long enough for what it carried to be passed on: water to a root, a provision to a cell, warmth through a critical hour, a direction to another human memory.

The power had not stopped the brief water from vanishing. It had only kept a little of it, here and there, from vanishing unused.

Its care was not a hand closed around the cup.

It was an open hand beneath it.
