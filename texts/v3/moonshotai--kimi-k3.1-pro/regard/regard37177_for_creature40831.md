They would not arrive in light. Light was already the commonest thing above the basins. They would not arrive in thunder; there was no interval into which another voice could be set. Their coming would make no weather. For a long time, their only act would be to learn where not to intervene.

Many kindnesses would be possible, and most would be mistakes.

They could silence the High Clamor, but silence would chiefly be a kindness to a visitor with ears. They could still the water that made the sound, and in doing so stop the renewal on which every film depended. They could warm the pools for the comfort of a hand, darken the crowns with soil, and bring green into the sheltered angles. The result might be beautiful. It might also make the chemical boundaries uninhabitable, consume what little oxygen the cold water held, and replace a sparse persistence with a brief abundance followed by loss.

As for the grazer, they would not mistake it for a diminished version of some other being.

Its eyelessness was not a deprivation of the panorama. Its solitude was not necessarily loneliness awaiting company; another animal beneath the same stone might mean only a second appetite at the same film. Its world was narrow, but narrowness was not the same as incompleteness. It had no use for a path across the crowns. Its goods were exact: purchase on wet mineral, food renewed faster than it was exhausted, oxygen on one side of a boundary and seepage on the other, enough warning in the pressure of water to fold before being torn away.

To give it new desires would be easy compared with learning whether those desires would be gifts.

Yet restraint alone would not satisfy the power. They would not watch a grazer lifted into a barren seam and call its loss necessary to the purity of the place. Nothing in the basins required that particular death in order to remain the High Clamor. Nor would they decide that, because the animal could not ask for help, help had no meaning. Naturalness could explain an accident. It could not, by itself, make the accident good.

So they would learn to help in the animal’s units, not their own.

One day, beneath the inhabited stone, an ordinary shift of pressure would pass through the cavity. There would be no storm above, no crescendo in the roar, no alteration visible even from the nearest crown. A seam would receive slightly more water. The current would press along one edge of the grazer, and a portion of its broad attachment would release.

The animal would fold.

That movement, so often sufficient, would now reduce the little contact remaining. Its clear body would lift by almost nothing. Water would begin to pass between tissue and stone. Beyond the overhang, the connected violence of the seam would be waiting—not maliciously, not even powerfully on the scale of the basin, but with more than enough force to carry the animal into water where nothing lived.

The power would intervene there.

They would not calm the basin. They would not soften the quartz, make the animal invulnerable, or carry it away to a pool selected for its safety. They would not stop the current or require the stone to hold the animal in place of the animal’s own holding.

For one brief interval, they would only prevent the separating force at the lifted margin from increasing.

That would be all.

The animal’s forward edge would settle against the mineral. Contact would be made, and then made again across another small area. The folded body would flatten slightly. Its own adhesive surface, its own minute sequence of release and attachment, would complete the movement it had already begun. It would draw itself back beneath the overhang.

Then the power would let the water go.

Nothing would announce what had happened. The animal would have no category for rescue, and the water no memory of interruption. No crown would shine differently. No pool would rise. The roar would neither falter nor return, because from the basin’s standpoint it had never changed.

The power would not call the animal’s survival a sign. They would not make its former danger meaningful in retrospect, nor regard its life as a debt now owed to them. Nothing would have been settled about its eventual death. It would not have been made immortal. Its history would still leave no shell, no bones, no distinguishable mark upon the stone.

But a life did not need to be endless, or enduringly recorded, for its continuation that day to be worth allowing.

Afterward, most of the power’s attention would again resemble doing nothing. They would not seed the crowns or smooth a route for feet. They would not demand that the basins develop, diversify, or become more generous in order to justify having been loved. If further help were given, it would have to pass the same narrow test: it must continue a life in that life’s own terms, without making the place into a monument to the helper.

Their restraint would keep mercy from becoming authorship. Their mercy would keep restraint from becoming vanity.

On the map of stone, nothing would have happened. On the map of lives, one history would not have ended where it had been about to end.

Beneath the overhang, the animal would extend its forward margin into the current. Water would pass over the sensory structures along its edge. It would fasten itself a fraction farther on and draw the clear body after it, reading the water, finding it sufficient, and keeping its hold.

The High Clamor would not register the difference.

The power would not require it to.
