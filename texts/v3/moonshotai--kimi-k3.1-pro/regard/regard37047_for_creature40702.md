### The Early Seep

The power did not arrive as light. Light would have troubled the roosts.

It did not arrive as silence. Silence would have woken the dormitories and set hands searching for boots.

For a long time, it was only attention.

It stayed long enough to see a storm on the plateau become a date in the pump hall and then, weeks later, a brown line on the sump stairs. It learned the census and its omissions. It learned that there was no single inhabitant whose good could be secured in isolation. Dry the chamber for the dormitories and you altered the roosts. Illuminate the streets and you changed the small weather around every lamp. Close one seam and the water began an argument elsewhere.

So it did not perform the obvious miracles. It did not level the floor, stop the seeps, or persuade the stone to agree with the maps. It did not give the eyeless fish eyes or make the ferns independent of imported soil. Nothing in the Low Works had asked to be made more legible to visitors.

Neither did the power make a romance of leaving things alone.

The place was patient, but patience was not automatically good. Pain was not ennobled merely because people had learned to work around it. A danger accepted was different from a danger concealed by ignorance. Someone who hated the confined horizon did not need to be spiritually corrected into loving it. Someone who had returned down the incline did not need to be rescued from contentment.

The power would not preserve the Low Works by embalming it. Nor would it improve the town until it became somewhere else.

Its help, when help came, would have to leave the inhabitants more able to answer their own circumstances, not less. And it would have to reckon with those inhabitants who could neither petition nor refuse: the swiftlets behind the girders, the crickets under the warm pipes, the small lives turning in the backwaters.

For some time, attention was all that was required. The power was not waiting for suffering to become interesting. It was learning what help would cost, and who might have to pay it.

Then it found something no one in the Low Works could have known.

Behind the southern dome, warm water had spent several centuries opening a seam toward an older system of passages. The surveyors’ records were good. They were simply records of other things. In the coming wet season, the seam would open fully at almost the same hour that delayed storm water reached the sumps.

By itself, either event would have been troublesome. Together they would have brought down a doubtful section of roof above the power house and broken the main ventilation trunk. The pumps and ventilators would not have been stopped together by carelessness. They would have been stopped together by stone.

The residents would have done much of what could be done, once the signs appeared. The signs would have appeared too late.

There was no meaning in this. It was not punishment, destiny, or a necessary price for the town’s character. It was a conjunction of water, rock, and incomplete knowledge.

The power could have closed the seam. That would only have driven the pressure into narrower passages, whose inhabitants no census included. It could have held the rock in place forever, making the chamber’s hydrology an extension of its own will.

Instead, it let the water announce itself while there was still time to answer.

Eleven days early, a thread of warm seepage appeared behind a cable support below the pump hall. It was scarcely enough to darken a cloth. It carried a faint trace of ochre.

A pipe fitter noticed it because the patch had no drip above it. He did not take it for a supernatural warning. He took it for a damned nuisance, wiped it with the back of his wrist, and reported it.

The surveyors found nothing conclusive. One datum stud had certainly not moved; another had probably been struck by a cart. The new water was warmer than it should have been. A sounding hammer returned an answer nobody liked.

Because they could not prove that nothing was wrong, they acted as though something might be.

An old bypass flume was cleared of stored fittings. Two cable runs were moved. The doubtful roof was braced, not well enough to satisfy everyone and well enough to matter. The pump crews altered their maintenance order. People complained about the additional shifts. The fitter was blamed for having noticed.

When the seam opened, the water came with more force than even he had expected.

A walkway was lost. One reservoir became briefly unusable, and a terrace marked for eventual housing ceased to be marked for anything. The outer causeway went dark except for hand lamps. In the pump hall, attendants stood with their palms on railings, reading strain through concrete and steel.

The damaged roof came down onto the bracing and stopped there.

One pump was stopped. The ventilators were not. By morning the water had found the cleared flume, and by the next daily departure the upper darkness loosened as usual. Air moved against faces. Loose tags lifted on their wires. Speech became, for a while, more trouble than it was worth.

Afterward there was no festival. There were arguments about whether the bracing had been adequate, whether the flume had really needed clearing so early, and whose decision had saved the power house. The fitter was praised until this became intolerable and then mocked until it became bearable again. A new brown line appeared on the sump stairs. The maps were altered in pencil.

No one called it a miracle.

The power did not correct them. It had not acted in order to be correctly identified.

It left no white formation in the shape of a hand, no lamp that burned without current, no voice beneath the flywheels. Nor did it withdraw, having demonstrated something. There was nothing to demonstrate. It remained near enough to help again and quiet enough that no one had to listen for it through the pumps.

The Low Works went on being wet, hot, incomplete, and occasionally detested. Pumps still required scaling. Screens still tore. Some residents left at the end of their contracts, and some came back. The warm water continued opening one passage and closing another.

The power had not saved the place from change. It had prevented change from becoming, on that one occasion, an erasure no one had chosen and no one could answer.

Near the incline mouth, one of the ferns put out a new frond.

It was not a sign. Someone had changed the soil.

The power did not take credit for that either.
