### No Rain

The power would not bring rain.

For a long while, it would do nothing visible at all. It would remain through intake and exhaust. It would learn that the clear lamplight was not a substitute for some better sun, that the open basins were not emptiness awaiting improvement, and that a place could be quiet without being lonely.

The sixteen kilometres of the hollow might draw its notice, but the mite on the stair would teach it the scale of the place.

Dead fiber. A pellet. A decomposer cord. Rootlets taking what the cord released. Somewhere above, a leaf thickening by almost nothing. The power would follow this whole sequence before permitting itself to form an idea of help.

It would watch an engineer protect one shoot from freight wheels and plane several tonnes of related growth into sheet. It would listen to a district meeting argue over moths behind a warehouse wall. It would see a failed pattern become a row of nesting hollows, and a herd’s appetite become the curve of a service road. Only then would it begin to understand that care here did not mean preservation, and interference did not become benevolent merely because it was powerful.

So it would put aside the greater miracles.

It would not make the roots perfectly obedient. Too much of the Rootworks had come from noticing what disobedience made possible.

It would not establish a durable boundary between helpful and harmful life. The people had failed to draw one not because they were foolish, but because a burrower could be a pest in a cable run and useful in a fissure, and because even a dependable mite could foul a contact.

It would not give the city a single mind so that it could finally speak with one voice, or be governed efficiently, or offer gratitude. Gratitude would not be worth that amputation.

Nor would it make every arch permanent, every memorial safe from revision, every accidental chamber inviolable. A gift that prevented the inhabitants from cutting away what the power had found beautiful would only be another kind of enclosure.

It would not ask for worship. In the Rootworks, a steady current was an instruction. The power would not set itself up as something around which every living thing must thicken.

But it would not confuse restraint with purity.

If a worker lay beyond ordinary rescue beneath a fallen slab, it would lift the slab, openly if necessary. It would not sacrifice a person to preserve the elegance of its non-interference. It would not call suffering necessary because the surrounding city was finely made, or allow someone to die so that the excavation could retain an instructive silence. Benevolence was not an aesthetic.

Its preferred intervention, where intervention was possible, would be smaller than rescue and larger than luck.

Beyond the eastern survey lines, it would find a failure waiting in the stone.

Not an ordinary bad roof, of the sort crews were right to fear and equipped to reconsider. This one would give no useful warning. When the excavation reached it, the failure would travel faster than instruments could report and farther than any local arch could resist. The people would not be defeated by an error in their judgment. There would simply be no subsequent moment in which judgment could occur.

The power would not make the geology harmless. It would not teach the stone to hold forever, or move the inhabitants elsewhere, or place a voice in the gallery announcing what they must do.

It would let the stress spend a little of itself early.

One tick in the wall.

A worker would stop. Another would come over. Someone would say it had been a cutting head; someone else would point out that the cutting head had already stopped. Instruments would be unpacked. A freight movement would be delayed. There would be an argument about whether the delay was justified, followed by a longer argument about what the instruments meant.

The rest would be ordinary: survey filaments, temporary braces, an altered sequence of excavation, a roof design revised twice. A herd might crop through one of the new lines and make everything more inconvenient. Moths would settle behind a work lamp before the crew had finished using it.

No one would call any of this a miracle. A disaster would simply fail to acquire a history.

That would please the power. Its intervention would have returned a problem to those who had to live with the solution, rather than making them live with its solution.

Afterward, it would be tempted to remain and prevent every such ending. It would recognize the danger in that temptation. A guardian who prevented all irreversible loss would eventually have to decide which risks the inhabitants could take, which growth could proceed, which plans were too foolish to permit. Without announcing itself as author, it would become one.

So it would not promise that the roof could never fall. It would not guarantee the survival of every district, every herd, every peculiar rib, every seat-sized hollow retained beside a tram stop. If later generations filled the moth chambers, dismantled the oldest towers, altered their bodies, abandoned the excavation, or became a people who no longer wished to live in anything resembling the Rootworks, it would not recall them to the place it had first admired.

Its care would be for their continuing, not for the permanence of its favourite view.

At the next reversal of the ventilators, mites would draw into seams. Moths would lift or settle according to warmth and district. People near the collectors would look up because a blocked frond sounded different from an open one. Across the inhabited region, millions of leaf wafers would flex, making their soft, dispersed turning of pages.

The city would not respond with one will.

The power would be glad of that.

Then it would go, if going was what love required. It would leave no rain, no altar, no eternal arch, and no name in the plan revisions. On the old stair, the copper-shelled mite would open another seam. At the frontier, the work would stop sometimes and proceed sometimes. Somewhere a child would produce a crooked bracket and be told, patiently, why.

The power would leave the Rootworks unfinished.

And it would not require their future to resemble the place it had loved.
