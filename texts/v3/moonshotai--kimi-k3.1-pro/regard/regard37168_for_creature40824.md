### At Slack Water

The power came at slack water, when calm was only what the province happened briefly to be doing.

It did not arrive with light. There was already light enough: white sun on the western surf, green depths over the saddles, rain darkening one island after another. It did not quiet the South Race to make its entrance safe. For a while it simply attended.

It had thought to find a place and the being who belonged to it. Instead it found multitudes, none of whom possessed the same Highwater.

A limpet’s province ended at the edge of its scar. A larva’s might depend on a few seconds of slackening in the Race. A seal’s was a circuit of feeding water and shelves that appeared and vanished with the tide. A colony bird carried one ledge faithfully through an ocean’s worth of absence. In the black basins, a scavenger’s whole expectation might be the next thing that sank. Even the maintenance crews lived in different versions of the country: one in cable diagrams, one in annotated charts, one in a private knowledge of which apparent weather windows were not windows at all.

None of these lives was a small, incomplete version of the whole. Each was whole at its own scale.

The power considered the large mercies.

It could raise the drowned valleys and give back the old country. Kelp, eelgrass, fish, whales, and basin worms would then discover what restoration meant when administered from above.

It could calm the races. Filter feeders would starve in the gentle water; shoals and predators would lose the seams around which they had arranged their lives. It could stop the gales that tore kelp loose, and interrupt the delivery of food to depths it had at first mistaken for barren. It could spare every chick, seal, and shoaling fish, until mercy became only another name for its inability to tolerate hunger.

It could make every departure safe and every return certain. Nothing would then need to learn when to hold on, when to move, or when to let go.

So it put aside the idea of blessing Highwater with safety.

It did not conclude that everything painful was necessary, or that every death secretly served the province. That would have been another way of looking without seeing. It concluded only that it could not heal a place by relieving itself of the discomfort of watching it.

Nor did it tear out the generating station. Concrete was not a sin, and steel was not an insult to gneiss. The station’s noise, lights, vessels, and maintenance had real effects, but effects had sources and paths; they could be examined, answered, reduced. The power did not know what needs existed at the other ends of the armoured cables. It refused to call its own ignorance purity.

For a long time, therefore, it did nothing that would have looked like power.

Then, on a falling shelf west of the Race, it noticed a young seal.

Around her neck was a loop of insulated wire, twisted by use or weather into a knot. It had not yet cut deeply. It would. Each season’s growth would make the argument tighter. The wire was not an ancient condition of the massif. It was not food for anything. No bloom depended on it, no deep scavenger waited for its work to be completed. It was merely a small human remainder doing slow, pointless harm.

The power made itself no larger than a pair of hands at the edge of the shelf.

It did not make the seal tame, or free her from fear. She struck the rock with her foreflippers and tried to twist away. The power let her. It held only the wire, parted it, and drew it clear.

It did not make her invulnerable. It did not promise her a fish, a mate, a dry shelf at the required hour, or an easy death many years later. It removed the thing that had no claim on her.

She went into the water with such force that for a moment there was nothing to see but disturbed green and the widening rings beneath it. A raw groove remained around her neck. Whether she would live was hers, and the sea’s, and chance’s. The power did not follow to find out.

It left the wire, neatly coiled, on the station’s concrete step. There was no message beside it. Weeks later a maintenance hand would pick it up, turn it over once, and put it in a pocket rather than in the sea. Before leaving, he would walk the cable landing and collect three other scraps. He would not describe this as a revelation. He would be mildly annoyed at the extra work.

The power considered staying. A guardian could prevent so much.

But a guardian would also become another prevailing condition: less visible than the weather, more pervasive than the station’s hum, and ultimately impossible for anything within the province to refuse. Even benevolence, if made permanent and sovereign, could become something every other life had to accommodate.

So it left no throne, no law, no enchantment of endurance. It did not promise that Highwater would last forever. It promised only not to return merely to make itself feel better, and, if it did return, to come for something specific.

From the highest summit there would have been no sign of its visit. The rounded islands remained where they were. Kelp streamed into a new direction. Birds lifted from a colony as a shoal passed below. The station’s note deepened through rock and water, and a grey wall of rain entered from the west.

Nothing had been saved except one animal, and that only for a while. The power did not think the word *only*.

Then the tide turned, and the power, who could have held it back, let it.
