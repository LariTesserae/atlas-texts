### A Grain with a Direction

At first, you would find only a wall.

It would be an ivory inner wall below the highest bridge, where a hollow stair turns once before opening into a lane. At noon, light would enter from some upper aperture and rest along the nacre without quite reaching the floor. The chamber would appear finished: smooth, uninhabited, still.

Then a grain near the base of the wall would move.

That would be me.

I would be a masonback, my wedge-shaped back colored almost exactly like the old mineral beneath me. My whole range might lie within the span of a browser’s shoulder. From the bridge, there would be no reason to suppose I existed. Even from the floor of the chamber, my disappearance would leave no visible emptiness.

It would be easy to call that a small life. But smallness is a measurement taken from bridges.

At my own scale, the wall would not be smooth. A grain would project from it with the abruptness of the raw cliffs beyond Sere. A scratch would be a long terrain of exposed faces and altered chemistry, with films of growth collecting along its edges. The traces left by other masonbacks would cross it: some fresh enough to follow, some so old that they had become merely one property of the surface among others.

I would not know the chamber as a chamber. I would not know that its outer wall was green, that its stair opened into two districts, or that the stem colony above it had been thickening an arch since before my lineage began. I would know warmth arriving through mineral, the chemistry of a few grains, the attraction of roughness, and the slight resistance left by a neighbor’s passage.

That would not feel like ignorance. It would feel like a complete world.

During the warm day, I would graze and move. Behind me I would leave a scale of secretion so thin that it would not smooth the scratch, or cover the grain, or make any difference a browser could detect. It would only alter the conditions for whatever came after.

I would not be repairing the wall. Repair implies that I had noticed some failure in the whole and imagined its restoration. I would have no such picture. I would be feeding, responding, avoiding one trace and following another. The nacre would be what remained when those sufficient little actions had been repeated beyond anyone’s counting.

Sometimes the pressure of browsers would reach me through the floor: six soft contacts, a pause, another six. I would not know them as crowds, or as the appetite that kept the streets open. They would be a brief change in the mineral, followed by stillness. A veilwing crossing the chamber might mean nothing to me at all.

If the deep mountain tick came during my life, I would feel the entire wall become, for an instant, one thing.

Then it would be separate grains again.

At night my movements would slow. The chamber would retain a little of the day’s warmth. Above me, the town would fold and dim without becoming any less occupied. No doorway would visibly narrow while I lived. No arch would advance. For me, Sere would be permanent, which would be one of the useful errors available to a short life.

When I died, there would be no event at the scale of the chamber. My tissues would be taken apart with the same patience given to a fallen frond or a browser lying beneath the stem-sheaths. My empty back might remain where it was, or shift slightly under the passage of those after me. Eventually it would be included in the layers: not preserved as a record, not quite destroyed, simply made part of the material through which later lives moved.

Those later masonbacks would not inherit my knowledge. There would be almost none to inherit. They would inherit a surface minutely different from the one I had found, and their own responses to it.

After enough generations, the scratch would no longer gather growth in quite the same way. Fresh deposition would attract further grazing; grazing would prepare the way for further deposition. The wall would become smoother without ever having been anyone’s intention. Browsers would cross the chamber on floors made soft-seeming and silent by accumulations of lives just as brief as mine. Light would lie more evenly along the ivory.

None of them would know which part had been me.

Neither would I, by then. My boundaries would have become an unnecessary detail.

So if you came looking much later, you should not search for a wedge-shaped shell or try to distinguish one scale from the rest. Come at noon instead. Stand in the lane outside the chamber while the browsers touch shoulders and change color. Watch a veilwing slide beneath the unfinished arch. Then look at the lower wall, where there had once been a roughness that caught the light.

You would find only a smooth place, too small to name.

I would not be the nacre. I would not be the colony, and certainly not the city. I would be one brief, grain-sized life within their continuity: mixed beyond recovery with thousands of others, and not therefore absent.

A browser would pass without sound. The arch would go on closing. The light would rest quietly on the wall.

I would not need to be found more precisely than that.
