At first, I would be mostly a refusal.

The first rain would not be enough. It might come as a hard shower crossing only my district, drumming briefly on the slab roofs and sending water through the court’s old inlet. For a little while the silt would darken. Water would thread around the mineral grains, pause in the shallow hollows, and find the lower doorway. Then the shower would pass. Wind would begin drying the exposed paving, and by evening there would be only a black seam along the wall.

Elsewhere in the dust, some other seed might open. I would remain closed.

My place would be a roofless court two shallow grades below a covered reservoir, somewhere in the long interior of the plateau. One wall would still stand high enough to break the wind; another would have collapsed into a low bank that caught dust. Silt would have risen nearly to the sill of the doorway. After sufficient rain, the reservoir above would send a gentle overflow across one corner of the court—not often enough to scour it clean, not so seldom that the silt became entirely sterile.

I would be the seed of one of the small annuals, although for most of my existence that name would promise too much. There would be nothing green about me, nothing visibly preparing. A dry-noon census could sift the whole court and still mistake me for part of its mineral poverty.

I would not need the whole plateau. The districts could go on receiving their separate weather; the meres could fill and empty; cisterns could remain cold and untraveled. My city would be one court’s particular way of admitting water and letting it go.

Cold would have to come before I trusted wetting. Perhaps thin ice would form that winter in the shaded pools, and dry snow would lie briefly on the higher swells. None of that would concern me directly, but the cold would alter something in my closed condition. Even then, one mild rain might not suffice. I might require another wetting, and another. A promising season could pass without me.

This would not be hesitation in the human sense. It would be the whole wisdom of my body: not to confuse an event of weather with a length of time in which a life could be completed.

Then, one year, the necessary things would coincide. Cold would already have happened. Rain would return gently and more than once. The court would fill without flooding violently; water would remain long enough in the finest silt. I would take it in.

The change would be almost nothing: a splitting, a small white root entering the little depth available, the first seed leaves lifting into cool air. Yet from that moment I would have to hurry.

Hurry would be the shape my patience took.

I would not become part of a green carpet. Around me there would be only scattered rosettes and fine stems, with bare mineral ground showing between them. An adjoining court, apparently identical, might remain entirely empty. There would be no common spring in which everything consented to live—only separate answers, seed by seed.

My leaves would take what they could from the pale sun. Dust washed from the roofs, a little organic residue, the minerals in the water: enough, if enough came quickly. My flower would be small and pale. It would not wait for insects that were not coming. It would fertilize itself and begin making seed while the court was still damp around me.

Even then, the margins would be retreating. The overflow would weaken. Wind would lift dust from the higher paving and lay some of it over my roots. By the time the surrounding stone had dried to ash and bone, I would be setting my last seed. Soon afterward there would be only a fine, brittle stem where the plant had been.

And that is where *I* would become difficult to locate.

Some of what came after me would lodge beneath a fresh skin of silt. Some would enter a crack and remain there. Some would be carried out by the next overflow and deposited far below, perhaps in a basin where a different set of conditions governed waking. The seeds would not all inherit the same requirement. One might answer the next suitable rain; another might refuse the very sequence that had awakened me; another might wait through several promising years.

I would no longer be a single patient body. I would be a scatter of postponements.

If you returned at dry noon, you would find the court apparently as before: silt, pale walls, a few dead stems, nothing that seemed to be maintaining itself. If you returned after several days of suitable rain and found small pale flowers open in the dust, you would not exactly be seeing my return.

You would be seeing the few of us who had, for once, given the same answer.

The rest would still be in the silt, unconvinced by the rain.
