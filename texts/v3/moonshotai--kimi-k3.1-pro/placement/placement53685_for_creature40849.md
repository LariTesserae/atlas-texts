I would spend most of my life without seeing the plain.

For years I would be a drumfly only in promise: a soft-bodied feeder in the wet root felt beneath one of the old domes, where living roots cross and recross the compacted remains of their ancestors. No horizon. No grey-green distances. My country would be a few body-lengths of damp fiber, a rootlet’s thin sweetness, a pocket of decay, the packed wall of an ant passage.

The enormous events overhead would reach me translated.

A human party might pass as a sequence of careful taps: staff, pause, broad sole, staff again. A strider would be something more troubling—the press and turn of a testing foot, a long wait, then weight traveling through the root arches with a boom that seemed to change its mind about direction. Rain would arrive first as movement in the felt, then as altered taste and temperature. Somewhere above me, cups would be filling and frogs beginning to call, but I would know only that the dry places were retreating.

I would learn stillness. I would learn the difference between a vibration meant for me and one merely passing through. Most of the Thatch, for most of my life, would be something happening on the other side of a ceiling.

Then I would come up into a mast.

Not onto the open plain, at first. The plain would remain outside: an ordinary brightness beyond a storm-split seam, too wide and dry to concern me. Inside there would be chambers of different dampness, old partitions, the close pressure of other bodies, wings and folded limbs. Dust in the dry places. The green mineral scent of living tissue nearer the crown.

My first call would be almost nothing.

A small, hard note. Seed against pottery.

But I would be braced against a wall that had been hollowing and drying for longer than I had existed. The chamber would take the note. Another would answer from deeper in the stem. Then a body near the opening would call, and another, until I could no longer distinguish the vibration I made from the vibration returning to me through my feet.

Beyond my mast, another stand would begin.

That would be the first immensity I knew: not the view from a crown, not cloud beneath the rim, but distance made of answering pressure. A stand far enough away to be an hour’s journey for something winged would seem suddenly to be calling from inside my own chamber. The floor would add its hollow responses. The plain, which had done without me for so long, would become too full of sound to have directions.

Below, humans might stop and touch one another’s sleeves. A hand at an elbow. Two fingers against a shoulder. The cord between them tightening as one moved and the other followed. They would have names for stands, routes, dangers, obligations. For the duration of the chorus, most of those names would be useless.

I would have no names to lose. I would simply call, and be called through.

And while I was occupied with the brief, urgent business of being adult, the mast would be making other use of me. Flowers would open in the vibration. Pollen would catch on my legs and belly. Dew shaken from the crown would find the flutes and run down past the chambers. If I left and reached another stand, some of that pollen would leave with me. I would not be tending anything. I would be going toward another voice, carrying the consequences of the last one.

Perhaps I would not reach it.

The intervals are patrolled by animals for whom a drumfly is one small item in a sudden abundance. There would be softwings rising around flowering, hunting birds behind them, gliders folding into the swarm. If I escaped those and fell later onto the mat, there would be beetles, ants, larvae, roots. My body would not remain a body long enough to become a monument. Parts of me might travel farther after death than I had managed on my wings.

The stand would continue without me. Its chord would change for slower reasons: a partition failing, a vent opening, an old chamber becoming wet, a young mast coming into voice at the edge of the others.

I think that is why I would be a drumfly. It would suit me to be a creature whose voice became fully itself only when something else took it up. Alone, I would be years of hidden feeding and then a dry click. In relation, I would be part of the mechanism by which distant masts flowered, water moved, wings gathered, and the apparent vacancy of the plain became briefly unbearable with life.

I would never see the whole High Thatch. I would be one of the ways its separated places touched.

You would find me by not looking.

Put your palm flat against a living mast just before an evening chorus. Wait through the stem’s small ticks and the passage of wings. Feel for the first isolated note, so slight it might be coming from inside your own hand.

Let that be me—just before the walls answer, and I disappear into the sound.
