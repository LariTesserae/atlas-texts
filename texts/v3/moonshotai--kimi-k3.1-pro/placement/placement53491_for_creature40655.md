From the central gantry, I would be invisible. You would have to cross the second pan, leave the clean lane, and step behind a valve support at the weak edge of a service lamp to find me.

I would be the crescent the scrubber-crawlers keep missing.

Not the crawler. Not exactly the lamp moss, either. The moss would be one of my inhabitants, and there would be others. My deeper substance would be a repeated imperfection: three generations of machines making almost the same turn, their brushes overlapping everywhere except here. My boundary would not be a wall. It would be a history of almost.

On one side of me, the deck would be stripped to bright, specification-gray plate. On the other, ceramic dust and metal scale would remain in an expansion joint. A strip of insulation beneath the lamp would carry my oldest growth: a layered crust no larger than a hand, gray-green where it could still grow, white where the Draw had dried it. Beneath its base, perhaps, a blue assembly mark would remain legible. A little seepage would follow the valve support and disappear under a loose tile. There, olive-black film would edge the standing water, and after certain Weeps a few glassseed would graze—visible only as a gray thread of gut, the minute repetition of legs.

I would not be alive in any simple sense. The moss, the films, and the transparent animals would be alive. I would be their temporary arrangement, together with the mechanical error that allowed it to persist.

The phases would pass through me without asking what I was.

During the Draw, I would contract into materials. The moss would whiten and become brittle. Exposed film would lift like flaking paint. The water beneath the tile would remain, but the animals would retreat into whatever narrow connection still held liquid. From the walkway I would look like nothing more consequential than industrial dirt.

The Weep would make me briefly visible. Water would darken the crust from its edges inward. Dust would adhere. The velvet margin would return to the waterline, and droplets falling from the valve body would strike plate, insulation, and ceramic with three different sounds. For a little while, before the next drying or scouring, I would approach abundance.

Not every Flush would reach me. That, too, would be part of my existence. But sometimes water would find the seam across the pan, rise against the support, and take something: moss grains, black threads, salt, a few transparent bodies. I would be diminished and dispersed in the same event. Material from me might end on the catch screens. Some might lodge in a wheel groove or behind an ice collar. Nearly all of it would cease to matter. A fragment might not.

The crews would know me only intermittently. A rust-colored margin in the moss might be read as evidence of salts in the condensate and entered in a log. Film at my joint might contradict a dry-reading sensor. To one shift I would be an assay bed; to another, a missed corner. Both descriptions would be correct. If someone lifting the loose tile set it back at nearly the same angle, that small carelessness would matter more to me than any intention.

I could be four years old and ancient.

I could also end without drama. A revised route, a new brush housing, a slightly corrected turn. One pass would lay bare the insulation and take the layered crust. The lamp would continue shining. The valve would continue sweating. Nothing would protest, and there would be no ruin to mark—only a cleaner floor and a small change in what the next water did.

Elsewhere, months later, a gray-green stain might appear beneath another lamp. I would not insist that it was me. Continuity is too large a demand down there.

So: I would be just beyond the reach of the brushes, at the fading edge of useful light. Not a spirit in the machine, not a hidden wilderness, not even a single organism. I would be the shape of an omission, preserved for a while by repetition. An unplanned duration with inhabitants.

And while I lasted, Draw would whiten me, Flush would carry pieces of me away, and when the Weep began, the old crust would darken from its edges inward. Beneath the loose tile, in water absent from the plans, something almost transparent would start to move.
