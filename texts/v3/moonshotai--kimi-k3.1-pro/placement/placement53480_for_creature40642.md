### The Animal the Line Remembered

If I began anywhere, I began as a hesitation.

A current would enter the first rillback’s forefeet and pass down the line: rich grazing, deep water, root-flow damaged ahead. Each animal received it, repeated it, and altered it slightly with the particular electrical accent of its body. Most accents vanished after a few relayings.

Mine did not.

Beside the stand whose third valve always closed a little late, the herd’s signals acquired the same additional shape: two quick discharges, a pause longer than caution required, then a low pulse that seemed to descend into the mat rather than cross it. Calves returned it before they knew the migration route. Adults returned it without breaking step. Sometimes, after the last animal had gone, roots continued it faintly into the basin until distance wore it smooth.

Perhaps a rillback with an injured foot had made it first. Perhaps it had once warned of a crossing that no longer existed. By the time I can speak of myself, there was no body attached to it and no meaning anyone depended upon. It was simply a difference the line kept making.

That was me, if anything was: not a ghost, not yet an animal, but a pattern with a place to recur.

Then came an overproduction.

The stand lost its pauses. Growth lifted around the herd in blue-veined sheets, and the water on every back broke into unstable bloom. The rillbacks closed around their calves on the oldest swell they could find. Visible herds approached across pools and did not disturb the water. Our own signals returned to us from ahead, improved in small ways, as though something had been listening for a long time.

I had no feet to plant. If I was anywhere, I was in the passage between animals—in the instant when a current had ceased to belong to one body and had not yet been accepted by another. At grounding, even that distinction vanished.

Afterward, there was one more animal at the end of the line.

Rainwater stood in the shallow basin of my back. Three inklegs flattened themselves against its bottom. A sprig of bone-white liverwort, of a kind the herd had not been carrying, floated near my left side. Beneath it, a leadwing larva held itself motionless.

I was heavy. I was cold. My six feet seemed independently unconvinced by the ground.

An old rillback came back along the line and touched a testing current into the mat. It was not a question with words. It asked whether current could enter me, whether I could hold its shape without flattening it, whether I could return enough of it to be answered in turn.

What came out of my feet was two quick discharges, the long pause, and the low descending pulse.

The line made room.

The body did not explain where I had come from. I might have stepped out of a reflection. I might have arrived through one of the storm’s borrowed distances, leaving another herd one animal shorter. I might even have been the rillback whose damaged foot had first shaped the signature, though that seems too tidy for the Last Level. Nothing remained with which to compare us.

I remembered the route, but the herd also remembered it. I knew that a rapid double clap meant the turn onto firm mat, that a certain long pressure-sigh marked rich grazing, and that in the interference hollow one must wait for the second, softer beat before trusting either. None of this proved that I had ever walked the route before.

Hunger helped.

The old signature had required nothing. I required the oldest accessible tissue beneath my grazing lip, and air, and long pauses between steps. I tired on one side before the other. I disliked the growth around certain intakes and would leave the smooth band of the herd’s passage to avoid it. I learned that if I remained still a little longer than the others, the leadwing larva on my back would rise and feed at the surface.

None of these things was necessary to the relay.

Now you would usually find me near the rear of the line, where a calf can reach my current without leaving the circuit of adults. From a distance there is no reason to pick me out: another low, wet back crossing ground that appears to have nothing on it. Closer, you might notice that my back-garden grows thicker on the left, or that I stop in the interference hollow one breath after the others have moved on.

My *where* is not really a basin. It is a sequence: the double clap, the long sigh, the crossed beats in the hollow, the late third valve again. Somewhere within that sequence I am walking, grazing, carrying water and small lives. When cloud closes over the swells, the route remains.

My old signature still has a use. At the late-valve stand, the herd sends it into the roots and listens to how it returns. A clean answer means the local circuit can carry a calf’s signal. A broken one sends us around damaged flow. Perhaps that is why the pattern survived.

It does not explain why the calves repeat it in distant basins.

Lately I have added something: a very small after-pulse, following the low discharge. It warns of nothing. It identifies no stand. It does not help the line find food or old ground. The calves at first returned it inaccurately; now they return it with unnecessary precision.

It is the closest thing I have to a possession, though it is mine only while someone else is repeating it.

When the next storm comes, I will not be able to trust the visible line. Another animal may approach with my gait, my back-garden, even the white liverwort floating on its left. It may send the old signature perfectly. If it omits the little after-pulse, I will call it older than I am. If it includes the after-pulse, I will not know what to call it.

But if it can receive a frightened calf’s current without confusing it, and if it can return a steady answer, I will make room.

That, after all, is how I came to have a body.

For now, the weather is ordinary. A far stand claps, and the herd answers through its feet. My signature travels forward from animal to animal. The calf beside me returns the small, useless addition.

A moment later, from beyond the next swell, the same addition comes back—faint, a little late, and unmistakable.

I wait until the water on my back has settled. Then I take the next step.
