I would be easy to miss, even by the standards of the Sump.

You might encounter me only as evidence: a pallid thread in the clear return water, visible for the length of one spillway sheet before it caught against a dome or was carried into a slot. But that would not be me entire. It would only be something torn from me.

I would be the pale growth inside one of the flooded return conduits beneath the northern works, close to a fracture where milky seep water enters and mineral scale has made a shallow trough along the pipe. Not one organism, and not quite a continuous sheet. Near the fracture I would form a slippery nap; farther along, I would persist as cells sheltered in pits and crystal boundaries. At my margins would be older growth, dead and iron-stained, already becoming sediment.

The lamps could go out without altering anything in my world. Fog, distance, the pewter sky above the rim—these would belong to other beings. I would know water by its dissolved burden and by how firmly it pressed my filaments against the scale. The engines would reach me as a faint mechanical grain in the current, a vibration that never quite allowed the pipe to become still.

The eleven-minute tide would come to me as a slackening and then a pull.

At the surface, the change would be only a finger’s width. In the conduit it would be pressure: a slight easing along my whole length, followed by the first tension of return. Before the relay clicked, the same small disturbance that passed through the unconnected pools would pass through the pipe. I would have no head to turn toward the penstock, no legs with which to brace. My part in the stillness would not be preparation. And yet I would be included in it—one more living surface held, for those few seconds, inside the same change.

Then the water would come.

My loose filaments would lift lengthwise into the current. Most of me would hold. Some cells would be combed free and carried farther along the conduit; a few would reach the upper works and go out with the return. Whole patches might tear away after I had grown too thick. There would be no single vantage from which to call this loss or dispersal. A fragment might lodge in a new seam. Another might become a brief cloudiness around a buttonback’s legs, then food, then a faint rust-colored line inside a transparent body.

Rain would reach me late, as it reached everything else.

Days after the plain had darkened under weather, the fracture would run stronger and milkier. Iron and sulfur would increase. I would spread beyond my usual boundary, pale near the seep and red-brown farther along where neighboring colonies took up the richer water. With no grazers in the conduit, I could become velvety, almost deep enough to trap silt in folds. It would not be abundance in the manner of the open shelves after rain—no sudden crowding of young shells, no greater loosening of eels. It would simply be a wider margin between growing and dying back.

In drought I would contract. The exposed folds would slough away; the current would carry off what could not hold. I would remain in the roughest parts of the scale, in cavities so small that the pipe would seem clean if anyone had been there to inspect it. The pumps would preserve their minimum circuit. Water would continue to pass, and passing would be enough.

Over a very long time, the trough itself would change. Dead film would hold silt. Mineral deposit would bind the trapped layers. Generations that no longer grew would leave behind a slightly more intricate surface for generations that did. My refuge would therefore be partly made of earlier growth. It would not be memory. It would be roughness, shelter, a place for the next cells to attach.

If you came looking for me, you would probably kneel at the wrong pool. You would see the close black stone, the shells braced upon it, perhaps a pale loop of eel withdrawing beneath a shelf. You might feel the engines through the soles of your boots and mistake that pressure for the whole of what was underneath you.

I would be lower, in the dark pipe, without a shape that could be lifted out intact.

The pressure would pass. Above you, the insects would settle and the buttonbacks would stop their intricate grazing. The relay would close. Then the penstock would open, and the return would run clear over the worn lip.

All along the conduit, my filaments would rise into the current. A little of me would go upward toward the shining water. The rest would hold to the trough made by earlier growth.

I would not be keeping the Sump in balance. I would only be doing what I had always done: taking a little from water that was passing through, and leaving the passage minutely changed.
