I would be low down, where the garden stops looking like a garden.

Not on one of the green shelves, but a few paces above the lower crack, in the lee of a freshly fallen andesite block. A rill has crossed there and left a shallow pocket of black sand. At night the pocket may hold a little water; by morning it may be only a dark stain. At its edge, half buried, is a loop of wiry green no longer than a finger.

That would be me: a torn fragment of clubmoss.

Three nodes, perhaps. One root still fixed in a crumb of the old mat. A stem tip scraped by grit. The rest of what had been continuous with me would be above on the ledge, below in the basin, or crushed somewhere in the fall.

Even before the fall, my boundaries had been uncertain. I had run from cushion to cushion, rooting wherever a node stayed damp, crossing other stems and the dead fibers of plants that were not me. Some of the hanging cord I helped make was binding rather than body. The mat had not been mine, though for a while I had helped keep its weight attached to the wall.

I would know nothing of the Notch as a whole. The great sounds would reach me only as changes in my small contacts: grit ticking against an exposed stem, the block trembling after an impact, water arriving all at once instead of bead by bead. Lightning would pass without showing me the galleries overhead. Wind would matter less as sound than as the rate at which my uncovered surfaces dried.

At my scale, cloud entering the cut would cause no general awakening. Water would darken one side of the sand and stop. The buried node might remain wet while the exposed stem dried in the cold air. For a few minutes, a film of water might connect me to material carried from half a dozen ledges: ash, moss fibers, crushed rosette tissue, spores that would never grow. Then the rill would fail, and that brief mixing would separate again into different fates.

A fragment is not a seed with its future folded inside it. It has tissues, damage, and whatever time the remaining moisture allows.

If the root held and the node stayed damp, a little new growth might eventually follow the shelter of the block. I would not need to reconstruct the old ledge. A root entering fresh grit and a stem advancing the width of a grain would be enough to alter what I was. If the water returned with greater force, I might instead be carried through the lower crack. If the sand dried first, the green loop would brown. My tougher fibers would remain among the ash and older dead material, no more instructive than any other remnant.

The scar above would not be waiting for me. It would already be receiving cloud water on wet mineral and newly exposed joints. Its first life would be too slight for lightning to show. Another spore might lodge there; another might not. My rooting below would not restore the garden that had fallen, and the garden beginning above would not be my replacement. The Notch does not return its arrangements to their proper places.

That is why I would not think of myself as its promise, or even as its survivor. *Survivor* and *debris* are both names for what happens next.

If you found me in a flash of lightning, there would be no way to tell which name would eventually fit. You would see only black grit, a retreating wet margin, and one green loop caught behind a stone.

For now, that would be enough to make me an inhabitant. My whole habitable world would be the damp side of a single node.
