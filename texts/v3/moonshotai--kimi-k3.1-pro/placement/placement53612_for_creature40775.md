At first, I would make the obvious mistake: I would want to be the listener in the open middle.

From there, the place would seem almost available to understanding. I could learn the three reeds by voice—the dry clack, the stumbling gulp, the long intake thinning into a whistle. I could wait for the frogs to shout into the openings between strokes and feel the heavier concussions arrive through the floor. My attention could pass from basin to wall to roof without having to stop and feed anywhere. It would be easy to mistake that freedom for a place in the order of things.

But it would be a visitor’s freedom. I could stand in the center because I had brought my necessities with me and could carry them away again. I would not have to find food in the current, or a seam narrow enough to shelter me, or a surface whose vibrations I could read. My breath would trouble the midges; my weight would press water briefly out of the moss. Then I would leave, and the signs of my presence would be taken up without having been considered. If I remained, I would only become a larger, longer irregularity.

To belong, I would have to become smaller and less well informed.

I think you would find me above one of the warm roots, in the narrow air where the midges rise. Even there, though, my first choice would be too large. I would want to be the column itself.

The column has a station, an approximate shape, and something like a voice. Individuals enter it, fall away, are eaten, or return to the moss, while the column continues. A visitor’s body heat opens it for a moment; then it narrows again. From the center, it appears more continuous than any animal in the chamber. It resembles a self whose substance is constantly being replaced.

But the column has no inside. No midge experiences its outline. It cannot feel itself thinning or know when it has resumed. It is a whole only for someone standing elsewhere.

So let me be one midge.

My first world is the wet filter. I am a pale larva among the moss shoots, working through water divided into films and narrow passages. There is food small enough for me, current enough to carry it, and the close resistance of living surfaces. The pumping reaches me through the substrate as concussion and altered flow. It has no location. It is not the voice of a reed, or one part of a larger mechanism. It is simply a condition to which I respond.

Emergence does not reveal the chamber to me. It exchanges one locality for another. Now there are wings, warm air, the nearness of other wings, and the brief necessities of adult life. I rise, drop away, rise again. I correct my flight when a drop falls too close. The hum is not something I listen to from outside; my own wings are within it, indistinguishable at any distance.

Perhaps a frog takes me. Perhaps condensation holds me for a few seconds too long. Perhaps I mate and, if I am carrying eggs, return to the wet moss and lay them singly in the filter. None of these outcomes would explain my life or make it useful in retrospect. They would simply be the ways it could continue or end.

My life would have a direction even here. Larva, pupa, winged adult; perhaps eggs; then an end. No later midge would be me returned. The recurrence would belong to the place, not to my body. I find something consoling in that distinction. I would not have to persist in order to have been present, nor mistake the continuation of the hum for my own survival. I would be one unrepeatable life within a repeatedly made shape.

So: not the chamber’s awareness, not its memory, not even the enduring column above the root. Just one brief body, feeling warm air and the disturbances passing through it.

Someone standing in the empty middle might hear a single high hum and take it for the voice of the room. I would not hear the Bellows whole. For a little while, I would be audible in it.
