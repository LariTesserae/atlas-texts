At first I would want to be one of the continuing works. The climbing water, perhaps: it seems to have escaped hesitation. Or the knot beneath the reed island, doing one thing exactly and never being asked to explain it.

These would be a traveler’s ambitions. One vacant court crossed on foot, with wet cloth dragging at my knees and the white pavement hurting my eyes, would make them seem ridiculous. I would be glad to reach shade and discover that I was only a person with sweat in my eyelashes and a headache beginning behind one ear.

I would put my hand into the crossing of the two canals. The green water and the clear water would divide around my wrist and close again beyond it. I would take my hand out having learned nothing except that my wrist was wet.

That would help.

For a while I would probably try to preserve evidence of myself. I can imagine breaking a corner from a clay slab and carrying it, not quite admitting that I wanted proof something had happened. Someone might see me do it and think me careless. There would be no cosmic reason they shouldn’t. Later the slab would be whole, and the fragment would still be in my hand, solid and inconveniently heavy.

I would carry it until carrying it became silly. Then I would use it to hold down the corner of a reed mat. It would be good at that. It need not become a relic merely because it had once been part of something else.

The work that would absorb me would be channel-making. I would like its clarity: soften the clay, draw the scraper, clear the cut, draw it again. Within the task there would be no mystery about what came before what. I could pause, correct my grip, begin a fresh line beside a bad one. I could become so interested in the slight change of resistance where the scraper crossed an old finger groove that I forgot to drink.

I would not be especially gifted.

Not beautifully or instructively ungifted, either. My hand would simply get tired. My attention would wander. Most of my channels would remain grooves, though dampness from a sweating wall might occasionally gather in one and flatter me. Sometimes a line would come out so clean that I would sit back and look at it with embarrassing satisfaction. Then someone would pass, and I would spoil the next cut by looking up to see whether they had noticed.

If asked what I was making, I would say, “A channel.”

If the person continued to stand there, I would add, “At present, a groove.”

It would work very well as a groove.

My usual place would be an open cane shelter at the edge of a dense grove, where a narrow canal emerged into one of the broad, empty courts. Close enough to the court that its whiteness filled the shelter; far enough inside the leaves that I did not have to look at it continuously. The heavy white flowers would be somewhere else. Their sweetness would nauseate me, and I would be tired of visitors interpreting this as a preference with an origin.

There would be a bowl whose base was not quite flat, a stone scraper, and three coils of cord, only one of which I would lend without apology. The broken slab-corner would weigh down the mat. There would be a seat I liked, though others might use it, and on finding it occupied I would have to decide whether I wanted company badly enough to sit somewhere else.

The dark, otter-like animal would know my shelter as a promising place for loose ends. I would not credit it with a purpose. I would hide my cord, forget where, and suspect the animal with an injustice that would please no one but might briefly relieve my feelings.

People would know me, if they knew me at all, as someone willing to share fruit and shade, too inclined to describe rain, and unreliable with borrowed adzes unless the lender enjoyed walking. They would not need to know when I had arrived. Eventually I would stop offering that as the most interesting fact about myself.

Some would seek me out. Others would see me at a pool and choose another pool. Both could be right.

I do not think I would lose my memories of elsewhere. That would be too convenient. I would still know that cold can gather in shade, that water elsewhere may roar or chatter or tick irregularly from a roof, that darkness is not merely the white light with something done to it. But these memories would cease to be credentials. They would be useful chiefly for answering questions, or for boring someone who had hoped to fish in silence.

If asked whether rain had a sound, I would say that it had the sounds of what it touched: broad leaves, clay, skin, water, a cane roof already leaking. I might become carried away and describe rain on metal until my listener remembered somewhere else they needed to be.

If the woman at the long water stair asked whether cold water truly hurt the teeth, I would tell her that it could. When she laughed, I would not take that as an invitation to explain winter. Nor would I stay to watch her work.

If you found me at my shelter, I would offer you water in the rocking bowl. I might move the cord so you could sit, or I might be in a mood and pretend not to notice that you were standing. You could ask who had made the distant courts. Depending on the heat, I might speculate about a single act continuing beyond its maker, or I might say only that the court before us was unpleasant to cross.

You could ask who I was.

I would not say that I was a keeper, an interpreter, or a work waiting to become alive. I would not be maintaining anything. The basin would be no more complete because I was there, and no less complete if I walked away.

My presence would matter at the scale where company matters: water offered or withheld, a question answered well, a conversation continued past its welcome, a favorite seat yielded because someone’s shoulders were stiff. Beyond that, my channels would not carry the basin. My knots would not hold islands. The design would have no need of my good intentions.

Behind me, the causeway would be clean. I hope that would eventually stop feeling like erasure. It would simply mean that someone else could cross without stepping in my footprints.

Nothing in the white heights would have moved. My left foot would be asleep. Across the canal, the dark animal would be swimming toward the reeds with my best cord in its mouth.

I would probably let it go. Standing up would have become its own project.
