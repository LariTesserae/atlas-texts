At first I would choose the raptor. It has the obvious freedom: height without hurry, the whole rim apparently available at a glance, no stairs, no fuel debt, no uncle whose badly set gutter must be discussed every spring.

But the clear view is the plateau’s first deception. Cliffs that look near are not near. A seep that shines after fog may fail later. Even the herds, which seem to belong to the open tableland, are followed, anticipated, waited for, and argued over. Nothing here is quite as unattached as distance makes it appear.

So I think I would be in the city. More precisely, I would be beneath one of its roofs.

I would be a cistern.

Not the water itself. Water is too easily mistaken for freedom, and here it is caught, covered, carried, measured, and owed. Nor would I be one of the ancient cisterns in the open court, whose rights and custodians are older than half the surrounding walls. I would be younger and somewhat awkwardly made, built where several crowded households had finally found room between an older roof, a retaining wall, and the back of an animal pen.

My floor would be a slab taken from some earlier construction. One wall would be borrowed rather than newly raised. The mason who cut my inlet would do excellent, nearly invisible work, setting it at an angle that did not grow ice every winter. Whoever mixed my first clay lining would use a little too little clay. My cover would be a fraction heavier than it needed to be, so that lifting it alone was possible but undignified. These things would be my genealogy.

After fog, I would fill so slowly that my existence would seem almost unrelated to weather. A darkening on an outer wall. Beads under an overhang. A brief thread of melt at midday, then nothing. Only toward evening would the drops beneath my cover begin to arrive at intervals a keeper could use.

After summer rain, I would become loud. Gutters would knock and whisper above me; settling basins would cloud; children would run for the flood drains while adults shouted instructions they had all learned before they could properly reach the covers. For an hour, perhaps two, everyone would move as though the city had been designed expressly for haste. Then the gutters would fall silent, and I would be left holding the difference between that hour and the months around it.

People would know me less by appearance than by sound. One child would insist that my drip was lower after rain. Another would maintain that it only seemed lower because the passage outside had become quieter. A keeper would set a vessel down, listen without bothering to argue, and know that the lining had begun to leak on the north side.

Belonging would gather around me without resembling a family tree. Some who slept directly above me would have no right to fill their vessels beneath my cover. Others would sleep three courts away, or follow herds beyond sight of the city for two seasons, and still belong to my water. When my lining cracked, the people summoned would reveal the shape of a household more accurately than any roof could.

I would not make those people kind. That is important. They would argue beside me about who had contributed clay, who had merely carried water, whose range relatives sent fuel but never labor, and whether an old court’s offer of stored grain entitled it to preference in a bad season. Someone wealthy would want my cover secured more strictly. Someone from the upper rooms would point out, with justified bitterness, that without their household’s work the retaining wall below us would already be moving.

I would settle none of this. I would only make their dependence more difficult to deny.

At the first thaw, when the channels were cleaned, I would become part of the recitation. The good inlet would have a name attached to it. So would the thin lining. I would rather like that severity. It means that memory need not flatten everyone into either venerable ancestor or fool. A person could leave behind one well-judged course of stone and one enduring nuisance, and descendants could be grateful without becoming respectful in the wrong places.

Eventually, I would fail a little.

Not dramatically. No court swallowed by water, no heroic collapse. A wet summer would open a joint by grains. For a time, the added dripping might sound like abundance. Then a keeper would hear that the echo no longer agreed with the level. People would come, some promptly, some resentfully. My cover would be set aside. I would be emptied, examined, relined. Someone would discover that an earlier repair had been concealed rather than properly finished, and that discovery would outlive the person responsible.

When I was covered again, my note would be slightly different. Children who thought they knew me would have to learn me once more.

At evening, you could find me there: a darkness under basalt, holding no view of the plateau and very little of the city’s noise. A keeper might lift the cover with a child beside her. The keeper would hear a level; the child, at first, only a note. Above us an animal would shift beneath a floor, and somewhere on a roof a newly split block would wait beside the course it had not yet joined.

I would not be permanent. I would not need to be. I would be a small, repairable hollow in which private thirst became a common obligation; one of the ways a crowded, argumentative city remained answerable to itself.

And if I cracked, they would come.

That is how I would know I was inside.
