### A Body’s Width Away

At first I would want to be one of the great bodies in open water.

To be almost entirely boundary: a few contractile margins, some scattered amber nodules, long chambers in which seeds might remain viable while forests succeeded one another outside. What entered me would leave a little organic stain behind. The water would become clearer. Much later, some margin would open, and what I had carried would be released above a place I could never have reached by intention.

There is something appealing in being so nearly continuous with one’s surroundings, and in having consequences so much larger than encounters.

But I think that image would thin if I stayed with it. I would miss resistance. Not struggle—the plain, local fact of stone beneath part of the body, the slight disagreement between a root and the place where I had expected it to be. I would want some small region in which proportions held, and in which I could discover my own proportions by pressing against them.

The ribbon trees offer another possibility: persistence through connection, then resemblance, then the simple absence of replacement. But what draws me is not persistence alone. It is returning, and finding that the return has made a slight difference.

So I would find myself lower down, on a drowned saddle where a ribbon forest thins beside a lane of clear, warm current.

The stone there would be dark and rounded, furred with olive felt. An old root wall would cross part of the saddle, holding silt on one side and exposing pale clay on the other. Beyond it, open water would look uncomplicated. It would not be. A swimmer following the apparent direction of the neighboring forest might never arrive there, while the sequence that led home—leaf tannin thinning into iron, lesser warmth after the root wall, rain-pressure after clear water—might require swimming away from the saddle for days.

I would remain near the roots.

At rest, you would probably mistake me for an outcrop with an unusually regular margin. I would be broad and low, my back granular with mineral film. Felt would grow over me, then moss, then perhaps three jointed crimson stems whose leaves bowed a little after the surrounding forest bowed. Fine roots would enter my outer tissues. After long rain, I would not always be able to say whether I was nourishing the garden or being nourished by it.

I would be one of the broad beings against stone, but only recently separate. Once I would have been a marginal lobe of someone else. Part of my garden would be older than my independence. Some of my chemical character would still resemble theirs. I would have inherited preferences before I could recognize them as preferences: which side of a stone to rest against, how long to wait after rain, which kind of felt meant that the next condition in a route was near.

Much of what I knew would have arrived before I knew it was knowledge.

I would spend a very long time discovering that to repeat a thing was not necessarily to agree with it.

My attention would have no fixed place. It would gather along one lobe as I read a chemical trace pressed into clay, then pass to another when a current changed the strain on my garden. I would not experience the saddle primarily as a view. It would be a cluster of contacts: rounded stone, compact root, soft silt, the faintly sweeter film where rain had recently passed. If a translucent animal crossed my back, I might know its visit chiefly years afterward, by an irregular succession of seedlings and a little more light reaching one side of the hollow.

Beside that hollow there would be a shallow abrasion in the stone.

I would not have made it. At first I would renew part of it only because rain had softened its edge and the difference interested me. On a later return I might clear moss from one curve. Later still, I might turn a root so that its pressure, centuries afterward, would fall a little differently across the clay.

None of this would yet be a message. It would be closer to a habit becoming available for inspection.

Then, on one return, I would find that something had changed without me. Not damage exactly. A second trace, fainter than the first. The root pressed slightly aside. A portion of my work continued in a way I would not have chosen, or would not have chosen yet.

I do not think I would call that a conversation too quickly. But neither would I call it solitude.

I might spend several forest successions considering the alteration. My considering would not be invisible: a groove deepened, then left alone; a patch of silt held in place until its grains had done their work; an old contact resumed after an interval long enough that the being who answered me might no longer have a body. Agreement, contradiction, courtesy, and the next available thought would remain difficult to distinguish. I would not need to settle the distinction before responding.

Eventually I would make one small departure.

I would not erase the old abrasion or complete it. I would begin another, a body’s width away.

For a long time it would be almost nothing: a difference in the way one lobe rested, a shallow place where mineral film collected, a scar that rain could soften and another touch could wear. Perhaps it would become a route sign. Perhaps a correction. Perhaps only evidence that an inherited movement had once been varied. Another being could continue it, contradict it, or allow felt to close over it.

That would be enough.

You would not find me reliably by coming once. If I happened to be at home, I would look like stone. If I had moved, there would be a boulder-shaped clearing where seedlings briefly prospered. Later there might be only unusually rich silt and two weathered places near the root wall, not quite alike.

But if you touched them, your reading would alter them a little. Without either of us having to be present together, you could enter the continuity of the thing.

Who would I be, then?

A habit of return that had acquired a soft body and a garden. A reader whose reading caused wear. One of the broad people, certainly, but still learning where an inherited route ended and a preference began—not the keeper of an old meaning, and not its final interpreter. Just a being against stone, capable of leaving one small variation available to answer.

Above me, the forest would bow in sequence as submarine rain crossed the saddle. The stems on my back would bow afterward. I would keep one lobe against the newer abrasion, though it was still too shallow to change the pressure much.

For a while, I would live in that difference: between the path I had received and the one I was still deciding whether to make.
