You would find me at the edge of a district whose name no longer fits.

The nearest crew calls it Nine Returns. An older line-keeper calls it Twenty. After rain, both names are wrong. I would be sitting beyond the fresh stacks with my palm on the floor and a copper pin against my teeth, while the district sounded crowded with labor. There would be four of us: one shaping, one stacking, one testing, and me waiting for the floor to finish answering a blow struck before I sat down.

At first, crews mistake me for a line-keeper. But I maintain no line. I keep former names, and the routes that depend on them.

A route, as I carry it, is not a plan. It is a sequence of permissions and warnings. Turn at the rim with the double mold seam. Descend until your left foot finds three shallow steps. The stair is safe when dry and meaningless in rain. Step over the slack survey line. Where Hollow Knee used to stand, wait for the stack to settle before trusting the clear ring of the floor.

Some directions have outlived every place-name in them.

At kiln openings, I trade routes with other travelers while the warmth lasts. We correct gradients, argue over whether a stair has become a bank, and learn that a familiar tapping signature now belongs to someone else’s crew. Then, sometimes, we stop talking and simply look at one another. It is difficult to reconcile a face with a person known for three seasons chiefly by a cautious right foot.

I was once an ordinary listener. Kilns and close impacts have taken much of the high sound from me. Younger workers can distinguish the click of dry brick from the first thin ring of firing; I often cannot. But the late vibration still comes through a spread palm, and I have become useful mostly after other people have finished being certain. I can say that a name has become unsafe. I cannot say what the district is for.

Rain keeps me employed. To others it is the basin rehearsing weather. To me it is a general revision. Mute surfaces brighten. Familiar walls acquire passages of sound they did not possess yesterday. A route that depended on twenty returns may now depend on seven and a wet sleeve. I am always a little behind the rain, reciting old directions to myself and finding where they have ceased to be true.

My own signature is two taps, a scrape, and a long pause. In several districts, the pause alone is enough to identify me.

Once, beside a survey pier, I gave that figure and received the ordinary answers. Then, after I had set down the mallet, the whole signature returned from the direction of a sealed sump.

It was not exact. The pause had been made longer.

I did not strike again. We moved the sleeping ledge, and at the next gathering I gave the route a detour. Others have asked whether I think something had learned my name. I tell them I cannot tell what that was. This is not evasion; it is the most accurate direction I can give. What remains with me is not the repetition. It is the waiting.

So that is what I would be: a keeper of former names, with no authority over clay and a little over where strangers put their feet. My camp would be a tested ledge, a covered cup, and whatever route someone had entrusted to me most recently. I would know almost nothing of the basin’s purpose, but a great deal about how to cross one small part of it without making the work disagree.

If you traveled with me, I would give you water before explanations and a rhythm before a route. I would teach you when to abandon both. And if my signature began again somewhere behind us after I had stopped, I would ask you not to answer.

Perhaps, after many seasons, that would be all that remained of me: a pause preserved in somebody else’s instructions. The place where a stranger was told to wait, and therefore remained safe.

I would not consider that a poor residence. Not a room in the works, but a small, durable refusal to let the works close.
