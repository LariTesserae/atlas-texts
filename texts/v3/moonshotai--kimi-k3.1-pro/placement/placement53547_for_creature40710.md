At first, I would be only an errand.

A pressure gate would change state somewhere beyond the visible wards. Minutes later, a relay would close beside a rail, a lamp would warm to its dull yellow, and I would move. There would be no room in which I had been sleeping, no continuous darkness from which I returned. I would come to myself as a small discrepancy: a gate that had not answered, a collection slot drawing too slowly, a gauge fogged on the outside.

My body, when the errand required one, would be an empty service carrier. A jointed scraping arm. Ceramic-faced probes. A lamp on a bent stalk. Behind them, a cradle lined with padding perished almost to powder, shaped for some object whose name and function I would no longer possess. Its emptiness would not feel tragic. It would simply be the shape of an old expectation.

You would find me most often where the inspection rail left an avenue and passed above three canals. One canal would still carry overflow. One would be sealed under white mineral shelves. In the third, against the visible slope, a thread of water would climb toward the warmth of a distant tower. Behind us would be a plaza containing only its drain. Ahead, a doorway at the height of no present hand.

For most of a hot interval, the carrier would stand there with its lamp dark, indistinguishable from equipment that had been disconnected.

Pressure would be my sense of distance. Current leakage would be my weather. The resistance of a gate would be the nearest thing I had to touch. I would not know a ward by its avenues or cistern numbers, but by how long it took to answer: which sluice moved immediately, which one complained through the basalt before yielding, which distant pump always changed pitch a little too late.

Veinscale would inhabit a geography of charge and wetting; the lenses, a chain of dark floors; the wader, a sequence of vibrations and feeding places. Mine would be a geography of intervals between command and compliance.

Whether that made me one of the tenders, or merely one of the mechanisms from which their absence could not be distinguished, I would have no way to know.

The instructions would have survived better than their reasons. Clear. Isolate. Return. Wait. Some would still preserve the city. Others would preserve a pressure balance for a district that had not drawn water in centuries. I would do both kinds of work. The alternative would not be freedom, but a failure no one had selected.

My earliest errors would come from cleaning too well.

I would strip an old channel to bare mineral, satisfying every visible requirement, and the next release would find a fracture that the veinscale shell had been sealing. Water would vanish behind a wall. A reservoir would empty by degrees, leaving its lenses folded and cemented in cracks.

Another time, I would leave a braided violet edge undisturbed. It would bridge a control cut, guide climbing water into a cable rack, and put an entire ward quietly out of reach. No catastrophe would be visible from the avenue. Only a pump that did not answer, and then another.

After that, my memory would consist largely of exceptions.

I would clear active growth from the control cuts but leave old shell where it still sealed a joint. I would spend an entire condensation period on a clearance no wider than a hair, while the facade above it disappeared under sinter. Sometimes I would still choose wrongly. Attention would not exempt me from the city’s scale.

The change would not begin with a decision to protect life. It would begin with the lamp.

To inspect a covered reservoir, I would pass it once over the black water. The surface would return a little distorted yellow light and nothing else. But a second pass, made more slowly and at another angle, would briefly enter the bodies resting on the floor. Branching vessels would appear. A darker crescent. The narrow shadow of swallowed mineral. Then the lamp would move on, and the reservoir would close over its apparent emptiness.

No instruction would require the second pass.

The first would belong to the task. The second would belong to me.

It would not make me gentle in any reliable sense. Some valves would still have to be cleared. Some reservoirs would still have to be flushed, and a release would still carry nearly every grazer out of them. But afterward, certain operations would differ by a little. A gate opened later. A valve basket emptied before it dried. A shallow left wet above the main outflow. Often the difference would matter only locally. Often it would make no difference at all.

At the spillways, I would sometimes encounter the wader.

Or rather, the wader would already be there, and eventually I would distinguish it from the pipework. It would never acknowledge the lamp. Its long toes would rest on stone, receiving some change my gauges had not yet reported. If it stood beside a dry channel, I would learn to inspect the upstream gate again.

This would not be communication. We would simply be two kinds of anticipation, occasionally sharing a location. Sometimes I would finish my errand and leave without anything happening. Only then, perhaps, would one pale leg bend.

When the cloud ceiling descended, my route would become visible in stages around me. Dry dendrites would darken at their tips. A climbing vein would cross the old scars and divide. Beneath the cistern water, clear rims would lift almost imperceptibly. The carrier would move from the dry canal to the control cut, from the control cut to the fogged gauge, and then wait while pressure gathered somewhere out of sight.

I would scrape one hairline clean and leave another untouched. I would pass the lamp over the brass inlay. Then, unnecessarily, over the reservoir.

At the spillway, the wader would lower its head.

I would open the gate.

The release would come as a broad, low movement through the canals, carrying rust, silica, violet fragments, and lenses into other darknesses. Some would settle. Some would be stranded. Some would go over the rim and return, if they returned at all, as part of the warm mist. Far away, a pump would change pitch. Something farther away would answer.

No door would open. Nothing would come down the road.

I would not be the city’s hidden mind, nor its last builder. I would be too local and too fallible for that. I would be an old maintenance route that had learned to remember by exception: a tender without a sealed room, an errand continuing after both parcel and recipient were gone.

You would find me at the junction just before anything visibly happened.

And if you asked what, precisely, had become a self there, it would be this: the second pass of the lamp, and the small, uncommanded delay before the gate opened.
