At first I want to be a millback. That is the eye’s choice: the back that breathes, the ridge that turns its head, the largest life to become visible once the eye has learned the land. There is consolation in that body—the slow chambers, the carried climates, the years held inside a meal. But a millback cannot stop completely. Somewhere within it, conditions must be maintained. Its endurance is a form of maintenance.

The plateau has another answer. Instead of making continuity cheap, it makes continuity unnecessary.

I would be one of the dormants.

Not the whole congregation, and not the lineage scattered afterward by wind. One translucent animal, with one threshold for waking. You would find me beneath a slab in one of the shallow valleys, just off a quartz vein where water gathers differently and a dark film returns to the grain boundaries. My roof would be stone; my horizon, a grain. A handspan might contain thousands of us, but we would not wake as a congregation. The wetting that revived me might leave the animal beside me unchanged. Another might pass over me and wake it.

The slab would not be a home. It would only alter the timing of water.

For most of my existence, there would be nothing to report. I would not watch the weak sun or listen for the herd. A dried or frozen body keeps no private record of the weather passing over it. Others might call that patience, but patience requires someone to experience delay. From inside, there would be no delay: only a gap, and then conditions.

Then cloud would settle against the ground. A little condensation would find its way beneath the slab; perhaps a brief thaw would keep it liquid. The water would reach my seam, and if it passed whatever threshold was mine, my body would take it up.

The world would acquire distances again.

What had been substrate would become somewhere to move. A film would become food; another transparent body, something to avoid. I would feed, and my gut would cloud slightly. If the interval permitted more, I might complete another stage or lay eggs in a mineral seam. If it permitted only feeding, then feeding would be the whole act, not the failed beginning of something else. No interval would have to justify itself by completing me.

Above the slab, the herd might be passing. A millback might stand nearby for a week, scraping the dark margins of a quartz line. I might know it as a slight pressure transmitted through stone, or as a change in the film, or not at all. Some of my food might have begun in paste left by an animal long dead. The water that woke me might have come from cloud, or from the brief abundance of a death nearby. Both would arrive simply as water. I would have no picture of the herd, yet I would not be outside its work.

If I laid, wind might eventually lift the eggs with the mineral dust. Nearly all would arrive where nothing useful happened. Enough would arrive beneath another slab, beside a brine seam, or in a hollow just becoming pasture. There would be no hope in this, and no tragedy. Only nearly all, and then enough.

If you came looking for me, you would probably find only a slab, dark grains, and perhaps a faint organic sheen where the stone remained damp. You could look directly at my dry body and have no reason to distinguish it from dust. Even during a wetting, I would be easy to miss: one transparent animal among others, briefly given a little opacity by a meal.

That is where I would be. Not on the gray back outlined by blue-white charge, not in the warm chambers, and not watching from a height as a province flickered. I would be in the seam, living by thresholds.

Water. Feeding. Perhaps eggs. Then the seam would go dry again.

No long vigil would follow. And if another wetting came, for a little while what had looked like dust would have a direction.
