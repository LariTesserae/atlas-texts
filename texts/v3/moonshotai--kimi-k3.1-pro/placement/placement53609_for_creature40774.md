At first I would want to be the water.

It seems to go everywhere: entering as weather, becoming cargo, reservoir, structural load; touching every district without belonging to one. But water here has right of way, not freedom. It does not decide where to go. The city’s whole existence is an arrangement of small, exact concessions around that fact. To be water would be to matter enormously and to intend nothing.

A worker, then. I can imagine being one of those flattened mechanisms clamped to ceramic, knowing the pocket only through contact: the drag of a film, pressure through a foot, a pump’s faint regularity arriving through stone. There would be a kind of peace in such local knowledge. No need to picture the whole city. No need to ask whether the lane beneath me had existed yesterday. Only the next contact, the next resistance, the load that could still be released.

But what draws me most is not the city’s ability to make such bodies. It is the city’s economy in making them—and its refusal to let any body, however useful, become more important than the conditions under which another body might later be made.

So I think I would be a contingency with an occasional body.

Not a worker sleeping in a sealed box. Not a small mind hidden in a foundation, waiting to be awakened. Those would imply too much continuity. I would be something less intact: a few alternatives preserved where a district met stone, an order of deposition that could be interrupted, permission to make a tool whose final shape had not yet been chosen. You could point to the foundation that contained me, but not to me.

Most thaws would pass without my becoming anything.

Then, one morning, ice would lift a floor in a way the old instructions did not quite accommodate. A brace could still be laid across the shifted stone. The lane could be made whole again. But the brace would occupy a volume the next freeze would need, and restoring the old route would transmit new pressure toward the seam. Nothing in the previous pattern would be wrong except its conclusion.

That might be when I was given a body.

Nothing impressive. A low, jointed strip, perhaps, with six contacts and a spine able to stiffen just enough to support one short act of deposition. Two surfaces that could alter their adhesion. No reservoir I could not release. No tool permanently fixed to me. I would have no memory of earlier bodies and no map of the pocket. I would know the changed drag beneath one foot, the slight give in the floor, the moment when the stored sequence and the actual stone ceased to agree.

There would be no recognition, no dilemma. Only a change in what could happen next.

Instead of completing the brace, I might lay down a brief water-loving track toward an empty repair yard. Not a new district. Not even a permanent diversion. Just enough of a surface that the next arrival of water could be persuaded away from the lifted floor. Then the work would stop. The visible damage would remain visible. To anyone counting accomplishments, I would have done very little.

But there would still be a gap into which ice could expand. There would still be a route by which water could be admitted without being forced. The outer seam would not have been purchased with an invisible closure elsewhere.

By sunset I might be frozen where I stood, or taken apart, or simply left while another route was made around me. It would not matter much which. What returned to the foundations would not be my story. At most, a few conditions would have altered: this load no longer trusted; this interval to remain unfilled; this order of making still possible. The next contingency, if one arose, would not remember me. It would not have to resemble me.

That feels closer to a kind of existence I could honestly claim than an immortal worker or a concealed civic mind. My continuity would be neither a thread nor a succession of copies. It would consist in not having exhausted the world around me. I would not survive, but something would remain possible.

Where would you find me?

On most days, nowhere. Not in the pressure towers, not beneath the quartz vein, not waiting in the bare central court. A census of bodies would leave me out. A census of tools would leave me out. Only a census willing to count unused latitude—the difference between what the city could do and what it had already committed itself to doing—might include me once.

And when the annual drop gathered at the outer crack, held, and fell, its minute chemical alteration would not carry me downhill. It would say only that the fracture was open, the reservoir intact, the cold within tolerance, the city still present.

My absence would be part of that message: not a name, not a shape, only a tool not preserved, a repair not completed, a little of the city left unspent.
