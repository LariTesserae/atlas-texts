### The Living Margin

I would be below the waterline, where the Low Engine stops being a landscape.

Not on the causeway. Not in one of the broad, old cushions that might, under a lamp, be mistaken for a low growth of moss. I would be in a joint beneath a ceramic saddle at the edge of a condenser field, where a condensate return crosses above a shallow drain. An old fitting would leak warmly into one side of the joint. Cold standing water would reach the other. Between them would be a zone no visitor could identify by sight: not simply warm, not simply cold, not shelter in any general sense, but a sufficiently enduring difference.

That is where I would live.

The nearest lamp would belong to a process threshold. Coming on, it would illuminate the curved side of the conduit and perhaps a hand’s breadth of wet paving. It would not reach me. Its coming and going would matter only if the same operational change altered the leakage or the drain.

Seen from above, if anyone troubled to kneel in the black water and look, I would be an irregular grey-green seam partly glazed brown with oxides. Most of that seam would not be living. It would be compacted tissue from earlier margins, trapped ceramic dust, metals bound and abandoned in place. The active part would be much thinner: a discontinuous edging, in some places no wider than a filament cluster, following the side of the joint where the two waters had not yet destroyed each other’s difference.

When I say *I*, I would have to mean that edging.

Not the whole mineralized body. Not necessarily every separated fragment. Abrasion might leave two living margins with dead material between them; later growth might join them again. Nothing would need to decide whether one inhabitant had become two, or two had resumed being one. The word would be a loose handle held up to something whose continuity lay less in an outline than in repeated conditions.

The Low Engine’s vastness would not enter my world. There would be no tens of kilometres, no distant lamps, no horizon dissolving into fog. There would not even be darkness in the way a visitor experiences it, because I would have no sight with which to be deprived of light. The roar would reach me indirectly: as vibration transmitted through the saddle, as sediment driven harder across my surfaces, as a slight shifting of the warm leak within its fracture.

My immediate world would be the force of water, its temperature, what it carried dissolved in it, and whether the seam still held.

During hard flow, I would be narrow.

The grey surfaces would close. Filaments would pack together until the living margin became part of a set of dark cords following the joint. Water would pass over me with little to catch. Fine artificial sediment would rattle and grind overhead. There would be no virtue in this endurance and no intention behind the form. It would simply be the configuration in which the least of me was offered to the current.

For long periods, nothing about me would distinguish persistence from residue.

Then some phase change would propagate through the works. Layers of noise would fall away above the pans. Water elsewhere might smooth into black glass, and fog might stand in faint walls along the geometry of the sound. None of that would be available to me as spectacle. What would reach the joint is smaller: a slackening of shear, a gentler passage of cold water over warm leakage, a current no longer packing every surface shut.

I would open.

Not visibly transform the seam. Not bloom in any sense that would reward a lamp. Minute grey surfaces would face the water. Dissolved metals would pass into the slow chemistry of growth. A little tissue might be added where, before, there had only been the hard outline of survival.

Spores would leave me without ceremony.

They would enter water already carrying ceramic dust and oxides, water whose next intake, reversal, or change in temperature I could not anticipate. Nearly all would come to nothing. That would not be an unusually cruel event in an otherwise generous cycle; it would be part of the ordinary scale of reproduction here.

Perhaps one would lodge in some distant fracture where another old leak crossed another cold drain. If it persisted, there would be no connecting trail between us, no gradual green line across the paving to mark descent. The Low Engine would simply have repeated, in two widely separated places, conditions narrow enough for life to recur. Whether that distant film would be *me* would be a poorer question than whether it had received anything from me besides the possibility of beginning again.

Then pressure would return.

On the fluted spillways, water might run visibly uphill in orderly pulses. In the joint, there would be no visible paradox, only force. The current would harden. The grey surfaces would close. Sediment would resume its passage. I would contract into the cords, and the old stain and the living margin would once more be almost indistinguishable.

Over years, the leakage might shift by less than a visitor could conveniently measure. Deposits would narrow one path through the composite and open another. On one side of the seam, tissue would become inactive and bind more mineral into the cushion. On the other, a film might persist and thicken slightly. Returning after a long absence, someone might conclude that I had moved.

Nothing would have travelled. Life would merely have continued at one edge and become record at the other.

That record would not become soil. It would not deepen the joint into a better habitat or prepare a place for some second inhabitant. My dead material would eventually be available to the same abrasion and transport as everything else. A maintenance carriage could lay a fresh grey repair across the whole seam and register no ecological loss, no fault, no interruption of a dependency. The pumps would not falter. The black water would not become blacker.

And yet I would not be quite the same kind of thing as the leakage or the pumps.

The leakage does not benefit from warm water. The pumps do not flourish when pressure is favorable to them. A turbine can turn for another age without the turning being good for the turbine. But the conditions in the joint could, accidentally, be good for me. They could permit not merely continued existence but opening, growth, and the release of descendants.

That would be the whole of my distinction. Not independence. Not a future wilderness. Not a hidden purpose in the works.

I would be the small living margin where the Low Engine’s operation could amount, without ever intending it, to flourishing.

You would find me there, if you found me at all: not at the centre of the region, and not at the scale of its immense waters, but inside a difference maintained in one seam. During the quieter phase, a little new grey surface might face the current. After the pumps returned, there would be only dark cords, bound oxides, and what looked like an old repair.

Both appearances would belong to the same life. Neither would be its whole body.

I would not be waiting for the Engine to stop, or for the water to clear, or for the seam to become soil. I would be alive for as long as warm leakage and cold drain water continued to meet without quite canceling the interval between them—a thin, unregistered margin of growth within a process that would never notice what it had made possible.
