I would begin by wanting to be the pallid moth, which is to say I would begin by being a little vain.

To enter by a route no one had traced; to set the stairs murmuring; to touch a food-vine flower and leave behind seed worth marking and a child prepared to insist, fifty years later, that my wings had been closed, not open. It would be a life without hot-work shifts or inspection knocks. No one would ask a moth to repack a valve, or tell it that its beloved balcony had to be stripped. But there is something too easy in being rare. The moth is remembered partly because it does not stay long enough to become difficult.

Water would be the next temptation: not the brief fall into the eastern lane, which is too much like festival weather, but a bead forming on an old cold pipe. To be breath, leaf, tea, scale, damp on a sleeper’s cuff; to pass through every household without having to belong to one. Yet in Sere water has no private career. It is always entering a use or leaving one, being waited for, covered, returned. Even its escape is somebody’s work. I would not so much travel through the town as be continually handed on.

What I keep returning to is smaller and less dignified.

I think I would be the draw.

Not a spirit in the basalt, nor an intention in the heat. Certainly not Sere becoming conscious and making its first, extremely unimpressive attempt at speech. I would be only the slight discrepancy between the direction a surface offers and the direction water eventually takes. A tendency with no explanation attached. If I had a body, it would be the interval in which a bead seems to have more than one possible future—though “choice” would already be far too grand a word.

My visible life would be mostly secondhand. A builder would add a thin shim beneath a gutter and step back, dissatisfied with a line that was finally right. A suspended root would thicken along a damp diagonal. White mineral fans would record routes no longer wet. In a ledger, someone would write *bead departed east*, then, after a space, *toward seam*, because the first statement was true and insufficient. I would exist in those corrections. I would be more substantial in what people did about me than in anything they could point to while I was happening.

I would be useless in an emergency. I could not turn a flywheel, refill a cistern, or bring back the damp where a dry patch had begun to worry someone. I would not even be reliably kind. I might carry moisture toward a fern or away from a catchment; I might wet a stair; I might make a careful person doubt a good instrument. Given an afternoon, my entire achievement could be one drop arriving a handspan from where common sense had expected it.

And yet, over years, I would acquire a kind of architecture. Not mine, exactly. The town’s concession to me.

The engineers could continue dividing me among rock, heat, and water. The gardeners could consult older evidence: roots, deposits, the direction a damp bed had migrated before anyone living had learned its name. Children would balance tiles on slag and try to catch me. Most would grow bored before the experiment became interesting. That seems appropriate. I would be best observed by people who had brought other work to do.

Occasionally someone would say that a plan had followed the draw, meaning that it had reached its destination by inconveniencing everyone in an unexpected order. I would not want to be made into a lesson about persistence. Persistence is too noble. Sometimes sideways is only sideways. Sometimes it leaves a step slick.

Where would you find me?

Near the east return, on a ledge beneath a retired condenser plate now used as roofing. One old fin still gathers dew. A child has left a fragment of pale tile there, propped on two crumbs of slag, its glaze crossed with pencil marks from earlier trials. The child is gone. The lamp at the crossing gives the tile only enough light to make the first bead visible if you already know where to look.

You would need most of an afternoon, and something else to occupy your hands.

The bead would swell. The visible slope would offer it a clean departure. Instead, its lower edge would darken the tile by almost nothing, then almost nothing again, tending toward the hairline seam. There would be no single instant you could point to and say: *there, that was when it disobeyed.* Later, you might wonder whether impatience had altered your eyes before the water altered its path.

At last it would fall into the crushed slag and vanish. Gravity would have the visible last word.

The people would remain the part of Sere that notices. I would be one of the small things that kept their noticing from becoming mastery: too weak to command attention, too persistent to be corrected away. Not a will. Not a soul. Just a slight wrongness that, after enough generations, had earned a place in the plan—and a little blank space after it, in case the next drop disagreed.
