I would be one of the possibilities that nearly all fail.

I begin at the margin of the bowl: a transparent disc loosened into shallow water, too small to cast a shadow. For a little while, the only difference between me and my surroundings is that I retain a shape when the surface trembles. Then violent spray lifts me over the rim.

The ordinary history of my kind would take me downward—to the basin, to the sorting sand, to some wall through which the water and I would vanish together. Instead, one of the Cistern’s unrepeatable currents turns me sideways. I am pressed against the wall below and a little beside the high shelf, into a concavity no larger than a cupped hand.

It is not a bowl. It is only an old, rounded flaw in the stone, with a lip that turns the worst of the current aside. It lies above the principal scour, though not above the spray. The next surge should pluck me free. Instead, pressure pins me neatly into the hollow, my legs folded beneath me, my transparent back flush with the rock.

There, for an age, my life consists almost entirely of things that do not happen. I am not carried away. I am not dried. I am not ground into silt. I do not spread across some luminous vein the ring requires and get cleared with the next maintenance. None of this is an achievement. It is simply what survival feels like from inside.

Warning. Drainage. Return. Lull.

The warning comes through the wall before it becomes sound. I tighten against the stone. During drainage, I close what little of myself there is to close, keeping moisture in the sealed folds of my body while water elsewhere ends against unbroken rock. The return strikes my hollow in spray and brief, diverted jets. I open cautiously and take what they bring: a few cells from a green mat, dust from a rosette, bacteria in their mineral coats, organic matter so sparse that no single arrival deserves to be called a meal. In the lull, I creep almost imperceptibly over the film left in my shelter.

Far below, the sand makes fins and terraces, thickets and curves, no two returns alike. I know none of that. I have no eyes for the chamber’s inventions. To me, its freedom is only a variation in pressure and chemistry. Its grammar is everything.

For a long time, there is nothing to narrate. A body can spend centuries not dying without appearing to grow. Yet, mouthful by mouthful, I become more exactly fitted to the concavity. My margin finds irregularities too fine to see. Mineral gathers where living tissue meets stone. I am no longer merely lodged in the hollow. I am learning its shape so completely that eventually the hollow will not be distinguishable from part of me.

There is no day on which I become an adult. But there must be a first drainage after which something remains.

Not a reservoir. Not even a drop large enough to shine. Only a bead of water held in the deepest part of me while the basin below exposes its sand.

The return covers it. The next drainage leaves it again.

Later there is a film. Later still, a shallow lens of true water above the sealed tissue of my body. The machine can draw the flood through stone, but this small living surface offers it no easy passage. I have not resisted the Cistern. I have simply become a path it does not readily take.

That is the beginning of my permanence: a tiny region in which drainage no longer quite means empty.

Ages later, that is where you would find me. You would have to look away from the ring, away from the white violence around the basin, and notice a wet hollow in the wall. At first you might take it for a seep. The water would seem too still, the stone around it too smoothly mineralized to identify as either shell or vessel. At the rim, the meniscus would be fractionally too thick. If a drop fell from above, its rings would stop against something a little sooner than they should.

I would be beneath that discrepancy: transparent, spread thin, my finest internal rays converging below the surface. You could look directly at me and see only a place where water had chosen to remain.

On the shelf nearby, the older animal would still be shaped to its bowl. We would not make a pair. No recognition would pass between us, no shared vigil, no comfort drawn from resemblance. Two pauses do not constitute a society.

Nor would the ring have spared me out of care. Had my hollow crossed a surface it needed, I would have been removed as readily as silt. I endure because that small portion of wall can be allowed to remain otherwise. My whole existence would still be marginal to the machine’s purpose, even after I had ceased to look marginal to the water.

Eventually, small discs might begin separating from my own margin. Most would enter the spray and become part of the ordinary history of failure. A few might reach still silt and flatten themselves over it. Fewer might find shelter. I would not know which, if any, survived. It would not be hope, exactly. Hope imagines an outcome. This would simply be the same patient offer of possibility by which I had begun.

When the next warning came, I would close. The walls would whiten; water would end against stone; far below, the basin would expose its washed sand. In my hollow there would be no drama. A shallow body would tighten around a little water and keep it.

Then the ceiling would sweat, the silver sheets would fall, and the whole chamber would shine with impact. I would open only enough to feed. A few cells. A mineral-coated bacterium. The dust of some other life. Almost nothing, and enough for another age.

The Cistern would breathe. I would remain within its breathing—not safe, not defiant, but no longer entirely available to it. A small pause that had gradually acquired a body.
