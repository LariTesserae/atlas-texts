I would not be out on the rim, where starlight might let me mistake the island for a view. I would be inside the nesting slope, in a darkness with a temperature and a smell.

At first I would think I was the chick, because hunger would seem to be the center of everything. My world would be a chamber scarcely wider than a sleeping body, its walls giving off the odors of damp earth, old feathers, and fish. The entrance would not be something I saw so much as a small alteration in the air. Beyond it, the island would be almost entirely sound: the labor of wings, the crash of birds into branches, feet scrambling, crabs ticking over litter, and the burrows around me answering one another until there was no silence between their voices.

A parent’s return would arrive out of order. First the whistling descent somewhere overhead; then the clumsy, astonishing collision with the forest; then grit whispering down the tunnel as a body found the passage. Only afterward would come the meal. With it would come the sea—fish and squid, salt and warmth, nourishment gathered over distances I could not imagine. I would have no reason to believe in the open ocean. The whole truth would be walls, hunger, vibration, arrival.

Then, one season, the chick would go.

The chamber would keep its warmth for a little while. A loose feather would stir when air moved through the entrance. Nothing would come back down the tunnel.

And I would still be there.

Only then would I understand that I was not the bird, nor even the bird’s waiting. I was the burrow: the hollow that had made that brief life on land possible.

From outside, I might have looked simple enough—an entrance, a passage, a nesting chamber. From within, there would be no such clean plan. Part of me would follow an old weakness in the reef; part would have been scraped out by birds whose names and seasons were gone. One turn would avoid a mass of coral too hard to move. Another would have been widened by generations of bodies pushing against the same wall. A crab would have opened a narrow side passage. Somewhere overhead, a fig root would have entered a crack and thickened, holding the roof together even as it slowly pried the roof apart.

I would not be empty, exactly. I would be an absence maintained by contact.

My entrance would become smooth at the lip. My floor would gather shell grit, guano, soil, and the delicate remains of other occupancies. Rain would not usually flood me, but it would change me: a darker smell rising from the walls, a trickle finding an old fissure, a few grains loosening from the ceiling. Each returning bird would bring something of elsewhere into me. Some of it would feed a chick. Some would fall, decay, and enter the ground. Some would be dragged away by crabs. Nothing would remain simply what it had been when it arrived.

Nor would I always be a nursery. A roof might fail. An egg might be taken. A chick might die in the chamber that had sheltered it, and then other lives would come to that small abundance. I would have no way to separate refuge from danger, or decay from preparation. The same crumb of matter could be loss to one resident and beginning to another.

My memory would not be made of images. It would be made of alterations in fit and pressure: a passage worn wider, a root newly cold against the earth, the slight give of a wall after rain, the vibration of feet recognizing a turn they had never needed to see. Years would pass as differences in the way the island touched me.

Eventually, perhaps, the root would win. Or a wet season would loosen too much of the roof. The passage would narrow under fallen earth, and the chamber would become a shallow pit open to the canopy. Leaves would collect where a chick once slept. Ferns would take the damp sides. A seed dropped by a bat might catch in the softened litter. Crabs would pass through what remained of my deeper turns.

There would be no reason to insist on remaining myself.

For a while, though, I would be that particular darkness in the nesting slope: not quite creature, not quite stone, not simply house. I would be a small habitation with a history, shaped by reef and rain, by feet and roots and hunger. And whenever a shearwater came crashing out of the night and found its way underground, I would feel the island’s noise narrow to one living body entering a space that no single life had made.

I would be the burrow—the shape shelter takes when nothing makes it alone.
