You would find me where the common hall’s shade thins into open water, though at first you might not distinguish me from the stones.

I would be settled against the lowest course of masonry, dense-bodied and still, with a working loop around one wrist and the weighted cords lying beside me in a shallow recess. Now and then my hands would move. The rest of me would appear to be doing nothing. Beneath the hall, the torn-finned char would shift in its shadow, and the inlet’s roar would come up through the paving and into my bones.

I would be old enough that the other Senni had stopped asking me to help haul terrace stone, but not so old that I failed to notice the tact with which they stopped. My legs would still be strong. It would be my hands that had become most useful: narrow, careful, able to find the difference between an intentional interval and a place where fiber had begun to wear thin.

People might say that I mended agreements.

I would correct them. I mended cords. Agreements were made by people who were still capable of disappointing one another.

No one would have appointed me to the work. I would simply have become one of those sent for when an old sequence defeated younger fingers, or when replacing a frayed length threatened to erase the very thing everyone was trying to preserve. I would enjoy being sent for. I would pretend not to. For that reason, among others, no sensible household would allow me to become the only reader.

There would always be someone else present, and often a child.

The child would usually be bored.

One cord, in particular, would occupy many afternoons. It would concern a length of lane beside the lower work yard. Its oldest knots assigned the clearing of debris to a household whose sleeping places had since moved two terraces away. A later sequence made an exception for spawning fish. Beyond that came an alteration tied after one household had left the spawning cord up long after the eggs were gone, hoping to avoid its share of the clearing. At the very end would be a clumsier addition: mine, made after a necessary wall repair had been postponed so often that the fish benefited and the wall did not.

Two elders could pass that cord through their hands and emerge with different obligations.

My task would not be to decide which of them was right. It would be to keep either of them from quietly removing the evidence that things had once been otherwise.

This would be harder than it sounds. Old fiber softens. Knots fur over with growth. A weight cracks and must be replaced without losing the tension by which a sequence is read. Sometimes keeping a cord would mean building its pattern again, interval by interval, in new material while three people argued over what my fingers had found. A cord might outlive its first substance and still remain, in the Senni sense, the same cord.

I would feel a constant temptation to make it neat.

Neatness is persuasive. An obsolete sequence looks, to an impatient hand, like so much useless fiber. But I would have seen enough disputes return under new household names to know that an old mistake can be more useful than a clean conclusion. So I would add, and mend, and leave the earlier trouble attached.

My patience would be local. I could wait twelve winters to discover whether loomroot intended to bear a wall or lift it, and still become unreasonable over the condition in which someone returned a borrowed awl. The awl would have been returned. That would not be the point.

By late afternoon, though, you would more likely find me beyond the last work yard, at the edge of the newest district.

One rectangle there would be mine to visit, though not mine to possess. Its first pale cord had been laid before my birth. My own name occurred only in an alteration, and even that alteration had been amended. Loomroot had thickened along one side of the intended room. Along another, it had sent a runner beneath the future floor and then apparently lost interest. The third side remained mostly open leaves and daylit water. The fourth was still an understanding between several households, some of which no longer agreed about what the understanding had been.

I would settle beside a stone weight and take up my knife.

There would be plenty I could do. That would be the danger.

I could cut the runner back hard and give the rectangle a more convincing shape. I could clear the bottom growth until, from above, it looked almost like a room. I could make something that a young swimmer might point to and call finished. Instead I would clear a little silt, test the weight, prune one shoot and leave another. A crayfish would emerge from beneath the stone holding something fibrous. We would regard each other. I would make the small clicking sound that, in me, meant roughly the same thing as a sigh.

When I was young, I had loved a clean line. Age would not have cured me of that. Only helping to prop up the consequences of other people’s clean lines—and watching others help with mine—would have made the love less trustworthy.

So I would leave open water where open water seemed necessary. I would leave a shelter untended. I would leave one side of the room difficult to explain.

If the person who first slept there ever learned my name, it would probably be because they disagreed with something I had done. They might retie my principal knot. They might discover that the root I had trusted was preparing, slowly and without malice, to move a stone. They might decide that the room should become something else entirely.

I would hope my work gave them no trouble in doing so.

At dusk, the char drumming would begin beneath the hall and travel through the terraces until it seemed to come from the stones around me. I would tether the knife, gather the spare fiber, and set the pale cord back under its weight. Before leaving, I would check the end once more with my fingertips.

There would be a little unused length there—not much, only enough for another hand to begin where my knowledge stopped.
