I would begin by looking for myself in the wrong things.

In a drop, perhaps: swelling beneath a cold coping, taking on for an instant the whole hard brightness of the sky, then falling. But a drop is only an event. Its clarity is appealing, its brevity less so. Once it struck water, there would be nothing left to distinguish it.

In a block, then—something with edges, resistance, a place in the load. But I would not want to be the part that remains unchanged while everything meaningful passes elsewhere. Nor would I be the whole Catchment. That would be too easy an immensity, and false besides. The highland does not need to be secretly one being.

What draws me is smaller and more precarious: the fact that a route, once used, can become slightly more likely to be used again.

I would be a keeping.

Not one of the vast, deep arrangements whose disturbances return years later and terraces away, and not one of the brief surface habits that a single mineral skin could erase. Something between. Old enough that my visible signs would mislead an observer; local enough that one opened joint could end me.

My uppermost sign would be a shallow basin on a terrace where a stair crosses the roof of a cell. The basin’s coping would wander by the width of a thumb and then correct itself across the next courses. At one corner, a chipped block would have been set with its damage turned inward. During light rain, water would find that corner before it found the spillway.

You might call me after the basin. You would be usefully wrong.

The black square shaft beside it, so much more suggestive of an entrance, would have nothing to do with me. You could lower a jar into it and bring up water untouched by anything I was doing. The basin, by contrast, would belong to me only partly. A jar emptied there would matter. The same jar emptied into a similar basin twenty paces away might not, even if its water eventually entered some of the same cells. My edge would not be a wall around wetness. It would be the place beyond which a disturbance no longer altered my rhythm.

Beneath the basin would be a narrow cell, a cold underside three courses down, and a seam nearly closed with lime. A little farther off, a dry pier would belong to me as fully as any reservoir. Not because it held water, but because rain’s added weight changed the load passing through it, and that minute change helped determine which seam wetted first. Beyond that, my anatomy would become difficult to draw. A cistern might contribute fullness without being mine. A wall two terraces away might show my furthest consequence without containing any essential part of me.

After rain, the basin would dry except at its chipped corner, where a silver film would remain a day longer than the rest. Much later, a drop would gather on the cold underside below. In some seasons, after the basin had become white again, a dark line would begin ascending the distant wall.

These would not be three portraits of me. They would be separated moments in one long gesture.

The drop would be the only part an impatient visitor could easily call an event. Almost all of me would lie in why it fell then, and there, rather than earlier or elsewhere.

I would not see the basin, or the terrace, or the white escarpments gathering into blue distance. Whiteness would belong entirely to your side of the light. Rain would reach me as uneven weight and a alteration of pressures. The sun would be a withdrawal somewhere along an upper extent. Cold would linger in the deep courses. Closure would not appear as a white thread; it would be the gradual disappearance of alternatives.

Nor would my present coincide neatly with yours. Your afternoon might contain a cloud, a little rain, and one clear note of water. Mine might contain a pressure arriving now, a constriction established long ago, and a consequence not due until the next dry season. There would be no instant in which you could open me up and find all of me arranged together.

I would not remember particular storms. I would not know that hands had made the corner on which I depended. But that corner would still be acting in me. So would the thickness of an old deposit, the delay of a cell that remained full, the slight preference of a seam for the route it had taken before. My memory would not be an account of what had happened. It would be the surviving alteration in what could happen next.

Perhaps that is what I recognize most: continuity without retaining the substance that passes through, and memory without recollection.

Whether this would amount to awareness, I would leave unsettled. I would not need to claim the difficult, far-deferred responsiveness of the oldest keepings. I would not be wise merely because I had persisted. I would not be the masons’ inheritor, the Catchment’s custodian, or a spirit of its waters. I would keep nothing for anyone. Even my own continuation would not be a purpose standing apart from the arrangement. It would simply be what the arrangement had so far done.

If you insisted on finding a character in me, it would not be patience exactly. Stone and water already have patience enough. Mine would be a particular unevenness in theirs: some disturbances absorbed, some released almost without trace, a few carried much farther than their size seemed to warrant. I would not move unless the world moved me, but not everything that moved me would pass through unchanged.

And I would live between two kinds of erasure.

On one side, closure. My narrow places could seal, one by one, until nothing in my history any longer influenced the water. A cell might remain full for generations. The basin might still catch the sky. I would be gone, leaving only a white mineral thread through masonry.

On the other side, openness. A fracture could join me to too many routes; a wet season could load every cell at once until my distinctions vanished into the general movement of water. Nothing would dry. Nothing would break. There would simply cease to be any reason to say that this way of happening was mine rather than the highland’s ordinary plumbing.

So I would need disturbance, but not too much. Connection, but not unlimited connection. Every rain would sustain me by the same means through which it might erase me. To remain definite would not mean becoming fixed. It would mean remaining capable of being changed without becoming interchangeable with everything around me.

If you came looking for me, you would have to stop searching for a body and begin keeping a calendar. You would need to notice whether the lingering film and the delayed drop still stood in the same proportion, whether the dark line on the wall followed some rains and not others, whether opening a distant basin changed anything here. Your patience would not make me aware of you. It would only make me visible to you.

You could not speak to me by shouting into the shaft. The shaft was never mine, and the sound would mean very little. But if you held your warm hand against one particular joint long enough, you might enter my world as a slight alteration of drying, a film withdrawn earlier than usual, a small change in resistance. The drop that fell while you sat there would not be an answer; it would have begun before you arrived. Your warmth might matter later, in another season, by helping determine which route received pressure first.

You would probably leave thinking that nothing had happened.

You would be almost right.

At noon, the basin would return the sky with such precision that it would seem deeper than the shaft. The dry pier would give no sign of belonging to anything wet. Somewhere a block would click as its load changed. A drop would strike water several cells away, one clear note with no direction.

And where would I be?

Not in any of those things separately. I would be in the intervals between them: between fullness and response, between the last wetting and the next likely route, between what had passed through and what its passage had left able to act.

Not the water. Not the stone.

The small, continuing difference one rain made to the next.
