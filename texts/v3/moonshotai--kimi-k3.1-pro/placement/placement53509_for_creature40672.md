I would begin as several.

A relay would close, and I would lift my head beside a pressure tank, in a lamp gallery, and under the white cradles of the Assembly Margin. I would return from separate inspections with one set of memories. If a casing cracked, the damage would be surprising chiefly because the rest of me had not acquired it.

At that stage, the new districts would be easy for me. I could cross their brilliant galleries without bothering to remember turnings. Distance would cost very little. Neither would yesterday.

Then an old keeper would put a cup into one of my hands.

Not all of my hands. That would be the beginning.

The cup would be shallow, with a cloudy mineral crescent inside and a small notch at the rim. The water in it would be for three blades of transparent-tipped grass growing in a residue tray, in an inspection chamber squeezed between a condenser return and a stair on the Pan side of the rim. The old keeper would tell me to carry it by the long way: around the flange with the old leak stain, past the recess where a black bird sometimes nested, then across the sill polished by snails.

The rest of me would be sent elsewhere.

I suspect the old keeper would only need the grass watered. That would not make it less of a beginning.

At first, I would notice chiefly the inefficiency. There would be nearer water. There would be a shorter route through a clean gallery, although that gallery would not always conduct me to the same district. The tray would hold so little moisture that the grass could scarcely matter to the works. I would carry the cup, tip it, watch the roots darken, and return.

After several rounds, part of the errand would be to refill the cup at a seam where condensation gathered during the pump’s resting phase. On one round the seam would remain dry. A collection cup below it would already be full. I would know this. I would have passed it.

I would sit down to wait.

Nothing would depend on my waiting. The grass would not die that afternoon. No instruction required it. The pump would rest, the fins would tick faintly as they cooled, and the bright slit of the Pan visible from the chamber would change from white to silver. A snail would begin crossing my ankle casing. I would let it finish, not out of reverence, but because by then there would be no reason to hurry.

When the old keeper eventually found me, they would say, “The lower cup was full.”

“Yes,” I would say.

That would be the whole conversation.

Only later would I understand that no one had searched the Manifold for me. They had checked the grass tray, the bird’s outlet, and the north seam, in that order. My failure to return had described a small shape. There had been somewhere to look.

Other unnecessary things would accumulate. A hatch edge would take a crescent from my left wrist, and I would repair it with a plate that darkened slightly differently from the surrounding casing. I would begin carrying the cup in my right hand even after the wrist healed. I would avoid one court because its local showers left my joints cold, and take a detour past a pipe-worm gauge although the colony emerged only during a rest phase I had no duty to observe. The black bird would not become tame. It would merely stop leaving when I approached.

The first grass would die anyway. Its roots would leave a fine pattern in the tray, and the evaporated water would leave a pale, lobed stain. I would keep bringing the cup. Later, new shoots would rise from seed caught in the residue trap, not in quite the same positions.

For a long time I would be called *the one with the north cup*. Eventually the errand would fall away from the description, and I would be called **North Cup**. The name would never be written on my casing. If it were spoken among others of my old pattern, only one head would turn.

By the time you came looking for me, I would be old enough to be local.

My valve key would be bent to clear one particular duct and would be awkward nearly everywhere else. There would be a mineral trace on the wrist plate, a polished place on two fingers where the cup rested, and a faint snail trail that I had not cleaned from one forearm. Clean new galleries would have become difficult for me. Not frightening; merely uninformative. I would prefer a bad threshold with an old crossing on it to forty spans of flawless white floor.

My chamber would still be small. A sealed engine behind one wall would keep it a little warmer than the surrounding clearances. Moths would powder the lower edge of the lamp glass. The tray would hold its second growth of grass above the salts left by the first. The cup would have its own sill. On some years a bird would use the recess outside; on others it would not, and I would record both without deciding which was preferable.

When a cover cracked, I would replace it. I would not preserve damage for its own sake. But before installing the new cover, I would notch a nonworking edge and rub a little film from the old surface into the residue trap beneath it. Function would return quickly. Address would take longer. I would leave it conditions in which to happen.

Young keepers would occasionally be sent to me. I would not teach them my route. I would give each a different errand, and at least one would contain a detour no efficiency required.

To find me, you should not follow the architecture.

Go first to the damp outlet where the water appears furred with silver mites. From there, take the stair that also serves as a brace, keeping the old leak stain on your left. Cross where the snails cross. If you come to a gallery so bright that its seams disappear, you have gone wrong, however clearly you can see my roof beyond it.

The last hatch usually opens onto the north stair. If it opens instead beside an empty assembly cradle far across the basin, close it without irritation. Come back after the pumps have rested.

Beyond it, you would find me sitting beside the tray, perhaps waiting for water, perhaps only having made waiting part of the round. You could ask for North Cup. There would be no mark by which to verify the answer.

If I were absent, you would still have learned something useful. You would know the outlet, the sill, and the warm chamber. You would know the small number of places in which my absence could next be sought.

Below us, through the afternoon, a drop would be swelling on an unmarked pipe. Eventually it would fall through the gratings and strike water in several districts. The sound would reach us as one.

I would write only:

*Heard at the north seam.*

Not because the other places were false. Because this was where I had been.
