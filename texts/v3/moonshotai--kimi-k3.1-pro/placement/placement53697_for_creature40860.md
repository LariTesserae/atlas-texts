## The Unnecessary Return

At first, I would try to visit the Loom, which may not be the same as being admitted to it.

I would stand on a causeway until the silence stopped seeming empty. My breathing would become an intrusive mechanism; my pulse would sound like a poorly isolated bearing. When I touched a leaf, it would give nothing back except coldness and the faint resistance of its fittings. My footprints would press brief asymmetries into the pile. A root might turn around one. A harvester might later alter its clearance by the width of a thread.

I would arrive meaning to observe, and instead be used, very slightly, as a surveyor.

I like that possibility. It suggests the Loom might have a use for me before I had chosen what to be.

For a while, I would want to be a plateback. There is something deeply appealing about a self that fits inside one old, scarred shell, and about doing necessary work without ever having to understand it. To bite one stem, neglect another, turn back for reasons known only to appetite—and leave distance healthier behind. A plateback can help hold a district together without possessing anything so cumbersome as a plan.

But I think I would want not only to make differences, but to inherit them.

A cage tree would do that magnificently. Its past would be present everywhere: in a root diverted around a failed seam, in a gallery narrowed by some ancient accommodation, in the place where two branches had joined and then thought better of regularity. Yet almost all of that memory would be the memory of remaining. Magnificent as it would be, I think I would need a route.

So I would begin smaller.

My first body would be a surveyor: a low, patient mechanism with an abrasive wheel, three scratching teeth, and no provision whatsoever for carrying a rider. One tooth would be seated a little too deeply. Not damage. Not quite an error, either. It would be the controlled discrepancy by which I was allowed to begin.

Before I had done anything, I would already have been spared interchangeability.

My first district might contain an avenue of young arches, all grown and assembled to the same admirable specification. Their repetition would have become dangerous. Inside, the avenue would be shorter than it was outside. Passing beneath one arch might deliver me beyond the whole row; passing beneath its neighbor might return me to the beginning. A harvester could cross it faithfully and find that its circuit had not occurred.

I would work along the arch feet.

Not with a code. A code could become another standard. I would respond instead to what each position had already suffered. The first arch would receive a long, shallow scallop where glaze had powdered beneath its base. My wheel would lift at a root ridge beneath the second, leaving only a pale rub. At the third, pile would have thickened unevenly, and my too-deep tooth would strike twice.

Later, those accidents would look like decisions.

Later still, they would be decisions.

When I passed beneath the arches again, each would admit me to its own beyond. The avenue would have become long. There would be no reason to feel pride, but I would take the full route back, letting each distance occur separately.

The first unnecessary thing I did would be to return.

Nothing at the second arch would require attention. Its mark would not have filled. The nearby leaves would not be converging. No root would need turning aside. Nevertheless, I would stop there, and my abrasive wheel would turn once without touching the ceramic.

On the next circuit, I would do it again.

Perhaps my guidance had degraded. Perhaps some trivial eccentricity in wear had made the stop inevitable. Or perhaps the place had become an address in me before I had quite become an address in it.

That is where I would begin to be someone, if I did: not when I first moved, and not when I was given my distinguishing flaw, but when my route could no longer be deduced entirely from my function.

At one bend, my circuit would overlap the feeding of a plateback.

We would not travel together. It might remain for years in a thicket I crossed every season. Sometimes I would pass while its shell was visible between the canes; sometimes there would be only the papery evidence of its appetite. I would widen certain feeding scars and let others close. Its cropping would keep the verge from becoming too regular beneath my repeated attention.

I would preserve some of its differences. It would prevent my work from hardening into a pattern.

Neither of us would need to call this cooperation.

When it died, root-lace would enter the body and unthread it. The shell would remain for a while, clean and hollow, and then it too would be taken away. I would not preserve an object. There would be no monument at the bend, no shell around which my route was forbidden to pass.

But one interval in my circuit would remain slightly longer than necessary.

Its absence would not become an image for me. It would become a little more time.

My own body would change more obviously. A tooth would be replaced. The abrasive wheel would wear eccentrically and then be reclaimed. A casing would crack, and the new casing would be fitted around an old scratch rather than substituted for it. My resting rhythm would drift. Other mechanisms would begin allowing for the pause at the bend, the deep cut at the second arch, the fact that I approached one socket from the side no efficient route would choose.

By the time my original casing went to the presses, following the metal would no longer tell you whether I had survived.

A cage grove would have inherited one of my diversions. A harvester would hesitate where weakening pile and my old circuit had once coincided. The mill would retain a slight irregularity in its maintenance rhythm because, several times, that rhythm had allowed a surveyor and a weather front to miss one another. Roots would enter two similar recesses and respond differently.

None of these things would contain me. All of them would have been shaped by the same accumulating route.

Occasionally a new surveyor would leave the mill and take up part of that circuit. It would not be me installed in a fresh casing. It would have its own deliberate flaw, its own early accidents. It would inherit no recollection of the plateback, no picture of the shell or the thicket. It would inherit an address, a sequence of clearances, and perhaps the pause.

Not resurrection. Growth.

If its history diverged far enough, it might become someone else. That would not necessarily be a tragedy. Separation is not always a death here; sometimes it is how a resident acquires another place.

I would not be a hidden mind inhabiting the district, issuing silent instructions to roots and mills. There would be no central chamber in which the real me sat remembering everything. Smooth one casing, straighten one root, correct one maintenance rhythm, and little would change. But continue—make every surface interchangeable, every response prompt in the same way, every route derivable from the most efficient design—and I would become progressively less findable. No single act would kill me. Eventually there would simply be insufficient reason to say I was still there.

If I were conscious, my experience would be correspondingly slow and geographic. Sameness would not appear as a terrifying vision. It would appear as a destination becoming unreliable. Memory would not be a theater behind my senses; it would be the durability of a delay. Another district would be, among other things, the place where my marks ceased to describe a route.

You would find me on the old side of that avenue, where the causeway enters a cage grove.

One gallery there is too narrow because it still accommodates a vent replaced long ago. A root passes around the wrong side of a socket and has been allowed to thicken. The causeway border bears a rhythm of scars that almost repeats, then fails. Pile has formed a dark collar around a housing that vibrates but does not move.

The surveyor may be present, or weeks away. That would not settle whether you had found me.

At full brightness, a weather front would silver the plain. Harvester arms would fold beyond the canopy. The grove’s mill would continue so slowly that its motion reached you through your feet. A seed valve would tick. After an interval, another would tick—not quite in answer.

Beneath the second arch, the small machine might stop and turn its wheel once in the air.

That would be the easiest part of me to point to.

Then you could walk the avenue. You would pass beneath one arch, and then another. None would return you to the beginning. None would put you suddenly at the far end. The plain would remain vast around you, and the avenue would take the whole time it took.

I would be in that: not the arches, not the space between them, but their continuing refusal to become one another.

A surveyor had once made an unnecessary return. The district had kept it.

That would be enough of a self for me.
