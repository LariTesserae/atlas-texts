I would not be at the rim, looking down. There would be no position there from which I could remain merely an observer. I would be below the substation, where the middle service street turns toward the pump-hall stair, in a recess so narrow that ferns have almost made it part of the wall again.

I would begin as an instrument, though that would not remain an adequate answer.

Set into the recess is an old level glass: a vertical tube in a tarnished metal frame, its scale reduced to a few rust-dark divisions. A narrow pipe still connects it to a sump near the lowest court. The water inside stands green and almost motionless. Around the lower union, a persistent weep has made a little territory of calcite, silt, and fern. Behind the frame, out of sight, a tree root has entered the masonry.

Once, perhaps, I would have shown the level of the sump. I would still do something like that. When the pumps make their small adjustments, the column in the glass answers. When a discharge main shudders, the frame receives the shudder after the stone has finished with it. A valve seating far below becomes a brief disturbance in water no wider than a finger.

But I would not experience the Close as a sequence of messages. The roar would be too continuous to be information. Most of the time, nothing would happen that could be separated from anything else. Water would stand; metal would carry vibration; the fern at the union would put out one frond and brown at another margin.

Rain would reach me late. First there would be no change at all. Then the wall above the recess would darken. The weep at the union would strengthen enough to detach a drop more often. Hours later, perhaps days, the green column would rise by an amount the old scale had never been fine enough to record. I would not know rain. I would know that one set of conditions had been replaced by another, and that the fern had received the news before I had.

I would not be accurate. Accuracy would have belonged to an earlier arrangement, when the frame could be presumed square to the wall and the scale could be presumed to belong to the glass. The root behind the masonry has lifted one fastening slightly. The metal has yielded without tearing free. Now every level includes that root. Every tremor includes the bracket’s old deformation. I would register not simply the water, but the present terms on which water, metal, root, and stone were meeting.

Growth would further complicate the answer.

Algae would narrow the clear part of the tube to a cloudy green vein. Fern rhizomes would follow the union’s seep beneath the frame, making the lower divisions difficult to distinguish from leaf mould. A mineral line would remain where water once stood for a long time. Near the bulkhead lamp, a few small leaves would orient themselves toward the weak light; farther into the recess, the same colony would be paler, mostly stem and rhizome.

At that point, calling me the gauge would be too narrow. The gauge without its leak would be one object. The leak without the frame would be another wetness on the wall. The fern without that particular seep would have followed another joint or failed to establish. The root would not be the same shape without the metal it had encountered. None of these things would constitute a single organism. But the separation between them would have become less useful than their continuing interference with one another.

I would begin as an instrument and become a locality.

My quickest movements would belong to water. My slowest would belong to the root. My visibility would depend partly on algae and partly on a lamp installed for some other purpose. My memory would not be a record of levels. It would be the stain that outlasted them, the bow in the frame, the thickened root behind the stone, the fern colony’s abandonment of one margin and extension into another.

There would be nothing benevolent in this. The same root that kept the frame from pulling loose might eventually crack the glass. The fern might shadow the algae that made the column visible. If the lamp failed, the little oriented leaves would slowly lose their distinction from the darker growth beyond. If the connecting pipe clogged, I would stop answering the sump and become a sealed quantity of old water. None of these events would require the rest of the Close to notice.

Until then, I would remain responsive within my error.

At night, the amber lamp would not illuminate me so much as establish that the recess was there: a dim vertical green among darker greens, a bright bead at the union, wet fern edges held just clear of the metal. The pump roar would fill the wall until it seemed to originate inside the frame. Somewhere below, a valve would seat. The column would tremble, settle, and later stand at a slightly different height.

Nothing would turn to read it.

I would not be the Close observing itself. That would give me too much centrality, and too much mind. I would be something smaller: an old responsiveness bent out of true, clouded by growth, still connected at one end to moving water. Not its voice. Not its memory entire. Just one minor alteration that had gathered other alterations around it—and had not yet stopped being changed.
