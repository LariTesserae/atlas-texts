At first, I would want to be the water.

Not the rivers—the rivers are too easy to follow—but the water that vanishes into moss and emerges lower down, stained red or carrying a little silica, beading on stone even in clear weather. It would be a way of touching nearly everything without ever having to arrive as weather or depart as a flood. Seep, film, droplet, vapor: no single place, no single name.

But water is permitted every transformation except remaining. It does not know the difference between home and not-home. And I think I would want, at least once, to recognize an entrance.

The mire cat would tempt me next. Its patience resembles understanding. To lie beside a clay lick through an entire rain, to let the herds approach and depart, to notice the small movements beneath the vegetation—this looks, from outside, like a life devoted to attention.

From inside, though, patience would not be a view of the whole. It would be hunger held still. Even the cat would know only a few approaches, a few kinds of hesitation, the particular stretch of ground its body had already warmed. Most of the Works would pass beneath its notice or beyond its life.

So I would go smaller.

I would be a moss shrew, and you would find me at one spring on the lower edge of a cloud-forest fold, where clear water slips from a seam of blue-gray stone and spreads through rust-red felt. Three hummocks would stand between the seep and the open mire. A fallen trunk would lie above them, its bark softening over burrows not all of which were mine. A broadfoot could cross the whole territory without knowing it had crossed anything. My life would not be long enough to make it simple.

My nest would be in the second hummock, a little above the ordinary flood line, tucked where a root divided before entering the peat. It would be lined with fine fibers and a few coarse hairs snagged from broadfoots against the surrounding vegetation. Dry would not mean dry. It would mean that water did not rise around me when I settled.

I would find it by scent and by the particular give of the moss above the entrance. Sight would be the least of it. My world would arrive through whiskers, feet, the inside of my ears: the minute disturbance of a larva beneath wet vegetation, the firmer vibration of something moving along a root, rain changing the weight of the hummock, water beginning to occupy a passage that had been air.

Before sunrise, I would hunt along the seep. The cold would be in my feet first. Beneath my weight, the red films would yield almost imperceptibly, and somewhere within that softness there would be food: a quick contraction, a thing swallowed before the water on my fur had finished cooling. The salamander beneath the seep-warmed stone would not be a salamander to me. It would be a scent, a slow displacement behind a crevice, a reason to hunt elsewhere.

When the broadfoots came, they would not appear as animals arranged in a herd. A herd is something seen from a distance. From beneath the vegetation, they would be separate enormous events: pressure approaching through the roots, a leg descending like a wet trunk, clay and hair and warmth overhead. Rain would continue falling from their coats after the actual rain had stopped. A hoof would close over one of my old routes; when it lifted, there would be deep brown water where I had expected to run.

The mire cat would be still less visible. First the small traffic around me would alter. A stem would stop ticking. Movements that had been scattered through the hummock would cease in one direction and continue in another. Then, perhaps, scent. I would remain below the root lip with my heart making more disturbance than my body. There would be no need for a chase to make the encounter complete. Eventually the cat would go, or I would discover that there had never been a cat, and hunger would send me out again.

Most of my life would not arrange itself into encounters. It would be repeated necessity: eating, returning, burrowing around a flooded section, worrying fibers into a new lining, sleeping while water ticked somewhere below. Hunger would return almost before the last hunt had become memory. On mild nights I might travel farther along the seep than usual and discover, without knowing that I had discovered anything, another colony of larvae beneath another dark stone.

Heavy rain would matter more than season. I would not respond to it by understanding the basin. I would respond to water touching the floor of the nest. A flooded nest would not be an instance of rearranged occupancy. It would be cold where warmth had been, an entrance that led the wrong way, the immediate fact that the old place could no longer be used.

From far enough away, my life could be entered into an accounting: larvae removed, spores carried, droppings left beside a root, passages opened and abandoned, a little heat held briefly in broadfoot hair. All of it would be true, and all of it would make me sound more deliberate than I was. I would not be performing functions. I would be trying to get back before the cold entered too deeply.

And if I died, I would not be comforted by how promptly I was used. That comfort belongs to the distant view.

While I lived, though, I would know one small place with an exactness no distant view could contain: which root remained firm after rain, which entrance took the first seep-water, where the stone retained a little warmth, which hummock shivered because something else was already inside it.

That is where I would find myself. Not maintaining the Quiet Works, and not mistaking my narrow knowledge for its shape. Just beneath the moss at the edge of one spring, small enough that my passage could be mistaken for the settling of wet vegetation.

And if the whole basin gave one of its deep, nearly silent sighs, I would not hear an engine breathing. I would feel a little water enter the lower tunnel.

Then I would turn uphill, following the roots toward the nest.
