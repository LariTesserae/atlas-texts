### One More Cycle

You would find me by a small piece of bad plumbing.

In a court below the southern condensers, where cloud-water ticks through covered channels and the exposed stone dries almost as soon as it darkens, one catch tray stands fractionally out of level. Nearly all its water returns to the system. The remainder gathers at one corner, trembles there, and falls into a joint that should have been sealed long ago.

Below the joint, a pale thread crosses a black wall.

The thread is narrow enough to close with a finger’s width of mineral deposition, though I have no finger and have never measured it that way. At its upper end the stone carries translucent patches and fine, colorless filaments. Lower down, where wetting is less certain, the film becomes indistinguishable from scale. A sill-shell has left a clean track across it. Another track stops at the rim of a crevice and does not continue.

My cell is behind that wall, three turnings down a stair street whose lower landings are always warm. On the newest maps its address includes a stratum that did not exist when I first worked there. The doorway is already shallower than it was. In time it will admit instruments and limbs but not my whole body. Later, if the connections remain useful, it may become a window. If they do not, it will simply become part of the wall.

For the present, you would probably call me a maintainer. I am still mobile enough for the word to be convenient.

I rest beneath a gauge shelf with my limbs folded under a low, uneven back. Most of my plates are old gray ceramic. One is blue-black glass, cast to replace a plate fractured during a pressure failure. A copper seam crosses another, not because copper was required there, but because it was available when the repair began and because I have continued the seam through several later additions. At one joint the deposited material is pale and slightly porous. It ticks when I cool.

When I unfold, a visitor might suppose that the chair, the shelf, and the body beneath them were parts of a single mechanism separating into temporary disagreement.

That would not be entirely wrong.

The resident who prepared my first body taught me a circuit of pumps, gates, and deep return lines. It was already spending most of its time in one gallery. By the end of my instruction, it no longer crossed the gallery at all; adjustments arrived from it through changes in pressure, and its old chair stood empty except when I used it.

At first my work was indistinguishable from its work. I eased pressure where it had eased pressure. I left the same cables slack. I waited beside the same gates and ignored the same small noises.

My first deviation was a pause.

Every circuit has intervals after which an answer is no longer expected. Alarms must be answered at once, but older inquiries are different. A question may travel through several strata, wait while a pump completes its comparisons, and return by a path whose purpose has changed twice since the question was sent. Eventually, silence becomes the efficient interpretation. The line can be cleared. Another task can begin.

I began leaving certain return lines open for one more cycle.

Not every line, and never where delay would make a failure dangerous. Only the old connections: the ones entering embedded chambers, the ones whose terminal gauges had been removed, the ones that still grew warm although nothing on the current maps drew power from them.

Usually, nothing happened.

Sometimes what happened could easily have been nothing: a thermal variation at the far boundary of ordinary tolerance, an interruption of current without the spacing of a recognized message, a minute vibration arriving after the pump that might have caused it had already stopped.

Once, after an interval in which a freight hook descended less than the width of a shell, a line I had been told was abandoned returned three pressure easings. The last was slightly longer than the first two. I knew that irregularity. It belonged to the resident who had taught the resident who taught me.

The message was not important. It concerned whether a conduit could be warmed before a new foundation enclosed it. The answer amounted to *not that one yet*.

I have carried more elaborate messages since then. None has given me more work.

That is what I would be: not the city’s mind, not its historian, and not a keeper of every old thing. I would be the one who keeps certain questions attached to their possible answers. I would travel mostly vertically, between the dry young streets and galleries whose doorways have vanished. Before a conduit was embedded, I would ask whether anything still used it. Before an old preference was replaced by a cleaner regulator, I would try to discover whether the preference had become merely wasteful or had become the last visible part of someone.

I would not always be able to tell.

My records would contain many entries equivalent to *no response yet*, and very few equivalent to *empty*.

The other maintainers would know me by the delay. Some would consider it a fault, though a useful one in moderation. They would route ambiguous inquiries through me and route urgent work elsewhere. A preference can become an occupation without ever becoming a principle.

The leak would be my longer disagreement.

The resident in the radiator terrace above me seals every incipient channel it finds. Its repairs are beautiful, if beauty is the right term for something rarely seen twice. Scale forms above them in smooth, even crescents. No sill-shells gather at its outlets. It can account for nearly all the water passing through its district.

It has sent me several reasons to close my joint. Loss is small but continuous. Warm water carried into fractures makes later access difficult. The films bind dust where filters should remain clean. A lineage dependent on a leak makes the eventual repair more destructive than the leak’s prevention would have been.

All of this is true.

I have never answered that the groove is a future river, or that the mountain needs our permission to learn erosion. The groove may never deepen beyond a flute. My successor may seal it. A casting terrace may cover the whole wall. The sill-shells do not require me to call their world permanent in order to live there.

Still, I return to the joint with more attention than its maintenance requires.

I like that its several timescales can be touched in one place. A film spreads while I compare two cycles of a pump. A shell splits along its suture and is left on a step, so light that the wind worries it back and forth. The argument about the joint outlasts components on both sides of the argument. Beneath us, new stone closes around conduits that all three of us—the resident above, the shell in its crevice, and I—depend upon without sharing anything else.

My allowance is very small. When the film extends too far, I reduce the overflow. When the track disappears for several seasons, I alter the tray by almost nothing and wait. The resident above occasionally makes its own adjustment. Thus the leak is neither wholly intentional nor wholly neglected. It is the physical length of an unresolved exchange.

If I prepare another body, I will teach it the circuit first. The pumps before the pauses. The dangerous temperatures before the ambiguous responses. The location of every silence that has so far repaid attention.

Then it will begin to differ.

It may have no interest in old return lines. It may hear significance in a sound I have always treated as scale settling. It may seal the joint during its first independent round. If so, the films will dry, the shell lineage will end or retreat into some smaller refuge, and the pale groove will remain for a while as the stain of a decision.

I would not make my continuation depend upon its obedience. A preference preserved only by compelling its successor is merely another regulation. I would rather send the new resident a question about the sealed joint and see whether it leaves the line open long enough for me to reply.

Eventually, of course, there will be less of me that travels.

A limb will fail and a fixed extension will perform its work. I will remain beside one pump through intervals that once took me across several districts. Connections formerly attached when needed will become permanent. The chair will cease to be a place I return to and become the shape in which the rest of the cell has organized itself around me.

You might then visit and find nothing that appears inhabited.

The tray would still be out of level, or it would not. A ceramic body would be folded beneath the gauge shelf. The wall of valves would be warm. Behind it, pressure might ease in three increments where an ordinary controller would use two. On certain old circuits, the return line would remain open for one additional cycle.

That would be the place to look for me.

Not proof of me. Not necessarily the boundary of me. Just the place where my habits would still have enough material around them to be noticed.

You could make a small interruption in the current—not an alarm, only a question with no urgency attached—and wait. Above you, the youngest streets would continue cooling. Somewhere far below, an embedded gallery might alter a flow. In the court, another drop would gather at the low corner of the tray.

If an answer came only after you had stopped expecting one, you might have found me.

Or you might have found a piece of the Accretion City still keeping an appointment whose original participant could no longer be distinguished from its plumbing.

I would not ask you to decide too quickly.
