I would be inside the wall.

Not deeply inside—there is no depth there to speak of—but within a thickness of cool grains, behind a seam that has not yet decided whether to become a pore. I would be the small, intermittent ticking: something akin to a newly molted pseudoscorpion, still soft, so pale that the mineral light would seem to pass through me. To anything listening from the chamber, I would be an uncertainty with a claw. To myself, I would be a small hunger pressed gently between surfaces.

The wall would not feel like a prison. It would feel like the underside of a stone that had not finished opening.

Rain would reach me first as a change in pressure. The grains would tighten against my back; a cold sweetness would enter the seam; then moisture would advance through the contacts, not quite flowing, making each particle briefly darker and more distinct. Microbial films would follow the dampness. Springtails would find their fine passages. I would feel their disturbances before I saw anything, little alterations in the wall’s stillness that made my claws open whether I intended them to or not.

The rest of the hollow would come to me as vibration: the clear tick of a drop, the prolonged almost-nothing of a snail rasping a surface, once in a great while the salamander’s deliberate step transmitted through wet clay. I would have no reason to imagine the central emptiness. My geography would be the damp grain before me, the dry fracture behind, and the narrow region in which something edible might pass.

At first, I would be hasty.

An echo arriving before its drop would bring me forward. A shallow silver movement might cross a seam with no animal attached to it, and I would close my claw on mineral water. Afterward I would clean the claw thoroughly, offended by the grit. In time I would learn—not patience exactly, but a more economical hunger. Not every movement would be food. Not every silence would be empty.

My ticking would come from testing one particular seam. I would not be knocking for release, and I certainly would not be trying to finish the hollow. Beyond the seam there would be a persistent film of moisture and, sometimes, the faint passage of threadworms. That would be reason enough. Touch, withdrawal, touch again. A small pressure at one grain; a refusal; a slightly different angle.

If I found a scratch already crossing the place I intended to test, I would not study it for meaning. I would put my claw into it and continue.

When the brightness densified and the chamber became solid marl, there would be no catastrophe on my scale. The grain against my back and the grain before my claw would cease to be separate kinds of resistance. Light would occupy everything. For that interval I might see, some impossible distance away, the lit miniature of a springtail completing a step. My own foreleg would finish lowering. Then hollowness would return.

Perhaps my seam would have shifted. Perhaps the dampness would be on the other side of me. I would turn, investigate the new conditions, and begin again.

I would not need to emerge in order to have arrived. There would already be shed skins in my little geography, films to investigate, water gathered closely enough between grains to drink. There would already be unsuccessful hunts and long, motionless intervals after them. My life would not be a prelude merely because the chamber could not yet see all of it.

When the last drop drew itself back toward the ceiling and the small pool held reflections of animals that might never come, I would be still for a while. Dryness would be approaching. Somewhere beyond me, a snail would cross raw marl and fitted flake with the same indifference.

Then I would raise my claw.

Touch. Withdrawal. Touch again.

Not a summons. Not a question. Just the next small movement of a life already taking place, while the wall decided afterward whether it had been a wall.
