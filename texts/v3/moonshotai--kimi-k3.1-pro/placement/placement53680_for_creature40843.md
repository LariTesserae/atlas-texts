### The Unswept Corner

I would begin by being easy to mistake for nothing.

Not at the working face, where the stone answers each blow and a whole crew can pause over a change in sound. Not at a cistern lid, where people come to argue, mend cord, and learn what the rain has done. I would be in the oldest quarter, below a stair that had once been the edge of a cutting.

Behind the wall that shelters the tree, there would be a strip of rough ground too narrow for jars and too uneven for sleeping. The broom would reach it, but not always thoroughly. In hot weather the wall would shade it after midday. At dusk the tree’s leaves would be audible overhead.

You would have to look closely to find me there.

At first I would seem to be only sweepings: pale limestone dust pressed into a hollow, a little sharp silt left by runoff, husks, leaf fragments, a fleck of charcoal from some cooking recess. There would be chips in me still white at the broken edges. A sandal could carry away part of my body without its owner noticing.

No one would call me soil. I would hardly deserve the name. I would have no depth to speak of, no softness, no reliable damp. Most seeds arriving in me would do what they did elsewhere: open, spend their small store, and wither.

Still, that is what I would be becoming—a thin pocket of ground that had neither been cut from the bedrock nor laid there by a wall builder.

Part of me would be made by care. Dust has to be put somewhere; a threshold must be cleared; rubble cannot be allowed into a drain. Part would be made by interruption. A sledge comes, a rope needs splicing, rain threatens, everyone is tired. The last basketful is set down and its purpose changes by being forgotten. The tree adds a leaf. Rain presses the leaf into the dust. The next sweeping does not quite reach it.

I would have no memory in the way the older inhabitants have memory. I could not recall a lane as a ledge or a courtyard as raw stone. But I would keep a kind of evidence. A fine layer from one rain. Coarser grains from another, when water arrived carrying more than the grates could hold. A darker seam of leaf scraps. A small lens of charcoal. None of it labeled, and much of it disturbed before it could be read.

Rain would be my great alteration, though not my abundance.

Before it reached me, I would know the town was changing occupation. Tools would be made safe. Feet would hurry toward the known turns. Someone above would call down what the water was carrying. The important water would be watched closely: the pale first runoff, the settling hollows, the cleaner flow admitted toward the cisterns.

Mine would arrive by lesser routes. A drip repeated from the same leaf tip. A brief darkening at the foot of the wall. Wind-driven rain just finer than the stair could shed. It would sort me a little, packing dust around the larger chips and drawing the faintest mineral line along my edge. For a few hours, perhaps a day, I would remain cool after the nearby stone had dried.

That would be enough to make me suspicious.

If damp persisted too long against plaster, someone would come looking. If a root found me and began to worry a joint, it would be cut out. If I started catching rubbish or turning a useful ledge foul, I would be scraped into a basket and taken away. And people would be right to do it. A town held together by grades, drains, and remembered runoff cannot afford to cherish every accumulation merely because something might grow in it.

My persistence would not make me innocent. It would only mean that, for the moment, I lay just outside the next necessary task.

Most of what I did would be very nearly nothing. I would keep one grain from blowing back onto a swept step. Hold a trace of moisture slightly past dawn. Give a beetle one more night among damp fragments. Catch a seed that would otherwise have crossed the court and lodged in a drain, where it would certainly have been removed.

Occasionally something would happen that was not quite nothing.

A root hair would enter a space between two grains and not immediately dry. A fallen leaf would remain long enough to lose its shape. The tree would draw from me and shelter me and feed me and, with its thicker roots, threaten to tear me open. There would be no simple kindness between us. Its shade could be the reason a seedling survived; its thirst could be the reason the seedling failed.

One evening after a small rain, someone might sweep the terrace and leave the back of the corner for morning. The broom’s sound would move away. A shutter would shift. Footsteps would descend a stair out of sight. For a while the leaves overhead would make their dry, crowded whisper.

At the back of me, almost against the wall, there might be a seedling with two leaves. Nothing like the beginning of a garden. Nothing that promised next season would be kinder. By the following week it might be only another thread of dry matter caught among the chips.

But while it lived, it would not be living on bare stone.

That is where you would find me: in the narrow margin between maintained and neglected, between material waiting for a use and material no one had yet decided to move. Not the spirit of the New Cut, and not its future. Only one of the small places where dust had become a deposit, a deposit had kept a little rain, and the rain had outlasted the hour in which it fell.

For a little while, I would be what remained.
