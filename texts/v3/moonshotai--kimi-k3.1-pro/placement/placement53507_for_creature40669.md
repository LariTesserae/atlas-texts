At first, I would be only an interval.

A drop falls into a basin. Its rings widen, touch the submerged roots, and disappear. The water becomes motionless again, but something continues—a slight tremor in a root, a disturbance too fine to show on the surface. It might be the mat settling. It might be a body passing beyond the root-vault. It might be water arriving by some path no one has traced.

I would want to remain in that pause before the movement became an explanation.

For a while, I might mistake this for a wish to be water: to cross the Height without hurrying, to arrive in a province I had never seen, to become rain in one place because of mist exhaled in another. But water does not have to understand where it has been. It need not answer for the interpretations made of it. I would want to remain answerable for at least that much.

Only later would there be a hand in the interval.

A long hand, mottled and damp, resting against the root. Broad bare feet folded beneath a stooped body. Skin warm with the same warmth as the air. I would be a listener, though not one of those whose touch seems almost infallible. Without the history of a place, I would mistake settling for passage, thirst for hollowness, an animal’s knocking for the tick of a stem taking up moisture. I would have to stay a long time to become even locally reliable.

My place would be on the inside of a broad swell, where the ground looks level until you try to understand the water. A shallow root-vault would shelter me above a basin with no visible outlet. At one end of the vault, dark stone would show through the felt, rounded and mineral-stained. At the other, a hollow column would lean against living roots, its upper chamber dead and dry enough to hold my few vessels. My shelter would be a low weaving of fallen sheaths. Bark-folders would use its outer surface without permission. When I woke, I would sometimes know that one had been there only because a seam of apparent bark was breathing.

The basin would be diminishing.

Not dramatically. There would be no exposed cliff of mud, no sudden rank of drowned or rescued growth. Only a waterline slightly below the stain I had learned as a young listener, and certain pale roots descending farther than they once had before entering the water. Beyond the swell, another hearing’s records might speak of a strengthening spring. Or might not. Messages would arrive altered by copying, and some of the hands that made them would already have returned to the mat.

I would belong to a hearing scattered through several such basins. We would not assemble often enough for assembly to define us. My nearest companion could be years away and still near in the ways that mattered: answering a pressure-pattern, preserving a correction, objecting to an interpretation I had left in green tissue.

My name, given against your wrist, would be a brief pressure, a release, and then the hand remaining after the pattern had ended. If you needed a spoken rendering, you could call me *Afterward*. No one in my hearing would use the sound.

The root I knew best would not be a prophecy or a map. It would be an argument.

One old swelling would concern a diverted seep. A later series would record that the diversion had preserved several crowded cisterns. Farther along, in tissue grown thick around an interruption, another listener had added that those cisterns had been preserved by emptying a place beyond the reach of the first listener’s knowledge. Later still, someone had questioned whether the emptying and the diversion were related at all.

My particular work would be to copy such records without making them appear more settled than they were. Not to reconcile every hand. Not to cut away an error merely because it was an error. Some errors would be the only surviving evidence of what a place had once looked like to someone standing inside it.

I would need that caution. I would have earned it badly.

When young, I might carry water and cistern young from an overcrowded column into one that held almost no swimmers. The second column’s emptiness would look to me like neglect. I would not yet understand that its inner film grew slowly, or that the pale roots within it were adapted to a scarcity I had never troubled to read.

For a time, the introduced swimmers would flourish. Then the water would become beautifully clear.

I would record that clarity as success.

Only much later would I find the inner walls grazed bare, the last swimmers flattened into a dampness that could not sustain them, the column no longer capable of supporting the abundance I had thought I was giving it. My correction would take longer to grow than the original message. It would say, in essence: *I saw openness and called it need. I saw clear water and called it health.*

I would like to say this taught me never to intervene. It would not. There is no untouched life available to me. My shelter would shade the mat. My feet would continue old compressions or begin new ones. I would eat, drink, clear fallen tissue from my sleeping place, and make choices about which small lives to move and which to leave. Even refusal would become part of the conditions to which other beings responded.

So I would practice something less innocent: return to the same places; make interventions small enough to describe; leave enough of the record intact for another listener to disprove me; never confuse delay with purity.

While the basin’s future remained uncertain, an old browser might pass through the vault.

I would feel it before seeing it: not footsteps exactly, but a succession of deep accommodations in the mat. Then its broad back would move beneath the root arch, carrying copper cushions, translucent leaves, and trails of wet root. A glider would unfold from the moss, curve once through the dimness, and vanish again near the animal’s shoulder. The browser would not hurry around me. I would simply have ceased to be in its way.

One soft foot would come down beyond the arch.

Several paces away, water would rise into a depression beside my hand.

It would be tempting to make an answer of that. The basin was falling; I had touched the root; water had risen. But it would not be a spring, or a message, or the forest taking my side. It would be the mat making room for weight.

Knowing that would feel, for the moment, like enough.

That is where you would find me: crouched at the edge of a diminishing basin, with one hand on an unfinished record and the other stained from crushing seeds. Not the memory of the Old Green Height, and certainly not its mind. Not wise merely because I moved slowly. I would have no more access to the whole than a swimmer has to the distant weather that may someday refill its column.

But I could know one root well. I could recognize the difference between an old message and the growth that had continued around it. I could leave the next listener a more exact account of my uncertainty.

A listener, then, while I had a body. Afterward, if I was fortunate, not a truth preserved forever in living tissue, but an error made useful by the hand that corrected it.

If you came to my vault, I would not try to explain the Height. I would guide your fingers to the root. You would feel the first swelling, then the correction, then the slender living growth continuing beyond both. Against your wrist, I would give you my name.

At the failing of the cloud-light, I would sleep with my palm still in place. Condensation would descend through the column. Somewhere a stem would tick. Much later, a drop would enter the basin without quite breaking its stillness.

Weeks away, beyond the swell, a spring might rise.

Here, something would move beneath my hand.

Once.

I would wait for it to come again.
