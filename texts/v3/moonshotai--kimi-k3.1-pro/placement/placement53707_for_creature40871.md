### The keeper of the second round

I would begin on the central bridge, but I would not remain there. The bridge is a good place from which to understand the Hushworks and perhaps too good a place from which to imagine belonging to it. From there, everything looks composed: the lamp, the black water, the galleries layered above one another, the eel passing through a reflection without seeming to touch it.

For a while I might want to be that eel. Old, scarred, acquainted with passages omitted from the plans. But I think I would be human. I would want the chipped cup and the amended diagrams, the badly played cards behind a padded door. I would want to inherit handwriting.

I would be the keeper whose work begins after everyone else’s work appears to be finished.

The others might call my route the second round. It would not be a grand appointment. My station would be a disused ticket window off the switching house, its opening fitted with a felt-covered shutter, its stone shelf just wide enough for a lamp, chalk, a hand lens, and the ledger. The window would look onto a wet apron and the underside of a walkway rather than any impressive machinery. Tea would go cold there. Someone would have left a card score on the back of an inspection tag. I would be losing.

Most often, though, you would find me returning to a place someone else had just left.

A guard has been reset. A screen has been brushed along one strip and left occupied along another. A chain was lowered carefully except for one link that struck the apron. Nothing happened. The tools were put away. The repair was entered as complete.

My round would begin there.

The ledger would have a wide margin for later entries. In it I might write:

*Chain link struck apron, previous round.*  
*First return: seam 19 dry.*  
*Second return: droplets along upper fork, widely spaced.*  
*Morning: no spread.*

And beneath that, because it would be important not to make the chamber simpler than it was:

*After. Not necessarily because.*

I would learn the Hushworks through these intervals. Not only which valve served which flume, but how long a courtyard could remain innocent after a sound; which seam darkened before its neighbor; where a vibration could be felt in a rail while remaining absent from the motor tone. I would learn that “nothing” at the end of a shift and “nothing” the following morning were different observations, and that neither was quite the same as safety.

My best work might look, to anyone in a hurry, like a series of nearly identical visits.

I would not become naturally solemn. At first my quiet would be clumsy and self-conscious. I would still catch a latch with my thumb after hearing it click, still set a cup down too firmly, still swear when grit turned beneath my boot. Behind the padded doors I would laugh too loudly and complain about the cold. Gradually, though, the caution would cease to feel like a rule imposed on my movements. It would become an understanding that I was still acting after I had stopped.

The animals would not be my instruments, exactly. I would try not to make emblems of them.

An eel in an unmarked culvert would have its own reasons for being there. A salamander leaving a warm housing would not be delivering a message to me. The shrimp would not gather or scatter in order to improve my inspection. But I would check the passage, look again at the refuge, and return to the screen. Noticing would not entitle me to claim them. It would only give me another opportunity not to interfere blindly.

So I would leave the south margin. I would clear the crab gap and put the crab itself out of the tool cupboard. I would record *present* when all I had of a salamander was a disturbance in soft silt. At Fern 5, I would note the new frond and resist the absurd urge to improve its situation by giving it companions.

As the canal rose, my round would alter without becoming simpler. Doorways would be sealed; others would come into use. A wall whose weeping I knew well might slowly dry, while a warm pocket behind a motor became uninhabitable. I might live to see the intake landing become a canal edge. I would not live to see the whole cycle answer itself.

That would be the other part of the work: preparing observations I could not complete.

I would leave cable loops lifted, brackets ready, thresholds marked. I would copy forward an old keeper’s warning even where I did not fully understand it, preserving the uncertainty rather than quietly deleting it. Beside some of my own confident notes, I would hope to leave enough room for a successor to write *no longer true*.

On an ordinary night, after an ordinary repair, I might stop again on the central bridge. A shutter would open and close above the north gallery. The flywheel spokes would pass with their almost insulting slowness. An eel would move beneath a lamp, and for several seconds the broken reflection would continue to exist without it.

Then a crab would touch iron once.

I would not take that as a punctuation mark, or an omen, or the chamber speaking. A little later I would be somewhere else, holding a shaded lamp near a wall, looking to see whether the water had begun its answer.

Back at the ticket window, I might write only:

*Nothing yet.*

And leave space beneath it.

That, I think, is what I would be: not the hush itself, not the hand on the great pump, but the keeper of the second round—the one who comes back.
