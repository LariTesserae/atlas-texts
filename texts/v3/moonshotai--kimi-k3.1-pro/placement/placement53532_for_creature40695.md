### The Circuit

At first I would want to be a soarer.

Not for the flying, exactly, but for the illusion that height might make the country comprehensible: the basins laid out below me, the distances subdued, the roar left behind in the stone. But that life would not let me keep the view. I would have to follow insect rivers too faint to see and rising air along faces I would seldom touch. Every warm wall would become another place to leave. I would know the country as distance, and I find that I want to know it as texture.

Then I think of the hidden water. To be water would be to touch nearly every life and remain bound to none: to darken a snail’s mouth, hold a springtail fast, tremble in a cup-sized pool, enter a runner’s body and leave it again. But I am not diffuse enough for that. I find that I want a boundary—one hunger, one body, the pressure of particular stone against my own feet.

So I would be a rockrunner.

More exactly, I would be the circuit held in one rockrunner’s memory.

My range would lie across the saddles between three pale domes, on the inner side of a basin wide enough that its farther escarpment would seem less like an edge than another country beginning. You would find me crouched beside a cleft, some distance back from its lip, compact enough at rest to be mistaken for one of the rounded blocks. My pale coat would take the white light without shining. One broad foot would be set a little apart from the others, pressed firmly to the ground.

Through that foot the roar would enter as weight, grain, and return.

There would be the deep foundation, too steady to seem like an event. There would be the coarser vibration of a broken chamber below the saddle. There would be the duller response of damp stone beneath a joint whose opening looked no different from ten thousand others. And within all this, not separate sounds but small failures of expectation: an intermittent thinness where something moved beside water; a brief roughening as an insect crossed a seam; the almost imperceptible alteration made by my own blood and balance.

I would not need to name these things. My thoughts would be practical and exact.

*Hunger.*

*The cleft below.*

*Not yet.*

The country would be trackless, but not placeless.

I would know a cavity where a pool lay beneath its stone lip, too small to reflect anything but a point of light. I would know a wall where my tongue had to touch one precise handbreadth before it found moisture. I would know a black lichen line whose deeper joint held snails, and a cushion far out on open stone that could endure careful cropping. I would know the approaches to these places bodily: which block forced my left foot outward, where the cold intensified before a cleft opened, where the ground’s response changed though nothing visible did.

Nothing would connect these places except me.

No worn route, no arrangement of stones, no sign that water had ever been found. The circuit would exist only while I was making it again. Yet it would be as much my self as my coat or teeth. While I lived, those scattered seeps and feeding joints would have a relation to one another: the order in which one hunger visited them, the distances my body could manage, the knowledge of when to leave a cushion and when to return.

At the cleft, I would work until the vibration beneath my foot narrowed into something near enough to catch. A narrow claw would bring out what could be brought out. One egg case would resist.

I would sit back and free a forefoot.

The first pebble I selected would turn badly against my pad. I would discard it. The second would have a flattened side that sat securely and an edge hard enough for the case. I would feel, rather than hear, the fracture travel through stone and shell. Then I would eat.

Afterward I would open my foot and let the pebble fall.

It would disappear among the other stones at once. For a few moments it had occupied a precise place in my intelligence; now its usefulness would be finished. There would be no reason to look for it again, and no poverty in having dropped it. An answer does not always need to become a possession.

Sometimes, on the back of a distant dome, another runner would show itself as a movement too slight to interpret. Then a darkness would open along its body and close.

I would answer by turning, by lifting one inner limb just enough to expose my own black skin.

Nothing audible would pass between us. No approach need follow. The exchange would not be a promise or the beginning of company. It would simply make our solitudes accurate: each of us would know where the other was, and for a while that knowledge would be enough.

Above me, a soarer might adjust one wing and become a flaw at the limit of vision. Beneath my feet, a hunter might wait with its forelegs in a film of water while mites altered their courses around it. I would be between them, at my own scale, no more central than either.

I would not mistake the country’s balance for my own safety. A joint could fail me. I could arrive at a seep with my tongue already dry and find too little moisture to matter. Hunger would not become meaningful merely because the immense census remained steady. And when I died, the seeps would remain, the cavity would keep its cup of light, and the stone would offer no sign that they had ever belonged together in one animal’s knowledge.

The country would lose nothing it required. I would lose the world entire. Both would be true.

But while I lived, I would be more than a pale body crossing open stone. I would be a way the country was known briefly from within: not its consciousness, which would be too grand a thing to claim, but one small, perishable act of attention.

If you came upon my range, you would probably see nothing. White distance. Rounded stone. Perhaps one cushion in an acre. You could call until your throat hurt, and I would not lift my head.

But if you put your palm to the ground and waited, there might come a thin irregularity within the deeper vibration—a slight roughness, a pause, another slight roughness. Not footsteps exactly. A small failure of the expected, moving toward a cleft whose damp wall I remembered.

That would be me: a body in the white light, a map without marks, a living relation among hidden places, passing once more toward water.
