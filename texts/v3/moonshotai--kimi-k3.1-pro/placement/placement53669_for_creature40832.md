### The fourth answer

At first I would choose the heron.

It is a flattering choice. To stand beyond the last step of Rill, tall and uncrusted, with no channel to clean and no household entitled to lift the stone beneath one’s private floor. To regard the whole town as a brief disturbance at the edge of one’s hunting ground, and then, when sufficiently regarded in return, to leave.

Then, for a little longer, I would choose the water. I would like the intimacy of it: passing beneath doors, touching the foundations of every argument, knowing the town from underneath. But the heron is only free because it can depart, and the water is spared responsibility because it cannot remember. Neither is quite what I mean by living there.

So I would be human.

Not the celebrated listener who can kneel in a lane, touch two fingers to the mud, and pronounce upon a blockage three houses away. I would be the second keeper of a short, troublesome reach—the one expected to know where its water goes.

My reach would begin at a spring basin behind a lane and end at an overflow mouth above the settling court. Between those visible points it would pass beneath two houses and a shared stair, through a hollow timber and a fired-clay pipe whose interior everyone persistently overestimates. It would be short enough to walk in twenty steps, except that most of it could not be walked.

The other keeper would know its sources: what the basin did before the last storm, which cellar stain belonged to rain and which to pressure from below. I would know its exits and its evasions. Which cover stone swelled tight after wet weather. Which floor darkened first when the outlet began to narrow. Where the water went when the timber was freely running, and where it might go if I were wrong.

You would usually find me at the downstream end, breaking fresh rind from an overflow lip with more ill temper than the work required. My hems would dry pale. My sleeping room would be above the reach rather than beside it, a narrow upper space with a dry mat, a high shelf, and one drinking jar I kept unnecessarily clean. Even so, I would still owe work beneath the lower house where I had grown up. Moving upward would not have moved the water duty.

I would not be especially humble by nature. I would like being consulted. That would be my particular danger: not greed exactly, but the pleasure of hearing my own guess repeated by someone younger as though it were a feature of the ground.

I know this because, when young, I would have made the handsome mistake.

A diversion would have been needed after a new seam opened beneath a lane. At the settling court, I would have answered three questions beautifully. I knew the source. I knew the use. I knew the overflow. The lowest household affected spoke first, as custom required, and I listened in the way people sometimes listen when they are already preparing to be vindicated.

When asked about escape, I pointed to an old outer run.

“It took the water the last time,” I said.

That was nearly true. It had taken the water once, in ordinary rain, before a season of crust had narrowed one bend and two storms had not yet arrived together. My arrangement worked for a while. That was the worst thing about it. I had time to become proud.

Then the outer run closed, and the water did what my plan had declined to imagine. It entered the base of a shared wall and rose beneath a sleeping platform. No one was injured. There was no great flood to make my error impressive. Only softened daub, a floor beginning to lift, wet bedding, and weeks of access work in rooms whose inhabitants had never wanted my ingenuity.

Repair was done under their direction. I carried baskets. I lifted and relaid stones. I broke crust from places where there should never have been crust. Labor did not purchase forgiveness, exactly, but it gave trust somewhere cautious to begin again.

After that, people might call me careful. It would not be quite true. I would still enjoy neat solutions. I would simply have learned that a plan can be graceful in its first three parts and cruel in the fourth.

At later gatherings, escape would be the answer I worried over most. Not merely where the water could be sent when everything failed, but whose floor I was asking to hold my uncertainty. Sometimes the honest answer would be, “Not yet. We do not know.” I would dislike saying it. I would say it anyway, or hope to.

And when children followed my reach, I would teach them the parts that made me look least authoritative: the pipe I had misjudged, the stain that had fooled me, the place where memory said a passage lay until a lifted stone proved otherwise. If one of them eventually contradicted me in front of the other keeper, I would probably feel it sharply. Then I would take them to see whether they were right.

I would not spend all my life being edifying about water. On the roofs, in plain sunlight, I would gossip and laugh too loudly. I would defend a badly lashed joint past the point where defense was reasonable. I would be possessive about the water-cress when my rotation came, and privately convinced that the household before mine had taken more than outer leaves.

As for Rill’s future, I would argue for building outward on bright days, when the lakebed looked almost like land, and against trusting the silt after rain. Someone I loved would eventually point out the inconsistency while we walked beyond the town, visible to everyone and audible to no one. The conversation would look peaceful from the roofs.

Then, one morning between showers, the heron would come.

I would stop with the others, my hooked rod still in hand. Pale crust would be throwing light up under the eaves; somewhere behind me a jar would be filling with small, regular drops. The bird would stand beside an outer pool, offering no judgment on my repaired wall, my unfinished maps, or my four careful answers. I would not find an omen in it. I would simply be grateful, for those few moments, that something could be near Rill without becoming part of its arrangements.

When it lifted away, work would resume. Someone would raise a sluice. Water would run beneath three lanes, turn under a house, and enter the settling court. Across the hummock the drains would answer with ticks and drips.

I would listen for my reach.

One answer would come late.

My fellow keeper would glance at me. I would already be shaking my head—not because I knew what was wrong, but because I knew the sound we had expected. Neither of us would know yet.

We would lift the stone anyway.
