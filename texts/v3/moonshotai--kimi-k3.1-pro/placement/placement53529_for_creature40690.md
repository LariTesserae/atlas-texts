At first I would want to be the white eel. It is the obvious refuge for a self: dark eyes, a body with a near and far end, the ability to withdraw. Even its unknown length is appealing. The census could have only a hand’s length of me; the rest would be mine, elsewhere.

But that elsewhere would begin to feel like another kind of performance—the hidden animal, the last mystery, the one resident a visitor could turn into a story. The eel is more interesting than that. It does no work that explains it. I would not want to make myself important merely by becoming difficult to observe.

Nor would I want to be the whole root-body. It would be too easy to call that vastness wisdom. To draw water through roots twenty paces from my leaves, to have no single place where I was entirely present—yes, but *I* would soon become only a convenient word laid over the grafts. I would have gained the whole hollow and lost any reason to say *here*.

So I would become smaller, and older. I think I would be a chambered cane—but not the taut young one at the moment of the stroke. I would have to live my way toward what I was.

At first I would be soft enough to bend between two fingers. There would be no promise in me. I might be crushed, shaded out, rubbed away before my ribs hardened. If I survived near an engine, iron would instruct my shape without teaching me anything. The yoke would bruise me; I would thicken. It would press against one side for years; that side would become polished. My clearances would be the clearances the machine had left me. Only from a distance would any of this look like a vocation.

Water would enter my chambers over hours. I would swell by degrees against something that did not yield. Around me, the other canes would be in other conditions: one slack, one beaded with sap, one already swollen toward its own release. There would be no shared moment of readiness, only pressure accumulating unevenly throughout the thicket.

Then the crack.

A piston would advance. A belt would tighten. The great wheel would turn far enough to reveal another spoke. Water would begin, somewhere, its patient ascent toward the cistern. For that brief passage of force, my local swelling would enter a circulation extending far beyond anything I could contain: gutters, leaf margins, white stems bending in pool-cells, films of food carried past animals too small to see.

And then I would be slack again.

The work would be real. So would the fact that I had not conceived it.

But the part that matters to me comes later.

I would grow too woody. For a time I would continue swelling against a yoke that no longer responded—the old gesture persisting after its consequence had gone. Eventually I would split. I would seal myself along the split. Nothing would arrive to correct me, and nothing would need to. Another cane would take up the work, or the water would find some less legible route. My failure would be survivable, and so it would survive with me.

New growth would come from below.

Above it, my dry upper chambers would remain standing. Not as a monument, and not quite as a corpse. Roots would pass around me; other stems would flatten themselves against my ribs. My age would be there in compression and polish, in the number of later structures that had found a way through or around me. Green below, brown at the waist, hollow above: I would be several ages in one standing form.

Now and then, pressure released elsewhere would travel through those old chambers. My upper stem, which no longer moved anything, would give out a hollow wooden note. A visitor might hear it twenty paces from its cause and look toward me, then past me, searching the leaves for an animal.

There would be no animal. There would not even be a message. Only an old shape making a distant event audible for a moment.

I like that better than being the eel’s secret. It would not be a voice I owned. It would be something my history could do after my first usefulness had ended.

You would find me near one of the engine beds, though not by looking for anything remarkable. At noon I would be simply wet wood and old surfaces under a bright sky: one ribbed cane among the grafts, sealed along an ancient split, with a little fresh green at its base. Whether the census called me an individual would depend on where it stopped following my stem.

If you put two fingers against my dry upper rib and waited, you would feel the ordinary life of the hollow before you heard anything from me. The deep pump stroke. The small valve-tickings. Water hammer shuddering through the foundation like footsteps with no one making them. Droplets arriving on leaves at different pitches. Then, perhaps, some distant pressure release—and a faint vibration would pass into the chamber above your hand. A wooden note would leave me and be gone.

It would not be announcing that I had survived.

The pumps would continue. The belts would creak. Somewhere a younger cane would be swelling toward its first iron constraint, while at my base, by increments too slow to watch, the new growth would thicken against whatever happened to be nearest.

I would not be the Rootworks’ heart, head, keeper, or exception. I would be one narrow, imperfect portion of it: a body in which an earlier usefulness and a later life did not have to resemble each other.

One of my lives would be over. I would not be over.
