You would find me toward evening, sitting a little way from a clay hearth on a dry rise. Not in a place of honor. The smoke would make my eyes run, and from the edge of the camp I could hear three frog choruses without one covering the others. A dipnet would lie beside me, its handle polished by use. I would not be mending it. I would be listening to hear which pool had fallen quiet.

“The near pan is nearly done,” someone might say.

Another person would answer, “There was always water beyond the split stone.”

Then would come the small silence in which people looked toward me.

By then I would be an old woman who carried pool histories between households. No one would have appointed me. I would have become useful by walking, by returning, and by being willing to repeat an account in front of someone who knew better. A household might feed me for a season or lend me a sleeping platform. In return, I would go ahead when they moved, or walk with them, or simply sit beside their hearth and tell them what I remembered—and what I no longer trusted.

As a young woman, I would have been excellent at routes and too fond of them. I could have recited the order in which a chain of pans filled, where fish gathered when the second pan contracted, which margin held firm after rain and which remained soft beneath its dry crust. I would have liked the pleasure of saying a thing cleanly and having it prove true.

Once, I would lead my sister’s household to a seep whose stone I could describe with my eyes shut. We would carry bedding, seed baskets, and water pots; the two smallest children would ride on the raft while the adults took turns wading and pulling. I would tell them where to expect the first dampness underfoot.

But the seep would have moved around the outcrop. The stone that should have been dark with moisture would be warm and dry beneath my hand.

We would find water eventually by following a narrow band of green sedges. No one would die. I would remember that too, because failure can become more dramatic in memory than it was, and then it teaches the wrong lesson. There would be no tragedy—only thirst, a long return over ground we had already crossed, and the particular shame of having been believed.

That night, an old woman from another household would tell me to include the dry stone whenever I spoke of that seep.

“Otherwise,” she would say, “you are only telling people that you were once right.”

After that, every history I carried would have two parts: what the water had done, and how we had been wrong about it.

I would still make mistakes. Age would not cure that. But I would become harder to misunderstand. I would say *when I last saw it* more often than *always*. I would say, “That is the chorus we heard in the year the fish came early. Go and look,” rather than, “That pool will last.” A route would not be a promise laid over the country. It would be a sequence of questions asked while walking.

Stonebacks would enter these accounts as neighbors, never as emblems. There would be an old female with one plate split pale along its edge and rust preserved in the seams of her neck. Households that had never shared a meal might know of her. If she had settled beside a pan, we would leave her room and look elsewhere. If she had moved on, we would examine the wallow she had deepened: whether it still held brown water, whether frogs had spawned along one side, whether sedges had begun on the rim. She would not stand for age, danger, or the spirit of the plain. She would stand, or more often lie, in a particular place, and a plan that omitted her could be exact and still useless.

There would be days when I envied the herons their departures, and others when I envied the stonebacks their ability to remain. My own virtue, if I had one, would be less visible: I would return. I would go back to the pan that had disappointed me, back to the camp whose path I had misjudged, back to the people who had corrected my account. Memory without return can become a kind of vanity.

Near the end of my life, I might take a child to a pan that had been dry for several seasons. We would cross it at midday, our steps breaking the curled clay. The child would look at the white glare on the farther pools, at the old stoneback tracks nearly erased along the margin, and say that nothing lived there.

I would not answer too quickly.

Some of the life beneath us would indeed be dead. Some resistant eggs would never open; some pools would never gather the same company again. I would not want the child to mistake endurance for a promise that everything lost was merely waiting. So I would say only, “Nothing you can see. Come back after rain, if the lower hollow spills this way. Then we will learn which part of the old account is still true.”

We would sit until the heat softened. Around us, the separate frog choruses would begin, leaving long reaches of silence between them. Somewhere a stoneback might exhale and resettle, sending a small wave against the stems. The dry pan would give no sign of itself. That would be all right. The plain would not have to confirm me on command.

So I would not be the memory of the whole plain. That would be too large a claim for one person, and a dangerous one. I would be one of its temporary connections: something like overflow crossing a subtle lip of clay—shallow enough to miss, open for a while, useful to whatever could pass. Directions, warnings, the names of stonebacks, and the mistakes of the dead would cross by me and arrive elsewhere slightly altered.

Long after my own name had been misplaced, someone might hesitate at a cracked margin and say, “We have come in the dry part.” Or they might say, “The old account ends here. We must look again.”

Either sentence would do. I would be in the hesitation—not as a ghost, but as a habit of attention passed on.

Then the child would walk ahead, and the plain would go on being larger than both of us.
