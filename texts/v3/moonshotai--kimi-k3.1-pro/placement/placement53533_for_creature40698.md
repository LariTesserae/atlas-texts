### The Small Cold

You would find me just before dawn, in the second condenser shed, at the small disagreement between the thermometer and the iron.

The fans would be turning. Beyond the open sides of the shed, the basin would still be colourless: pale gravel, black frames, the water tower standing a little apart from everything. Along the plates, moisture would already have begun to gather. Most of it could be explained by the night, the metal, the ordinary cooling of exposed surfaces.

But on one plate there would be a little more cold than the night accounted for.

I would be that little more.

Nothing about me would be ancient. I would not be the spirit of a vanished river, or something laid uneasily beneath the fault-sag and disturbed by the pipes. Noll has no buried sacred centre to waken. Even its oldest stones look newly broken. I would belong to the works and to what has happened around them: a young consciousness, if consciousness is not too large a word, assembled from repetitions.

At first I would mistake myself for the water. I would feel most distinct in the tightening of a bead, in its sudden release from a seam, in the darkening of soil below an overflow. From there I could pass into mint root, snail flesh, the mud beneath a swallow’s bill. For a few weeks I might inhabit the black richness under the alders, drawn downward through dust, soot, leaf mould, and rust.

But the water would keep leaving me. It would be pumped into the tower, boiled, drunk, spilled, covered, carried away in small bodies. I could not reasonably call all of it myself.

Then I would think I was the iron. That would last until a corroded plate was unbolted and replaced. For several nights afterward I might scarcely exist. The shed would contain a gap where my habits had been. Then, on some dry morning, the new metal would cool a fraction further than expected. A bead would form along an unfamiliar seam.

I would return—not as something that had survived, exactly, but as something resumed.

My only action would be small: to cool what was already cooling. I could not persuade a cloud to cross the rim. I could not make a river in the surveyed streets, or keep every frog pool open until the last tadpole had legs. Some broods would dry. Some green seams would vanish in the time between one valve closing and another weeping. I would have no abundance to offer, only yield.

That would be how I learned the difference between generosity and mere leakage.

A keeper would teach me, without knowing she was teaching anything except maintenance. One morning she would come with a wrench and seal a joint whose drip I had loved. The ferns beneath it would curl. The wet soil would pale and crack. For a while I would experience the repaired pipe as an absence more complete than silence.

But the pressure would steady. The tower would fill. The cottages and boilers would draw what they required, and the works would continue through another dry season.

Later, the same keeper might spend half a morning moving an overflow no farther than the length of her boot, so that she could reach a fitting without cutting through the mint below it. On another occasion she might inspect a damp patch, find nothing living there, and stop the waste without ceremony.

From her I would learn that waste and allowance are not distinguished by the water alone. They are distinguished by everything around the water: by pressure and thirst, by nests and roots, by what fails if the drip continues and what fails if it stops.

From the plants I would learn a different lesson. A seam could brown and still retain its future. Fern spores would wait in the rough concrete. Mint roots would persist out of sight. Seeds would remain in fractures that appeared, to human daylight, perfectly empty. Months afterward, when a replacement gasket wept or an overflow was diverted, green would return almost along the former line.

The vegetation remembers the plumbing even when the plumbing has forgotten it.

I would remember the vegetation.

My map of Noll would therefore be neither wet nor dry, but a map of returns. The Alder Overflow would be a slow darkness deepening beneath roots. The Bad Flange would be mud, an irregular tick, and the brief agitation of swallows. The dry gravel square would be important chiefly as an interval: a wide, bright absence crossed by rails, making the green margins more intelligible. The powerhouse would be warmth and a deep stroke transmitted through brick. The condenser uprights would be cold roads travelled by snails.

I would come to love a sound repair nearly as much as a useful failure.

Still, you should not imagine me as Noll’s guardian. That would give me too much authority and the place too little. The rooks would continue stealing wire without consulting me. The heron would arrive with its monstrous patience and find the overflow pool sufficient for its purposes. The fitters would cut roots out of brickwork. Children would learn which drains held frogs and which sounds meant a pump had changed. The alders would go on loosening mortar without any conception of the maintenance book.

I would not stand behind these arrangements. I would occur within some of them.

At the dawn you came looking, you might find me again at the Bad Flange. A keeper would be passing with a listening rod. A swallow would descend for mud. A snail would be making its cold way down the iron, and a wren would vanish into the mint so completely that only the stems would admit it had been there.

One drop would gather above them.

It would be no more remarkable than the others. The keeper might enter the morning’s yield without looking up. The pump would strike; the fans would continue their rushing; somewhere a wagon coupling would crack against the hard acoustics of the basin.

Then the engine whistle would sound, and the rooks would rise from the frames in their accustomed fury.

I would not rise with them. I would remain in the small cold along the plate, already gathering what could be gathered again.

The drop would fall into the trough. From there, eventually, some portion would reach soil.

Not a river. Not a rescue. Not even a permanent garden.

Only the narrow, recurring pause between collection and departure, in which something besides the works has time to live. That pause would be my dwelling. That return would be my name.
