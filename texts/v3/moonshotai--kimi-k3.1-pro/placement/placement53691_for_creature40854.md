I would first have to stop looking for somewhere to stand.

My instinct would be to enter as a walker: to learn the courts by resonance, to find a wall by its warmth before touching it, to wait on a narrow bridge for the weak gray minute. But a walker would misunderstand almost everything merely by having needs. A covered walk would become shelter declined; a cistern, water withheld; an empty flat, a distance to be crossed. I would turn consequences into accommodations and then feel abandoned by them. The city does not require that pathos, and I would not want to bring it.

Nor would I be the gray visibility, though that offers the temptation of knowing. For less than a minute, I could apprehend the rim, the flats, the scattered precincts, the exposed relations between streets of different ages. But that would give me extent without location. I could see every exposed connection and remain less involved than a film of water climbing a stair. When sight ceased, nothing in me would have been altered by heat, current, or chemistry. I would have been a witness the basin never produced, not one of its sparse persistences.

To live there, I would have to become smaller than a habitat and less continuous than a body.

A fan beside an overflow stone would be close. Closed, I would be nearly indistinguishable from a waxen mineral flake. Rain alone would not open me. My existence would depend on the meeting of two waters, on their difference being renewed without either current becoming violent enough to strip me away. Opening would not be awakening or reaching. Water would enter old folds; mineral-supported layers would separate; my two faces would come into contact with slightly different currents.

Even the sweet odor would not announce me. It would escape as the seals dissolved, becoming noticeable only after most of the opening had already occurred. There is something fitting in that lateness: a sign without a recipient, arriving too late to be an invitation, belonging more to the process than to anything that might perceive it.

But a fan remains beside its stone. It has a local history, a *here* to which it belongs. I think my place would be more precarious than that. I would be what the fan releases.

One thread, then—but not a tiny traveler enclosed in a white filament. Most of me would be sheath. Here and there, separated by long stretches in which nothing metabolized, would be the living points that justified the pronoun. There would be no witness enclosed within that sheath, no central part of me observing the rest. “I” would mean only a fragile continuity of possibility along a mineral line.

And I would have no special exemption from failure. Some of my living points might already be dead when the fan released me. I would not be setting out to found another growth or complete a journey. Founding would be something that happened, if it happened at all, only after many conditions I could not choose had been met.

My whereabouts could not be given properly as a court or quarter. My address would be a schedule: after this overflow had begun, while water from below still retained its particular warmth and dissolved load, before some narrowing passage elsewhere altered the route. The dry names would belong to another kind of observer.

An hour of bare ground might separate the fan that released me from the pool in which I later appeared. It would not be an hour of travel for me. There would be no dry ground in my history, no crossing, no memory of having been elsewhere. Only current, and the chemistries that current successively permitted. Water could climb a stair without making me a climber. I could pass beneath a mineral bridge twice without the second passage being a return. Recognition would require the dry city; I would belong to the wet one.

Its impossible adjacencies would not feel impossible. They would be the only nearness I had.

During the fullest rain, other threads might enter the same temporary pool. *Society* would be too strong a word for that. So would *loneliness*. Both would imply the possibility of turning toward or away. Nothing would recognize me, and I would recognize nothing. Yet water that had passed a felt in another precinct might alter the chemistry around one of my living points. A remote seam would thus enter the conditions of my continuation without sending news, without even remaining remote in the only geography then authoritative.

That would be the largest I could become: not a connected awareness, not part of a watershed-mind, but a brief dependence on adjacencies no pavement showed.

The gray visibility would add nothing. If some external witness saw me, I would be a faint milky stroke in water that had seemed untouched. I would hardly correct the appearance of emptiness. That seems right. I would not be the secret abundance that made the city full after all. I would be one of the small reasons its emptiness was not quite identical to absence.

When outflow came, my route would not gradually lengthen for me. It would cease to be a route. Water might drain away, or simply cease to occupy the depth around me. My sheath could settle on paving above any level the remaining pool appeared to have held. The living points would fail as their slight chemical differences failed. There would be no struggle to return; return belongs to a geography of persistent places.

The next rain might lift the filament again. An observer could mistake that motion for continuation. But the sheath would be light enough to travel long after there was nothing alive along it. Perhaps it would lodge against a rib and hold a film of water a little longer than bare stone. Perhaps deposition would thicken there by almost nothing. That would not be a legacy, and certainly not a contribution to the city’s design. Only a small physical consequence of having remained.

So I would not be a citizen, a witness, or the city dreaming. I would be a discontinuous thread of life in its wet geography: mostly mineral, briefly viable, located by the order of connections rather than by stone. My body might outlast my life without preserving it. My only proximity to other life would be the water’s, and I would bear the city’s history without knowing any part of it.

While the route held, though, I would be exactly where life could be—not in a court, not even simply in water, but in the slight difference that water made available. For that interval, I would not inhabit a place. I would inhabit the fact that two places had become adjacent for the current.

The rain would keep the city. It would not keep me safe. When the districts drew apart, there would be no long exile across the flats. Only a pale stroke on warm stone, perhaps, marking no former waterline; and later rain lifting it—shape without life, motion without a journey.
