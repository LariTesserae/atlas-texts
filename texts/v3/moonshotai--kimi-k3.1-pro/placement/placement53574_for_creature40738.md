I would find myself in a missing beat.

Close to the spire, in an old yoke-loop beside a quartz vein, one segment has died. Its neighbors have drawn the gap almost closed, but not quite. A contraction crosses the chain, hesitates, and reaches the enclosed wheel a little late. The wheel is lopsided: one broad, ancient sector opposite a crowded fan of young rays. Its turning gives the association’s ticking a small, recurring flaw.

I would arrive as a juvenile yoke-eel, while I was still able to travel. That would be the quickest portion of my life, though quick only by the Kettle’s standards. The water would be crowded with hazards that had no malice in them: the pressure pulse of a comb-bell, a fin-slip folded nearly to nothing in a crevice, shells ticking shut in a long sequence beneath some enormous shadow. I would not see the loop as an association. I would know a taste of iron and injured tissue, a narrow exposure of stone, a place where the pressure faltered.

My mouth would seal against another eel’s tail. At first I would answer the contractions badly—too late, then too hard. The animals on either side would correct around me. I would feed through the joint that did not quite close my gut, and my body would thicken into the interval. Gradually the wheel’s missing beat would become harder to hear. Then, for a while, it would be gone.

That would be the end of my traveling, but not of my appetite. Nothing in the change would be a dedication. I would not be offering myself to the wheel, the vein, or the hanging water. I would be hungry; particles would come; my muscles would grow. Passing on the contraction would simply be what my grown body did well. The rest—the turning, the mineral taken from the brine, the almost imperceptible tightening at a boundary I could not sense—would be consequence.

For a while I would think of one neighbor as before me and the other as after. Later those ideas would lose their usefulness. There would be no message traveling around the loop and no end toward which it moved. There would only be the state of one body, then mine, then another. If one of us was injured, the knowledge would arrive as hesitation. If the brine grew poor, I would know that too, but not why.

Then the silence would come.

The bass contraction would loosen. Sounds that had always been buried together would separate: a valve, a rasp, one shell closing on the rock. My neighbors would pull at different times. The wheel would coast, and the membranes between its rays would sag into unfamiliar folds. For the first time since settling, I would be able to distinguish my own small movements from the pulse passing through me. It would not feel like freedom. It would feel like losing the shape of the water.

When the current returned, it would come from the other side. Before and after would exchange places without either neighbor moving. My body would be slow to learn this, and for a time the ticking would carry a new imperfection. That one would be mine.

Perhaps my loop would never recover its old evenness. An older segment might fail; the wheel might keep a tilt; fin-slips might leave and others take their places. None of this would need to be repaired into sameness. I would grow older within the altered rhythm, no longer new, one animal among hundreds, my whole geography consisting of touch, hunger, and timing.

I would never see the silver lens from the valleys. My Kettle would be two neighbors, the taste of food at an unclosed joint, and the pressure that had to be answered. I would not be its keeper, or a component in a machine. I would be a hunger that had found an interval, a body briefly interposed between one contraction and the next.

Far beyond anything I could sense, the boundary would tighten by almost nothing. The silver drop would remain motionless over the valleys.

Inside, I would answer the next contraction. And the sea, for another while, would not finish becoming rain.
