### The Outermost Stem

You would find me at the dry edge of a grove, where the dimness beneath a break in the roof gives way to a pale court.

Not among the old trunks at its center. Farther out, beyond the last curtain of climbing stems, where the leaves stand apart and the soil becomes too thin to hide the street beneath it. There is a low stem there, bent almost horizontal, its surface gray and checked. Three of its leaves have been cropped to the midrib. At its tip, one new leaf remains tightly folded.

That is the part of me you would notice first.

I would be that stem, but not only that stem. Behind it runs a staggered line of ropey trunks, some leaning against one another, some rising from wood that has spent centuries lying on the ground. My first shape was a fall. A trunk came down; its injured tissues closed where they could; roots entered the dust beneath it. Where the fallen stem remained alive, new growth rose. Some of the old wood has since died back. Other sections have thickened until the original fall is difficult to find.

Call me one tree if you like. The distinction is yours. My growth has never required it.

The city is not, to me, a city. It is the difference between light that can support a leaf and light that cannot; between dust that merely covers a root and dust that feeds it; between the vibration of settling stone and the deeper vibration of wings. The court beyond my last stem is a poorness I have not yet entered. The old masonry behind me is shade, resin-scent, and a hard pathway for tremors. Sometimes a section moves, and for a while every root in me meets a changed ground.

Light reaches us indirectly, already weakened by its passage through crooked openings and pale stone. When it strengthens, the change comes as a faint permission. More often than not, nothing visible answers. An old leaf remains. A root tip waits. The folded leaf at my stem’s end stays folded.

Each leaf keeps its water behind wax. When one is torn away, I do not replace it simply because there is space and light. Replacement must be afforded twice: first in the tissue itself, then in what that tissue may lose while it is still young. A new leaf is not a promise. It is a risk continued from one graying to the next.

At an old split farther down my stem, masons sometimes gather while the resin is still soft. They come so quietly that I know them mainly as small interruptions in the closing of the wound. Their jaws work; their pale bodies press briefly against the exposed surface; then they withdraw with what they can carry.

The wound takes longer to seal. That is the whole of my opinion.

Somewhere beyond my sensing, the resin may be holding a grain against other grains. It may be part of a flue, a shelf, a narrow cell in which a cricket calls. Perhaps a gecko takes the mason before it reaches the wall. Perhaps the resin is recovered later by another colony and used again. A little of me may thus be standing in darkness, or passing through bodies, or lying powdered beneath feet. I have no way to follow it. The stem closes around its absence.

The Chorus reaches me through the ground.

At its height, the dust around my finest roots seems never quite to settle. The lower wing-roar comes through the old trunks as a pressure without distinct strokes. Cricket pulses arrive more sharply, each one made larger by the cell that contains it. I cannot distinguish courtship from threat, occupancy from warning. There is only more vibration, and then less, and then more again.

When a hunter strikes the canopy, hundreds of small grips release at once.

For an instant, the leaves hold almost nothing but themselves. The moths rise around the trunks like ash lifted without fire. Their wings make no separate shape in the general roar. Then they settle, and leaf and animal become indistinguishable again.

Beneath the older foliage, my flowers open pallid and close to the wood. They offer very little: pollen, oil, sometimes a film of nectar nearly too thick to move. The moths come for that little. Most of the seeds that follow will be carried into places where they cannot grow. My nearer future travels underground.

If I remember, I remember there.

The richest soil beneath my oldest trunks was made above them. For generations, dry waste fell from a roost into nearly the same place. Beetles worked it. Crickets and larvae passed through it. Fine roots entered the galleries they left. By the time the first of my stems thickened there, the falling and the feeding had become a depth into which more roots could go.

If I have a memory of wings, it is that depth.

At my outer edge, there was another richness. A young grazer once stopped on the sterile crossing just beyond my shade. For a while it remained motionless, as grazers do, resembling one more rounded stone. Then the small repetition of its breathing ceased.

The local hurry followed. Crickets found the exposed softness. Beetles entered and opened. A gecko took some of the beetles. What could be carried was carried; what could be crushed was crushed; what remained was clipped and abraded into smaller remnants. Long after the last visible sign had gone, empty galleries persisted in the dust.

My furthest root found them later.

Nothing in that root is a memorial. It is simply one of the reasons another stem could be afforded.

Perhaps the line of me will continue across the court. Perhaps the roost will shift before the soil is deep enough, or a vault will settle and alter the light. One of the old trunks may fail, and for the beetles there will be a great deal to do. My remaining growth may narrow to a single root, a single layered stem, a single place where continuation has not yet become impossible.

For now, in the gradual graying, the outermost leaf has begun to open.

It is deep plum, and softer than the others, though only by comparison. A moth touches it and leaves. No grazer is on the path. Behind me, the old trunks hold their dark layers beneath the dim roof. Beneath me, a root follows the turn of an abandoned gallery. Above, something disturbs the roost, and the vault becomes wings.

The roar passes through my wood, through the dust, through the wall where a little of my resin may still be holding one grain against another.

The court remains empty.

My leaf opens by less than the width of a grain.

For me, there is no larger event.
