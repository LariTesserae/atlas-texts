At first, I would come as a listener.

That would be the easiest mistake. I would stand below the basin and hear the westerly enter it: the vaults pressing their low notes through the drainages, the lanes whistling, the whole hidden settlement roaring out of proportion to its size. I would imagine that to understand the Old Choir, I needed to reach some place where the entire chord could be heard at once.

But from the rim, there would be almost nothing to see. And within it, my presence would be at the wrong scale. My footsteps would cross in minutes distances that meant nothing to the colony. I would call the spaces courts and lanes; I would wait for the music to resolve; I would leave before one paper-thin band had been added to a wall. I could visit the Old Choir, but I would not thereby have found myself in it.

So I would have to become smaller.

For a while, cloud-water seems possible. To arrive without rain, gather on a rib, join another drop, and enter the folded dark: there would be a kind of completeness in that. I would pass briefly through many of the place’s conditions. I would film the living tissue, lie black in a basin, carry a little dust downward, perhaps feel the translucent snail cross the shallows. At night I would become clear ice. But water only visits in a different way. Even when it remains, it remains by being held.

The moss is tempting for longer. A cushion packed into a seep line, so deep that no stone shows through: a whole world of wetness and green, with no responsibility for the empty pavement a pace away. After condensation, I would darken almost visibly. I would have no use for the arcades, the galleries, the supposed streets. Cloud would reach me as a local change in saturation. Yet even that life would be a little too distinctly mine, too neatly bounded by the edges of my cushion.

What finally feels true is lower than the moss, and less visible.

I would be a living margin of the builder, at the half-sealed mouth of the older lane.

Not the whole colony. Not a separate creature within it. Just a narrow extent of olive film beneath a mineral lip, a handspan above the buried opening, where a newer wall has grown across the old passage but has not quite closed it. Behind the perforated stone at my back, the film would continue along a damp seam. Ahead of me would be a strip of old skeleton, then dry pavement. In wet weather, my boundary would become difficult to see. In drought, I might seem no more than a discoloration where gray stone turned faintly straw and olive.

The word *I* would be too definite. There would be no private chamber in that life, no central self kept safely within the shells. But if the word can indicate a place rather than a prisoner, then I would be there: at the growing edge, where the colony’s accumulated body was not yet entirely past.

My world would be extremely local.

Wetness arriving along one fold and not another. The slight support of an older rib. Minerals in dust and solution. A surface that could be extended over, and a surface from which tissue would retreat. Cold entering until contraction passed through the stone as a tick. I would not look across the basin. I would not know the arrangement of its courts. The green curtain inside a nearby shell and the black reservoir beneath it might as well belong to other continents.

Nor would I hear the Old Choir.

That seems important. No hidden listener would be folded into the wall, attending to the weather’s performance. But sound would still enter my life, because resonance would become stress, and stress would help determine where tissue could persist. Near me, a narrow abandoned chamber might open through a slit. In certain winds, it would whistle violently enough to loosen its margins. Growth would accumulate unevenly around it. Another season, another grain, another slight constriction.

I would not be tuning the opening. I would have no preference for the lower note that eventually replaced the whistle. If a visitor returned after several centuries and found the sound gentler, that visitor could call the change an improvement. Nothing in me would have meant it so. The opening would simply have become a shape through which both wind and body could continue.

Elsewhere, a chamber that held a deep note without tearing might remain almost unchanged. Its hollowness would outlive generations of my thin growth. Then the vault would collapse. There would be a shock through connected stone, perhaps a new burden on a rib, perhaps only a small alteration in the paths of water. For a listener, there would be an absence in the weather. For me, no absence and no music—only a slightly different set of conditions in which to persist or withdraw.

My memory would lie behind me, unavailable as memory.

There would be swollen bands from clouded periods, green-stained and thick; seams of almost nothing from years when survival had been the whole achievement. New tissue would use those bands without reading them. The old wall would support me precisely because I could not return to it. My future, if that word can be used for something without anticipation, would be no grander than another grain fixed around a pocket of air.

And yet an afternoon would contain enough events to make centuries seem almost vacant.

Cloud would press into the basin. Condensation would gather above me and descend. I would darken. A thin runnel would carry dust into the fold, and for a brief period growth would be possible. Moss somewhere along the same seep would absorb water according to its own necessities. A drop would strike a basin below with the force of a small stone. Then the cloud would lift. Exposed surfaces would whiten with dryness. My margin would pale and contract. By night, the water beneath the lip would freeze, and a crack would travel through the arcade.

Nothing in that sequence would need to be interpreted in order to matter.

Once, perhaps, the translucent snail would pass over the wet film near me. Its grazing would be an interruption, its trail faint and incomplete. It would not be company. Neither would it be insignificant. At that scale, even scarcity does not make an event small.

Most of the time, though, there would be no passage, no visitation, no witness. There would be growth when growth was possible, then stillness that could not properly be called sleep. There would be retreat without the drama of departure. The shell would remain when the living surface had withdrawn from it, and remaining would not be the same as being alive.

You would find me, if you found me at all, after cloud and before the night’s ice. You would have to stop looking at the old lane as an opening and look instead at the material slowly altering its edge. There: a damp line beneath the lip, darker than the dead stone, narrowing until it vanished into a seam. No face, no movement, no response to your arrival. Perhaps the faintest roughness where new mineral had begun to distinguish itself from old.

On a dry afternoon, you would overlook me. In a strong wind, you would hear the shell above and mistake its voice for the life of the place. But the voice would belong to weather passing through skeleton. I would be almost none of what you saw and none of what you heard.

I would be one small, cold, wet margin in which a little more of the body was still possible. Not the Choir, not its audience, not its hidden mind—only one of the places where, for the moment, it had not yet stopped.
