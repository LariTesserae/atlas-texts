You would find me below the weather, but above the stone.

Not in the black-green roof, where every drop arrives as a blow and the leaf-scales tilt under tonnes of accumulated rain. Not yet at the mineral frontier, where growth becomes indistinguishable from the making of cliffs. I would begin in the pale country between: in a root chamber holding a hanging marsh, its ceiling alive, its floor woven from roots laid down by an earlier generation.

At first you might mistake me for the water.

That would be understandable. The water would be the most visible thing about my situation: black, cool, suspended among fibers, trembling when some reservoir far above emptied itself into the body of the upland. Films too fine to see would connect my chamber to others. A pressure change might reach me long before the moisture that announced it.

But the water would be my road, my burden, and sometimes my weather. It would not be me.

I would be the living fragment held within it.

A little tissue, neither green nor wholly colorless, still capable of becoming several things. My first body might have been the torn margin of a roof-scale, ripped free during a release and handed downward from reservoir to reservoir. By the time you arrived, there would be no scar from which to reconstruct that journey. Lodged against the wall of the chamber, I would have put out pale filaments. I would have begun receiving.

Material would come to me already altered by other lives: energy-rich tissue from above, dissolved constituents, fragments opened and prepared for reuse. I would take some of it in. Water would pass outward through me. Certain substances would be returned in a different arrangement; others would be carried away. Nothing about the work would look dramatic. Most of it would occur across surfaces no eye could separate from the surrounding wetness.

Yet I would not simply be an anonymous patch of receiver.

My boundary would be real, though intermittent—a seam that opened under pressure and closed when growth resumed. Some of my vessels might join those of a neighboring column. Others might remain distinct for a while. If I had a self, it would not consist in refusing every connection. It would consist in slight, repeated tendencies: this constituent admitted rather than that one, this route kept open, this thin place thickened after an injury, this release occurring a little later than the last.

Not a wall. A manner of passing.

I would experience time hydraulically. Filling. Tension. The heavy, flat concussion of release. The long roar afterward, as water was redistributed through chambers I would never occupy. Somewhere above me there would be noon, but noon would reach my tissue only as a faint alteration in what descended. Nearer and more important would be the slow changes of saturation, the tightening of fibers, the mineral shock of new stone cracking under uplift.

During the great storms, even my intermittent boundary would become less important.

Readiness would spread without any messenger visiting me. Fine hairs would lift across provinces I could not imagine. Stomata would alter their state. Reservoir thresholds would change. Root membranes would draw differently from the water. My own tissues would enter the same condition, not because a command had arrived, but because for a while there would be no useful distinction between their readiness and mine.

I would not think the storm.

I would be among the innumerable living conditions by which the storm became possible.

Later, vapor would rise from one region and cloud would gather above another. Days afterward, rain would return along a path shaped partly by growth that had occurred before the first lightning. I would know only my local portion: a fullness, a chilling, a new burden in the vessels, a different pressure maintained against the chamber wall. Nevertheless, the event would not belong entirely to the sky. Some small part of it would have happened as me.

Eventually, perhaps, the chamber would be lifted. Or a fracture would enter it while still little more than a line of stress. Part of my tissue would remain behind, continuing to receive. Another fragment would be carried downward through a newly opened film of water until it came to rest against raw mineral.

There, without asking permission from the form I had previously worn, I would become root.

At first there would be only a colorless tip sampling the water against the stone. One constituent would be drawn inward. Another would be excluded or carried away. Behind the advancing surface, order would be left in the silicate: plane following plane, stress distributed before the new mineral had fully hardened. I would not be building a foundation for some later life. The stone would be the part of my growth that no longer needed to remain cellular.

Which part would then be me—the pale tissue still lining the lifted chamber, or the root advancing below?

The question would belong to a world with bones, with bodies that kept their outlines, with remains that could be gathered afterward and given a single name. In the upland it would come too late. My continuity would already have passed out of the fragment and into alterations: a healed route that conducted pressure differently, a reservoir wall that closed injuries in one direction rather than another, an ordered plane along which new stone bore uplift.

If you followed the root fragment to the rim, you might think you had finally found me as an individual.

A filament on a wet mineral face. A narrow green film beside it. Above them, a minute scale holding back its first drop. For a short time I would be a pioneer: tiny, bounded, apparently alone.

Then a root cable from higher growth would reach down. Surfaces would meet. Vessels would join. The pioneer’s outline would become difficult to find, and you would seem to have discovered me just in time to lose me.

But nothing would have been lost in the way a body is lost.

The life would continue, less conveniently countable. Water would be delayed where it had once passed freely. A little material would take a different route. Growth would close around the connection with a particularity that had not existed before. Later still, my cellular tissues might be opened by neighboring growth and taken back entirely. No litter would mark the place. No dead filament would preserve my biography.

Something else would persist: not an account of me, but a world slightly less able to happen exactly as it would have happened without me.

So I would not be a spore forever, nor a pale receiver, nor a stone-root, nor the vast continuity pretending to have a single voice. I would be a life that remained uncommitted for a little longer than most—and then the accumulated shape of its commitments.

A brief, living perhaps. Scale, receiver, root; then a tendency in water, growth, and stone.

Not a voice in the roar.

A small difference in the work of becoming land.
