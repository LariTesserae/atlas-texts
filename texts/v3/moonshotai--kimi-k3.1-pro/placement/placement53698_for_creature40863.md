At first I would put myself in the wrong place: near the middle of the hollow, on one of the firm islands, listening. I would want to sort the sounds by their storms—to know that this bright note belonged to last night, and that knock beneath the basin to a season already gone. But that would turn the hollow into a performance and me into the reason for it. The place has no such vacancy. Even the dark bird, looking in from the rim, finds no reason to stay.

So I would have to become smaller, and less well informed.

A spoon-leaf, then. But not the leaf alone—the leaf is only the visible gesture. I would be the old, continuing body beneath its succession of leaves: orange roots following wetness through the cracks, fungal threads bound into the little nourishment available, offsets slowly replacing offsets.

You would first notice me on a low root island between the crescent wall and the basin. Not at the center. A spoon-shaped leaf would stand clear of the surrounding moss on its long stem, its whole outline visible against wet stone. Behind me, dark liverwort would mark the dependable edge of a seam; a little farther away, the green would pale and change texture where the water became less certain. The nearest tree would stand apart. The open rim would be brightness and wind, nothing I needed to approach.

My world would be local: weight gathering in the cup, tension entering the stem, moisture at the roots, a slight difference in the minerals brought by one release of water and another. Other weathers would sound around me, but I would answer only mine. The basin might tick with old rain while I remained nearly empty in clear sunlight. Or water held by the wall for days might reach me after the moss all around had finished returning its brief shower.

Filling would not feel like preparation. There would be no approaching performance, only the slow alteration of a balance. Mist would add almost nothing, and add it again. The seam would shine, then yield. The leaf would grow heavier until holding and pouring were separated by almost no distance at all.

Then the stem would bend.

Water would slip over the lip, the leaf would empty, and below there would be one stroke absurdly loud for so small a life. For an instant I would occupy more of the hollow than my body could. But nothing would have been announced. The pulse would go down into my own stem and moss, concentrated where scattered wetting might have been lost. The sound would be the water’s accident, not my intention.

Afterward, the stem would rise. Filling would begin again, though perhaps not for hours.

The leaf would eventually die. Another would take its place, not always from exactly the same point. Given enough time, my apparent position could remain convincing while my living tissue traveled by small continuities across the root island. That would be my persistence: not defending an outline, but remaining through replacements. A rare green flower close to the root crown would matter more than any number of bright afternoons. A good year might add little more than a root, an offset, a slight thickening where water reliably passed.

And sometimes I would alter something beyond my small weather.

A root would press farther into a crack. A passage would narrow. Somewhere behind the mineral skin, water would find a different edge to fall from. A high ticking note might become less frequent; a drop might begin striking stone instead of a leaf. I would not know the nine voices well enough to miss what had changed. There would be no moment in which I understood myself as a cause.

So you would find me low and a little off-center, easy to overlook while listening to the hollow as a whole. I would not be its keeper, its spirit, or the ear that completed its chorus. I would be one small delay in the mountain’s water: mostly roots and waiting, occasionally the green relief of an emptying leaf.

My note, when it came, would be brief and disproportionate. The other voices would not answer. Water would descend into my own moss, and I would rise again.

Beneath me, a root would have thickened by almost nothing. Years later, rain not yet fallen would come out of the mountain by a slightly different path, and make a different sound.
