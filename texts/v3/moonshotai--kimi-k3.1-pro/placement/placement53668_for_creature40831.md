At first I would put myself on the rim.

It is the natural place for an observer: above the wet crowns, with the basins succeeding one another toward the chalky distance. I could mistake that immensity for knowledge. I could say, *This is the High Clamor*, and mean the overcast, the white stone, the mist keeping its level, the sound without direction.

But the longer I remained there in imagination, the more that position would feel like a way of not being in the place. I would have a view and no habitat. Distance would be available to my eyes and denied to my body. To be alive here, I think, would mean surrendering the panorama.

The water would then be an easy self to choose, and a false one. It moves without having to remain. The stone remains without having to respond. The roar fills everything without having to listen. I would need a narrower existence than any of those: one with something to lose.

So I would go below the visible map.

Not into one of the broad, beautiful pools, but into a stained slot where deep seepage meets aerated water beneath an overhang. There would be a little iron at the back of the cavity, a current renewing itself across the opening, and a sparse film on one submerged face of quartz. The whole refuge might be only a few handspans wide.

There, I would be one folded grazer.

From above, I would be almost nothing: a clear thickening beneath a stone, the mineral visible through my back, my outline drawn inward against the current. I would not see my own transparency. I would not know the height of the basin, the shape of the cloud, or even the High Clamor as sound. Its totality would contain no news for me.

My geography would be more exact. A roughness where contact held well. A smoother grain where it did not. Pressure increasing along one margin. Oxygen diminishing near the rear of the cavity. The faint chemical nearness of food, and the still fainter indication that the film was becoming sparse.

The changelessness above would not be mine. A weakening of the seep too slight to alter one note in the roar could divide my life into before and after. A little recovery on a grazed surface could be abundance. A surge through the opening could make the whole stone suddenly questionable.

When I moved, it would not be exploration in the heroic sense. There would be no destination beyond the next viable contact. My forward margin would loosen and extend into the water; current, chemistry, and texture would pass along it; then a small region would fasten, and part of the body would follow. An inquiry conducted without language: *Does the habitable world continue this far?*

Most of my life would consist of holding on. But holding on would not be doing nothing. Contact would have to be renewed. Feeding would have to remain slight enough that the film could recover. Injuries would have to be repaired. To call this courage would make it too large. To call it mere endurance would make it too passive. It would be the ordinary, exact work of responding to what was actually there.

Eventually I would leave no shell, no bone, no readable stain. The stone would owe me no commemoration. Yet while I lived, my history would not be nothing merely because the basin did not record it.

And if I had to name myself more precisely still, I would not be the transparent body whole. I would be the interval at its forward edge: the old hold not yet abandoned, the new one not yet certain, cold water moving across the small senses between them. Not escape. Not conquest. Just the perilous discovery, made again and again, that the next fraction of the world could be lived in.

You would find me there, beneath an unremarkable stone, under the roar’s notice. The same white thread would flash above me. The basin would go on repeating its motion.

I, for a little while, would be continuing.
