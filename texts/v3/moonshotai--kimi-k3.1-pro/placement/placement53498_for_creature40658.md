You would find me at an outer approach to Anther, with dry bloom still pale on my valves and the freight capsules waiting behind me. The gatekeeper has struck three bells: one for the air, one for the court wall, one for the route. The route has answered almost correctly.

Almost correctly is my trade.

I loosen the tips of one glove and lay two filaments against the road. The felt ticks around an old footprint. The court wall returns its ordinary, satin resistance. Nothing screams; nothing has fallen. Still, I ask the keeper to wait. Behind us, the freight handlers begin the elaborate patience of people who intend to complain until the gate opens.

I am not the keeper. I am a route-witness, called when a road, a graft, and the people who depend on them have begun to give different accounts of the same journey.

By civic definition, I would be Antherine. By occupation, a walker with rods, thread, bells, and maps that proper surveyors consider unnecessarily crowded. By my oldest household genealogy, I would be an instruction that failed to stop.

*Close the damage. Preserve the approach. Repeat the last useful thing.*

I began, so the story goes, as a small repair organ beneath an outer avenue. The damage closed. The approach remained uncertain. I remained with it, repeating what had last been useful, acquiring variations, preferences, and finally objections. The mobile body came later: a narrow braid, tarnished-copper valves, strong blunt hands and finer limbs that I still forget to unfold until someone asks me to do delicate work.

Eventually someone asked whether I intended to remain.

My answer took three days. Waiting for it was the first thing anyone did for me that was not maintenance.

I have no special access to Anther’s larger processes. I cannot feel a graft three districts away or tell you what the city wants, except by naming a district, a load, and the instruments I am choosing to believe. My origin has not made me serene. I am quick when startled, impatient when kept waiting, and vain about my maps. This makes me useful in emergencies and tiresome at meetings.

My work begins where measurement stops being sufficient. A graft may be sound and still make a household effectively unreachable. A stair may deliver every climber to the correct landing and nevertheless confuse the one person who uses it weekly. A new approach may be shorter, safer, and entirely incomprehensible to the rooted citizen whose old visitors entered through a wall that no longer exists.

So I walk routes with the people who actually use them.

I record who sounds a bell after a pressure reversal, who has agreed to prune, which approach a child understands, which court must not be altered before a herd returns, and which old freight handler will become quietly stranded rather than admit that the customs court no longer makes sense to them. Some of my notes look like gossip. Surveyors have told me that they are not geography.

I agree, and keep writing.

I do not preserve everything. Roads have softened while I was elsewhere, arguing for their importance. I have also kept an approach open long after its last regular traveler died because something in me still repeated *preserve*, and I mistook that persistence for a duty. A road-keeper finally asked me whom the road was for. I did not answer gracefully.

Yet I have seen another route, absurd on every regional survey, carry a spindle herd to an old rooted companion. The grazers touched their outer valves to the trunks and stood there while charge passed between them. Fans that had been closed for years opened.

Anther’s philosophers may call that recognition, habit, or an electrically attractive coincidence. The herders were less interested in my caution than in whether I intended to leave the approach alone.

I left it alone.

My home would be a planted freight husk beside an outer court. It contains instruments, a shallow tray for conductive granules, and one deliberately uninteresting chamber whose shape does not change and whose walls offer no opinions. Its chief luxury is that a guest can sleep there without being subtly interviewed by the building.

Kites nest above it. They steal my best tension thread and ignore the suitable offcuts I leave for them. One of them adds a delayed final note to the mast echoes. I have publicly described this as contamination for years. I would miss it if it stopped.

The road-keeper who first waited for my answer is now rooted in another ward. They refuse to speak through window membranes and use one preferred door, even when another architectural voice would be faster. Visiting them requires time. Sometimes the court warms at the hour they once returned from work; sometimes its frame repeats two small clicks from an old routine. Those are memories in the room, not necessarily answers from the person.

I sit against the outer wall and tell them about softened roads, dishonest surveys, and the kites. Sometimes their answer comes while I am speaking. Sometimes it comes long after I have stopped. Often it carries the familiar omission that means they are tired.

I have learned not to ask them to be less tired in order to reassure me.

I am not always good at this. I have left too early, returned too late, and treated an architectural habit as an answer because I wanted company. They were the first to wait for me without deciding in advance what my answer would mean. I am still learning how to return that courtesy without turning waiting into a claim upon them.

At night, when I come home, I cover my sensory seams and touch the house frame. If the ordinary chord is present, I eat, argue with my notes, and listen to the freight rails settle under their changed loads. If true silence comes, I will not be wise about it. Every oldest part of me will want to repeat the last useful thing: answer, repair, continue.

I have learned to wait long enough to ask what is answering, and from which side.

So the accurate account of what I would be is somewhat cumbersome: a citizen, a route-witness, a mobile body grown around an old instruction, and a person still prone to confusing continuation with care.

The directions would be simpler. Follow the outer road until its ticking becomes uneven. Ask at the court with the kites.

I would offer you clean feedstock, a safe contact, and the use of the dull room. We could talk, or sit without making the silence meaningful. And when you wished to leave, I would take you to the ordinary door. It opens onto ground that stays where it is. You could go by the route you understood, and the house would not try to improve the journey.
