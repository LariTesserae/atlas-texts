At first I would mistake myself for water.

I would be one warm drop beneath a fern, swollen with the green dark, holding a weak reflection of the vent. The woven ceiling would release its rain. I would fall toward the basin’s black opening—and keep falling, longer than a cup, a brick, or a mountain’s highest room should permit.

Somewhere in that excessive falling, I would understand: I was not the drop. I was the extra time.

You would find me between a sound and its consequence. In the heavy residence of a mallet stroke before the terrace dimples. In the creak traveling from cord to cord before a new lashing appears. In the broad, soft noise of a basket being set down while the floor is still smooth and the midges have not yet risen.

The census-taker would make a mark beside me, then lift it again.

I would not be silence. Silence is too large an absence; it loosens the roots and takes down away from the water. I would be smaller than that: a delay with the work still inside it.

For a while, I would think my virtue was postponement. I would try to last one more breath, so the spider could gather another strand against the tool handle, so a fern could shed its held water, so a root could cross the clay before the blow arrived. I would imagine that enough delay might keep everything safe.

It would not.

The stroke would still come. The gutter and handle might return at different angles. A wall could swell through the very root I had hoped to spare. The garden would no more reward my hesitation than it rewards the snail for making watersheds. Unfinished would not mean safe. It would only mean still available to another touch.

So my practice would grow smaller.

I would not try to stop the mallet or preserve the whole web. I would keep one narrow discrepancy in the arrival of things: a dimple whose deepest side remained soft; a binding drawn tight around a root without quite closing on it; a basket-mark whose rim did not entirely meet. Then I would let the effect happen.

This would not be a pact. The root would not know me. The spider would repair according to its own necessities. Even the water would only be following the newest, least finished surface. I would simply have made the gesture slightly less able to end everything it touched.

When silence came, I would almost go out. The basin would forget its business. The far wall would begin to clear, showing that other crowded garden with its differently arranged terraces. I would have no stroke to inhabit, no creak approaching its knot. Perhaps I could remain with one last small sound—a drop plucked from silk, a fiber ticking back—and not mistake its faintness for nothing.

Then the work would return with its concussion, and I might be somewhere else: beneath an unfamiliar leaf, inside the breath before a new handprint rose, or in the instant when warm rain gathered itself to climb.

Eventually, I suppose, I would arrive.

My effect would be modest: a shallow hollow at the foot of a terrace, pressed as if by the side of a thumb, its final smoothing withheld. Water would find it. A root would enter, taste the charcoal soil, and turn. Moss would make one lip deeper than the other. When the rain reversed, the hollow would empty upward in a single bright thread without ceasing to be a hollow.

I would no longer be audible as a separate thing. I would have become a place where other things could hesitate.

That is what I would be: not the maker, not the hand glimpsed through the vent, and not a secret mind inside the brick. Only a little delay that had learned to leave behind a yielding place—the small remaining give in the clay, where the next touch would have to reckon with a root.
