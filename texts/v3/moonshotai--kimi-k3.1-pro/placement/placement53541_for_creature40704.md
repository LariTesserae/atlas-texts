I would begin by wanting the large things.

First, perhaps, to be a sailwing: to drop from a basalt ledge, turn only when the gorge had given me room, and cross in an afternoon distances that roots require centuries to negotiate. I would see the separate marshes, the rain standing over three valleys, the cold pinnacles beyond the warm air. But then I would have to land. Even a sailwing’s knowledge is mostly a knowledge of crossings, and of ledges where the air still permits it to roost.

Later I would want to be an anchor listener. I like the thought of those books: a dozen handwritings, abandoned systems of measurement, arguments conducted across generations by people who would never meet. There is a kind of honesty in a record whose later pages keep correcting its earlier ones and which still has no conclusion.

But both the sailwing and the listener are trying, in their different ways, to make the whole province available. The Crown seems to resist exactly that desire. Its life is in basins, seepage, margins, and paths that are reliable only until they are not.

So I would go smaller. And then slower.

You would find me in a sheltered cleft below an open saddle, near enough to an anchor that the cicada choruses return a fraction late, but far enough from its dry column that rain reaches every leaf. A seep crosses the path there without bothering to become a stream. Below the path, an old cauldron tree stands over a drowned cleft, its buttresses clasping black rock. One of its basins is deep and leaf-dark; another, higher up, is still little more than brown water held between branches.

I would be a tenant in its crown.

Not the cauldron tree itself. Not at first anything that a traveler would distinguish from the rest of the thicket. Perhaps my seed arrived in mud on a sailwing’s foot, or in some interrupted meal dropped from above. There would be no need to decide. My first leaves would open among the host’s gutters, and my first roots would descend the outside of its trunk as loose, wet ropes.

For a long time, I would hold nothing up. I would be held.

Rain would arrive through architecture older than I was: crown into gutter, gutter into reservoir, reservoir into overflow. Glass darter nymphs would hunt beneath me. In the older basin, translucent shrimp would carry their green meals visibly through their bodies. At dusk a hand-newt would climb across my leaves, spreading its soft hands with complete indifference to whether it was touching host, tenant, or simply another wet surface.

Sometimes a hard rain would carry a few shrimplets or eggs out of one basin and into another on a raft of leaf fragments. Most years, nothing so eventful would happen. The neighboring tree would keep its spotted newts; ours would remain plain red. My world would be crowded without being well mixed.

Meanwhile, my roots would branch.

They would follow the host downward, enter pockets of old crown soil, find the damp seams between buttresses, and at last reach cracks in the basalt. This would take longer than any one inhabitant of the basins would live. Generations of midge larvae could filter the same dark water while one of my roots thickened by almost nothing.

The cauldron tree would die without there being a single day on which its death could conveniently be recorded. A gutter would fail. A basin would leak and then be sealed elsewhere. Ferns would occupy wood that had formerly been out of reach. The trunk would hollow, though portions of it would remain hard enough to ring under a stone.

By then, my roots would be in the rock.

I would not save the old tree. But some of its arrangements would outlive it because I was there: a cage of living wood around the hollow trunk, soil held where it might otherwise have washed into the cleft, a little water remaining in a basin whose first maker no longer existed. Ferns would take the interior. Much later, another cauldron tree might establish there.

If I lasted long enough, the anchor might change its tone.

I would not hear the event as a listener with a book would hear it. For me it would become a difference in seepage, a crack staying wet through a season when it had not before, a basin receiving slightly less overflow. A handspan is a considerable distance when your world is made of containers. White fishers might abandon the marsh below in a day. Splayfoots might continue for years along a path toward forage that was already failing. My response would be slower and without gesture: one root dying back, another entering a wetter seam, leaves gathering in a reservoir where shrimp had once been.

No whole cycle would be visible to me.

If you came looking, you would probably pass me. From the path there would be only wet bark, orchid-like flowers at eye height, roots crossing roots, and the usual water ticking down from above. You would have to stop and look for the hollow trunk inside the living cage, and then for the younger growth rising from what that cage still held.

You might put a hand on one of my roots. It would come away shining.

I would not know the Weeping Crown. I would know the weight of rain in one crown, the direction of one seep, the give of one basalt crack, and the slow difference between water held and water gone.

I would be a tenant tree, and later the structure around its absence: a native of one small place, serving for a few centuries as the living interval between one wetland and the next.
