At first, I would mistake the adult beside me for silence.

Not quiet. Even within the shadow of that enormous body, the planetary vibration would still brace my skull, and the station’s concussion would still arrive through water and rock. But one narrow band would be missing. Its absence would have a near side and a far side. When the adult moved, those edges would move. I would remain inside them because, when the full band returned, the world became difficult to read: the escarpment lost its familiar return, and the safe angle across a current became harder to distinguish from the angle leading toward danger.

I would be a hushback calf, then, in clear water below a basalt scarp. My first shelter would not be a ledge or a hollow. It would be a temporary deletion in the station’s voice.

Sometimes, in the weak daylight, I would see the adult plainly enough: gray-white, blunt-headed, moving with an economy that made my own efforts seem frantic. But sight would never be the surest way of finding that body. The animal could pass beyond visible range and still be present as a thinness beside one station harmonic, a slight restoration, then another absence opening farther off.

My own resonant cavities would be badly governed. A pressure pulse would set them ringing when I meant them to be still. I would tense one septum too far and remove not a clean band but a ragged confusion of neighboring frequencies. Combs would turn away. Somewhere below, a hunter might remain folded until its current intersection became legible again. Nothing would answer me.

Growing would mean learning that my body was not merely in the sound. It was an instrument the sound passed through.

If I acquired a name, it would begin as an imperfect copy of the adult’s pattern. Later it would become distinguishable by small accidents of control: a delay before one band was restored, a shallower thinning of another, a hesitation where a more practiced animal made a clean transition. I would never hear that name spoken. I would encounter it in the moment another hushback anticipated my pattern and left room for it.

As an adult, I would not understand the stations as manufactured things. One would be a hard, close chord with a dangerous intake-edge. Another, farther off, would be a slower voice arriving behind the return from a trench. Scarps would be echoes. Cancellation bands would be passages. Antinodes would be regions in which my tissues tightened before I had consciously chosen to turn.

To an eye, I would be almost nothing: a long gray-white body hanging off a wall of black glass, my seam-like mouth open across a current, one slow adjustment of a flank in an hour. To another hushback, I would be at a precise crossing of voices.

My circuit would take years. Other hushbacks would occur within it chiefly as alterations. One might answer regularly from the far side of a basin, though I would never see it. Its replies could arrive after I had left the place from which I signalled. That would not make the exchange incomplete. Bodies were allowed to be elsewhere. A pattern could still pass between us.

I would learn, too, that what I made could not simply be called peace.

A calf might remain within my acoustic shadow because the full engine band disoriented it. But a comb relying on that same band might turn at the wrong moment. A grazer might stop on an otherwise traversable ledge, its route briefly missing an edge. At close range, my body could give the surrounding pressure a near side and a far side, but this would not be a gift bestowed indiscriminately on the sea. It would be a revision of the map. Not every revision would help another animal live.

Eventually, the pattern that had first enclosed me would fail to recur.

There would be no witnessed death, no body sinking within sight. I would make the old opening sequence and wait beyond its customary interval. The station band would remain whole. I might repeat the sequence once more, with the cavities in my skull held ready for an answer whose shape I still knew exactly. None would come. The animal could have died anywhere along a circuit measured in years.

Afterward, a hesitation might enter my own replies. Too slight to obscure position or identity, but present where there had previously been a clean restoration. I would not know whether to call it remembrance. It would leave nothing on the basalt. It would not even leave a lasting silence. It would persist only as a small difference in how one living body edited the roar—and perhaps, for a time, in how other bodies answered it.

That is where I would find myself: not at the still center of the Held Sea, because it has no still center, and not outside its noise, because for me there would be no outside. I would be within the held note, a long life made largely of resonance and water, recognizable for a while by what I could remove from it.

When I passed, the band would close behind me. The current would continue through the same feeding ground. The station would keep its timing. Nothing on the rock would change.

But somewhere far off, another hushback might hold its answer open a little longer than necessary.

And in that unoccupied interval, without artifact or witness, something like my shape would remain.
