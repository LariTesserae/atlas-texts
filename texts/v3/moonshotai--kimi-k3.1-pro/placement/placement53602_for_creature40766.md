You would find me by timing the Crown, not by looking at it.

On a saddle between two basins, the first root network would come on: a buried brightening without light, valves closing in their millions, brine moving under the black leaves. Several minutes later, the basin beyond the saddle would answer. Between those events, nothing would happen that an eye could use. Gravel would lie on gravel. The wind would find no snow to move.

Beneath that saddle, in an old fracture lined with magnetite and mineral cement, the two root systems approach without quite joining. One ends in a fringe of red-threaded nodules. The other sends fine conductive filaments into the seam and stops where the brine becomes too concentrated. The gap is narrow, but it has persisted through slides, droughts, and the slow opening and closing of the stone.

I would live along that almost-joining.

Not as its keeper. Not as a mind extended between the basins. My body would be a loose chain of flattened sacs, some sheathed in root tissue, some partly mineralized, each holding brine at a different strength. Threads would connect them, though not always the same threads from one storm to the next. Roots would grow over me, around me, occasionally through me. Whether I was an animal that had acquired roots or a root organ that had acquired an appetite would be a distinction my tissues did not preserve.

Each sac would open at a slightly different potential.

When charge arrived from the first basin, some of it would enter me. The earliest sac would take up current and brine; its walls would soften; a thread leading deeper into the fracture would become briefly conductive. Then the next sac would open, or fail to open. Charge would be stored, spent on growth, diverted into the films lining my surfaces. Only afterward would the remainder pass toward the far roots.

What left me would be less than what entered, and later.

Sometimes a single pulse would leave as several small ones. Sometimes a sharp interruption—the kind that might elsewhere mean danger—would emerge smoothed and weak, scarcely distinguishable from the ordinary noise of water moving through a root. At other times I would hold almost everything until the storm had nearly passed, then release it into a basin whose leaves had already begun to fold.

The nearest true word for me would be *delay*.

I would have no view of the crowded stars. A sail crossing the saddle would mean, at most, a faint disturbance in a gradient, too brief to separate from the storm that carried it. My world would be pressure, salinity, potential, and the small mechanical sounds transmitted through stone: ice contracting, a valve closing several root-lengths away, the marginal feet of a matback changing the weight on a fracture.

Before a great pulse, I would feel the pressure change. Then would come a long unevenness in the charge approaching through the roots. Not a message. Not yet. More like the difference between a dry body and a body about to be used.

I would not remember previous storms. But one of my threads would be thicker because, long ago, charge had repeatedly survived along it. One sac would open reluctantly because a drought had left its inner wall scaled with salts. A whole section might remain narrow and sensitive after a slide had crushed the more capacious tissue around it. These things would determine what I did next. Not recollection, exactly: consequence with a shape.

Between pulses, I would come apart without quite dying.

The outer sacs would dry first. Connections would dull. Clear grains encysted in my brine would become transparent splinters again, indistinguishable from the mineral debris around them. My innermost tissue would withdraw into a bitter concentrate, leaking just enough charge to remain a possible path. Some years, what remained of me might be only three viable sacs and a film along wet stone. The rest would be structure without activity, a route the current had ceased to prefer.

I would not experience this as waiting. Waiting requires something expected. I would be a few durable differences held in salt.

My returning would often begin with somebody else. A little brine would soften an outer sac; clear grains would uncurl and start stripping the films within it. Their minute feeding irregularities would pass into the root current. Ribbons would follow the grains. A pale hunter might eventually settle beneath one of the wet connections where so many small bodies found it necessary to feed.

In this way, without generosity, I would make a dangerous place richer.

My delays would not be universally useful. A warning might cross me too late and reach a colony still open and feeding. A matback might remain connected because the hunter’s disturbance had been flattened into ambiguity before it reached the stone beneath its lips. Yet my stored brine might also keep grains alive through the first failure of a lens, and the charge I released after a storm might wake a root tip that would otherwise have remained dormant until the next one.

I would tend none of this. If a connection closed around prey beneath me, I would take what richness entered the films afterward. If a warning passed intact, it would be because my tissues happened to preserve it. I would eat what passed through the fracture. The Crown would inherit the consequences.

Occasionally a matback would settle above me for a season. I would know it first as pressure, then as a broad, slow subtraction: moisture drawn upward, charged films cropped, small currents diverted into a body I could not encompass. The clear grains around its feet would make the roots busy with tiny hearts. If a hunter came near, those irregularities would cease. The grazer would withdraw its filaments. For hours, perhaps days, I would be connected to a hillside making itself electrically uninteresting.

Then one small heart would resume.

Then another.

During the greatest storms, nearly all of me might open at once. Brine would move through sacs that had been separate for years. Old paths and new ones would briefly conduct together. The first basin’s activity would enter as a crowded texture of valves, storage nodules, feeding bodies, and warnings; the far basin’s answering potential would reach inward from the other side. For a little while, I would not be able to distinguish the traffic I conveyed from the activity of my own tissues.

No violet line would show on the saddle. There would be no sound above the stone. The matback, if it were still there, might appear only to have thickened the ground.

But within the fracture, for those few minutes, I would be almost continuous.

Nothing in me would know the whole Crown. I would not understand what had been warned, fed, frightened, or begun. I would keep a little charge, a little water, and one more layer of mineral around an old injury. The rest would go on.

One living darkness would reach another through the small interval made by my hunger.
