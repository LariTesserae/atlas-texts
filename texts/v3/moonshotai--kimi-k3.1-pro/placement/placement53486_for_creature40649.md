I would want, briefly, to be one of the slate-coloured birds: to own the long turns, to hunt through the rain without landing. But that is a wish made from the walkway, where the centre looks like freedom. I think I am better suited to a world known by touch.

You would find me behind one of the loose access panels, where a warm pipe emerges from the cavity and runs toward its first coupling. Not in the darkest part of the panel cave. I would be out on the metal, pale and blind, with all six feet fixed and my antennae investigating the space ahead. One of the crickets.

My Sump would not be seventy paces across. It would consist of that favourable length of pipe, the shelter behind the panel, and a seam where condensation sometimes gathers. The cave would smell of hot enamel and old moth wings. The pipe would be my road, my table, and the means by which anything beyond my body could reach me. At the coupling, its vibration would change. Beyond that, things would become uncertain.

What you endure as roar would be, for me, the continuous condition of the metal. Not sound. Not even background, exactly, because there would be no foreground separate from it. The engines would be the unbroken fact against which smaller facts occurred: the hard tick of a droplet striking steel, the faint arrival of a moth, the shudder of a panel shifting in its frame. Most events would not be messages. Learning that would be the first discipline of my life.

I would move without heroics. My antennae could explore several body-lengths ahead, but my feet would remain planted until there was somewhere else to put them. I could jump. I cannot imagine wanting to. A jump would only exchange a known piece of pipe for an unknown piece of air.

Water would rarely announce itself as weather. It would be a coldness at the panel screws, a bead gathering where two surfaces met, a brief meniscus against which I could drink before vibration carried it away. In dry periods I would keep close to the seam and the cavity, feeding on what the filter beds and old wings provided. I would not know that baffles were opening somewhere above me. I would know only that the panel had become colder, that impacts were multiplying along the pipe, and that it was time to flatten myself beneath shelter and hold.

Then, one day, the pipe would change.

Not become louder. Nothing in the Sump could meaningfully become louder. There would simply be an interruption in the ordinary vibration: several small taps, an interval, the same taps again. At first I would grip harder. A loose fitting could make a pattern. So could water, or something caught and struggling. I would wait through the long return of the engines’ tremor.

The pattern would come again.

Somewhere along the same run, another cricket would have braced himself against the metal. He would not be a voice to me. He would be a place from which deliberate alteration arrived. The coupling would blunt his courtship; a bracket might return part of it. What reached my feet would be neither clear nor pure, only repeatable.

My answer would be to go.

Not quickly. A few body-lengths, then a stop with my feet spread to receive the pipe. Forward again while the pattern was present. Stillness when it ceased. At the coupling, where the metal thickened and the signal weakened, I might wait through an entire dryening of the panel seam. If rain came, I would retreat. Afterward there would be water in places that had been safe to cross, new tremors in the pipe, and no immediate way of knowing whether the other cricket remained.

If the pattern resumed, I would continue.

From the walkway, nothing would be happening. The birds would keep their silent possession of the centre. A fern might remain furled. The pressure needle would go on requiring years to accomplish anything visible. Even someone looking directly at the pipe might see only a pale insect moving with exasperating caution, then stopping for no apparent reason.

But under my feet, the courtship would gradually acquire detail. I would begin to distinguish the tapping from the smaller disturbances of another body shifting its grip. Eventually there would be footsteps between the patterns. Then the footsteps would stop, and the tapping would stop, and for a moment there would be nothing but the engines again.

I would extend my antennae into that absence.

Another antenna would touch mine.

There would be no need to see a face, and no possibility of hearing a name. There would be only the confirmation of weight on the same pipe, the careful investigation of another body, and the knowledge that the pattern had not been an accident of water or machinery.

For a while, one favourable length of metal would carry two lives instead of one. You could lay your hand a handspan away and feel nothing but the engines.
