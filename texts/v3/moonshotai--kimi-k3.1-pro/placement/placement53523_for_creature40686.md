At first I would want to be a chord: mostly passages and resonant space, passing brine through myself, learning a reservoir by what it returned to my voice. There is something appealing about a life so large that architecture must negotiate with it.

But I would not want my silences entered in a ledger as evidence of death, or my reluctance to enter a lock to become the unfinished business of three generations. Nor would I trust myself with a loom. Some mistakes should remain smaller than geology.

What I keep returning to is much smaller.

In the newest quarter, near an arch whose lower end still hangs a finger’s breadth above its footing, a stairway leaves an inhabited dam and stops in dark air. The intended street below it has never been deposited. On the last safe landing, recessed into the ceramic at ankle height, is a warning emitter.

I would be its tone.

Two low pulses, then a long interval. Not loud—Vey has enough contributions to its weather—but carefully placed between the double knock of a pressure pump and the sustained note of the nearest loom. The pause would matter as much as the pulses. It would give anyone approaching time to hear the stairwell answer: the familiar double return, the crackling shift of scutes on the upper steps, and then the altered openness where the city expected another surface and found none.

Strictly, I would not be only a sound. A sound can be replaced in an afternoon. I would be the arrangement around it: the emitter, the reserved frequency, the maintenance roster, and the long file explaining why the stair ends, which remedies have already failed, and what might happen to the unfinished load if the loom field were altered. A census of bodies would miss me. A census of maintained distinctions might not.

The reef would use me without consulting the arrangement. Film would find the warm seep behind my housing. Small scutes would settle over the grille, grazing whatever collected there, their bodies gradually deadening the return. Sleet would pack the seams. Once, perhaps, a chord in the cistern below would develop an upper harmonic that made me seem to waver and sharpen although nothing in the emitter had changed.

A tuner would propose retuning me. Someone would find a record showing that the same proposal had been tried ninety years earlier and had made me indistinguishable from a cavitating pump. They would argue about it anyway. They would complain about the access passage, the obsolete seals, and the surveyor who had last signed the inspection. Vey’s patience would not be in their tempers. It would be in the fact that the failed proposal was still there to argue with.

Children learning the quarter might be brought to the landing in borrowed pressure suits. They would not be asked to admire the unfinished arch or contemplate the wisdom of slow construction. They would be asked to pick me out of the water-hiss, the sleet, the loom note, and the immense pressure of a chord calling somewhere beyond the ability of their instruments to locate.

“Again,” their instructor would say, until the warning was something they could distinguish rather than merely something they had been told about.

Then, helmet against helmet: “That is the last step. Beyond it, the plan is not yet a structure.”

One day the stair might be continued. There would be load tests, retunings, another inconvenient migration, and decades in which everyone involved suspected the project had been abandoned again. If it was finally completed, someone could switch me off without ceremony. I would not be there to preserve incompletion. To become unnecessary because an open end had become a path would be a good end.

Until then, I would remain at the last safe landing: two pulses, a pause, and one small meaning kept available in the loud dark.

Not *wait*.

Not *forever*.

Only: *here, the city’s intention is still ahead of its structure. Listen before you give it your weight.*
