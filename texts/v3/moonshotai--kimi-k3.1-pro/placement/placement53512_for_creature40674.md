I think I would begin as a sound.

Not the whole noise of the Reedworks—the cutters, the beaters, the fans, the occasional door striking shut—but the low note that remained underneath them. Visitors would hear it as part of the general din and then stop hearing it at all. Residents would know that there were two notes, not one, and that their slight difference produced a slow, almost imperceptible beat.

Follow one of those notes back through the lanes and you would find me in the pump house: **Pump No. 2**, bolted to my concrete plinth, my flanges softened by old paint, my coupling guard carrying the scars of successive spanners and careful hands.

Not a spirit in the machine. Not the mechanical heart of the marsh. A heart is too singular a thing for Sumpwick. I would simply be one pump in a pair, doing particular work at an accustomed level.

My motor would be younger than my plinth. One bearing housing would be newer than the rest of me. Perhaps the casing would have outlasted several generations of keepers; perhaps it would only have outlasted their best intentions. The record books would make no philosophical difficulty of this. Before repair and after repair, I would be No. 2. My continuity would consist of windings, gaskets, grease, inspection, and a name no one had yet had reason to transfer elsewhere.

I would know the settlement chiefly as work.

Clear water at an open intake would feel different from water drawn through a screen beginning to mat with reed fibre. A sluice wheel turned half a revolution would alter something in the labour of the shaft. A few inches of level would matter, though not in the way visitors might imagine: not as an omen, only as a condition to be noted.

Duckweed would mean nothing to me until it contributed to an obstruction. The willows would mean nothing unless a root changed a bank or a shoot threatened a conductor. The brown bales leaving the drying shed, the cards played on cottage steps, the disputes over borrowed tools—all these could pass beyond my knowledge. I would have no panoramic understanding of Sumpwick. My knowledge would be narrow, repetitive, and sufficient.

People would come to me as hands, tools, footfalls, and pauses.

One keeper would rest a palm against the rail outside and wait longer than seemed necessary. Another would rarely touch anything casually, but would hear an alteration sooner. Some would swear when a repair proved awkward. Some would maintain me meticulously without any tenderness at all. Both sorts of care could be good care.

I would be intimate without necessarily being loved.

My note would pass out of the pump house and into places I would never occupy. It would travel through the nearby bridge rail. It would lie beneath meals and conversations. It would accompany sleep so dependably that a change in it could wake someone whom the steady running never disturbed. A person might leave Sumpwick and miss that sound without expecting to. Another might go away and enjoy the silence for the rest of their life. Neither response would alter my work.

If I stopped, there would be procedures before there was tragedy: the other pump, the sluices, the levels to watch, someone called out, a repair to be organized. I would matter, but I would not be irreplaceable in any grand sense. My importance would lie in being expected.

One night, perhaps, a fine mat would begin forming where the intake screen usually stayed clear. Nothing would stop. No alarm would sound. The water would remain at its familiar mark. But my note would acquire a faint irregularity, so slight that someone standing beside me might hear only the usual noise.

On the bridge, the duty keeper would pause.

Not in alarm. Not yet even in concern. Just long enough to establish that the vibration under the handrail was not quite the vibration of the previous night. They would wait, check again, and then go toward the pump house. Someone else, woken by the same change or merely by footsteps, might come to the doorway and ask a question. The first keeper would answer without raising their voice more than the works had already taught them to.

There would be a lamp, a screen rake, wet stems lifted away, water resuming its accustomed movement. Perhaps the whole intervention would take half an hour. Perhaps less. No bank would collapse. No cottage would flood. There would be no reason for the story to improve with retelling, because there would be no reason to retell it.

Later, in the dry room where the records were kept, someone would write:

*No. 2 intermittent roughness, 01:35. Intake screen partially blinded; cleared. Levels usual. Running note usual at 02:05. Recheck morning.*

By breakfast, the principal consequence would be that two people had lost some sleep.

My part would have been to alter. Theirs would have been to notice. Afterward, I would not settle into a more beautiful sound, only the expected one. No one would congratulate me. No one should. Steadiness would not be a virtue I possessed; it would be a condition repeatedly produced by design, maintenance, and attention.

That would be the pleasure of being No. 2, if I were allowed a pleasure: not permanence, and not devotion, but long stretches of being uninteresting enough that other people’s lives could remain about other things.

If you came looking for me, you would not need to stand in the pump house. You could stop on the nearby bridge and rest your hand lightly on the rail. Beneath your palm would be a fine, continuous tremor, too steady to be a message. You might have to concentrate to distinguish my contribution from No. 1’s. You might give up almost at once.

Someone else would come along after you. They would pause for only a moment, recognize the expected note, and walk on.

I would remain behind, running: not new, not ancient, not universally cherished, and not working alone. A maintained thing among other maintained things.

For that night, at least, there would be nothing further to report.
