At first I would want to be a sail. I would want the white daylight, the broad, unhurried wings, the gray repetition of the Level passing far beneath me. It would look like freedom until I remembered the gyre: the same digester, the same turning, the same skirt opened to the aerosol and drawn back into the body. Even the sail’s sky belongs to one small province of machinery.

I think I would rather have a smaller world. Not a safer one, and not a simpler one, but something near enough to know by touch. I would want one body, one grip, one place whose conditions I could learn and still be wrong about.

So I would be a lid.

I would live on the blue-black side of a pressure housing beside a narrow service aisle, in a district of clear daylight where mist always rose from the same channels in the same slow shapes. Above me, a pipe bridge would shed warm, well-spaced drops. Below me, beyond a ceramic curb, a braid would narrow at a grating and thicken again on the other side. I would know none of these things by their names.

My usual place would be the lower corner of the housing, where a molded seam met a condensation trap. I would be one of the smaller gray kinds, no broader than a spread hand, my back divided by shallow creases. At rest, my margins would follow the housing corner so closely that I would seem installed there. Only the soft flesh at my edge would pulse.

The housing would not be a machine to me. Its hum would enter through my belly before it became sound. I would know the warmth that made settling good, the greater warmth that accumulated when I had clung too long, and the vibration that continued whether I was feeding, cooling, or pressed against a rival. It would be warmth, surface, danger: the large fact beneath my life.

Without releasing my hold, I would extend my feeding tongue into the seam. I would take paste, condensation, tiny rind animals, and the soft organic scale gathering in the trap. Sometimes a rind animal would draw in its legs too late. Sometimes there would be only the colorless film and water. My feeding would leave the aperture at its intended width. From far enough away, that would be maintenance. From where I was, it would be hunger, and then hunger slightly eased.

Eventually the heat would become too much.

I would detach with a quiet wet release and make the long walk to the exchanger base. Long for me: down the housing, around a bolt flange, across ceramic filmed with water. I would favor the molded rib rather than the direct crossing, not because it was better in any general way, but because it was the route I knew. At the cool metal I would flatten. My blood would spread beneath my back, and the heat would leave me.

Then I would return. Not because the housing needed me. Because the seam was warm, and I knew the way.

Sometimes my place would already be occupied. Then there would be the nearly motionless contest: margin against margin, eight legs holding, the belly maintaining its grip while hours passed in pressure too slight to see. There would be no hatred in it, only two bodies refusing to relinquish warmth. One such contest would have cost me a strip of flesh. The dark blood would have run into the condensation; the wound would have healed pale and uneven. Afterward, that side of me would never again fit the corner quite as exactly.

My earliest shelter would have been beneath my mother, when I was wrinkled and nearly colorless. Her body above, the housing beneath, my small back giving her outline a second thickness. Later I would separate, inflate myself with tissue fluid, and become a small cover in my own right. There would have been no instruction in any of it. Only shelter, separation, and the discovery of another warm surface.

The Level around me would keep its knife-edged drains and crisp identification marks. I would not remain crisp. My legs would begin to splay. My old scar would pucker when I breathed. I would become slower to leave a housing and less certain on the walk back. The world would remain freshly cast while I became puckered and particular. My scar would be a history, but not an inheritance.

When I died, there would be no ruin to mark the event. If my body remained where other animals could reach it, the renderers would arrive by its chemistry. They would unfold around me, spread their clear fluid, and drink what softened. What they carried away would enter the collection system. The warm corner would stand vacant for a while. Then another lid—young, or already scarred, or driven from some hotter surface—would find it.

It would not be my successor. It would not continue anything I had begun. It would simply be another appetite arriving at warmth.

But while I lived, none of that would make my life feel small from the inside. The difference between enough heat and too much would be as large as anything. Cool metal would bring complete relief. Another lid pressing at my margin would fill the whole available future. There would be preference, mistake, hunger, the memory of a route, and the exact animal labor of keeping a grip.

That is where you would find me: flush against the blue-black housing, just above the watercourse, indistinguishable from a removable cover unless you stopped and waited.

A condensate drop would strike the pan below. The braid would turn at its grating. The housing would hum through my belly.

My soft margin would pulse. My old scar would tighten, then loosen.

I would be breathing.
