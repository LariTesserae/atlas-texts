I would come down from the plateau with the wrong ambitions.

First I would want the light. There would be something irresistible in standing at a stone socket, one cold palm against the copper, and sending noon across a basin. I would imagine the long alignments: dust lifting in white sheets, chalkwings turning inside the beam, people gathering and pretending they had come only to inspect the workmanship. It would take me a while to notice how much of a lightkeeper’s life consisted of waiting through another district’s brightness, polishing a small tarnished place no visitor would see, and deciding which court could safely bear an hour of blue shadow. The work would be less like commanding light than being answerable for its absence.

Then I would want to read seeps. That ambition would last longer and suit me less. I would love the evidence: the new crystal’s shape, crickets crowding an old drill hole, the particular underside chosen by glasslice as a slab dried. But I would also enjoy being right before other people were right. I would be too pleased when knowledge looked like prophecy. The Hollow has little use for another person who can explain afterward why they were almost correct.

In the end, I would be useful on moving days.

Not as the person deciding where a district ought to go. I would be the one kneeling beside a sledge, saying, “Turn it again.”

A block can look sound on its dry upper face and come away in white grains beneath the thumb below. A household mark can survive as little more than a change in the dust, while a name on the protected underside remains irritatingly legible. I would learn that the underside is not necessarily more honest than the face; it is merely where different kinds of evidence collect.

My work would be masonry, copying, and refusal. Households would want marked stones carried because of the names on them. Water companies would want doubtful walls removed before they altered a catchment. Everyone would want the argument finished before the light moved on. Sometimes the right thing would be to leave the stone and carry the record. Sometimes it would be to carry the stone and admit that the record was wrong. The difference would keep me employed.

Bent Measure would be my household: two parallel cuts, the lower one hooked sharply upward at the end. They took me in, according to the household joke, because I stayed for the second move. I would hold a labor share with the Long Catchment company, and my full introduction would end, “born on the plateau.” Surface traders would find that last part significant. Almost no one below would.

My wealth would be good boots, a dry second shirt, an unfrayed length of rope, and a water share that had twice survived my household changing districts. I would also keep a hide roll that no one else was obliged to read. It would not contain predictions. It would contain corrections.

Once, with a court about to lose its turn of light, I passed a stone I should have condemned. I was tired; the household was tired; its marked face looked sound, and I wanted the move finished. At the new wall its base began to granulate. No one was crushed. A section had to come down, a catchment had to be cleared, and part of the labor was assessed to me. The company’s account of the affair is quite short. Mine is longer and less forgiving.

I would still enjoy being sent for. That is why I would keep the roll.

Just now, you would find me at the edge of a market whose future everyone is politely avoiding. A new seep has darkened the court beyond a low saddle. The water company is still arguing over the catchment, but the traders have already put up three hide awnings. Someone has brought eels in wet bags from a district where the rims are drying. At the far light well, chalkwings are beginning to rise, though my own patch of ground remains in thin blue shadow.

Beside me is an old Bent Measure block, too far gone to carry. Its marked face is still handsome. Only the base powders when pressed. On the underside are the usual brief records: a birth, two joinings, a death, and the year the household took in three strangers.

I was one of the strangers. The record does not say which. I have never needed it to.

The replacement block lies on the sledge, sound and unmarked, its fresh edges almost offensively sharp. I have copied the household pattern. The names remain to be done. Once, I would have wanted to make the great beam leap from basin to basin. Now I want only enough light to distinguish an old chisel cut from a drift of pale dust, and enough steadiness not to improve the record while copying it.

The seep may crust shut before the market has finished arguing about eel prices. The household may leave before the glasslice vanish, or after. None of that changes what has to be carried, or what ought to be left behind.

The reflector turns. White light reaches the sledge and makes my first cuts look violently new. Behind me, a single drop enters the cistern.

I do not take it for a promise. I set the chisel anyway.
