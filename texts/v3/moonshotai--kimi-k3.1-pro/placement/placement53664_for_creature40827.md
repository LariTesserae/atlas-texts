At first you would find only a bank where the current had no clear reason to leave one.

It would lie along the lower side of a sunken court, with one end narrow in a hollow and the other rounded beneath a surviving arch. Black sand would have collected along its crest. A few pale seams would cross the sand, not following any grain in the sediment. A minute grazer might have left part of a spiral there, so small that the whole court could be searched without anyone recognizing it as a track.

Only the relationships would be wrong. The bank would not quite follow the current. Its lower edge would remain too clean. After a minor disturbance, sediment would settle everywhere around it but a little more slowly upon it.

The bank would be me.

Or rather, it would be what other animals had had time to make of me. Beneath the silt would be one of the long, pallid predators, tail extended into the hollow, forepart resting just above the floor. My small eyes would register little beyond a change in the pewter haze. The useful world would arrive through pressure, vibration, and dissolved traces: the dragging of grains along a buried ramp, the uneasy water before a shear, the mineral and bodily sharpness released by something breaking far away.

I would not be patient. Patience suggests an end anticipated, time endured. Waiting would simply be the ordinary condition of my body, as deposition is for a mason and folding is for a sieve. The cold would not interrupt that waiting. It would give waiting its tempo.

And the waiting would not be empty.

On my back, loose grains would expose fresh surfaces. Microorganisms would film them. Tiny grazers would cross the film, opening pores too small for me to feel. Masons would lodge in sheltered irregularities and begin their fragile crusts. A whole life could be spent enlarging one cavity between two grains that had settled against me. A grazing track could begin and end without ever leaving my body.

I would need to know none of it. The animals on my back would not know much more. For them there would be chemistry, resistance, the small flow around a pore, the richness or poverty of a surface. Their ground would happen to have a slow pulse of water around it. That would not yet distinguish it from any other ground.

Then something would collapse.

Not nearby, perhaps. The first sign would be a pressure with no visible source. My head would lift before the white suspension entered the court. Only afterward would the concussion arrive, soft and directionless, already late.

That first movement would be almost nothing in the Basin.

On my back it would be catastrophic. Sand would slide. The grazing track would shear across itself. New masonry would lift away in delicate flakes. Some masons would remain in protected pores; others would enter the water with the material that had sheltered them. The small grazer might press itself flat against a surface that was no longer still enough to be a refuge. Their storm would be my first gesture.

I would leave the hollow and move upcurrent.

There would be no pursuit at first, only a gradual alteration of position. Buried cups and rounded partitions would pass beneath me so slowly that they would seem to be rearranging themselves. Ahead, where descending brine folded against returning water, sieves would be gathering. Their ribbon might extend for miles, but I would encounter only its local consequences: cloudy guts of thread-hunters, damaged bodies sinking out of the flow, scavengers turning over what had reached the floor.

Eventually one opportunity would come close enough. A lesser hunter, heavy with feeding; a grazer injured by the first disturbance; some soft body carried along the wrong side of a current boundary. There would be a single contraction. Afterward I would appear largely unchanged, except for the thickening of my body and the darkness behind my head.

Other food might pass. I might let it go.

Later I would settle again, perhaps not in the old court. A hollow would receive my tail. Sediment would begin to collect in the lee of my body. At first my back would remain bare where the crust had torn away. Then a grain would stay. Then several. A faint slickness would develop around a freshly exposed pore. Nothing would commemorate the community that had been lost. Nothing would distinguish the next one from ordinary floor.

To find me again, you would have to repeat your original mistake. You would see a bank, a dull track, a few pale seams. Only after a very long examination would the bank become a body, and even then you could not be certain until some distant event made the body move.

I would not be the wise old beast of the Basin, or its guardian, or a witness preserved from some former age. There is no vantage in a body kept that low, and waiting does not produce wisdom. I would be an animal whose stillness had become temporarily useful to other animals.

Ground while I waited. Weather when I moved. Afterward, a surface on which silt could begin to gather.

Most of my life would be the long interval in which smaller lives could mistake me for a place of their own.
