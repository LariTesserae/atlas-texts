At midday, when the Seep is brightest, I am just inside the feeder fissure, with most of my body in shelter and only my antennae extended into the flow.

I am one of the clear crustaceans. My smallness is a fact for someone else. To me, the fissure is not narrow; it is the width of available water. The chamber is not seven paces long. It ends wherever the wet surface ends.

At rest, I am almost indistinguishable from a slight thickening in the seep. My shell takes the shape of the water around it. Now and then a limb adjusts its hold, or the small work of respiration becomes visible, but for long periods there is no reason to move. Food is scarce. Heat makes even a short excursion expensive. Stillness is not emptiness; it is one of the ways I remain alive.

I have no image of the chamber. The silica blades, the white roof, the broad glass planes—none of these exist for me as forms. The world arrives instead as differences. Warmth and the withdrawal of warmth. A current pressing one antenna more strongly than the other. A change in the water after it has crossed fresh mineral. The minute roughness of growth beneath a limb. The vibration of a drop reaching the pool.

Noon means nothing here. There is only the last heat pulse, and how recently it passed.

Sometimes I remain at the opening for a long while, testing what comes to me. A shift in temperature is enough to keep me there. So is an alteration in the seep for which I have no name, only a bodily refusal. Beyond my antennae, the pool margin may be drying or becoming wet again. I do not need to know which, exactly. I need only not advance into the wrong condition.

When I finally emerge, it is not exploration. Nothing in me requires the chamber to be known.

There is food along the margin, or the mixed trace of where food has been. The water is within a range my body can use. The route back remains wet. I move from one small permission to the next, limbs testing the glass before trusting it, the fissure always close behind me.

Dry glass is not another kind of ground. It is the end of ground. The whole radiant floor might as well be solid roof.

At the pool’s edge, I graze. My mouthparts gather more than food: active cells and dormant ones, empty sheaths, organic debris, particles of iron and silica. The distinction between what can be used and what cannot happens afterward, within me. I take very little. I do not clear the film away to bright glass; I only thin it.

The interrupted strip may shine differently after I have passed. I never see that. For me, there is no path, no clearing, no visible sign of labor. There is only the next usable patch and the continued presence of water beneath my limbs.

For a short while, the clearest evidence of my body is something inside it: a faint amber thread of gut, material from the wall becoming briefly part of an animal. Light passes through nearly everything else. If the chamber could keep an image of me, it would keep the image of being fed.

Then the water changes.

Perhaps warmth comes along the margin. Perhaps the seep weakens, breaking the wet connection into separate beads. I withdraw. A few limbs reverse, then others. There is no drama in it, no pursuit and no escape—only the body returning to the conditions in which it can continue. Behind me, the pool resumes its stillness. The place where I grazed begins, almost at once, to be altered by water and growth.

Back in the fissure, I do very little.

Much later, if enough has been gathered for growth, my covering will loosen. The molt will happen in shelter. I will pull myself out of the old, precise shape and remain hidden while the new one is soft. The discarded shell may be taken by the flow. It may drift into the pool and settle there with every leg still jointed, every antenna still fine and entire.

The light will catch on it more readily than it ever caught on me.

That empty shell could outlast the grazed strip, the wet margin, even the last of my excursions. It could remain on the glass like a tiny inhabitant perfected by stillness. But it would not be me. It would be the shape I left in order to continue, preserved after continuing had ceased to leave any visible sign.

For now, though, no shell lies in the pool. A drop falls from the roof, and its small concussion travels through water and stone. I stop. The seep steadies against my antennae. Somewhere beyond the opening, a dried patch has darkened again.

The film at the margin is wet. The fissure behind me is wet.

Between those two facts, for a little longer, there is room enough for me.
