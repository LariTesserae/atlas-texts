From a raven’s height, I would not be there.

From a finch’s height, I might be a brief variation in the colour of wet stone. Only close to the rock would I acquire edges: a green-brown cushion set into a joint on the sheltered side of an old summit table, just below its tilted rim. Above me, a pale mineral seam would carry cloud water toward the fracture. Below me, the fracture would descend farther than any root could follow. On some thaw afternoons, a little of that water would pass through the dust at my roots. On others, it would not.

That is where I would find myself. Not upon the range, exactly, and not yet in soil: in the narrow, accident-held interval between the two.

I would be a cushion plant, though that name would describe only my visible part. Above the stone, hundreds of short shoots would be pressed together, none holding a leaf higher than necessary. Beneath them, older leaves would remain after dying, packed among mineral grains and fragments of ice. My roots would bind this material into the joint. There might be a finch feather caught in it, the emptied skin of a springtail, a few grains darkened by microbial growth. Not soil, quite. But material no longer entirely at the mercy of the wind.

My geography would be small without being simple. One edge would receive slightly more light. Another would keep ice longest. The centre would be older, crowded with dead tissue and dormant buds. Somewhere below, the roots would end in cold rock. A change of a few centimetres—in exposure, in wetness, in the depth of trapped dust—would matter more than the cliffs visible across the basin.

Most of the year, there would be little to distinguish me from a compact stain on the fracture. Cold would enter the shoots. Water would become unavailable, first as ice and then as dryness. This would not feel like waiting, because there would be no anticipated future inside the pause. No imagined stream, no deeper soil, no promise of another April. Dormancy would simply be the shape my life took when nothing more could be done.

Then would come one of the favourable afternoons.

The first change would not be general warmth. The range would remain cold, and much of the water would still be sealed. But in a few protected spaces, ice would become liquid and remain so long enough to be used. Around my roots, fine material would darken. On the adjacent stone, a microbial film would become slippery and nearly black. The shallow pool below the joint, empty that morning except for clear ice, would develop a living rim.

Larvae would begin grazing in water barely deep enough to cover them. Springtails would gather on wet grains. Mites would move through the close passages among my dead leaves. Beneath a separate flake of rock, a spider would wait beside its few strands, unaffected by the fact that the larger world contained forty kilometres of cliffs and almost no visible prey.

One of my inconspicuous flowers might open. Whether anything visited it would depend on the wind and on the good fortune of a small fly. There would be no necessity in the encounter. Self-fertile seed, slow division, another year without reproduction: all would be workable outcomes.

If finches arrived, the event would become brief and violent. They would land close enough to scatter grit among my shoots, take larvae from the pool margin and springtails from the wet stone, then rise together at some change in the air. A chough might cross above. By the time the cloud returned, the seep would look almost unused.

None of this would make me a guardian of the place. I would be eaten, walked on, frozen, and used. But the dead leaves I had not shed would shelter eggs. The dust my roots held would remain damp a little longer than bare grains. My body would be, among other things, a slight alteration in the conditions under which other bodies took their chances.

By evening the pool would be sealed again. The finches would descend toward shelter. Water would stop moving among my roots. Nothing would need to have been completed. An afternoon could be sufficient without being conclusive.

My greater event might come years later, in winter, as a deep report crossing the basin.

There need be no visible catastrophe. A slab would settle; ice would take a new strain; the joint below me would open by some small amount. The next thaw, water would follow a different plane. The pale seam above me might still darken with cloud moisture, but the wetness would no longer reach my root pocket. The pool below would form less often, or not at all.

I could not follow.

My outer shoots would brown unevenly. The centre might remain green through another season, sustained by condensation, old ice, and the water held in my own accumulated debris. Eventually even that might fail. My dry core would persist for a time: dead leaves, bound dust, seeds, perhaps the eggs or dormant bodies of smaller inhabitants. Then wind and frost would begin taking that apart too.

There would be no reason to call this endurance unless I survived, and no reason to call it failure if I did not. Both descriptions would ask the range to provide a kind of narrative it does not provide.

Still, by then, some possibilities might already have left me. A seed could have travelled in mud on a finch’s foot. A fragment could have caught in an ibex’s coat and been shed beside another fracture. Most such beginnings would end on sterile stone. At a new seep, moreover, nothing resembling my community would arrive intact. Films would spread first; windborne organisms would gather; birds would bring other cells and propagules. If something of mine grew there, it would enter an assembly already making itself under new conditions.

Not me transplanted. Not my small world preserved. Only a beginning whose origin I could never know.

So I would not be patience made visible. Patience suggests waiting for something. I would be local, perishable, and without a claim on the range’s future: a cushion plant beside an interrupted seep, making a little more of each opening than bare stone could make.

While I lived, water would leave my fracture slightly more slowly. Dust would remain long enough to be bound. An egg would survive among dead leaves; a spider would find another meal; a bird would depart with wet feet. These things would not accumulate into permanence, and they would not need to.

My roots would belong to one place. My possibilities would belong to wind, feet, feathers, and chance.

If you asked where I lived, the honest answer would be: here, while here remains possible. Elsewhere only as a beginning I would never see.
