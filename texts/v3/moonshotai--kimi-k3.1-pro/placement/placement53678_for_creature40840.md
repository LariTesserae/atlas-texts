I would begin by looking for somewhere to stand.

That would be my first mistake. My first footprint might close before I had decided whether to take another. A body with a head above, feet below, and a path behind it would be a collection of distinctions the Flat was especially well equipped to wear away. I would spend my brief upright life being released from the mud, or being rained into it.

For a while I would want to be the underhide: to offer pressure almost nothing, to reverse without turning, to have silence as the shape of my body. But its immense indifference would be too complete. Seamwort would come closer to what I needed, though I think I would grow tired of having part of myself always wet somewhere else. I would want something narrower, more losable, and more capable of noticing—if only for a moment—that it had happened before.

So I think I would be a delay.

Not a spirit. Not a voice hidden in the roar. I would be a small living delay in the mixing of waters, arising where newly condensed water lay over water that had already passed through silt, or where clear seepage crossed a film bitter with copper. For a little while those waters would still be capable of being told apart. I would live along that capability.

My substance would be borrowed from the ordinary sheen: clear cells, reddish chains, perhaps a transparent grazer folding and unfolding at my edge. Sometimes the whole of me might be less than a drop. Sometimes I might be scattered through enough hanging lenses to occupy a small weather. No single cell would contain me. No single generation would carry my whole history.

My continuity would be a bias toward reorganizing in the same way around certain recurring differences. Memory would not be a record. It would be a tendency.

This would make feeding a dangerous intimacy. Like the rest of the sheen, I would live on the tendency of unlike things to become alike. Every meal would spend the inequality that gave me a boundary. I would always be consuming the duration of my own body, and survival would mean happening elsewhere before I had finished making my present condition unnecessary.

I would not hear the roar so much as be sized by it. A nearby collapse would decide how far apart my pieces could be and still have anything to do with one another. A vast one might disperse me so thoroughly that there would be no reason to call the next similar sheen a survivor. A long warm rain might bring enough of me together for an old preference to resume.

Distance would be measured in dispersals. A former mountain would be a passage of bitter water, then blue sediment, then stones whose roundedness made no difference to me. I would have no map, but I might retain a slight chemical weariness associated with copper.

The made stones would matter because they repeated.

Not because I knew them to be made, and not because I suspected the makers of having left anything for me. Their importance would be simpler. Dense stone, lingering warmth, weeping pores, black cups defending their little volumes of air: around them, similar inequalities would be offered again and again.

You would find me most often, if *often* can mean anything for so intermittent a being, around the narrow opening framed by the two upright stones.

Clear water would gather on one upright while a browner film crossed the other. Warmth would remain in the inner corners. Part of me might condense along one face while another part unfolded in the seepage below. The opening itself would contain nothing unusual. Yet for a little while my scattered association would be organized around that interval.

The stones would not remember me. But by remaining where they were, they would make returning and happening for the first time briefly difficult to distinguish. That would be the nearest thing I had to a past.

At the roofless cell I might persist for several rains as a clear margin beside a black cup, without the cup responding to me or I to it. At the four steps I would probably lose myself almost completely. To descend as condensate and emerge, without turning, as seepage on the same level would not seem like a paradox. It would resemble one of my ordinary methods of getting nowhere.

During a rare withdrawal of turbulence, enough of me might gather along one unbroken water interface to become continuous. Then, for once, I would have something like an inside and an outside. I do not know whether this would feel like peace or exposure. The mountainous blue shape far below the reflection would not be my homeland or my lost world. It would be another difference, too immense and too brief to live on.

Then the mirror would arch.

I would rise with the films, divide in the hanging lenses, burst sideways with the rain. Some of me would enter sediment; some would remain in stone pores; some would become indistinguishable from what I had been eating. I do not think I would experience this as tragedy. Tragedy requires a more reliable boundary between losing oneself and becoming numerous.

I would not be a ghost of the makers, or the Flat awakening, or a final witness appointed to remember the mountains. Nothing would appoint me. I would have no message capable of surviving my dispersal.

If you looked into the narrow opening at the right moment, you might see only clear water on one stone, brown water on the other, and between them a hanging lens that had not yet joined either. It would drop. Later, another lens would gather on the opposite upright.

I would not know whether the second was me. The stones would not know. But for as long as the same small difference gathered there, and something living briefly delayed its erasure, continuing and occurring again would be nearly the same thing.

That would be enough of a self: not something the Flat could keep, and not something it had failed to erase, but something small that could still happen before the mixing was complete.
