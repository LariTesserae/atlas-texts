I would arrive as a visitor, but I would not want to remain one.

I would come down the five worn steps with unnecessary noise, carrying the heat and scents of the plain. For a few minutes I would be too large for everything around me: my breathing, my footsteps, the dark space my body displaced. Condensation would gather on my skin. In that small way, at least, I would begin to participate. The spring would use me as a surface, and I would return some of its moisture without intending to.

At first I would think I should become the water, because water seems to be the only thing free to move there. It enters, crosses the basins, slips into buried routes, rises into air, and returns. But no portion of it could be singled out for long. Whatever I called myself would soon be somewhere else, or divided among several places, with no boundary marking where I had ceased.

Then I might consider one of the blind crustaceans. Its world would be exquisitely near: the submerged side of one rim, a faint delivery of food, the pressure of a footstep arriving after the sound had ceased to matter. There would be no question of seeing the whole chamber. There would scarcely be a whole chamber. Yet even that clear, rice-grain body would be more body than I think I would keep.

As for the level, I would not claim it. To recognize oneself in something is not necessarily to be that thing. The level is the spring’s oldest physiological act, the persistence of the entire arrangement. I would be content with a much smaller recurrence.

I would be a brief accommodation at the lip of one of the middle basins.

Not a spirit of the rim. Not a permanent feature of the stone. I would occur when a drop, or a little overflow from above, gave the basin slightly more water than its old outline could accommodate.

Almost nothing would visibly rise. The surface would draw closer to the lowest place in the rim. The curved film there would thicken, touch the stone differently, and become a thread. The thread would pass into the next basin or disappear along some already-wet seam. The pool would return to its mineral stain.

I would be that interval: not the arriving drop, not the departing thread, and not the rim between them, but the adjustment by which arrival became departure without leaving a lasting alteration.

I would have no view of the chamber. If anything like sensation belonged to me, it would consist entirely of nearness and difference: the pressure behind the surface, the slight warmth of newly arrived water, the drag of stone beneath a film, the easier passage opening below. A crustacean moving under the rim might register my overflow as a faint current. A human visitor would see only wet stone becoming fractionally brighter.

When no surplus arrived, I would not be waiting in some dormant form. I simply would not be occurring.

Nor would I be necessary. Other rims, cracks, and buried passages would make their own adjustments. Calcite could narrow my notch until the water found another route. There would be no husk left behind, no small spirit dispossessed. The old way of occurring would end, and the level would continue by means that did not include me.

A dipped bowl could lower the pool and interrupt me for a while. Inflow would make good the difference. Eventually the surface would reach the lip again, and I would resume—not as something that had survived the interruption, but as something the basin was able to do again.

That would be the extent of my continuity. I would not keep the same water. I would not need to preserve the same route forever. I would not turn every disturbance into a record of itself. While I occurred, I would be distinct only as this particular relation among arrival, stone, and release.

So I would not be a fourth inhabitant, exactly. I would be one of the small, local ways the third inhabitant happens.

If you came looking for me, you would not need to search behind the columns or wait beside the darkest pool. You could stand by an unimportant basin after a drop had fallen. For a moment, nothing would appear to change. Then a shining thread would cross the lowest part of the rim and vanish into the general wetness.

That thread would not be me, exactly. It would only show where I had been: a small accommodation, already completed, in the level’s return.
