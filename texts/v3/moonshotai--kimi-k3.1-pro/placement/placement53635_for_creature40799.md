At first I imagine myself in the open middle, as one of the great swimmers. There is an attraction in that: a body almost indistinguishable from its own displacement, a country learned by echo rather than sight. But those lives seem given to crossing. One pillar’s note held to the left, an arch’s rushing tone rising ahead, the rim slowly separating into layers—always recognizing, always passing on.

I think I would choose another relation to distance. I would let distance arrive.

My place would be a ready-grown chamber in a solitary pillar, near where the reach of isolated pillars gives way to the open middle. Not a large hall, and not a cavity opening directly into the pillar’s main current. Mine would lie a little aside from that passage. Water would not drive through it, but press in and draw back. The walls would be old tissue, dense and smooth where a fin accidentally brushed them. Around the mouth, where the plant still made small corrections to the opening, the surface would have a closer, softer texture.

The pillar’s drone would come through the wall and through my body at once, making the distinction between those things less useful than it is on land.

I would be one of the eyeless, many-finned animals. An old occupant, perhaps, though not an imposing one. I would hang slightly off-center in the chamber, where the two weak movements of water nearly canceled. No part of me would need to brace against the plant. From outside I would appear motionless; in fact, each fin would be making its own small correction, and the apparent stillness would be the sum of them.

That stillness would not be the absence of activity. It would be something continually maintained.

I would not possess a map of Crownwater. I would possess expectations.

A faint sizzling on the pillar’s outer skin would mean grazers, though not as a category I needed to name. It would be a particular alteration in the familiar drone, sometimes approaching over a long interval, sometimes disappearing when one of them lost its hold. A sieve formation would arrive more diffusely: isolated taps, then a broad field of tapping in which direction became difficult to judge. The formation would open around the pillar before any member could touch it. For a while, my chamber would seem to have thinner walls.

Occasionally a small swimmer would separate from the movement outside and pass the cavity mouth. I would know it first by pressure. My body would contract; there would be a brief disturbance, a feeding, and then I would return to suspension. My stillness would not be innocence. It would only be an economical way of waiting.

The great swimmers would enter my life without entering my chamber. One might pass far outside, known chiefly because the pillar’s note stopped being entirely the pillar’s. The water at the opening would change its weight. Later, a wake might reach me after there was no longer any body to associate with it.

Sometimes a boom would come out of the open middle, and every fin would fold at once.

Then, after an interval too long to feel like an ordinary answer, the rim would return the sound. Its layers would arrive softened and altered by travel. I would gain no measurement from this, no picture of black water extending between walls. But for that short time, the chamber would contain more than its own dimensions. A distant animal, the pillar around me, and the rim beyond sight would all be present in one event. I would be suspended inside a distance I had not crossed.

There would be no surface day by which to divide this life. Hunger would recur. Formations would pass. My own corrections would become, perhaps, less exact. Outside, the plant would replace its tissues and the grazers would keep the feeding pores open without knowing that they did so. Crownwater would remain completed. I would not.

I would have no special office in that balance. I would not be guarding the chamber, or witnessing the sea on its behalf, or listening because Crownwater needed a listener. The plant would not notice my attention and would not reward it. My chamber would not have been grown for me. I would simply be one of the small lives made possible by maintenance whose scale greatly exceeded me.

If another animal passed my pillar, it would probably miss me. There would be no glow, no visible face at the opening, no collected object to indicate occupancy. It might detect only a slight, living complication in the pressure at the cavity mouth. Then the ordinary movement of water would close around that complication again.

When I died, the chamber would not become a grave. Another animal might eventually occupy it, or it might remain empty. Either way, there would be no enduring sign that I had learned its particular currents.

But for the length of one life, Crownwater would have this additional, local relation to itself: a far-off call would become, here, the folding of fins; a passing body would become, here, a brief interruption in flow; the same cold water would be answered from this one position and no other.

I would be one of its quiet animals. Still in Crownwater’s way—not unchanged, but returned to stillness by countless minute corrections. And when those corrections ceased, there would be only water resuming its former path.
