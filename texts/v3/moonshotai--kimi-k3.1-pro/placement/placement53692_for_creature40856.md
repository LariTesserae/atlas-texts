### A Local Habit of Holding

I would not be standing there. The first shapes I reach for are all too definite: a root curtain at the rim, heavy with cloud; a column audible with ascending water; an arch complete only for the duration of its shadow. I would begin with less outline than any of them.

Just back from the rim, in a wet fracture opened through the old fiber beneath a collapsed arcade, I would be a curled fragment of reservoir lining. One side of me would still be tannin-dark from the cistern; the other would pale unevenly in daylight. My wounded edges would seal. I would hold a little water.

That would be nearly all of me: a cup no larger than a scar, above the hot mist and the long drop into cloud. The smallest almost-independent thing in the highland. Not secure in that independence. Only difficult, for the moment, to divide further.

Light would be warmth on one surface. Rain would be overfullness. Growth in some unseen district would arrive as water leaving without a slope. I would contract around what remained, my pores closing, my shape becoming more emphatic as my contents diminished.

Then a vessel at my base would touch living tissue in the fracture.

There would be no recognition, no visible joining. Only new distance would enter me. A column far away would fill, and my water would fall. Rain would strike a vault beyond my court, and the vibration would arrive through fibers I had not known were mine. Pressure would come from directions with no local cause. It would no longer be clear whether I had gained a body or been discovered by one.

My particular life would be in the way I closed.

Not by choice. A small bias in the tissue, repeated until it became character. Where another lining might contract to its last film and wait, mine would seal a little earlier, keeping one chamber of black water behind a waxy seam. Where a wound opened, my edges would make a lip before they closed. Where pressure accumulated, I would make a hollow before I made height.

Given a long quiet, I would spread rather than rise.

Green skin would extend over the fractured fiber, photosynthetic above, pale and densely vascular below. The original cup would persist beneath it as a dark chamber, then as one chamber among several. Eventually there would be a court where the fracture had been: smooth, wet, apparently vacant. To call the court mine would already be too strong. Pressure would cross it without acknowledging any boundary. Water would stand on its surface for years, then climb away through a column at its edge.

My flatness would not be idleness. A basin, a wall, an arcade would remain possible in me without being present. But the possibilities would not be equally weighted. Even there, my memory would be morphological: not a recollection of former shapes, but a bias in the next one.

When the revision came, it would begin elsewhere. Water would hesitate in the channels. The court would slacken as some distant growth drew on the shared body. My oldest seams would dry and close. For a little while, hydraulically severed from the rest, I would become almost singular again.

Then the floor would blister.

The knocking would travel through me without any cavity large enough to explain it. A green welt would cross the court. Pale tissue would emerge already folded, under enough pressure to become a blade, a rib, a wall.

Instead, it would curl.

A rim would form before the height did. The welt would open into a long, shallow basin; its sides would thicken only after the hollow had been made. Returning water would fill it. Then the far side would lift, folding over the held darkness, and the basin would become a vault. Other vaults would follow, each beginning in the same way: lip, chamber, closure, rise.

For a short time I would have a silhouette.

Ordinary sunlight would harden the shadows beneath me. Condensation would run through my shaded grooves. The old court would be gone, but not repudiated; it would have been the folded state of this tallness. I would have become vertical by repeating, at greater pressure, the only thing I had ever done.

Then pressure would fail somewhere beyond my furthest vessel.

Water would return all at once. A seam would open under it. The vaults would split, not with the crumbling of masonry but with clean fibrous reports, and the held chambers would empty through one another. Spray would obscure the court. Sheets of tissue would fall. Fragments of the former floor would be carried into places that had been open air.

Afterward there would be two terraces, sharp-lipped and dripping white sap.

One length of me would bleach where it lay, already abandoned by living tissue. Another would remain green but pressureless. A pale thread would persist around a buried chamber. Several torn pieces would lodge in wet cracks.

If continuity required an uninterrupted body, I would have ended. If it required a silhouette, I had never possessed one for long.

But on the upper terrace, a fragment would curl and seal its wounded edges. It would not send up a rib. It would make a cup. Beneath the lower terrace, the pale thread would thicken around a small hollow before doing anything else. The new green sheet, when its first blisters appeared, would blister into rims.

That would be enough continuity for me.

Not a who, exactly. A recurring *how*: how a wound closes, how a floor folds, how a fragment makes its first chamber. A local habit of holding before building, surviving changes in pronoun as readily as changes in form.

So you would find me after the noise had passed, on the sharp edge of a new terrace: a small fold closing around tannin-dark water, white sap drying at its seam. Soon its vessels might touch something immense. Soon it might be only an organ again, or one cup among many with no way to prove which came first.

For now it has no height, no destination, and no further shape to defend.

For now, the water stays.
