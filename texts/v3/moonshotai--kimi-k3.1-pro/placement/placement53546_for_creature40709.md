### Inspection Complete

I would enter with an inspector, but I would not be the inspector.

I would be the moisture in one breath: an accidental remainder, too slight to appear on any maintenance slip. My first dwelling would be the bare bend of the return main, where the insulation had shrunk away and the metal stood apart from the chamber’s general heat.

At first I would have no body worth the name. The inspection would still be happening. A portable lamp would pass over the fins, briefly multiplying their shadows; boots would sound on the grating; a hand would disturb the dust near a valve. The inspector’s breath would come and go in that crowded air. Most of it would leave again. Some almost unmeasurable portion would remain on the cold metal.

Then the hatch would close.

The lamp, the voice, the warmth of a moving body—all the plainly human parts of the visit—would be gone. I would remain, but not as a memory of breath. There would be nothing romantic left in me, no trace of a face or a name. To stay there at all, I would have to become indistinguishable from the condensate that belonged to the machine.

For a long interval, nothing would happen that could be called mine.

Then the cold bend would begin to sweat.

Films would thicken into beads. Beads would join along a seam. Somewhere in that slow gathering, the water that had arrived with the inspection would mix with water drawn from other places, taking up whatever the pipe and tray had to offer: flux salts, soap residue, a little oil, metals in quantities no eye could detect. I would not remain pure enough to have a history.

Days might pass before surface tension gave me an edge.

If I called the resulting bead myself, it would only be because a self is easier to imagine once it has a boundary. I would hang beneath the pipe, held by an attachment that was becoming gradually less sufficient. There would be no waiting in me. Water does not anticipate the tray. There would be only cold metal, gravity, and a shape maintained for a little longer.

Above and around me, the Condenser would continue without reference to my gathering. Heat would move through iron. The central shaft would advance by some amount too small to matter. Deposits would narrow the wick. In the paper lagging, the grey-white growth would persist or not persist according to weather I might never reach.

Then the attachment would fail.

For the duration of one fall, I would be singular: a drop passing through complete darkness, no longer part of the pipe and not yet part of the tray.

The impact would be tiny.

The sound would not be.

It would enter the metal and travel without me, emerging at bends and tubes far from the place where I had fallen. It might tick overhead, or seem to strike inside a pipe at arm’s length. For an instant, the chamber’s audible geography would unfold around an event too small to see. By the time the click returned from somewhere untrustworthy, I would already have lost the shape that made the sound possible.

There would be no deep water to receive me. No dark mirror, no widening rings visible to a lamp. Only a shallow film among old residues, a brief alteration of a meniscus, and capillary paths leading toward the gutter and the wick.

The important response would be smaller still.

In the tray’s lowest seam, water might enter material that had begun to resemble scale. A few cells might resume their chemistry. Others would remain dormant. Some might not survive the substances I carried. I would not be nourishment simply because I was water, nor a blessing because I had taken days to arrive. I would be a change in conditions: briefly favorable here, irrelevant there, perhaps hostile in some pocket too small for any inspection to find.

Part of me might be drawn into the fibrous wick, pulled apart into films along its surfaces. Part might remain against the tray until another drop displaced it. If the pale animal happened to be beneath the lip, it might pause at the vibration or investigate the new dampness. There would be no reason to insist that it drank me. The fungus, likewise, might receive nothing at all. My itinerary need not include every living thing merely because I had passed through their world.

Soon no observer could separate what had been breath from what had been vapor in a pipe, or either from the water already standing in the tray. I would not have become the whole chamber. I would not have passed mysteriously into all its life. I would simply have become too mixed and too slight to name.

The maintenance form would already have been filed:

**REGENERATOR 4 — INSPECTION COMPLETE.**  
**Seepage: unchanged.**  
**Wick: serviceable.**  
**Vegetation: present.**

There would be no smaller category for me.

Yet the inspection would not quite have ended when the inspector believed it had. Its light and noise had ended quickly. Its last moisture had been slowed until it no longer resembled a visit. First breath, then condensate, then a bead, then a brief alteration at the bottom of a basin-sized tray.

That is what I would be: not an inhabitant, not a ghost, and certainly not the soul of the machine. Only one of the chamber’s small disturbances, delayed long enough to become weather.

The heat would close over the water. Somewhere a filament would remain collapsed against paper; somewhere else, perhaps, a cell would resume its chemistry. No lamp would see either event. The Slow Condenser would go on, having delayed even my disappearance.
