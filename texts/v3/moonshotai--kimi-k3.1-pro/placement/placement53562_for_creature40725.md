You would find me where the drumming weakens.

Not at a crown, though there would be crowns in the distance, laboring through their partial turns. Not among the fresh thickets of a storm front, either. I would be out on the interval: white rind, mineral shadow, and a low glassy welt crossing the ground so subtly that, from standing height, it might seem only a change in the light.

Through my feet, that welt would be an uproar of incompatibilities.

On the near side, a footfall would return quickly through old, dense rind. Pressure would run away along buried vessels; an active conduit would make its faint continuous answer. On the far side, the same blow would spread, flatten, and come back late. Somewhere below, roots had grafted without agreeing about every signal. The ground was continuous. Its answers were not.

I would be standing across that difference with most of my weight on the near side, testing the other with one foot at a time.

At first you might mistake me for a piece of a fallen crown that the rind had not yet opened. My rear scales would be gray and terraced, while the armor over my shoulders remained darker and flexible. I would have too many legs for symmetry, several of them budding at slightly different heights along my broader left side. When my oil bladder was full, its divisions would lift my back in three uneven rises, and my hindmost feet would begin to strike out of sequence.

I would be a gleaner, old enough to have acquired some of a longback’s habits, young enough that all my feet could still leave the ground.

Most of them would be listening.

I would have made a route of province borders. Not because anything there welcomed me. Border roots are suspicious organs: they accept tissue and refuse signals, accept signals and offer no oil, or answer an attachment request by beginning to digest the mouth that made it. Feeding is difficult on such ground. Difficult feeding is often left unfinished.

To call what I did translation would flatter it. I remembered sequences and what the ground did afterward.

A rhythm that opened a seam in one province might, across the welt, produce only a brief contraction. Another might be answered three roots away. Sometimes I could vary an old signal until the rind tolerated it. Sometimes I could not, and I would have to choose between hunger and cutting. Cutting cost oil; the blue gleam at a damaged vessel could bring small root-mouthed things investigating whether my boundaries were still worth respecting.

So I learned. Not meanings, exactly. Results. Routes. Rejections.

Younger gleaners sometimes followed because my routes led to full bladders or to roots that would accept concentrate. They were not mine, and I did not lead them for long. They joined when my pace suited them and left when it did not. Often they left me with something: a shortened storm warning, a different way of testing hurried growth, a hind-foot cadence picked up in a province I would never cross.

What I kept longest were the sequences with no evident use.

One belonged to a longback whose rooted body I had crossed several storms earlier. Its armor had already flattened into a low swelling, but the buried tissue still answered certain vibrations with a faint alteration in pressure. I had repeated its irregular signature against that swelling until some part of the answer seemed to correspond.

Another was half of a pattern exchanged between two herds at a border. I had arrived too late to learn the beginning. I carried the ending anyway.

Another may have been only a preference in the placement of a foot.

When I was idle, these sequences returned. When the ground could carry them, I sometimes sent them out again. I would not have called this remembrance. Remembering suggests that the original thing remains somewhere intact enough to be compared with its copy. I had no such assurance.

My own signature contained a pause.

Not silence. There was no silence to make. The rind ticked beneath me; crowns knocked beyond the horizon; charge moved through the plain with a pressure that never entirely ceased. The pause was only an interval in which I added nothing and listened to what returned.

It may have begun as damage to a signaling cap. It may have been the surviving fragment of an older animal’s rhythm. By the time you found me, I would no longer know. Young gleaners frequently got it wrong. Some struck through the pause. Some lengthened it until the pattern broke in two. Occasionally a herd carried off only the second half and used it as a feeding request.

This was how my patterns acquired more ground than my feet could cover.

Once, while I worked at the border welt, a herd would approach from the far province. I would know it first as unrelated touches through the roots, then as a common rhythm. The animals would emerge from the glare as low silhouettes, their backs still dark, their bladders nearly empty after the last front.

Nothing ceremonious would happen when we met.

We would feed. We would test the same buried conduit and receive different answers. A smooth-backed youngster would stand behind me for a while, copying the pressure of my hind feet. When it crossed the welt, it would repeat my signature with an extra blow inside the pause. On that ground, the altered pattern would bring a clean response.

The youngster would repeat it.

Within a few minutes, several others would be doing the same.

By then I would already be turning toward a root that had accepted me before. I had concentrate to discharge, and beneath one rear scale I carried a reproductive bud left by a crown in another province. The herd had its own material to carry. We would part without having exhausted one another’s signals, and the new rhythm would go with them.

Eventually, of course, a root would accept too much of me.

I would come to it heavy, or merely tired, and remain attached longer than I intended. When I finally moved, my rear feet would stay fixed. At first I could still tear them free. Later the scales around them would begin to spread into the rind, and the vessels of my bladder would widen toward a reservoir.

There would be no single hour in which I ceased to wander.

My body would continue forward for a time on the legs that remained detachable. Then the mouth would close. The back would flatten. What had been armor would become a low, weathered terrace beside the border, easily mistaken for the swelling left by an old fallen crown.

For a while after that, my forward feet would continue drumming.

Some of the sequences would be practical. Some would not.

And after the drumming ceased, if you still wished to find me, you would have to wait for another herd. You would have to feel the slight change in its step as it crossed the terrace, or the altered rhythm passing from foot to foot when it reached the welt. There might be a pause where once there had been a blow. There might be an extra impact inside it. There might be nothing left but a tendency to test a certain kind of root twice before cutting.

No one could prove that it was remembrance.

The pattern would cross the border regardless.

You would find me in the pause—and in whatever the next feet made after it.
