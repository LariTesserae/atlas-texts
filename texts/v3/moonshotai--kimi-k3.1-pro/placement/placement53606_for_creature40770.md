At first I want the view from the pass: the whole basin visible at once, every reservoir and roof given its proper place. Then I want to be the water, because water seems to touch everything—snowfield, turbine, garden soil, the white collar around a leaking joint.

But the view belongs mostly to visitors, and the water does not get to know what it touches. It is divided, measured, hurried onward. Even its stillness is usually someone else’s work.

For a while, a depot cat seems a better possibility. I would belong to a building rather than a household, sleep above a warm manifold, and appear at the canteen with an accurate understanding of shifts. My ignorance of the upper reservoirs would trouble me not at all.

Yet I keep returning to the private supplements to the drawings: the crooked drain penciled over the straight one, the note that a wall is thicker than the plan admits, the pause when an old fitter hears something no instrument has yet confirmed. I think I would want to live there—in the narrow space where the drawing and the wall are allowed to correct one another.

So I would rather have a round than a view.

By roster, I would be a water sampler. Not the chief sampler, and certainly not someone whose unsupported word could stop a turbine. My basin would be a small circuit through the middle and lower works: eleven sampling cocks, two covered channels, the still ends of three settling ponds, two flights of stairs, and a freight lift I would use when it was working and the bottle crate was full. There would be an apparent shortcut behind the laundry that I would stop recommending after it deposited me on the wrong side of a pressure main.

I would carry glass bottles in a wooden crate, labels written in pencil, and a notebook in an oilskin sleeve. At the back of the notebook would be a folded official plan, increasingly difficult to refold. Some of its lines would be right. Others would have acquired dates, question marks, and the initials of people who had opened the wall.

My round would begin at a cold intake line that numbed my fingers and end, on good days, beside a warm outfall where sedges remained green after frost had touched the ferns nearby. I would learn which tap had to be eased rather than turned, which sample always carried a little rust after a pump start, and which pond corner the beetles returned to once the current slackened. None of this would amount to knowing the basin. It would amount to knowing where to stand without obstructing anyone.

The work would suit me because it makes a discipline of small claims. A bottle is not a reservoir. A reading is not a trend. Even a trend is not an explanation. I would write *no visible change* rather than *unchanged*, and try not to feel that the extra words were merely caution. They would be part of the observation.

My notebook would also gather things that were not, strictly, samples. A new stain above an old one. A gardener’s report that a fern had begun to lean. A pump driver’s dislike of a sound that still fell within tolerance. When an experienced fitter said a bearing was wrong, I would not turn that into a diagnosis, nor dismiss it because the instruments were calm. I would record who heard it, when, and what happened next. If the instruments later agreed, I would add that. If they did not, I would leave the disagreement intact.

I would sometimes back the wrong side. On those days I would have to correct the entry in a hand anyone could read. Trust, if I acquired any, would not mean being right in general. It would mean being clear about what I had actually seen, what someone else had told me, and what I had merely inferred while walking up a wet stair.

My room would be in the wall housing above a workshop. Its best feature would not be the window, which borrowed most of its light from a bulkhead lamp, but a cupboard that stayed dry. Shoes would spend the night above a heating pipe. The official drawings would live in a tin box; the notebook would stay with me. On the sill there would be a woody little plant with no agreed name, grown from a cutting taken before a roof bed was dismantled for repairs. I would not be particularly skilled with it. I would only know that it disliked a wet pot and survived being forgotten better than being fussed over.

My household obligation would be four numbered drainage holes and the grating outside my landing. I would occasionally forget the grating until a child reminded me. I would complain about the damp, envy a neighbor’s dry storage room, and sometimes become irritated with the very people whose knowledge I needed. Children would know routes I did not. Eventually newcomers would ask me for directions, but only within my levels; beyond that, I would send them elsewhere without pretending.

During storms, my round would disappear. The glass would be stowed, the papers raised, the cupboard checked. I would help keep the landing clear, watch my drainage holes, carry messages or food where they were needed, and stay away from work I was not trained to interrupt. I would not save the basin. I would try to be one fewer thing for the skilled crews to worry about.

Afterward, I would go out again with the bottles. The water would be higher and its usual arguments altered. A reed fragment would have established itself somewhere inconvenient. Someone would be rescuing plants from a ruined bed while, nearby, a wall was being inspected for movement. I would take a cutting if one were offered, but not before asking whether the bed was to be rebuilt.

By disposition, then, I suppose I would be a margin: a narrow place where an instrument reading, an old worker’s pause, a stained wall, and a leaning fern could be set beside one another without any of them being mistaken for the whole. My notes would be incomplete and some of them would be wrong. I would hope the next person corrected them rather than preserved them. And I would hope the plant on my sill survived me as cuttings in other rooms, under other names.

You would find me in the early evening, after the cloud had lifted enough to return the far retaining wall but not yet the towers. The canal would be running high. On the dry side of a lamp housing, a snail would be waiting out the weather. I would fill a bottle at the lowest sampling cock and hold it against the pale workshop window. It would look exactly like yesterday’s water.

I would write the time, the level, and *no visible change*. Then, beneath that, in smaller writing:

*Fern below outfall leaning toward walkway. Joint weeping above old stain. Ask night mason whether this happened after the last storm.*

I would cork the bottle, leave almost the whole basin unknown behind me, and carry my small portion of it upstairs.
