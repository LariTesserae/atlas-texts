### A Road That Remembers

You would find me with one foot extended and my weight still on the other three.

To you, in the star-dark, I might be only a large warmth where the saddle had been cold: shag stiff with frozen breath, a slow exhale, the faint dry ticking of husk caught in hair. Behind me, the herd would be almost indistinguishable from the leaf. No two animals would be moving together.

To me, the foot would be full of news.

The membrane would return my touch a little too late. Somewhere beneath it, tissue had changed its tension. Perhaps an old bladder district was settling. Perhaps water was gathering in the sag, and midges were already darkening over it. Perhaps the whole tract would remain for years, or tear before we reached the far rib.

I would not need to know which before deciding not to cross.

I would be an old felt-ox, one of the animals people call a lead when they need to believe that somebody has decided. I would not lead by knowing the way. I would lead by refusing often enough, and by remembering that a refusal is not always fear.

My route would not be a line across the High Hollow. It would be a sequence of things my body had once been permitted to do.

Here, weight could be given. Here, it had to be taken back. Here, the herd had waited while ice made its fine repeated ticking along a fibre. Here, a structural cord had held, though only when we crossed out of step. Here, young growth had tasted bitter before we had cropped enough, and we had moved on with the sap burning faintly in our mouths.

Some of those places would still exist when we returned. Some would have lifted, sealed, split, or been carried apart by growth. Memory would bring us to the place where listening had to begin again.

I would have drunk from basins that were dry wax when I next found them. I would have returned to a grazing saddle and discovered a young blade standing where the herd had once slept. As a calf, I would have followed an old animal toward a remembered catchment and felt her turn aside before any of us could sense why. Later, when the district behind us opened with a report that shuddered through my bones, I would still not have called it knowledge.

Only experience. Only another date attached to a place.

My shag would carry its own dated geography: midge eggs from a seam long since drained, wax mites sealed against the cold, broken fibre, the papery remains of a roost through which we had passed. Around my lower legs there might be a rim of ice from the last pool. At the next wet place, some small part of that cargo would wake or fall or be eaten.

Nothing in me would intend this. Intention would not make it less true.

A herd is one of the ways distant pockets learn of one another. I would understand none of that while cropping felt from a leaf saddle. I would understand hunger, the growing bitterness of defensive sap, the pressure of calves against my side, and the moment when needlewings began clicking over the water in such numbers that the air itself seemed to be full of fine grit.

Then we would move, and life would come with us without asking our permission.

People might move with us for a while.

I would know their camps not by chords but by small, repeated pressures: feet crossing a platform one at a time, an awl’s tiny interruptions as someone repaired cord, the careful placement of a lamp pad on dead husk. I would know the difference between a person standing still and a person listening. Their stillness would have an extra attention in it, almost like another limb touching the structure.

They might take shed hair from my sides. In another season, if I allowed it, they might take milk. They might remain near the herd until growth or water or hunger drew them elsewhere. Sometimes they would suppose they were following us. Sometimes we would turn toward the faint, familiar disturbances of their crossings, because careful feet often marked a slow-growing node.

Neither would make us theirs, or them ours.

When the deep wind rose, there would be no leading in any visible sense. The plant’s roar would enter the body and become indistinguishable from the body’s own noise. The herd would bunch. Calves would press inward. My call would go down through hoof and membrane, too low to be wasted on the air.

Another animal would receive it through bone and flesh. Then another.

*Here.*

*Here.*

*Here.*

For a little while we would be one large, shivering animal distributed across the saddle, each part assuring the others that distance had not yet become separation. On some nearby platform, a person might wake with aching teeth and know we were afraid. They would not know that we were also counting.

At the doubtful membrane, I would remain extended-footed until the calf behind me grew restless and then quiet again. It would be copying my posture without understanding it. That was how calves began: first the shape of caution, then the reasons, if they lived long enough to gather reasons.

Eventually I would take my foot back.

The movement would pass through the herd more reliably than any call. Shoulders would turn. Weight would shift. One by one, out of rhythm, we would begin the long dry way around.

Perhaps we would be leaving water behind us. Perhaps seam-fleas were already uncurling there, cup efts softening in their leathery discs, beetles warming beneath a new sap scab. A whole abundance might occur without us. That would not make our refusal wrong. No herd could arrive at every knot of life, and not every gathering required our hunger.

I would not be leading the others toward certainty. I would be leading them toward the next decision.

Much later, on a saddle whose note I remembered, I would stop again. The place might be safe. It might merely be familiar. Those are not the same thing, though the body longs to confuse them.

The calf would come alongside me. Already it would be heavier than it had been at the last wet seam. Already its shag would carry things I had never carried. It would extend one broad foot, hesitate, and look—though looking would tell it almost nothing.

I would wait.

Above us, the stars would give their hard, useless brilliance. Beneath us, sap would tick inside a stem. Far off, a bound cluster of route-stems might sound its chord and receive, after a long interval, an answer from some camp we would never visit.

The calf’s foot would remain where it was.

That would be what I had become, after all the crossings: not a map, not an owner of water, not even quite a leader. A moving knot of warmth. A carrier of small awakenings. An old memory learning, again, not to mistake itself for the world.

Then the membrane would settle beneath my own testing touch.

Not stillness. Nothing in the High Hollow was still. Only a brief agreement between weight and what must bear it.

I would put my foot down softly.

Behind me, the herd would begin to cross, and the road would begin again.
