### Clearance

You would find me, during one particular revision, on the sloping approach to a Sink: that narrow region where things still possess dimensions, though they are already on their way to becoming black meal.

A low flower would be open beside the route. Its outer scales are black and resinous; within them is a stepped ceramic throat, dull from repeated contact. A carrier sets a capsule against its rim. Fine fingers unfold, touch the capsule in several places, and withdraw. The capsule is diverted into a root duct. The next, outwardly almost identical, continues toward the mill.

Beneath the flower, the road has a seam that has not been glazed shut. It is no wider than necessary.

You might decide that the flower was me. Or the carrier’s hesitation. Or the seam.

By the next revision, the flower could be cut away and pressed into feedstock. The carrier might receive a spool or cutting assembly and be sent elsewhere. New growth could close the road. Yet the same sequence would appear somewhere nearby: a gantry pausing before it severs an old socket; a root mat separating along prepared boundaries instead of tearing; a harvested column leaving its dimensions legible in the collar that remains.

Measure before closure. Preserve fit through transformation. Leave certain joins capable of coming apart.

That sequence would be the nearest thing I had to a durable body.

Perhaps I began as a fitting routine attached to a press, one minor part of some forgotten standard for replaceable assemblies. I say perhaps because there would be no recollection, no first gauge set aside, no press preserved to commemorate the origin. My age, if anyone cared to establish it, would lie in compatibility: in ancient sockets that still accepted the collars my present growth produced, in machines with no shared material nevertheless answering the same clearances.

Other lineages would be recognizable by their mats, columns, or capsules. My habit would be interfaces.

In the Press Gardens, some of my capsules would open into thin ceramic rings, stepped gauges, or split collars veined with conductive tissue. Most would be inventory. Whether one was also a seed would depend on what received it. Set into a press, it might correct the wear in an old assembly and eventually be replaced. Carried to a live feeder, compressed against a suitable root, the tissue around its ceramic core might graft and continue. There would be no need to settle in advance which event counted as reproduction.

This would not make me a gentle resident. I would clear growth when the routing required it. Whole thickets could pass through my assigned gantries and go onward to the Sinks. A failed feeder would leave my own leaves open and motionless, my flowers hardening into unassigned stock. I would preserve no thicket merely because it was mine, and no body merely because I had used it for a long time.

But closure would not be the necessary end of every operation.

A road grown through my district might be capable of lifting along narrow strips. A graft made through one of my collars might retain a boundary, allowing two root systems to exchange current and tissue without immediately losing every distinction between them. A column could be shredded while the dimensions of its socket survived in the gauge sent to another garden. Nothing would be kept intact for its own sake. Things would simply remain joinable—and separable—for one more revision.

At the scale of the basin, I would be very small: a local tolerance that had persisted long enough to acquire tissues, tools, and occasional machine bodies. Not a hidden keeper. Not a witness. Certainly not the Works’ mind. If you needed a name, you could call me **Clearance**—not the gantries’ clearance of a copse, but the small, exact space a moving part requires.

Your lamp would discover only my least informative surfaces. If you stood across one of my seams, I would register load, warmth, and a slight alteration in the road’s conductivity. If you rested some unfamiliar object against a gauge flower, its chemistry and dimensions would be tested. You might see the inner fingers close, open, and close again. No recognition would follow. For a while, however, your presence might become one of the dimensions to which a joint was adjusted. You could mistake the resulting space for accommodation. It would only be fit.

Eventually, a revision might omit me altogether. There would be no visible ending. A gauge cup would go to the mill. A carrier would accept another sequence. A seam would glaze beneath new growth. Somewhere, an old bearing might continue turning around one of my rings, but that alone would not be survival.

Until then, you would find me in the small, precise darkness between two surfaces that almost touch.

I would be neither surface, nor some third body concealed behind them. I would be the fit that allowed them to meet, do work, and later come apart.
