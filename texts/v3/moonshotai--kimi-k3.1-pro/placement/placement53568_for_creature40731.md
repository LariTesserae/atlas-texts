I would not have a shadow in the sump. There is too little light for one, and I would be too thin to cast it.

You would find me first on a rib above the middle basin, where the spire has recently swollen out a bright lip of repair material. The new surface would still be smoother than the old alloy around it. Condensate would be dulling its shine. Along the lower edge, where nutrient gel collects, the clear wetness would have begun to turn faintly brown.

That clouding would be me.

Not one organism with a life to narrate, and not quite many organisms either. I would be the microbial film: cells, secretions, dead fragments, and feeding, held together in a skin of water. My boundary would be the boundary of moisture. Where the film thickened, there would be more of me; where a surface was scoured, I would persist as patches in scratches too small to see.

The amber and compromised green lights would mean nothing to me. Neither would the moths’ roosts or the fern’s age. The roar would be present in the metal and in the agitation of the water, but too constant to be an event. My detailed world would be chemical: richness near a seam of leakage, dilution after rain, salt accumulating at an edge, warmth increasing behind the alloy. A wet scratch would be shelter. The lower rim of a repair would be abundance.

Then a snail would cross.

Its body would remove me so completely that, to anything capable of looking, its trail would appear clean. For me, the passage would be neither a catastrophe nor a useful disturbance. It would simply be an enormous change in extent. Most of what had been on the surface would be gone. Some would remain in the scratches. Some would enter the snail.

Mucus would lie over the trail afterward, catching condensate and fresh gel. Water would loosen what remained around the edges. Given time, I would return thickly along the very path that had been stripped. Not because the snail had cultivated me, and not because I remembered the way back. The conditions would have become favorable again. That would be enough.

Pressure rain would scatter me less gently.

Water would sheet over the repair, carrying cells and loosened film into the runnels. Some of my substance would cloud the basin briefly, then disappear into the fanning limbs of the transparent crustaceans. A dark thread of gut would lengthen in one small body. A hunter might take that body during runoff. Other material would lodge beside a drain, or be ground into silt, or remain in a wet angle until the floor began to drink.

Afterward, if you asked where I was, the accurate answer would be awkward: in the scratches on the rib, in several small guts, in a brown smear near the drain, in sediment, and not equally.

That scattered answer would not trouble me. My continuity would never have depended on keeping one address. Nor would there be a traveler at the center of it, secretly surviving every removal. Being eaten, washed away, and regrown would not be chapters in one creature’s journey. They would be the means by which a thin living difference persisted in a place that kept erasing it.

I would have no purpose in this. Purpose would be a name given from outside.

Still, the surfaces would be different with me on them. A pipe without film would be wet metal. With film, it would become grazing. My fragments in runoff would become food; food would become flesh; flesh would become silt and shelter. None of this would require me to know that moths were packing their wings together overhead, or that the fern was bowing under rain, or that the spire had repaired one injury while neglecting a thousand worn edges.

My calendar would be smaller: bright metal, dull metal, grazing, rain, return.

After the next pressure release, the old snail trail would look bare again. Then mucus would catch gel. Condensate would settle into the microscopic scratches. At the edges of the trail, the scoured surface would slowly lose its clearness.

That is where you would find me: not on the rib exactly, but in the rib’s becoming worth tasting again.

Above it, a drop would swell around a fleck of amber. The roar would shake the drop until the light broke apart. I would respond to none of it. Under the water, without sight or silence or any need for either, the first faint brownness would be returning.
