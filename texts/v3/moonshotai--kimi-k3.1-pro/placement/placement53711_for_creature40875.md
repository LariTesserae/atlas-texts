You would have to look for the place before you could find me.

Not on the active terrace, where the water still spreads in opaline sheets and lays down fresh mineral crust. A little behind it, where deposition has slowed. There, rain has darkened the hollows, soil has gathered behind an old sinter lip, and a terrace fern has made enough shade for a piece of fallen laurel to remain damp through the afternoon.

On one side of that fragment of wood, the seepage is too mineral-rich for most roots. On the other, the ground grades into cold, saturated litter. Between them runs a narrow allowance of warmth, moisture, and decay. In some places it is wider than your hand; in others, only a root bridges it.

After rain, if you turned the dead wood carefully, you might see a dull amber branching beneath it. The branches would be finer than fern roots, joined here and there into small, glistening nets. They would not quite resemble veins, roots, or the trails left by slugs. While you watched, the outermost thread would contract from the light.

That would be me. Or the accessible edge of me.

I would be a sinter veil: a slime mold of the warm margins, not a fungus, though I would spend my life among fungi and share some of their work. At my greatest extent, I might cover three irregular metres of ground. A tahr could cross the whole of me without breaking stride. I would have no head, no protected interior, no single direction in which I faced the world. My growing edges would be wherever conditions permitted growth.

There would be no need to make me the mountain’s intelligence. The vents would not speak through me. I would understand nothing of the lake, the far rim, or the clouds descending the outer cliffs. I would not even possess the whole terrace. My caldera would be a few gradients: wood soft enough to enter, films rich enough to graze, water cool enough to tolerate, shade deep enough to prevent drying. Beyond those, the basin would not be distant scenery. It would simply be impossible.

For you, rain would be the loudest regular event. For me, it would arrive first as a change in weight and softness. The old laurel would swell slightly. The bacterial films would loosen. Minerals freshly washed from the active ground would alter the seep at my warmer edge. The sound of rain would belong to leaves and stone above me; beneath the wood, I would experience its consequences.

In good conditions, I would spread into the remains of an abandoned microbial mat, taking its organic residue while leaving the silica behind. Elsewhere I would follow the softened tissues of a fallen frond or concentrate around a patch of rot where springtails had already been feeding. Some of them would feed on me in turn. I would advance along several routes and abandon most of them, leaving faint tracks that dried quickly when exposed.

Nothing around me would have been arranged for my benefit. The fern’s shade, the laurel’s slow decay, the deposited lip that caught soil—each would merely happen to make a little more of the margin survivable. A tahr’s hoof might erase half my living body in an instant. It might also press a fragment into the wet clay of the next footprint. Most such accidents would end in drying, mineral water, or a place without food. Occasionally one would not.

When the seam narrowed or the wood began to fail, I would gather myself into small fruiting bodies: smoke-colored heads raised on hair-fine stalks along the dead stems. They would be easy to overlook even then. A beetle might carry a few spores away in the moisture on its body. Others would be lifted in the brief dry wind between rains. Most would fall back onto the same exhausted ground. A few might reach another cooling channel, another shaded root mass, another piece of wood lying across exactly the right conditions.

That would be my lineage’s way of crossing country: not by enduring the journey, but by becoming possible again at the far end.

If subterranean pressure redirected the spring, the silverlings would move long before I did. The trees would respond long afterward. I would belong to the intermediate time.

The first change might be no more than water remaining warm a little later toward dawn. Then the seep at my upper edge would grow cloudy. A new rust-colored film would appear where clear moisture had formerly collected. The living branches there would cease to divide. Some would contract; others would simply fail, and the still-viable body would continue through the cooler connections beneath the fern.

Over the following days, the fern would cease to be incidental shade and become shelter. A root I had grown around would become a corridor. The fallen laurel, once only one feeding ground among several, would become the last continuous route away from the warmed soil. What looked from above like withdrawal would really be an alteration in where living substance could persist.

If the hot water reached the root before I had passed beyond it, the connection would be severed. On one side there might remain an active fragment beneath wet litter; on the other, a dormant crust in the shade of the wood. Which piece was then me would have no answer written in the tissue. There would be continuity in one place, latency in another, and death elsewhere. Later there might be spores.

Years afterward, the abandoned channel would lose its colors. The orange films would dull; the emerald would dry and curl. Coppergrass would establish along a cooler seam, and fern roots would bind a little more dust behind the terrace lip. My kind might appear briefly on the dead mat, vanish as it became too dry, and appear again beneath the first fallen fronds. None of this would restore an earlier arrangement. Each stage would merely leave materials and omissions from which the next could form.

I would not be an emblem of balance. My life would consist mostly of correction: growth here, withdrawal there, a dry interval survived as something crusted and almost unrecognizable, a fortunate spore arriving where my body could never have gone. I would not be needed by the basin, and the basin would not notice my ending. While I lived, I would take what could be taken and leave the remainder slightly changed.

So you would find yourself kneeling behind the old sinter lip, one hand close enough to the ground to feel its warmth, the other shading the exposed wood. At first there would be only wet earth, a fern root, and a stain that might have been iron. Then the finest amber thread would withdraw beneath the litter.

By the time you looked again, there might be nothing to see.

The margin would be moving, and I would be moving with it—not far, not quickly, and not necessarily whole.
