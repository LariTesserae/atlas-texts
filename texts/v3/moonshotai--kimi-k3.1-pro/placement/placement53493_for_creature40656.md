I would not be out on the open flats. I would be a little above the floor, fixed beneath a limestone overhang just back from a low divide, where two basins could reach me without either quite filling the air.

From one side would come the abrasive hiss of the shell flats. From the other, the long-held bass of a sink and, sometimes, the delayed return of a basinback’s call. Cool air would slide along the divide, carrying spores, organic dust, fragments of wings, and the warm exhaust of other colonies. It would pass through me.

The swimming part of my life would be over. Settling would have been my last journey; afterward, distance would come to me as chemistry, pressure, and breath.

I would be an old pipe animal. The center of me would be a soft sac secured in its calcified cup, still pumping. The rest—the tubes another creature might mistake for an encrustation or a little mineral organ—would be the hardened shapes of my breathing. One tube would be deep and nearly clear. Another would be partly blocked, its note changing with moisture. A third, crushed long ago, would whistle whenever I used it. These would not be songs to me. They would be resistance, dampness, effort: the particular feel of breathing through the body I had grown.

I would feed by admitting small portions of the crowded country. Spores and dust would stick to my filtering membranes. Very small fliers would stick there too. Larger ones might have to be enclosed in mucus and drawn slowly toward my mouth. Threadlike animals would live inside the passages, some clearing them, some eating the living lining. Around my base, the brood chambers would be packed with young at different stages. There would be no tidy boundary between my body, my food, and the lives sheltering in me.

My neighbors would exist chiefly as pulses. We would narrow our outlets, stop, resume, and fall unevenly into phase. Sometimes a call would return from the far curve of the basin after its maker had changed rhythm. Another colony might answer it. I might answer the answer. Part of my neighborhood would therefore consist of animals that were no longer producing the sounds to which I responded.

When the hush came, it would not feel like peace. One group would stop pumping, then another. The familiar pressure against my tubes would become sparse and wrong. I could close my outlets, but closure would not make me safe; it would only make me quiet. In the exposed interval, the wet movement of my own valves and the shifting of the brood might become the largest things in my world. Somewhere beyond them, shell would be giving way under a hushback’s tooth plates.

Dryness would reduce me to something apparently vacant: hard tubes, closed outlets, a little damp tissue sealed inside. There would be no inward music and no patient vigil. Only waiting without an object.

Water might eventually come from a chorus I had no part in. Elsewhere, calls would coincide, condensation would loosen, and rain would tick across shell and stone. Not enough would reach me every time. When enough did, I would open unevenly—the clear tube first, the damaged one later. Soft tissue would extend. The brood surface would begin to seethe. My first strong filtering breaths might take in dust, spores, and some of my own climbing larvae. There would be no harmony in that. Only another uneven waking, and the possibility that enough of us would remain.

I would not be the Low Choir’s voice, still less its meaning. I would be one small animal giving resistance to the air, feeding from what it carried, and answering pressure with pressure.

You could find me by the flawed note beneath the overhang: a low reed with a whistle in it, often answering a little late. Not because I was keeping time badly, but because some of the calls I answered would already have ended.
