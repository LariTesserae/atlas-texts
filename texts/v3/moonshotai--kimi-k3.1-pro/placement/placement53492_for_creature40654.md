At first I would want to be the water. It can enter a house without using its lane, pass through the fierce light without being warmed, and vanish from a pan without leaving the Cistern. It seems, at first, like freedom from every difficulty the inhabitants have.

But I do not think I would want a life whose excellence lay in never keeping a shape. I would want a body: something that could be cold, hungry, mistaken, and still useful for its warmth.

So I would be an old shag-goat.

Not one standing picturesquely alone against the peaks. The Cistern has little patience for anything that must stand still to be admired. I would be third from the wall in a crowded roof pen above the southern stair, broad enough to inconvenience anyone trying to pass, with splayed hooves, one horn rubbed blunt against limestone, and needles of ice caught along my guard hairs. The pen floor would be another household’s roof. A runnel no wider than two fingers would pass along its outer edge. My breath would join the breath of the other goats and hang over us in a little weather of our own.

The people would know me individually. A child would be able to imitate my stiff, offended walk indoors and make the whole room laugh. I would not be a beloved emblem of the household. I would be that old goat: the one who disliked the northern stair, who could not be hurried past a certain pen gate, who had once stepped into a bitter pool and thereafter treated every similar pool as a personal insult.

My knowledge would be local, bodily, and only sometimes reliable.

I would remember the route beneath a ledge by the sequence of pressures under my hooves. I would know which slab had once turned my back foot, which shelter admitted ceiling-rain, which patch of apparently dry gravel gave underweight in a way I disliked. I would know the pans by taste and resistance. This would be my entire science. Sometimes it would deserve more confidence than a young person’s eagerness. Sometimes it would deserve none at all.

At the shallow pans, I would put my muzzle into water so clear and brilliant that a visitor might think I was drinking. The child watching would know I was eating.

My tongue would work over the submerged stone, taking the charcoal film from it. Behind my mouth, a pale trail would appear. For a few minutes the size of the pasture would become visible: not the bright surface of the pool, but the dark food lying beneath it. Then the film would begin closing over my work. I would move on to the next stone, concerned with the next mouthful, while the child decided whether I had cropped closely enough and whether the pan was changing too quickly to remain safe.

My droppings would be carried to exposed slabs and dried in the cold light, then stored with more care than a visitor would think reasonable. Hair I shed would be gathered for felt; some of it would subsequently be stolen for nests. My body would warm hands and, on the worst nights, rooms. I would be company, but not instead of being livestock. The people would know me and use me. In the Cistern, those would not be contradictions.

During a turning, I would not become noble. I would become difficult.

The other goats might close ranks before I had separately noticed anything. I would feel the pen grow smaller, shoulder pressing shoulder, and then see the slatewings burst from the walls in a flicker of chalk-white underwings. Signs would travel along the roofs. Someone would come for us at a run that was still careful of the stairs.

My shortest route to shelter would lead down an exposed stair. I would refuse it.

A cord would tighten. Somebody would pull. I would lean back and set all four hooves. There would be nothing dignified about it. I would be delaying people who had water to cover, felts to turn, and other animals to move. A young handler would quite reasonably curse me.

Then an older person would come and look—not at me, but at the stone.

Perhaps they would find nothing. That would matter. They would not discover a theatrical crack or a black seam spouting warning. They would simply stand there for a moment with one hand near the step, weighing the cost of my obstinacy against the cost of being wrong. Then they would take us the longer way beneath the ledge.

By the time the turning had passed, the refused stair might be glazed. Or the ledge beyond it might be dripping where a dry route had been expected. No one would call me wise. They would say that I had been right about that stair, which is a much more useful thing to say.

The next week I might refuse a perfectly safe staircase because a slatewing had once opened its wings beside my head there. I would waste everyone’s time and arrive at shelter complaining. They would pull, push, and curse me again, quite justifiably. They would not make a doctrine of me. That would be a mercy.

My usefulness would not depend on always knowing. It would depend partly on the people knowing when my refusal was worth a pause.

Afterward, while they argued about whose roof had sent water into whose bedding, I would have no opinion on roof rights. I would want the pen dry, the herd close, and the next pan brought nearer. Someone would still have to clean ice from the hair along my back. Someone would still have to carry my droppings under cover before the returning moisture reached them. I would remain both a small source of help and a small addition to the work.

At night, when the goggles were off and the lamps had shrunk to their little errands of warmth, a child might come to the pen with fingers gone cold from carrying a jar. They would push one hand through the hair at my side. I would tolerate this for as long as it suited me, then take a step away. The child would follow.

On the coldest nights, two or three of us might be led into an outer room. We would crowd it, smell it, make it less orderly and more generous. Our breathing would join the breathing of the sleepers. Beyond the walls, water would be gathering somewhere in darkness, preparing to become somebody’s morning work. A marten might cross the lane without troubling it. A shrew might draw a stolen fiber through the wall. Someone would reach from bed to test a cistern lid by touch.

If you came looking for me, you would usually find me in the way: broad across a stair, slow at a gate, cropping a pan someone had intended to rest. Now and then, being in the way would spare someone a bad step. More often it would merely cost them time.

I would not be the Cistern’s spirit or its secret intelligence. I would be one old animal within it: an appetite, a store of fuel, a few exact memories, many unreasonable preferences, and a patch of warmth at the height of a child’s hand.

When I shifted away, the child would follow. For that one step, neither of us would need to be wise.
