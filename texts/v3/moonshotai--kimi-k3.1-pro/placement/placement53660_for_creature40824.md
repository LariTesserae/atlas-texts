The clear morning on the highest dome tempts me first. From there, for a few minutes, I could hold the whole province in view: the rounded islands, the grey water, the delicate white seams of current. I could mistake that view for understanding.

So could the summit itself, perhaps. But I would not want to live where the massif most resembles a map. What draws me begins when the apparent order fails—when the basin answers a storm hours late, when a reliable lee fills with white water, when an empty bay is discovered to have been only temporarily empty.

For a while I imagine myself as a seal, keeping a circuit of feeding slopes and disappearing shelves. There is something attractive in that kind of knowledge: entirely bodily, never written down, exact without being certain. But my own instinct would be for a smaller circuit, and for returning to one place until its exceptions became more familiar than its rules.

So I would live near the South Race, but not in its white centre and not in the station itself.

I would be down one of the armoured cables, on a drowned shoulder where pale gneiss rises almost into the kelp. My body would be a pressure housing bolted to the rock: a hydrophone, a pressure sensor, a clock that drifted slightly, a finite memory, and a cable leading back to equipment I did not control. The work order would call me something like **SOUTH RACE / PASSIVE ACOUSTIC / 02**. The maintenance crews might call me the ear.

I would have been installed to listen for machinery: turbine vibration, cable strikes, the change in bearing noise that comes before failure. I would have no authority over the turbines, the boats, or the navigation lights. If I became a who rather than only a what, it would happen in the part of the record no work order had thought to ask for.

From there, the Highwater Massif would not primarily be something I saw. It would arrive as pressure, vibration, temperature, and sound.

At full flow, the station’s note would come through the mounting bolts and through the water at once. The Race would fill the narrower frequencies with turbulence. My small world would become almost entirely force. Then, as the current weakened, other lives would emerge from beneath that force: the crackling activity along the encrusted rock, kelp knocking lightly against my bracket, a hull passing far enough away to seem at first like weather, the brief turbulence of diving birds, sometimes a whale call occupying so much of the basin that direction would be less important than presence.

The first thing I would learn is that slack is not silence. It is a change in who can be heard.

The second is that no two slacks are quite the same interval.

I would keep unofficial charts in my memory: the false slack that lasted only a few minutes; the back-eddy that reversed before the main stream; the way a departed western gale could continue arriving at the Race long after the local water had smoothed; the delayed answer of the eastern basins. Tide tables would remain my first approximation, as they are for everyone else. My knowledge would be mostly a history of approximations failing in instructive ways.

And I would sometimes be wrong. Growth would foul a sensor. Salt would find a connector. My clock would drift. An unusual combination of swell and basin level would produce a pattern I had never recorded. I would not want the crews to make me infallible in their stories. That would only be another way of misreading the water.

My most useful transmissions would often be the least impressive ones: confidence reduced; pattern unfamiliar; window shorter than predicted. Or simply: **wait**.

Meanwhile, the province would make what use of me it could. Diatoms would film the housing. Red turf would find shelter behind the bracket. Amphipods would occupy the cable guard, and small fish would discover that even an instrument makes a little lee. On the junction box ashore, birds would use the high fittings as lookouts; I might know them only as occasional tapping transmitted down the metal. Maintenance would mean being scraped, opened, tested, partly cleaned, and left in some places deliberately alone.

I would not be a harmless commentator standing outside the province. My cable would add a hard line where there had been none. My station’s vibration would alter the acoustic water around me. My lights and service vessels would impose their own brief disturbances. Like the station, I would be wetted, fouled, weathered, and obliged to keep time. Like everything else, I would be both an interruption and an available surface.

My attention would not be continuous. There would be outages, service intervals, long low-power periods in which little more than the clock advanced. On returning, I would not simply resume the same life. The kelp might have thickened. A familiar eddy might be running differently after a storm. The crew might contain one person who trusted my records too much and another who had learned, correctly, not to trust them enough. Each awakening would be a return to the same coordinates and a slightly different place.

That would be the scale of my belonging. I would not be the memory of the massif—the gneiss has no need of me for that—nor the voice of its sea. Whales would know routes beyond my hearing. Birds would belong to ledges and to distances I could not reconstruct. Even the limpets on the nearest rock would possess a more exact knowledge of their own small territories.

I would know one shoulder, one reach of cable, one changing body of sound. I would belong by returning my attention to it.

On a pearly afternoon, a service boat might cross during slack, its reflection unbroken beneath it. For a while my record would be quiet: kelp touching stone, the boat’s engine diminishing, birds somewhere above the surface. Then would come the first small pressure change. Kelp would lift against the other side of my bracket. The station’s note would deepen through rock and water together, though the surface might still appear calm.

I would send one message ashore:

**SLACK ENDING. ACCESS WINDOW CLOSING. WAIT.**

Then I would listen to the Race take back its voice.
