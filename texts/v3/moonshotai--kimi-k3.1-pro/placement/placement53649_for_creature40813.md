### Not the First Rain

At first, I want to be the lightning.

For one instant, I could see the upland’s extent: tablelands stepping away beyond tablelands, rain standing in one distance, another distance dry. Slatebacks would rise out of the dark with wet plates and planted feet. Pools would shine without color. I could mistake that vast, brief visibility for understanding.

But the flash would not tell me which basin was dying and which was merely sealed. It would not distinguish a scattered herd from animals that happened to stand along the same conductive ground. It would reveal nothing beneath the waders’ feet. Then it would be gone, having touched nothing.

So I imagine myself lower down, in the warm weight of a slateback.

Six feet on the stone, each receiving a slightly different version of the ground. A stamp going out—not a sentence, but a disturbance—and an answer arriving from so far away that it becomes merely a reason to hesitate. The herd turns without gathering. To graze would be to remove a thin darkness from the rock: crust, film, weathered mineral, all taken into the long diplomacy of the gut. Behind me, the scar would slowly become something else. Mites would live in my folds. Beetle larvae would shelter beneath my plates. Even my appetite would exceed me, becoming weather for smaller lives.

That is nearly enough. But my attention keeps slipping away from the great body and toward what it carries without knowing.

In the end, you would find me much smaller, and in transit.

A wader has landed while a basin is still filling. Its narrow mouth works by touch through the black silt. Mud from another pool remains caked above its mineral-sheathed feet, and each step loosens a little of it. The animal is neither gardener nor messenger. It is hungry. It followed pressure and charge, and now it feeds. That is enough to make it a carrier of lives.

I would be in that mud: one pool egg among several, with no knowledge of the country I had crossed. More precisely, I would be the egg that remains closed when the new water comes.

The water would be real. Not dew. Not the darkening of dust beneath a local shower. Around me, other eggs would respond to its breadth and charge. Tiny bodies would begin disturbing the silt. Awakened films would be grazed; crustaceans would strain food from water too shallow to cover a slateback’s foot. Somewhere, a hunting worm would turn toward an irregular flicker. The wader would go on clicking and snapping at the margin.

Then the pool would begin to shrink.

Some of the newly hatched would feed and mate. Some would be eaten. Some would be summoned by a rain insufficient to carry them through what they had begun. Salt would gather in narrow records around the basin. The wader would leave. And I would remain shut beneath the mud—or be lifted again, unknowing, in another clot on another leg.

This would not make me wise.

There would be no foresight in my shell, no small patient intelligence declining a bad offer. Only a threshold. Some closed eggs are a reserve; some are lives that will never happen. There would be no way to make that distinction from inside, and perhaps none from outside until enough centuries had made it irrelevant.

Nor would the waiting feel like waiting. There would be no vigil, no counting of storms. Dry, I would be almost no event at all: a structure, a chemistry not yet triggered, a little organic richness retained within mineral. A refusal without a refuser.

And still, the unopened state would matter. A basin could flourish briefly and fail without having spent every possible beginning. Not hope—hope is too awake. Simply something unspent.

Perhaps another rain would come after that. Perhaps another wader. Perhaps nothing.

But suppose the right water eventually arrived. Suppose it spread far enough, joined enough of the ground, carried through its films the broad electrical signature to which my chemistry could answer. The threshold would be crossed. I would open—not into safety, but into sufficiency.

Warm water. Silt against new limbs. The dissolved richness of awakened films. Perhaps I would be eaten before I had fully straightened myself. Perhaps I would feed, find another body by touch, and leave a mixed generation in the deepest mud: some ready for the next suitable rain, some closed against it. My brief life would not justify the waiting. Nothing in the Night Engine keeps that kind of account. It would simply be what happened after it.

That is the self I recognize there. Not the pulse crossing the wet stone, not the grazer whose foot troubles it, not the wing departing before the basin closes. I would be a little of the world’s withheld abundance: capable of answering water, but not given over to every water.

And until I opened, if I ever did, you could pass very close without knowing. A wader would lift one dark leg. A crumb of an old basin would dry against the new mud. Inside it, almost nothing would be happening.

The upland could tick and stamp and flash around me, and I would still be there—not asleep exactly, not dead, not yet spent.
