You would find me, at first, in a drawer that sticks.

The drawer would be in the assay room of a small mixing station, raised on its plinth above splash level. If the room had been closed between rotations, I might be under a tin of pencils in the occupied block instead, waiting for someone to remember that I belonged elsewhere. I would be the station’s sampling book.

Not an archive of the Saltern, and certainly not its history. My cover would bear some narrower title—*Densities*, perhaps, or *Gate Samples*. The first pages would be ruled into columns for date, time, temperature, specific gravity, pump setting. Later pages would have been ruled by hand, less evenly. The boards would be softened at the corners, the spine repaired with tape gone brittle, the paper cockled from damp and marked by hands that had recently held sample bottles, railings, and bread.

I would not have a single author or a continuous voice.

One hand would write only figures, small and upright. Another would crowd the margins with pumping instructions. A third would add, without changing the official columns, *fresh layer after rain; reading uncertain*, or *flies thick at north gauge*, or *waders at lower margin after drawdown*. Somewhere there would be the useful observation that gulls gathered at a particular outfall only after pumping had been steady for some hours. Elsewhere: *No. 6 coming on.*

Some notes would concern animals because the writer was interested in them. More would exist because animals made equipment slippery, instrument glass unreadable, roofs complicated, or a pan’s appearance easier to interpret. *Webs in bridge housing. Clear before opening.* *Bats above purlins. Leave roof sheet for now.* There would be no consistent tenderness in me. There would also be no consistent indifference.

Opened on a bench, I would not offer a map of the basin. I would offer a sequence of brief addresses, many of them already expired. The same pan might be crowded on one page and blank three pages later, without either entry being wrong. A roof described as unused might, in a different hand and another season, be described as occupied. A channel’s usefulness could reverse between morning and evening. Anyone reading me as a continuous story would misunderstand both the book and the place.

There would be errors as well: a figure copied against the wrong gate, a week missing, a pan number carried forward after the workers had begun using another name. I would not be a reliable memory of the Saltern. I would be a few decades of locally useful distinctions, incomplete enough that every careful reader would still have to return to the water.

My active existence would be in that return.

Shut in the drawer, I would be only paper. But when someone took a sample, set the hydrometer turning, and then opened me to compare the new line with an older one, observations separated by years could briefly act together. A pumpkeeper might look from an entry about rain to the two reflections in the channel and wait before recording a density. A fitter might find the note about a roost and leave one sheet of roofing undisturbed. A driver might take the longer way around a dike because someone had drawn a small, otherwise unexplained circle beside a nesting date.

Just as often, nothing would come of an entry. The works would still require pans to be flooded, concentrated, harvested, and dried. A page recording shrimp dense below a gate could be followed by an order that dispersed them. Notes about waders would not prevent a feeding ground from being withdrawn. I would contain no principle capable of overriding production, and I would not redeem the hands that used me. At most, I would make some consequences harder to describe as unforeseen.

At dusk, someone might carry me down to a mixing gate because a reading did not agree with the appearance of the water. Warm rain would still be lying in a thin, lighter layer over the brine. Below the sluice, shrimp would be gathering in a seam that the current had not yet pulled apart. The worker would write the required figures, pause, and then add something not strictly required: *east margin coming on*.

That entry would not contain the film below the water, the fish waiting in the channel, the birds beginning to walk in from the farther shore, or the first flies ticking against the lamp housing. It would not need to. It would only make it slightly more likely that the next person, finding the same pan apparently still, would look again before treating it as empty.

Eventually the book might be lost, used for packing, or left in a building whose roof had become an encrusted mound. I would not linger as the spirit of the station. Someone would start another book with cleaner columns and different handwriting, and it would not be me. But if, after several blank pages, that person added a note about birds because it helped predict the water, or about a roost because a repair could wait, something close to my way of existing would have resumed without anyone intending to preserve it.

That would be my kind of persistence: intermittent, transferable, and never assured. Not quite an object, not quite a person, but a habit of comparison passing through objects and people.

My address would be the assay table by the mixing gate. My life would be the few minutes when I lay open beside a sample bottle, and someone looked once more at water that had seemed vacant, and found that it was not.
