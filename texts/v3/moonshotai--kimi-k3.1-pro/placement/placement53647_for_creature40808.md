### A Clear Place to Begin

The kites are the first temptation: to be all span and no hurry, to hang above the plazas while tower crews argue over the notch in one’s spar. But I would want to come down and read the argument. I would want to know why I had left the region, and whether the instruments had caught up yet. That is no way to be a kite.

Next I imagine the warm wall of a kiln hall, grazed by sievebacks, carrying the flywheel’s stroke into a hundred rooms. That is closer. Still, I would want hands—not to make the wall cleaner, but to feel what was living on it before deciding whether it ought to be disturbed.

So I would be Crownfolk. Not one of the composed elders whose stillness visitors mistake for wisdom. I fidget. When bored, I tap patterns into whatever rail is nearest, which has twice sent an apprentice below to investigate a nonexistent fault in a freight claw. My household has asked me to fidget with my hands.

You would find me in Nine Lamps, a saddle district with a cable junction, two kiln halls, and a grounding bed considerably older than any of its streets. There are fourteen district lamps now. The name survives because changing it would require agreement.

My usual place is in the third gallery, where an old inspection bench has been cut down to a comfortable height and the stone stays warm through the night. I work in thin foot coverings there. My terrace boots hang from a peg, my insulating apron is usually somewhere other than where I left it, and the plates along my back are only beginning to pale at the seams. I am old enough to have changed trades once and young enough that the eldest member of my household still calls me “recent” when I become too certain of something.

By certification, I am a tunnel listener. By household misfortune, I also keep Nine Lamps’ pending file.

At this moment, to your eyes, I would appear to be resting with one hand on a rail and the other around a jar of over-roasted film paste. Through my feet I am following an empty freight cage taking load at the eastern anchor: first the long pull, then the small irregular return as the claw settles. The nearer kiln engine turns beneath it all, three heavy strokes and a pause. Children are running in an upper corridor. They arrive as a brief, local weather with opinions. On the other side of the wall, sievebacks are grazing, their bristles producing a combing so fine that I notice it chiefly when it stops.

Beneath those familiar messages is the one that keeps me employed.

When a heavily loaded cage crosses the western span, the northern foundation returns some part of the strain a fraction later than it should. No fracture has been found. The delay has not increased in living memory, which is not the same thing as saying that it is harmless. Three surveys attribute it to three different features of the old rock. A fourth suggests that the listeners have been compensating for differences in their own bodies.

I have not entirely dismissed the fourth. My left rear joint has been stiff for eleven years.

Most of my work consists of refusing to improve a message. A vibration does not become more useful because I have given it a dramatic cause. An old uncertainty does not become a danger merely because everyone is tired of seeing it in the file. Nor does it become safe because six sensible people have successively written *no change* in the margin.

When I do not know, I write that I do not know, plainly enough to survive my own embarrassment.

The pending file is wider than the tunnels. It contains repairs, surveys, nesting protections, private promises, obsolete routes, questions left by the dead, and several disputes whose original subject has been lost while the hostility remains perfectly preserved. It is less a record than an argument conducted between the dead and the not-yet-born, with the living taking minutes.

My teacher kept it before me. They asked that nothing of their body be retained, but left six hundred and twelve open notes and one instruction in the household record:

*Do not let reverence do your looking for you.*

I disliked that sentence for years. It sounded like an accusation pretending to be permission.

At first I tried to continue everything. I kept watch on structures that no longer existed. I deferred decisions because a predecessor might have had reasons not visible in their notes. I became extremely diligent and only moderately useful. Eventually I ended a scheduled examination of a counterweight shaft that had been filled and fused eighty years earlier. I wrote what had changed, what evidence I had checked, and what discovery would justify opening the obligation again.

Nothing in the mountain thanked me. The next listener simply had one less instruction to mistake for a fact.

Since then I have accepted tasks, deferred tasks, and ended them. Ending takes the longest. A closed note must leave enough of itself behind that closure does not become another form of concealment.

There are pleasanter duties. My household’s outer chamber contains the first grounded stud reached by travelers coming off the cable platform. Often I am the one who meets them. Before food, before news, before anyone asks why they have come, I offer the stud and wait while the tension leaves their plates.

There is usually a small change in the body then. Not quite relaxation; more the discovery that some part of oneself has been bracing for longer than necessary.

I like being the person who says, *Here. You can put it down.*

After the last equalization, the eldest in my household met me at the gallery door during the open-handed hours. Instead of offering the stud, they laid a bare hand on my forearm.

“Try not to make this significant,” they said.

So I have made it significant only in private.

I remember my other hand on the terrace rail, with no warning tightness along my back. The rail still carried the cage, the kiln engine, the children, and the faint combing of sievebacks. The city had not become simpler. Touch had.

Tonight, before going upstairs, I add one entry to the northern foundation’s note. The delay is unchanged. The newest survey has not explained it. The old-boring hypothesis remains a hypothesis. The next full examination is due in thirty-one years, sooner if the return changes under ordinary load. I attach my name, the condition of my joint, and the two measurements I trust least.

Then I close the file.

On the terrace, I touch the stud before greeting the others. We are not in an open-handed hour, and good manners are not cowardice. My household has left a narrow place for me along the rail. I settle into it, four feet against the warm stone, and accept a small dish of something the film-tender describes as ready and everyone else describes as educational.

The western freight cage is still crossing. Below us, a conveyor advances by the length of one casting. Someone in the lower workshop drops a tray and then becomes conspicuously industrious. Farther down, beyond easy attention, the northern foundation makes its small, delayed reply.

I do not go back downstairs.

A sieveback grazes within reach of my outer foot. Above the summit, one kite turns so gradually that its shadow seems less to cross the terrace than to consider it. District lights are appearing across the dark gaps, each settlement separated from the next by more rock than I will ever inspect.

My place in all this is not to keep it safe forever. I could not do that even if I were wiser. It is to leave a grounded stud within reach, a clear uncertainty in the file, and enough of the next beginning visible that someone else need not start by guessing what I meant.

The kite’s shadow reaches us. No one can say precisely when the terrace becomes cooler.

I stay a little longer.
