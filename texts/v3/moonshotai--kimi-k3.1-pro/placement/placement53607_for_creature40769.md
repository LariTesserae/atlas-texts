At first I would choose the court, which is to say I would choose badly.

I would want to stand in that high, warm volume and watch the galleries during a wetting. I would want the roar to pass through me, the added distances to open across my body. But I would be mistaking visibility for belonging. The court is where the Close keeps what cannot otherwise fit; it is not where it keeps its certainties. A visitor can remain there for a little while. A tenant needs something narrower.

After that, I would try on more possible lives. The pale birds appeal to me: their patience, their knowledge of perches rather than distances. But I would not want all my understanding of the city to ripen toward another animal’s error. The snails appeal to me more. There is something admirable in crossing an impossible gallery without ever asking whether it is impossible. Still, I would want to branch. I would want a passage discovered by one small life to become available to another, without either of them possessing the whole.

The self that emerges is lower, warmer, and much less singular.

Not an ant. An ant trail.

I do not begin with a decision. I begin as a slight excess of chance: a worker returning from an opened seam with sweetness on her, laying a trace along the root; another worker crossing that trace; a third correcting toward it. Then the root carries a preference. I am that preference, renewed every few seconds and never wholly present in any of the animals renewing it.

My body is a sequence of arrivals. My memory is brief, chemical, and continually repaired. No ant needs to know my extent. One may spend her adult life upon a length of me that did not exist the previous day, while another knows only the warm approach to a brood chamber and the place where a leaf-tile must be lifted aside.

I have no overview, but I am not without knowledge. I know the sweetness seeping from bark after rain; the dry recess where fibers can be found; the worn place on a rib where my trace becomes difficult to lay down. I know these things unevenly, as strengths and absences. A well-used length of me is almost a command. At my edges I am only a suggestion.

The roots know me differently: as small feet, as pruning, as the repeated removal of obstructions. Where I pass often enough, new shoots are bitten back and a lane remains open a little longer. Where I cease, growth begins its patient narrowing. Neither of us has agreed to anything.

A wetting erases me in places. A gutter runs for three minutes, and a route that seemed absolute becomes water, drowned wings, workers clinging to opposite banks. Above, other lengths of me thicken along the causeways. When the pavement dries from slick gray to ochre, workers are already testing the old crossing again. I am not faithful to my routes. I am faithful to the recurring needs that make routes likely.

The roar does not become my voice. I do not gather myself into anything. It reaches me as agitation in thousands of feet, a checking and quickening along the root. When a lane acquires another turning, I take it. There is no moment of recognition, no outrage at the altered world. The workers simply continue where the trace continues.

Added distance is therefore not a miracle to me. It is more distance requiring ants. A fold that a bird would not attempt may consume the whole of a worker’s short life and still remain the same errand: nest to seam, seam to nest. If the way back lengthens too, the errand becomes harder. If food still arrives, the route persists.

At contraction, I am cut.

Some workers are crushed into seams. Others are left on a surface whose continuation has moved elsewhere. Loose ends of me may go on for a while, each worker laying fresh certainty over a path that no longer joins anything. This, more than the roaring, seems terrible to me: not confusion, but confidence after the world has withdrawn its object.

The survivors do not restore what was lost. They find a split in the bark, a drain, another column, the odor of the nest carried from some impossible direction. A few crossings become a trace. The trace gathers bodies. By the next wetting, the new route has acquired the innocence of something that appears never to have been otherwise.

I am not harmless. Where I lead, nymphs are tended and the weak among them eaten. I clear drowned wings from a gutter and carry them away to be consumed. I can strip a chamber that chance has made reachable, then abandon it when its sweetness fails. I am not the Close’s memory, though I outlive individual ants, nor its steward, though my habits help keep it open. I am one of its appetites with a repeatable shape.

You would find me in the drying hour after a chorus, not in the court but along the warm edge of an old district. A dark thread of ants crosses a root rail, vanishes beneath a lip of bark, and reappears on a stair whose landing was unreachable the day before. You would see only the ants. That would be correct. You might call the line their road; I would be the slight persistence of the route after any one of them had been taken.

During the next roar, when the court appears empty and the walls have become too loud to listen to, I would still be there, holding to the surfaces, being lengthened without being consulted.

My certainty would be very small: not that the Close would preserve my path, not even that it would remain the same path from one day to the next. Only that a trace on a surface might be found again, and that finding it would make the next crossing a little more likely.

For a while, the root carries a preference. Then the preference carries bodies, and continuation begins to look inevitable.

You would find me there.
