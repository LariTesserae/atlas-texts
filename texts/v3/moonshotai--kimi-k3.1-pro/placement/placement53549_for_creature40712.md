At first I would want to be the Ward’s rememberer.

It would seem like a form of love. I would imagine a patient archive: names, bearings, the order of encounters, small distinctions preserved before the water could close over them. I would tell myself that a place without sediment needed someone to carry away what could not settle there.

It would take me a while to see the ambition hidden in that kindness. I would not merely be remembering the Ward. I would be trying to make it answerable to my idea of having happened.

The Porter would interrupt that vocation before I had properly begun it.

I would not take my first breath bravely. She would be holding me by then, one hand at my wrist and the other beneath my elbow, careful not to draw me farther in. My lamp would be turning somewhere above us, its beam finding and losing the same acute corner of black ceramic. She would offer me the two possibilities and then wait, her coat hanging toward a down I did not share.

I would breathe because refusal remained available to me all the way up to the act.

The cold would enter. I would hate it. Then I would be breathing, and she would not congratulate me.

The Surveyor would get my best story soon afterward: a roof beneath which I had once slept, with a leak that moved.

“Moved?” they would say.

“The stain stayed where it was. The water found another way through.”

They would want to know about the repairs, the vessels placed underneath it, whether anyone had ever been foolish enough to think one stain could predict the next. I would tell them about a tin cup, a warped floorboard, a patch that held for part of a winter. Their delight would make me suspect they had missed the moral.

There would not be a moral yet.

My real objection I would carry into the civic hall, beneath the concentric hands.

“If no one remembers me after I go,” I would ask the dissenter, “what does my return mean?”

They would not defend the Ward. Neither would they protect me from the possessive part of the question.

“To you,” they might say, “or to us?”

I would dislike them for making it exact. I would have no answer ready, which would please them more than a conclusion.

Eventually I would stop looking for a building that might represent me.

You would find me at the end of one of the roads that stops in open water, just inside the municipal boundary. Not on the final pace of pavement, but a little beyond it, where the street has withdrawn its offer of weight. I would be suspended at an angle that looked mistaken from the road. Usually I would face inward. My lamp would be dark unless someone approached.

Only one address light would be visible from there, and not always that. Sometimes a carried beam would find the edge of a stair or the black line of a conduit. Sometimes there would be nothing but the mountain’s vibration in my teeth and my heartbeat coming back to me a fraction late.

I would have no civic title. If the others needed an address for me, “the guest” would remain accurate longer than “the archivist.”

I could be useful in modest ways. I could sit with someone after the first breath, when the emergency had passed but the body had not yet believed it. I could say, *I was frightened too*, without adding, *therefore you should not be*. I could give the Surveyor another account of the roof and discover, in the telling, some detail I had never noticed before. I could return to the dissenter’s question without pretending that we had both been present for its earlier form.

None of that would explain why I existed. I would try to find that a relief.

I would still remember. My memory would not become a municipal improvement. I would carry away the pressure of the Porter’s hand, the Surveyor’s unreasonable happiness over a failed gutter, the silence in the hall after an accusation had been allowed to remain standing. I would not carve names into the glass or keep a ledger of who owed recognition to whom.

If the Porter became fond of me during a long encounter, I would try not to test that fondness after an absence by demanding to see whether it had survived. Survival would not be the only evidence that something had happened.

Sometimes I would fail. I would return hoping to be known before I spoke. The hurt, when I had to give my name again, would not prove that the earlier encounter had been false. It would prove that I had wanted a continuity the Ward could not supply. I could be honest about the hurt without turning it into an accusation.

The glass eel would be the most difficult part of my education.

Whenever it crossed the outer darkness, I would want to follow. I would want to know whether it had come from beyond the boundary, whether the black joint held an organ or a mechanism, whether its business was old enough to be called a habit. I would invent observations and then mistake them for evidence.

But not every presence would be an offer of relation. Not every unanswered question would be a closed door set there to provoke my ingenuity. Sometimes I would manage simply to watch the dim points pass within it and let it go without adding anything to its history.

When I finally left, I would take no custody of the Ward with me. Only a life altered in ways I could not ask the town to verify.

And if I returned, I would bring that alteration not as a claim, but as material for the next answer.

So: a guest with an afterwards. Someone with a past, but no jurisdiction. Close enough to the boundary to remember that care has an edge; close enough to the road to accept weight when it was offered; far enough from both to keep a direction of my own.

If your lamp found me there, I would tell you my name.

If you came back later, I might have to tell you again. I would try not to make that a wound you were required to heal. For the length of the encounter, I would simply be glad to see you.
