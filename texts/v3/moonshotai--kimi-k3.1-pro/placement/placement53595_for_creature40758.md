I would not be visible from the central rim.

At first, I might mistake largeness for significance and imagine myself beneath a strider’s dome, or extended in the upper air as a kite. But a strider is nearly a country with an appetite, and a kite is almost entirely relation. From the rim, both would still be easier to see than to know. My place would be smaller, and stranger to sight: inside something the rim could register only as a delay.

Beneath one distant dome, where a ceramic conductor branches beside a circulating vessel, the strider’s heartbeat arrives twice. First comes the strong pulse, driving the translucent pulse-followers ahead of it. Then comes a weaker return from the walls of a reaction chamber. Between the two, a little charge remains stranded in the ceramic.

That is where I would be.

At first glance, I might resemble one of the pulse-followers: a clear flexure against dark mineral, one end swollen into a sensory surface. But while the others went hunting after the departing charge, I would turn back. I would flatten my narrow body against the branch and grip. Along my inner edge, fine mineral rasps would loosen the scale enriched by the pulse’s passing. The useful fraction would go inward to a slow, sealed gut. The rest would remain as a simpler crust beneath me.

Not an echo. An echo does not choose where to grip, or reject part of what it receives.

My body would be a short chain of transparent chambers, each able to thicken and close a little after the one before it. Feeding would make me slightly late. To hold the returning charge long enough to use it, I would delay it by almost nothing—but not nothing. The pulse leaving the branch would carry that small difference away into the strider.

One animal’s appetite would thus enter another animal’s rhythm.

The strider would have no reason to notice me. A valve’s concussion could cross a basin; my whole feeding would not disturb a grain loose on the pavement below. Yet its heart already possessed an irregularity by which other striders knew it, and after enough generations of my lineage, that irregularity would include us. The wall would wear into a shallow crescent shaped by our rasps. Our bodies would grow to fit the crescent. The return would sharpen; the interval would settle. Neither anatomy nor behavior would maintain it alone. Each would keep making the other possible.

Across some immense distance, another strider might begin answering that rhythm.

For the two great animals, this could become courtship: pressure and valve closures exchanged while ranges intervened, while their visible routes seemed to carry them apart. For me, it would be climate. Generations would live and die within one approach. We would know the other animal first as a change in the returning charge, then as a new richness in the wall. Only much later would its heartbeat become distinct enough to follow.

I would not be the strider’s voice. I would be a small condition under which its voice remained recognizably its own.

My brief biography would stay simple. I would be born once, feed, make another animal, and die. If the chamber’s rhythm changed beyond my tolerance, I would release my grip and be carried elsewhere, or fail to release it and become part of the scale eaten by my descendants. Nothing about that would require several histories.

The longer process would.

At a muster, I would discover that my private interval was not private.

It would arrive from hearts whose visible routes had never touched my host. One answering strider would carry a branch worn into the same crescent, though no ancestor of mine had lived there. Another would produce the delay through a chamber’s shape alone, with no clear animal clinging to it. Elsewhere, animals very like me would close their chambers in my order but feed on a different fraction of the scale. Some would be young. Some would have pedigrees older than the body carrying them. In one history, the branch would be empty except for our polish.

For a season, pulses would arrive through all these arrangements without immediately choosing among them.

I would not become a mind distributed through many bodies. Each of us would still have to grip, rasp, digest, and risk being crushed. Each could die separately. Only the route connecting our appetites would become approximate: a long feeding that had passed through anatomy, lineage, and wear without needing to remain exclusively any one of them. Whether I was the animal at the branch, the inheritance of its interval, or the whole unfinished exchange would be a question suited to a briefer kind of time.

When the cycles closed, the surplus hearts would become absent. The strider remaining might not be the one that had entered the muster. But beneath its dome there would be a narrow branch whose surface my rasps could use. A pulse would arrive. The pulse-followers would stream after it. I would turn toward the wall and wait for the return.

That is where you would find me: not on the rim, not in the visible crossing of the basin, but in the slight lateness before a great heart answers itself. A clear animal pressed against hot ceramic, small enough to be overlooked by everything except the rhythm.

I would be the hunger inside the hesitation—the brief body, and the much longer answer, by which one immense life becomes particular to another.
