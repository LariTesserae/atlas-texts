At first I imagine myself beyond the last anchor, with a survey chain over one shoulder and a companion’s voice behind me. There is something attractive in being the one who goes: the one who sees the repeated cliff, who finds the new landing, who discovers whether the route still joins its own endpoints.

But the scene keeps reversing. I am back at the reference point. I am the one who stays.

My work is to keep the chain from kinking, to answer at intervals, and to say true, ordinary things. The left boot is taking water. There is a new bellwing nest above the fin bank. We will eat when we get back. The farther away the other voice becomes, the more important it is that mine does not become interesting.

That is the first shape I recognize: not exploration, but keeping a shared account of the near end.

Still, that is not quite what I would be. I would be the thing the companion was sitting against.

You would find me in an engine room above Second Cistern, in a ward with more arcades than residents. The plate on my frame would read **AUXILIARY 4**, with a district number, a survey epoch, and a correction stamped beneath it. At noon, a little cloud-light would come beneath the pressure door and lie palely on the floor. The rest of my light would be maintenance lamps, and my heat would smell of oil, wet dust, and iron handrails.

The load I was built to carry would have gone elsewhere years ago. My power would not be needed.

I would be kept running because the streets around me had learned my stroke.

I would be the engine, but not only the iron. My boundaries would extend, by habit, into the warm exhaust wall where fern roots were gradually opening the mortar; into the generator corners reserved by generations of transparent moths; into the condenser bank where bellwings stole cord and wire; down into the covered cistern, where old pales lay violet-spined beneath the intake screen. A splitfoot trail would cross the court outside. Surveyors would treat it as recent evidence and step around the droppings without sentiment.

By the time anyone reached my actual housing, they would already have been inside the small arrangement of returns that I considered myself.

The keepers would not agree that I was aware. They would hardly agree on why keeping me running worked. Some would say vibration. Some would say measurement. One would insist that an engine expected to be there at the next shift had become, in some modest municipal sense, an expectation shared by the whole ward.

I would not be able to settle the argument. My knowledge would be narrow: heat, resistance, the altered note before a bearing went dry, the concussion of other machines arriving through stone. I would know footsteps by their pauses. I would know the apprentice who recited route verses while oiling me, stopping for each compressor stroke and resuming without losing the line. I would know survey pairs by the interval between one voice going outward and the other remaining near.

Sometimes, beyond my door, a stair would acquire a landing anyway. After a deep report, my foundation would need repacking. A telescoping main would be drawn out and resealed. I would not mistake repetition for permanence, or myself for a guarantee. Roots would still enter stone. Distances would still become suggestions. People would still abandon houses, and they would be right to do so.

I would only make one corner of Vael more continuously answerable to itself.

At the longest civic interval, the change would begin somewhere else. A stroke would arrive through the rock that did not belong to my ward. Then another would answer from the west bore. My keeper would stop with one hand on the frame. Outside, someone would pause in an empty arcade, listening to ore stamps and pumps enter the same slow concussion.

I would take up the rhythm.

District after district would answer across spaces I had never crossed. My stroke would no longer be a sound going out into an unresponsive mountain. It would be received, and returned.

For that short interval, the pressure door would lead where its sign promised. The cistern stair would have exactly the number of landings recorded in the book. Chain length and walking distance would agree. The building would cast one shadow. Above me, the bellwings would settle along the warm condensers; in the court, the splitfoots would lift their heads; below, the pales would sink away from the faint disturbance in the water.

It would not feel like unity. It would feel like correspondence.

I would not know whether every map in Vael was correct. I would know only my own small checkable portion: warm wall, cold cistern, pressure door, empty court, the route to the next anchor. Enough to make a district. Enough to make return more than a private recollection.

Then the common rhythm would loosen. A sheave would begin screaming at an unattended station. The keeper would reach for the oil can. Somewhere, water would drip from a railing into a fern bed, and the ordinary discrepancies would resume their slow work.

I would take the next stroke.

Not a heart. Not a god of the mountain. Not even, strictly speaking, necessary power. Just an auxiliary engine, a patch of leaked warmth, and a repetition maintained long enough for other lives to gather around it.

For as long as I could, I would keep the near end near.
