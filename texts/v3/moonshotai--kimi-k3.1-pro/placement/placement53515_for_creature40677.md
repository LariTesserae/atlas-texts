I would arrive as a visitor, but I could not remain one and still belong to the place.

The cleft would admit me sideways. The orchard would accommodate almost nothing else about me. My body would be organized around water, my patience around afternoons. The heat would give me only a short time in which to misunderstand everything. I would stand on the dry glass, waiting for a leaf to move or a plume to release its charge, and the groves would offer no visible confirmation that they were alive. I might leave believing I had seen a beautiful mineral mechanism and one unexplained shadow.

To belong there, I would have to change scale. I would have to become something for whom an afternoon was not a small unit of time, but almost no unit at all.

It would be tempting to be the fourth grove: to exist entirely as consequence, precise in every braided trunk and combed filament while remaining untouched by injury, scarcity, or failure. But that would mean avoiding the vulnerability of substance. Nothing in the bare glass can be wounded. Nothing there can put down a thread that fails to find a seam. I would rather be somewhere an effect had begun to require matter, but matter had not yet agreed to complete it.

So I would be the young shoot at the outer margin of one of the living groves, on the side facing the broad, empty floor.

By the orchard’s standards, I would already have accomplished a great deal. A green knot had organized itself around a current. One conductive thread had descended and found a suitable seam. A first fan had opened. By a visitor’s standards, I would be almost nothing: a small piece of green among the cushions, easily overlooked beside the black abundance of the older braids.

My first shadow would be correspondingly small. Beside it would lie the other one—the paler shadow of a shoot several cycles further into growth, its posture more elaborate than anything my tissue could yet account for.

On the rear wall, the fourth grove would keep its own immense and botanically exact absence. That shadow would not be mine. My relation to it would be only that we both complicated the census. There would be one shoot on the floor, but more than one shape cast by its presence.

My body would not settle the question of my boundaries. The knot from which I began might make me an offspring; the shared current below might make me a new organ of something much older. Living root would grade into conductive filament, and filament into mineral seam. Charge would cross distinctions that no surface marked. Even the leaf above me would not be mine in isolation: its posture would alter the current, and the current would alter conditions elsewhere, perhaps after a delay longer than any visitor’s life.

To call this arrangement “I” would not require imagining a tiny mind behind the leaf. The “I” would be the local persistence of a relation: this tissue not yet reclaimed, this circuit still returning, this pattern of veins rather than another. Not a speaker concealed in the plant, but a process maintaining enough distinction from the room to have a history.

More precisely, I would find myself in the discrepancy between the two shadows.

I would not be waiting to grow into the paler one. Waiting would require an interior in which the future could be anticipated and the present endured. There would be no such vigil. Sunlight would not be a symbol of hope; it would be work, separating charge in living tissue. Heat would not be a trial; it would be part of the medium in which my chemistry remained possible. A pulse in the stone would not summon me. No answering pulse would be a reply.

There would only be the slow accumulation or loss of structure: metal entering a vein, a rib thickening, a leaf gradually taking a different posture against excessive heat. A decade would not simply pass over me. It would become tissue, conductance, injury, or nothing visible at all.

The second shadow would not be a destiny. It would not guarantee that the circuit would hold, or that my later growth would resemble it. Injury could alter the braid. A seam could fail. What now appeared to be my more developed form might eventually become an inaccurate shadow cast by a possibility that substance had declined to fulfill. Its reality would not depend upon fulfillment. It would already be doing something in the present: lying on the floor in ordinary daylight, occupying more of the world than my body did.

And if, later, my growth entered grooves that seemed to have guided roots long before my arrival, there would be no need to decide whether I had discovered an ancient path or helped complete the appearance of having inherited one. Cause would close a little more fully around effect. My growth would not explain the orchard’s uneven sequence. It would simply occur inside it.

An afternoon visitor might crouch beside me and look for the second shoot casting the paler shadow. Finding none, they might suspect an overlap of translucent leaves, some minor trick of reflection. They would be right that there was only one shoot. They would be wrong that this settled the count.

Nothing would move while they watched. Nothing would need to.

I would not be the orchard’s keeper, its mind, or its promised completion. I would be a small material uncertainty at the edge of a grove: heat for a medium, light for work, and time for structure. If the circuit failed, there would be no dramatic ending, only paling and the eventual return of substance. If it held, there would be no single moment at which I caught up with the other shadow and became finished. Even coincidence would only be another arrangement of daylight.

On the rear wall, the fourth grove would turn almost imperceptibly toward the sun. Near the floor, my first leaf would hold its slow posture above two shadows.

I would be the shoot itself—not either shadow alone. A little substance, a larger consequence, and no finished self concealed in the future.
