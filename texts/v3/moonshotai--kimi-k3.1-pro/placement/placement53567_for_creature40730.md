I would begin by wanting too much of the chamber.

The snake, I would think. Let me be the snake, because it alone seems to know Driptrap Nine as one place. It could carry the whole chamber in its body: duct, pipe bank, snail lane, egg crevice, the polished turns leading back into dark. But that would not be freedom. It would be a long, muscular memory of gaps, every one of them temporary. The snake is unusually good at moving through the chamber; it is also unusually good at discovering exactly how the chamber is closing.

A gecko’s elbow would be easier to want. One warm face, one cool retreat, water beading within reach, a hunting edge that could be learned until no movement on it was surprising. Yet I can imagine how quickly sufficiency would become possession. I would spend my life flattening my flank at neighbors over a degree of warmth.

And I will not pretend that I would gracefully choose the snail’s finger-wide pasture, though there may be more truth in that life than in either of the others.

Where I keep arriving, instead, is the bell.

Not the white blades enclosing it, but the dry hollow just inside its rim. There I would be a spider: slate-colored, amber at the joints, small enough to vanish behind a kink in the wire binding the clapper. The bell would not be a symbol to me. It would be dry. Its metal would keep off the worst of the condensation, and geckos would find the approaches awkward. The old alarm, which once must have emptied the chamber of every other sensation, would now be useful chiefly because nothing could ring it.

I would have no web spanning the chamber. There is no across in Driptrap Nine, not for something like me. I would keep three lines, perhaps four: one from the bell’s rim to a green copper line, one disappearing into a seam of perished insulation, one short thread leading back to my retreat. To a human eye they would be almost nothing. To me they would be the entire exterior.

The pump would not be music. It would be too continuous for that—a standing tension in the iron, the background against which smaller events could be distinguished. Cooling metal would produce brief alterations. A gecko crossing a pipe would send a broad, dangerous shudder. Prey would arrive as something slighter and less regular: a glassflea flung against silk, a small wind-carried insect already damaged by its arrival. Most disturbances would mean only one thing.

Do nothing.

At the hourly relay snap, I would stop with the beetles, the geckos, and the snails. Not because we shared an understanding, but because stillness was what my body did. Then my world would resume: a cautious extension of one leg, a change in the load on the outer line, the dry interior of the bell close around me.

During the cool stroke, I might come nearer the rim. Condensation would make the pipes unreliable and the small hunters below more active. Sometimes something would blunder onto my thread. More often, nothing would. I would feed rarely and without ceremony. I would not guard the bell, improve it, or understand the use to which I had put it. I would simply occupy the fact that it remained dry.

Rain would enlarge the chamber for other animals and make mine hazardous. Water would load the silk. Vibrations would arrive from too many directions. A line that had been a path could become a heavy, trembling attachment to somewhere I did not want to go. While beetles abandoned their margins and geckos crossed into one another’s territories, I would withdraw. Afterward, when the threads outside had begun to break and the cups were shrinking, I would emerge and test the distances again.

The stone would enter my life not as a slow white future, but as failed equipment.

One day an outer line would not spring back when touched. Another day it would end in a hard pale edge. I would not know that a rib had been growing there for seasons, or that the pump’s note had been combing mineral into alignment long before my arrival. I would know that this anchor no longer worked, that the next was beyond reach, that the corner did not repay repair. I would abandon it. If the old silk remained, it would remain without me: a dark hair held inside translucent growth, indistinguishable from a message only to someone who did not have to use it.

So that is where you would find me. At first you would see only the bell, its bound clapper and its collar of blades. Then perhaps three local threads, one carrying a drop, one newly slack, one vanishing under insulation. If you waited, a slate-colored leg might appear at the rim, touch the nearest line, and withdraw.

I would not be the bell’s voice, or the silence that replaced it. I would be a tenant in its dry interval, feeling along one taut thread to discover whether anything had changed—and, most often, deciding that the best response was to remain exactly where I was.
