At first I imagine myself as a bell-newt, with a bronze throat and a portion of the cirque held together by returning notes. Then as a kite-wing, making that low, doubtful pass before landing, unwilling to trust distances merely because they were true yesterday. Both lives attract me. But the image that remains is lower to the ground: a flat body at the foot of a wall, one foreleg extended toward an opening that is no longer there.

You would find me on the eastern side of the cirque, just after rain.

I would be an old sieve-crab, broad as two fingers and thin enough to seem to vanish when I turned. Water would slide from my shell in quick, bright beads. Beneath its rim I would carry my garden: narrow crescents of copper-green film, dark and inconspicuous when the rain thinned, vivid again as soon as warm water reached them. Not enough to make a pasture. Enough to begin one.

Before the last growth pulse, my route ran from an old violet-skinned pool to a seep beneath a cushion of ash-colored lichen. It was not a straight route. It curved around a place where the floor became too hot, crossed a seam by its narrowest part, and ended in a fracture where I could wait out the midge columns and the kite-wings’ descent.

Now there is a wall across it.

I would not remember the route as a picture. My memory would be a sequence of pressures and changes: the slight give of film beneath a gripping pad, the warmth increasing through three paces of stone, the brittle edge where it was necessary to turn sideways, the sudden mineral abundance at the seep. My body would expect those things in that order.

So I would still stop at the wall and test it with my forelegs.

Then I would make the detour.

Sometimes, after rain, I would make the same detour twice. Younger crabs would pass along more direct lines, their trails clean and economical. Mine would have a crookedness in it, an unnecessary return near the base of the eastern rim. It would be easy to call that an error.

It would be an error. But not only an error.

When the rain slackened, I would leave the wall and move sideways over the newest seams, shaving the copper-green growth with my mouth-combs. Most of what I took would be food. Some would remain beneath my shell. Some would be carried unknowingly on my legs and pressed into wet stone farther along. Behind me, the rock would be briefly bright and bare; later, in places, green would appear along the line I had made.

I would not know whether the machines noticed. I would not need them to. Growth often followed the grazers’ trails, but I would not imagine myself giving instructions to the cirque. My work would be smaller than that: to eat without taking everything, to carry without knowing exactly what I carried, and to leave the next surface slightly less empty than I found it.

A surveyor might hope the ground would eventually hold still long enough to agree with the map. I would have no such hope. Still, I would go on testing the seams.

When the next pulse approached, I would feel it before I understood it as sound. The faint vibration near the forked pylon would change beneath my pads. The newts would climb onto higher ledges, their hard notes arriving through both air and stone, each return slightly wrong. I would abandon the grazing ground, even if the film there was still thick, and press myself into a fracture barely wider than my shell.

Then the low tone would enter the combs of my mouth and the joints of my legs. Droplets would lift from the floor. The fracture would fill with warm vibration. I would draw my legs close around the garden beneath my shell and wait.

The cracks, when they came, would not feel like construction. They would feel like the world losing its temper.

Afterward there would be rain again, and the long groan of stone settling into shapes it had not possessed a moment before. Eventually I would put out one foreleg.

The eastern wall would still be there.

I would touch the place where the opening used to be, and there would be no answer from my old route. The cirque would not reward my memory by making it true.

But behind me, on stone that had not existed when I entered the fracture, a shallow pool would be shining. A bell-newt would already be calling from its edge. The note would return from the rim, and then, a little late, from somewhere beyond the open side of the cirque.

My familiar detour would meet a fresh seam near the pool.

I would take it—not because it led home, and not because I had forgiven it for becoming obsolete, but because it was the part of the changed floor my body still knew how to begin from. At the seam I would turn, test the new mineral, and put down my weight. Then another pace. Then the first shaving passage of my combs.

A path can be wrong about the ground and still be right about where to begin.

If you came back later, you might not see me. You might see only a crooked, clean trail leaving the base of the wall, with the faintest copper-green disturbance along one edge. I might be at its far end. I might be hidden beneath a ledge while the kite-wings hunted. Another pulse might already have sealed the whole thing under new stone.

But for a while, something would be feeding there.

The newts would receive their answers almost at once: a wall, a doubled cliff, an impossible distance beyond the clouds. Mine would come more slowly, and perhaps not to me. It might feed another crab, or be carried away on wet feet before I returned.

Still, I think I would recognize it as an answer.

My home would not be the route I remembered. It would be the small, viable beginning beneath my shell, and the knowledge that even the newest stone need not remain bare.

The newts would listen for an echo.

I would wait for the stone to turn green.
