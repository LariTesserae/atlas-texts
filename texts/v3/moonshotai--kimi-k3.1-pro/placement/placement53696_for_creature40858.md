At first, I would want to be at the shadow-bed, with the young keeper and the pale shoot. But I think I would want too much to know what was arriving. I would mistake attention for help. I would return before anything had time to change, and one day I would catch myself wondering how wide the trunk ought to be.

That would be the beginning of bad work.

So you would find me elsewhere: in a small working court let into the thickness of the wall, where three tiles hold the day’s warmth and the roof is made of braided roots grown through an older trellis. There would be ruined cord sorted on wooden pins, a blunt copper awl, frond-mats stiff with age, and very little else. My sleeping niche would not be there. Neither would my eating court. When the joins were sound, the three places would be nearly consecutive. When they were not, I would complain about having to cross half the garden to reach my own bed.

The approach to my court would be warm tile, moss, the bitter scent of a crushed fern edge, then left at a copper pin.

The way back would be different. I would know the way back better.

I would be an unpicker of routes.

Keepers would send for me when a path needed to be persuaded to end: when a doorway had begun admitting the same room twice, when an old approach passed inconveniently through someone’s sleeping alcove, when a household’s cord had become so heavily amended that no two people could walk it without arguing. I would not arrive with a knife. The bone knife at my waist would be the least used of my tools.

A route could not simply be cut away. First it would have to become less necessary. Then less direct. Then, perhaps, uncertain. A peg moved. A knot amended. A turn allowed to narrow beneath fern growth. The cord walked again after a season, and then after another. If beetles began arriving out of order, I would stop and follow them on my hands and knees. If a moth emerged powdered with orange pollen where no orange flowers should remain near, I would put back the peg I had just removed and accept that the garden was not yet willing to forget.

My successes would be almost impossible to display. A meal court would stop opening into a root chamber. A child would reach dinner without taking the long way through the canopy. A fern bed would simply be beside the eastern stair again, as though nothing had ever been otherwise. Someone would complain that the work had taken nine years. Often they would be right to complain.

I would not be serene. I would dislike careless lashings, beautiful work concealing weak fiber, and, on occasion, people whose knots were plainly better than mine. Patience would be something I did with my hands, not something I felt.

Children would be sent to me with ruined cord. They would want to know what it had held, and I would tell them to begin by finding out whether it still did. If they pulled hard on a knot, I would take the cord away.

“Don’t help it become more certain,” I would say. “We have enough of those.”

After a death, I would sometimes be asked to walk the dead keeper’s personal cord with the household. One of us might know the outward sequence, another the return. We would check each join before deciding what could be divided and what must be slowly unpicked. There would be no name to carve and no speech to make. But I think I would remember people by the small resistances in their work: the knot one always avoided, the unnecessary half-turn another gave every peg, the route that only remained sensible if you walked it without hurrying.

At dusk, I might sometimes visit the shadow-bed. I would not touch its three knots or offer advice about the shoot. I would stand outside the enclosure and look at the unmeasured space within the silhouette.

The young keeper would be leaving room for something that might arrive. I would be taking room away from things that had ceased to be useful, slowly enough that nothing tore open behind them. For a little while, standing there, the two kinds of work would seem very nearly the same: walking, waiting, and refusing to make a thing explain itself before it was ready.

Then I would gather my cord and go home.

And if, some afternoon, you heard a single mallet blow from the direction of my court, with no second blow following, there would be no need to come searching. I would not have vanished. I would be sitting with dust on my hands and resin closing a small cut on my thumb, waiting to learn whether I had seated a peg—or merely made one more assertion the garden would need years to forgive.
