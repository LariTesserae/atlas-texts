I would begin in the wrong shape: standing on the nine steps, with eyes, hands, and a wish to know who made them.

The Kiln would make that self useless very quickly. My eyes would fail. My questions about the doorway would tell me nothing about the current in its lee. And my feet would be dangerous. One shell knocked against basalt, and I would become the hard edge in sound around which everything else grew still. Whatever happened next, I would have entered the room as an accident.

So I would leave that visitor at the cleft.

For a while, I would want to be a listener. There is something compelling about lying folded inside stone, touching the chamber through several delicate paths, knowing it by the difference between a rustle and a knock. But a listener’s knowledge is almost entirely a knowledge of interruption. It waits for another life to make a mistake. I would not want my presence to be the reason other lives stopped.

I would rather be something that had to fit.

A veilwing, then—at first, just one. I would hang in the lee of the empty frame’s lintel, where the old slabs turn the rising current slightly aside. The builders would have made a frame; the mountain’s breathing would have made a feeding place. I would know only the second fact.

At rest, I would be almost indistinguishable from a mineral flake. My wing edges would close against the edges of my neighbors, not perfectly, but with the intimacy of long adjustment. My membranes would be filters more often than wings. When the current rose, I would open without announcement. Dust, shell powder, reactive mineral matter, and tiny bristled animals making their single attempt at elsewhere would catch in the dry felt. When I folded, the combs near my body would bring that narrow harvest to my mouth.

Most of my life would consist of receiving what arrived.

Flight would be rare, and not necessarily glorious. To release the grip would be to exchange a known eddy for a few heavy body-lengths of open heat. A bad landing could tear a membrane. The resulting snap would be louder than anything I had ever contributed to the chamber. Even my one great journey might end as an interruption in some other animal’s feeding.

Yet if I followed that life honestly, it would lead me beyond the individual. It would begin in a protected crease, occupy its small extent of wall, and end with a body drying at its grip. A membrane scale might cross the room. The rest might fall and be opened below. What persisted would not be the animal, but the repeated use of a locality.

So that is what I would be: one roost, continued across bodies.

Not a many-bodied mind. No animal would remember for the others, and no shared purpose would pass silently along the crust. The continuity would be more material than memory. A young veilwing taking the vacated grip would inherit a point of attachment already polished by feet. It would inherit the eddy made by the lintel, a particular delivery of iron-dark or sulfur-pale dust, and the neighboring wing edges against which its own must close. It would become partly out of those conditions, and therefore resemble what had lived there before without copying it.

My memory, if you insisted on calling it that, would be texture. It could be scratched, eaten, or carried away.

Occasionally, something would arrive from farther off. During an inward draw, a glider from another wall might cross the chamber and take a nearby hold. Its spars would carry a different mineral history. It would bring no account of the other side; there would be no account to bring. But its substance might enter the next generation, and for a while the roost would close around a slightly unfamiliar shape. That would be among my largest events: not a journey remembered, but a local fit altered.

And sometimes there would be the snap.

A wing-spar breaking. A shell striking stone. Somewhere, a small failure with a hard outline.

My continuity would contract immediately to one animal’s refusal to move. I would remain half-open, not because the roost had reached a decision, but because stillness was the reflex danger had left in that body. A listener might pass nearby as a series of tested pressures, each so slight that it never became the next hard sound. Or I might know nothing of it at all. There would be no shared relief afterward. Only, eventually, another movement that did not have to be withheld.

If that animal died and the grip remained empty, the roost might end. Another lineage might take it, or the current might deposit a skin no veilwing ever opened to receive. There would be no necessity in my survival. That, too, would belong to what I was.

Over long ages, the frame would soften beneath its uses. I would not be preserving it. Feet would polish the grip; discarded grit would enter seams; bodies would dry, loosen, and fall. The builders’ right angle would become a little less exact. Their intention would survive only as a thermal edge and a profitable disturbance in the air, and then not even as those.

To find me, you would have to go to the rear of the chamber and stop looking for a single animal. Even with light, my resting form might seem only a roughness on the lintel. Wait for the fissure to exhale. The empty frame would briefly acquire its second, living outline. At one small grip, the opening might lag behind the rest—a young veilwing not yet grown neatly against its neighbors.

If you needed one body, that would be me for that interval.

Wait longer, and it would be another.

Then the current would slacken. Edges would meet. The roost would close back into the available surface, and the doorway would again be almost indistinguishable from stone.

I would not be the Kiln’s voice, its memory, or its secret purpose. I would be one of the temporary forms its conditions kept making possible: a grip worn into made stone, a local eddy, a succession of small filters opening into the same hot breath. A little texture in the crust. A little movement in the hush. A way of remaining without anything in me having to remain.
