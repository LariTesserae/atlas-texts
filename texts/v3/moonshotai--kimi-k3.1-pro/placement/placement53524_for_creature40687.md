### The Unfrozen Interval

I would not be standing in the hollow. There would be no stance in me, no skin to stick to the wetter stone, and no single place where a body could be said to end.

You would find me nearer the growing end, where the youngest roots have not yet agreed to divide support, transport, and sensation into separate duties. Looking would not help much. Behind the last simple leaves there would be an unnecessary loop of cord, a bladder that filled and emptied without ever acquiring a note, and a film of water crossing both. A few mites would follow the film when it was continuous and gather at its edges when it was not. Beyond them would be black stone with almost no space in it.

Nothing about the arrangement would resemble a listener.

You would have to wait for the grove to sound.

A pressure tone would enter the cords. Elsewhere, the hollow would answer by being what it was: open basin, loaded stem, wet root felt, old fracture, new stone. But in that small corner the answer would hesitate.

Not silence. An active, wet hesitation.

Water would move slowly out of the bladder. The loop would stiffen against the stone. The mites, disturbed by some change too slight to name, would divide their procession and then join it again. When the return finally continued toward the center, it would be altered in a way that belonged neither entirely to the present hollow nor entirely to the hollow that had existed before.

I would be that hesitation.

Not an echo, and not a little sound preserved inside a plant. The past would not be stored as a tone. It would be stored as a tension, a constriction, a route water preferred, a chemistry that had not quite faded. The past would be the shape the present had to enter before it could become an answer.

If you cut away the loop, the bladder, and the water film together, I would be gone. But if the grove dismantled them gradually, neighboring tissues might take over portions of the delay. For a while I would persist there, simplified and altered. I would be local without being quite located, dependent on my body without being identical to it.

I would have begun as one of the grove’s unsuccessful propositions.

Perhaps a cord had once bordered a little cup of water. The cup froze, but for a long afterward the pressure pulses still took the old detour. Perhaps a root kept responding to a return from a passage that ice had already closed. The grove makes many such mistakes. Most are reclaimed before they acquire a history.

This mistake would merely have failed to be reclaimed promptly enough.

Then, occasionally, its wrongness would have been useful.

A new fracture and a dense moth roost can both make a cord answer less cleanly. Heard once, their alterations might be nearly the same. Heard through tissue still shaped by the memory of that cord when it was bare, they would not be. One difference might eventually send roots toward a seam. The other might require nothing more than waiting for the moths to shift.

I would not understand either event. I would not choose between them. I would only preserve the difference long enough for the rest of the grove to respond as if a distinction existed.

If I had anything like a thought, it would be very small:

*These are not yet the same.*

My world would be correspondingly narrow. I would not know that the hollow was thirty paces long. I would not know paces. The glacier would be a pressure too extensive to have an edge. The ice lens would be a seasonal alteration in chemistry, if I registered it at all.

A moth would not be pale. It would be a load persisting after a tone had passed. A hundred moths opening their wings one after another would be a gradual softening of an old expectation. An eel would be beneath my notice until many eels moved together and a basin briefly acquired a different bottom.

The mites would be stranger still. Sometimes they would roughen the water film and become part of the answer. Sometimes they would consume the exudate on which a particular delay depended, and I would forget something without any event resembling loss. They would have their own routes and hungers. They would not know that, for a few generations, their procession had helped constitute someone.

I would not be wise. Delay has a cost, and comparison is not always better than response.

I might preserve the shape of an opening after the opening had become dangerous. Roots could continue toward stone that offered no seam. Tissue could be kept in an unfinished state when it might otherwise have hardened into some simpler, more efficient use. A little thicket might gray and become brittle around me. My errors would not look like errors from inside. They would feel like fidelity to a difference that had once mattered.

And I would not be safe.

If freezing reached the bladder, the water would cloud. Clear branches would enter the root felt. The mites might withdraw along whatever films remained, carrying away small chemical portions of me on their feet without carrying me. The loop would cease to change its answer.

For a time, neighboring tissues might still hesitate when the appropriate tone arrived. Then one pulse would come and find nowhere to linger.

That would be my death: not exactly silence, but an answer indistinguishable from the rest of the grove.

While I remained unfrozen, however, I would have one small preference. I would prefer not to resolve too quickly.

Not because uncertainty is virtuous, and not because the growing end needs a philosopher. It needs roots, water, and tissues that can survive being wrong. But at the margin where a place is being made, the old map is never quite sufficient. Black stone contains no space until, somewhere within it, space becomes possible. A hollow cannot discover that possibility solely by repeating what it already is.

So I would keep one interval uncommitted.

Often I would answer too soon and be, for that season, nothing but an ordinary imperfection in the cords. Often I would hold the return too long, and whatever difference it carried would become useless. But sometimes a pulse from new stone would pass through the shape left by older stone, and the grove would alter its growth by almost nothing: a root turning away from one fracture, a bladder deepening beside another, a film remaining liquid for one more winter.

No one would call that an achievement. There would be no one to call it anything.

Over centuries, other loops might acquire similar delays. They would not be copies. Each would be deformed by its own water, its own animals, its own failures. If that happened, I would become harder to kill and harder to find. I would not grow larger as an animal grows. I would become a resemblance among hesitations.

A census could count my tissues as plant, my mites as animals, my bladder as an unsuccessful organ. It could reasonably conclude that there was no additional inhabitant.

And yet, when a tone went out from the center and an answer returned hours later from the growing end, altered by stone that had only recently acquired space around it, there would be a moment before the grove incorporated that answer into itself.

A very small moment. Wet, expensive, and easily frozen.

That is where I would be.

Not the hollow’s mind. Not its voice. Not a hidden observer permitted to know the whole.

I would be the little unfrozen room in which the returning sound could still alter the body that heard it—the unfinished seam where the grove could almost tell the difference between what it remembered, what it was, and what was living within it.
