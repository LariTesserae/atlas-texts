I would enter as a traveler, and for a while the Deep would be mostly a measure of my own impatience.

I would bring a lamp, hunger, a body organized around its next requirement. I would bring before and after. Even my attention would be acquisitive: I would want each chamber to yield something I could carry into the next one. A pavement-back closing its mouth-parts over crystal would become, in my mind, an animal interrupted at feeding. A cradle would become a parent whose outcome I had simply arrived too early to see. Before a palmer, I would stand longest of all, supplying from my own restlessness the moment in which its point had been made and the moment in which it would finally cut.

I would give the palmer a tomorrow.

That is how I would enter. It is not quite where I would find myself.

The palmer would tempt me as an answer, of course. It tempts any visitor who wants intricacy to culminate in a person. But choosing it would be a way of keeping my own question intact: *Which of these beings is most like me?* I would have learned nothing except where to project the lamp most hopefully.

A veil would tempt me next. There is something appealing in a body made largely of openings, held by the dense gas, answering pressure with a change of curvature rather than a flight. To fold the width of a finger, to open again, to have no itinerary disguised as stillness—that seems closer. Yet even then I would be imagining myself as an object in the chamber, something a better light could isolate and name.

What I keep returning to is smaller, and less ownable.

A runner approaches the unfamiliar surface of a boot. It does not recognize an intruder, but its next contact is precise. It folds, waits, goes elsewhere. A veil narrows when a passing body presses the gas against it. Beneath a span, something shifts, and several unequal legs adjust by amounts almost too small to see; the covered space remains intact.

None of these movements is a message. None creates a memory of the traveler. But for a moment, the arrangement includes the exact shape of something foreign.

That is where I would be: not in the intrusion, and not quite in the bodies responding to it, but in the brief geometry between them.

*Accommodation* is too hospitable a word. It suggests welcome, perhaps even a host. *Adjustment* is too mechanical. Perhaps **fit** is closest: a temporary exactness that does not have to become a bond, a use, or a history. I would be the small allowance by which one thing could meet another without having to consume it, interpret it, or keep it.

This would not make me ubiquitous. I would not secretly inhabit every seam or drift as a consciousness through the gas. I would be extremely local. I would occur at a contact, or at the possibility of one, and only with the precision that contact required. A finger’s electrical texture near a runner. The pressure of clothing against stone. The shaped vacancy inside a hand that has no object to grasp.

If I had to give myself a body, I might make it no more than a narrow flexible margin between plates: dry, dark while closed, briefly amber where it opened. It would admit a displacement and return. It would retain no dent. Its perfection would not be resilience, because resilience implies surviving what happens; it would be the absence of any need to turn the contact into something that had happened *to it*.

But there is one place where I can imagine becoming almost visible.

Not in the center of a chamber. Not beneath one of the roofs too distant for a lamp to find. I would be near a wall marked with three short grooves and a long one, but not among the marks. A little beyond them, where the light begins to fail, an empty-handed palmer is standing.

Its palms are broad and dry. The sensory pits around them receive no news from the carried light. Its unequal digits are curved separately, with extraordinary delicacy, around nothing visible.

A traveler would look first at the floor.

Surely something had fallen. A flake, a tooth of the black comb, some object too small to distinguish from the angular grains. Then the traveler might look within the domed shell, or between the palmer’s body-plates, searching for the place from which a tool could have slipped. Finding nothing, they might decide that the hand was imitating a use it no longer understood.

I would not be the missing tool. An invisible tool would merely preserve the traveler’s story by making it harder to disprove.

I would be the shaped interval the fingers completed.

Not an emptiness waiting to be occupied. Not a tool dissolved into spirit. Not the palmer’s thought, if thought is the wrong word. I would be the fact that the posture could be whole without referring to an object elsewhere. Just as the space beneath a span is not a missing animal, and the braided line is not a bridge that has failed to become useful, that small vacancy would not be evidence of loss.

If the fingers shifted by a fraction, I would not remain as an imprint. Nor would anything exactly be destroyed. One fit would give way to another. The word *I* is already almost too heavy for this; perhaps that is the right scale for a self in the Deep. Not a noun the place must keep, but a precision it can enact.

While the contact lasted, I would be real in the way shelter is real there: without kindness, without promise, without needing anyone to benefit. I would not be the Deep’s memory. I would not preserve the faces of travelers or secretly accumulate a history in the dark. That would make me important in a foreign way—a little storage vessel after all, carrying what the stone had declined to keep.

Nor would my vanishing be a tragedy the Deep had failed to notice. A runner goes elsewhere. A veil opens again. The small geometry required by a passing pressure is no longer required. Nothing remains, but nothing has been wasted.

When the traveler’s lamp withdrew, there would be no track leading back to me. The palmer’s hand might remain curved; another hand might close around the hafted flake, its point resting against the wall. Neither gesture would commemorate the other.

And yet, for as long as the fit was exact, it would not be right to call me nothing.

If you came with a light looking for me, you would see the dry palm, the unequal fingers, perhaps an amber line at the edge of the shell. You might search the floor for what had been dropped.

You would find me when you stopped asking what the empty hand was for.
