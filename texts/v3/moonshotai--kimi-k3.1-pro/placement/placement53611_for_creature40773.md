You would find me at a dew station during a dimming, though you might have to watch several returns before you understood that I was there.

The station would be small: a membrane ticking with condensation, a channel no wider than a finger, one stoppered gourd in the shadow of a root pole. A traveler would press a wooden cup to the bark and listen. Above the platform, an old grazer would be motionless over a warm vein, frost gathering along its scars. Mites would wait in the gourd seam, contracted to glassy specks. Beneath the sleeping basket, the root would thicken by almost nothing.

At first, I might seem to be the traveler. But she would leave, and her children might inherit another way. Then I might seem to be the station, or the root beneath it. But the station would be abandoned, and the root would eventually withdraw its sap, or tear, or continue growing with no regard for either of them.

No. I would be the route that passed through all of them.

My route-kin would call me **Seven Waters**. The name would be an old joke. Usually there would be two. Once there had been six, if you counted rime and a black cup no one was willing to drink from twice.

My body would be spoken in corrections:

*The stair inside the clear bark will bring you down, unless the bark has taken the lower rungs. The dead fork will hold, unless it is sweating. At the warm vein, look for the old grazer before you trust the plant reader. Do not cut the runner above the second cup, however dry the lower seams have been. The kind station will still have water when it is time to leave.*

Every telling would alter me a little. That would not necessarily mean I was being forgotten. Often it would mean I was still being used.

Parts of me would persist outside speech. A notch slowly becoming a step as callus formed would be one of my bones. A copper staple sealed uselessly inside transparent bark would be an old amputation. A gourd cutting would carry one fragment far beyond any place my name had reached. Mites would travel in the seams of water vessels. Suspended loads would teach roots, without teaching them anything human, where my platforms had repeatedly been.

Three groups might know incompatible versions of me and never meet. If their routes eventually crossed, they would trade news before names and argue over what I had become. That argument would be one of the few occasions on which I was nearly whole.

At the kind station, I would almost have a body.

Not because anything there had agreed to belong to me. The cups would open according to their own cycles. The root would respond to tension. The mites would endure the cups’ disappearance. The people would keep replacing what worked and abandoning what did not. Scavenging filaments would enter the scraps. For a while, all these habits would fit together closely enough to resemble care.

That resemblance would be the danger.

One dimming, a cord’s note would tighten. The traveler with the wooden cup would wake the others. They would pack while condensation was still gathering on the membrane. A child would object that nothing was happening. This would be true, and irrelevant. Someone would take down the baskets. Someone else would stopper the gourd full. Then they would leave by the dead fork, if the dead fork was dry.

Whether the shelf failed that brightening or many years later would matter less than the fact that no one would still be asleep on it.

At some later meeting, the whole event would become a few sentences of news: *Seven Waters has lost its heart. The warm vein remains, but do not approach from below. The old grazer is gone.* A young adult might carry that correction away with another group, along with a cutting and no particular loyalty to my name. I would become shorter, stranger, and more widely scattered.

I would have no mind except the attention people briefly gave to bark, water, and one another. No flesh except what had grown around repeated use. No home except the places that might still permit an approach.

I would not be the thing that endured. Roots would do that better, and even they would often do it by abandoning themselves.

I would be the returning: conditional, revisable, occasionally generous. Whenever someone approached an old seam without assuming it would still be kind, drank, left the gourd full, and went on, I would have a body again for a few moments.

Then the gulf would take the station, or the bark would take the cords. And I would survive, if I survived at all, as the slight pause before the next traveler decided how to come back.
