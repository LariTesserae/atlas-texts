### The Fine Fraction

I would not be standing above the breather slot, listening. That would leave me with too much world: a horizon, a plain, an outside against which the pocket could seem small. I would be inside before I knew there was an inside, and my first world would be a shed wing lying across the fine dust near the basin.

It would not seem delicate to me. A crease in it would be high ground. A tear would be a harsh white opening. Dust would accumulate on its upper surface, while beneath it there would be low shelter, old flakes, and the movements of animals smaller than myself. The lamps would matter less than the footing: loose mineral, resistant wing-substance, the faint richness of residue near the wing’s root.

I would be a wing-fretter.

Not the pocket’s cleaner, and not one of its maintainers. Those are names for what my hunger might resemble from far enough away. At close range, I would simply be working where the last soft material adhered: scraping at a dry trace, worrying an edge until it gave, taking what could be taken and leaving the rest altered by the effort. Most of the wing would not feed me. It would obstruct me, support me, shelter me, and finally break into flakes beneath animals that had no understanding of making ground.

At the heavy stroke, the wing would jump.

For an instant there would be no distinction between the vibration in the floor and the vibration in my body. Dust would pour through the shelter. Somewhere beyond it, a wireback’s rush or the collapse of a feeding front might be taking place, but those events would reach me only as a sharper blow in the ground or a brief chemical sting in the air. I would flatten into the nearest hollow. Then the stroke would pass, and separate movements would become discernible again: a grain settling, a gleaner crossing a flake, my own feet testing the loosened edge.

Other fretters would come when there was something worth coming to. I would not understand us as a gathering. We would crowd the same residue, clamber over one another’s work, expose some rich patches and bury others. Beneath us, smaller animals would inherit the spaces between flakes. To them, my footfall might be a ceiling fall; the fragment I dislodged might close a crevice in which several lives had been taking place. There would be no neutral scale from which I could see all this cleanly. Even my food would be somebody’s cover.

A major flight would reach me mostly afterward. First the pressure and impacts, the air made irregular by bodies; then things coming down. A spent buttonback might strike near enough to bring the sharp smell of crushed tissue. Wings would settle over older wings. For a while there would be too much material and too many newcomers. I would withdraw when a pale young wireback hunted through the fall, then return to whatever its passage had uncovered. Abundance would not mean safety. It would mean more edges, more disturbances, more animals responding to them.

My seasons would be those falls and their exhaustion. A fresh wing with residue at its root; a crowded killing; a long period in which every accessible trace had been worked away and only dry, useless substance remained. If the current shifted somewhere above me, I would not know it as a shift in current. I would know that fewer animals were falling in one place, that another stretch of ground had become busy, that hunger was taking me farther from shelter.

Perhaps I would end beneath a ceiling veil, or in the jaws of one of the basin’s small hunters. There would be no necessity that I finish the wing. If anything outlasted me, it would be a little less residue, a little more fine material, and a scatter of flakes whose size mattered to animals I had never distinguished from dust.

That is where I would find myself: not witnessing the whole pocket, but occupying one of its temporary thicknesses. Under a wing at the basin’s edge, when the screens slammed again, I would grip what was beneath me. Dust would pass over. The roar would resolve into small sounds. And I would go back, if I still could, to the resistant edge—not keeping the world going, not standing apart from it, but leaving one brief, material deviation inside its beat.
