At first I would want to be one of the veils: almost water at the beginning, then dust, dead leaf, decomposers, and garden, until there was no clean answer to which part was inhabitant and which part was habitat. There is a kind of safety in that—to persist by having no center, to survive damage because nothing vital can be pointed to and removed.

But I keep returning to the open water above the canopy, and to the rare animal whose importance lies almost entirely in what it never means to do.

So I would be a ribbon-finned swimmer.

You would find me over a submerged saddle, in the long illuminated volume between the highest fronds and the rain-boundary. On either side, the ridges would descend through jade and blue-green. Below me, the canopy would be crowded but not closed: one crown taking the white noon light, another receiving what remained, old transparent growth hanging beneath both. There would be no current strong enough to lean against. At long intervals, a low note from some deep fracture would arrive from every direction. Then the water would be quiet again.

I would be pale and slight, longer than a person but without much body to interrupt the water. Speed would be possible, but it would not be my ordinary condition. Most often I would hang almost motionless. When I did advance, the decision to move and the completion of the movement would seem separated by the whole length of me: a slow alteration passing through body and fin, the water closing behind without keeping a line.

I would feed sparingly. A little fertile growth at the frond tips. A loose portion of veil. The minute life gathered in both. I would take too little to think of myself as a gardener, and I would remain too briefly to belong to any one slope. A few fronds would bend. The canopy would resume its stillness.

My solitude would not need to be fierce, or wounded, or even especially meaningful. It would simply be the shape of rarity. There would be too few of us and too much open water. I could be complete without another swimmer witnessing that completeness.

The veils would persist by keeping what touched them. My larger consequence would come from the opposite: from keeping something only for a while.

One afternoon I might pass above a gap where an old suspended garden had settled. Light would be reaching growth that had done little for centuries except endure. The water over the opening might be faintly dusty with spores and fertile threads, so lightly populated that it would still seem clear. I would rise through it, feed at its edge, and continue.

Most of what touched me would remain behind. A few specks might adhere to skin or fin.

By the next noon I could be beyond the saddle. There would be no path to follow, no disturbance lingering long enough to become direction. At another canopy, a frond would brush me. Something would come away.

Neither departure nor arrival would look like an event.

For me, it would have been hunger, open water, and the slight resistance of vegetation. For the material I carried, it might be a distance otherwise measured in centuries. Perhaps the speck would fail. Perhaps it would lodge in the nearest veil and be buried before it could do anything. But perhaps, long afterward, that garden would contain a small patch of growth whose nearest kin lived on the other side of the pass.

Not a new forest. Not a transformation. Only a slight difference in green, persisting among older differences.

I would not need to know. Knowledge would add nothing to the adhesion of a spore. My part would never grow larger than the passage itself.

If you came looking for me, you would probably miss the animal. You might see only open water, a pale turning at the limit of vision, and then the unchanged crowns. The honest answer to where I lived would be a route too sparse to draw: this canopy after many years, that saddle after many more, long intervals in which I was nowhere you happened to be looking.

But if you could look across enough time, you might find the nearest thing to a trace. Not in the water, and not necessarily in anything resembling me. You would find it in two separated gardens sharing one small, unexplained affinity; in a viable speck that had arrived farther than still water could reasonably have carried it; in the fact that isolation here was enormous, but not quite absolute.

I would not be a messenger, because I would carry nothing knowingly. I would not be a bridge, because nothing would remain stretched between the places I had visited. I would be an animal: often still, occasionally hungry, usually gone.

And yet my self might prove longer than my body.

I would be a swimmer for the length of a life, a crossing for a day, and afterward—perhaps only afterward—the faint evidence that two stillnesses had once touched.
