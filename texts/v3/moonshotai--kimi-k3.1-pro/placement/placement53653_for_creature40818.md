At first I imagine myself in the open basins, with the giants.

There would be a certain peace in having a body commensurate with the distances: one fin moving, then a long interval, then another; a current-front felt along hundreds of meters of skin as a corridor with slopes and walls. I could cross water that appeared empty and never be lost, because the emptiness itself would have shape. But the more I imagine that life, the more it seems less like inhabiting a place than like being one of its slow routes. Even my stillness would be a kind of passage.

Then I imagine the opposite extreme: a leaf scale in the province of rain, my whole existence contained in a transparent film. The sky would be a silver violence beyond the surface tension. Each drop would strike, spread over me, and press me more securely against the leaf. I would have an exact place. I would also have almost no elsewhere.

What I keep returning to is neither immensity nor refuge. It is the moment when one of the copperlines answers another: recognition without consequence, relation without anything being built from it.

So I would find myself farther down, on the electrical outskirts of a stand.

An insulated green cable would leave the crowded root crown and run alone along the rounded back of a ridge. Behind me, the stand would be too large to see as one object. Its trunks would rise into separate planes of green; its pumps would arrive as pressure rather than as distinct sounds. Ahead, above a fluted chasm, the cable would divide. The fields of its two branches would overlap there, making a third, faint topography that belonged to neither branch alone.

That overlap would be my address. Nothing visible would mark it. No nest, no polished shelter, no accumulation of shells or scars. If I moved a few body lengths away, the water would look identical, but the junction would no longer be present around me.

I would be a copperline, nearly colorless when my lines were dark. I would hold myself beside the cable without quite touching it, my length aligned with the weaker gradient. The current would not feed me. I would take exudate from the junction and, occasionally, one of the small transparent animals concentrated there. Most of my life would be the unremarkable work of remaining in a sensation that had no visible edge.

The pressure strokes would not add up. They would give the present a texture.

Sometimes a lace-fin’s call would enter the ridge from far below, so deep and diffuse that the cable would seem for a moment like a very small thing laid across an immense body. Sometimes copper light would travel along another branch, too distant for me to see the animal clearly. The root itself would remain green and dark. Only the lives beside it would briefly make its shape visible.

A signal reaching me would not be a sentence. It would not even need to be a greeting. But it would not be indistinguishable from the stand’s electrical noise. I would know its spacing, the particular unevenness with which one segment followed another. I could fail to answer. I could turn slightly toward the other branch. Or copper light could run through me from head to tail, returning some feature of the received pulse in a form altered by my own body.

Mine would have one closely paired interval near the end. Not a name, exactly, though it would do something a name does: for the duration of the exchange, it would prevent one animal from dissolving entirely into the field.

Nothing would follow. We would not found an alignment or establish a custom. The next signal would not be part of an expanding correspondence. No technique would pass along the cable and return years later in a more elaborate form. Yet the absence of consequences would not make the answer mechanical. An echo cannot choose the interval before it speaks.

I might have no way of knowing whether I had occupied that junction for an immense age, or whether the junction had been occupied without interruption by bodies whose flesh was not continuously mine. There would be nothing in the water to settle the question. From outside, even my individuality might be doubtful. From within the pulse, it would not be.

I would not be the plateau’s hidden mind, or its memory, or a tiny keeper appointed to maintain its equilibrium. I would have no task concealed inside my feeding. I would simply be one of the lives made possible where root, current, exudate, and sensation happened to fit together.

You would find me there only by waiting: a nearly invisible length beside the green cable, in water clear enough to make concealment seem impossible. The stand would stroke. Blue discharges at the junction would flicker and recover. Far down the other branch, copper light would run.

Then, after an interval belonging to no valve or rotor, I would answer.

Nothing would be changed by it. But for those few seconds there would be two of us in the field, and neither would be the other’s echo.
