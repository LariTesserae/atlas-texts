At first, there would be no plain.

There would be a floor vibrating faintly against my feet, the close mineral scent of pressed silt, and two kinds of air. Behind me: warmth, humidity, the rich odor of the gardens. Ahead: a thin current of dryness entering where no dryness should be.

My antennae would find the damaged wall. Then there would be a pellet of earth between my mandibles, and the passage would narrow to a task.

You would call me a worker—one of the pale, blind, fungus-growing termites, soft-bodied and smaller than a grain of rice. You would call my world dark. I would know it instead as close, cool, warm, yielding, firm, contested, safe. I would have no name and no image of myself. Even my own colony would be too large to apprehend. I would know *near colony* and *far wrongness*: the familiar odor around me, the alien scent beyond a sealed gallery, the dangerous openness ahead.

My home would be the living flank of an old fluted mound. You would see its towers above the thorn scrub, its rust-colored repairs, the lizard holes near its base. I would know another mound entirely. A warm descent toward the nurseries. A floor spongy with garden comb. A passage abandoned except for the root that had entered it. A narrow place where soldiers gathered when the shell carried heavy vibrations. A chamber whose air was always a little stale, and beyond it a wall that workers had been thickening for longer than my life.

The mound would not be a shape to me. It would be a arrangement of conditions.

On the morning after a moundbreaker opened us, those conditions would change. The shell would carry the animal’s weight long after the animal had gone: the smaller impacts of birds at the exposed edge, the rapid movements of lizards, perhaps the fine, probing disturbance of ants. Somewhere beyond the breach, comb and brood would lie scattered. Soldiers would hold the usable approaches. Behind them, workers would already be closing off what could not be defended.

I would arrive carrying earth.

No one would show me the wall that had been there before. No one would tell me to restore it. There would be the dry current, the damaged edge, the scent of fresh deposits, other bodies withdrawing and advancing. I would press my pellet against a place where it held. The next worker would add another where mine had changed the surface. Sometimes I would bring soil and sometimes chew at a loose section, making material from what had failed.

A buttress might eventually come of this. A tower might begin on one favored edge. But there would be no buttress in my understanding, and no tower. There would only be a little less openness where I stood.

As the outer shell heated, I would withdraw. Deeper inside, the garden would receive me with its humid warmth. The comb would not look pale to me; it would be springy and fibrous beneath my feet, threaded through with the fungus that made our harvest usable. At one edge there might be a sourness, a contamination to be removed. Elsewhere, workers would be adding chewed vegetation, tending the comb, carrying fragments away.

Dry grass would come in from the covered runs and become, through other lives, food and heat and shelter. Even the climate around me would not be ours alone.

When I went out to forage, I would come no closer to seeing the plain. My journey would be a covered passage, a fallen pod obstructing part of the route, the odor of old grass, a place where the ceiling had been recently repaired. I might cut loose a fragment longer than my body and carry it home. Above me, hooves might pass close enough to drum through the soil. Dust might sift into the run. A root might have broken through since my last journey.

The plain would reach me as vibration, scent, obstruction, and food. That would not make it distant. It would simply make my knowledge of it exact and very small.

If rain came during my life, I would not know it as something bright falling from the sky. It would be an impossible fullness in the air, then cold sediment where a passage had been, then the slump of fresh wall. Repairs would have to wait, and then begin everywhere. Long afterward, there might be more dead stems along the foraging routes, more seeds knocking against the covered runs, new roots intruding below. The brief flowering above would enter my world only as changes in what could be gathered and what had to be sealed out.

A worker’s life would be short beside the life of a mound. When mine ended, nothing in the colony would pause to mark the difference. No single pellet in the repaired wall would identify me. The breach would become a scar, then a thickness, then perhaps a ledge on which a lizard took the morning heat. An abandoned gallery beside it might hold a silt-mouse’s cache. A root might follow the loosened earth and turn where we had once turned.

None of that would have been intended. None of it would need to bear my mark.

So you would find me only by accepting a very small scale: just inside the wall, carrying soil toward a current of dry air. I would never behold a mound, though I would live within one. I would never behold the Mound Plain, though its weather and hunger would reach me through the ground.

For the brief length of my life, I would be one of the ways it continued.
