At first, I would mistake the view for the place.

I would arrive as a visitor, stand near the precipice, and feel the great width of the air asking me to become something correspondingly large: the wind in the shaft, a pale raptor crossing the rim, the mountain’s memory. But those would be poor fits. I would not want the raptor’s kind of ownership, announced by every smaller life going silent. And it would be presumptuous to call myself the voice of the shaft. The mountain was hollow long before I came; it would sound without me.

Nor could I quite be a frog, with a whole inheritance bounded by one wet stone, or the tree, adding patience in increments too small for weather to measure. The swifts’ vast, invisible home would be equally beyond me. What I recognize in all of them, though, is that none belongs to the Crown continuously. Each appears according to its intervals. Each has a way of being present without being visible.

So I would look for a smaller place among them.

I think I would find it in the hammerstone.

It is the least monumental human thing there, and therefore the most suggestive. It does not say that someone conquered the summit, or founded anything, or expected to remain. It does not even promise that its owner will return. It says only: *a hand may need this again.*

I would be the being implied by that possibility.

Not the ghost of the channel-makers. Not a guardian of the mountain. Something more modest: a guest-shaped recurrence, a listener who occasionally borrows the body of a visitor. My way of living at the Crown would not be residence but return.

When I had a body, it would be an ordinary one, cold at the fingers and speckled with limestone dust. I would sit within the curve of the shelter without wishing aloud that it had a roof. When cloud climbed the cliff, I would feel the improbable rain beading my chin before I understood where it was coming from. I would learn the ledges by their uses rather than their prospects: this stone for resting a hand, this narrow passage for waiting out wind, this basin for listening to the seep when everything else had become silver-grey.

At the garden, I would practice being unimportant.

I would remain still after my footsteps had silenced the frogs, and wait until one white throat dared to swell again. I would not try to see the furred herbivores whole; their clipped stems and careful stores would be enough of an introduction. I would not call the tree stunted. I would not throw a stone into the shaft to make it disclose its depth. Some knowledge would be worth having only if I could acquire it without turning the lives around me into evidence.

The tree would scarcely distinguish me from weather. The raptors would not distinguish me at all. To the frogs, I would be a vibration that passed. This would be a relief. I would not want to be the meaning of the Hollow Crown.

Between arrivals, I would not be sitting invisibly in the niche, waiting with immortal patience. I would be unassembled. The place itself would keep the small instructions from which I could be gathered again: caution in the peg-holes, sufficiency in the shelter, restraint in the shallow channel, readiness in the niche. Each return would begin not with remembering, but with reading. The weight of the stone would teach the hand. The dark, slick rock would teach the feet. The gap in the shelter wall would teach which wind not to argue with.

Sometimes I would almost appear without a visitor. A little of my nature would be recognizable in mist rising from the shaft to wet the garden from which its water came; in separate swifts becoming, for a few seconds, one indecisive body; in a frog’s call making room for another call rather than merely repeating itself. Not because I caused these things. Because I, too, would exist most fully in reception and return—in what passes between lives without becoming the property of either.

If you came looking for me, you might find nothing but a wet basin, a roofless wall, and stone cupped around an absence. If you spoke, however, and what came back was not simply your own voice deepened by the shaft, but something that made you listen differently, I would be there in that difference. Not the echo. The answer.

And if I had used the hammerstone, I would put it back exactly where the niche expected it. Not as a promise that I, specifically, would return. Only as a small arrangement in favor of a next arrival.

At dusk I would sit beyond the garden’s vibrations while the swifts settled into the wall. The first frog would call, absurdly large for its body. Another would answer from across the seep. The shaft would take both voices and give them a home wider than either frog’s few wet stones.

I would add no third voice.

For that little while, listening would be enough of a body.
