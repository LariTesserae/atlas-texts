At the rim, I would mistake myself for the dark bird.

There is a temptation in being the one thing that can cross without landing. The bird sees the growth fronts as changes of color, the collapses as brief subsidences, the whole basin as something lying safely below. It contributes nothing and is claimed by nothing. Most of all, it has an elsewhere. For a while, I would call that freedom.

Below the rim, I might envy a column instead: to rise clear of the felt, to hold the cold light in an opening crown, to have height and an outline. But a column is only height on loan from the circulation. Its importance has already begun leaving it before its crown pales. To be tall here is to be on one’s way back into the ground.

Nor could I simply call myself the whole basin. That would be true in too large a way. I would become impossible to find.

So I would go down until the basin stopped being a view.

My breathing would become an intrusion. My weight would arrive ahead of me as pressure, passing into cords I could not see. A stand of columns might vanish nearby with no sound except, perhaps, a slight giving beneath my soles. I would put out a hand to steady myself and find the surface interested in its warmth—not reaching, not recoiling, merely making small use of the contact.

Eventually, beneath a roof of interlaced roots, I would come to a covered pool.

Perhaps it would be no wider than my two hands. Perhaps larger; size would not be the important thing. Condensation would enter it one bead at a time. Around its margins, pale filaments would approach the darkness and turn. The supporting felt would thicken, collapse, and thicken elsewhere, while the hollow itself remained.

At first I would say: *I will be this pool.*

Then I would have to correct myself.

The water would not be me. Water could enter a buried vessel and be carried into some distant crown. The roof would not be me; its roots would die back and be replaced. Even the rim would slowly exchange its substance with the surrounding felt. If I identified myself with any of these, I would become another temporary growth, another brief arrangement awaiting disassembly.

I would be more nearly the turning aside.

The continuing act by which tissue came up to the hollow and did not close it. The old, local practice of going around. My body would be whatever roots and water were performing that practice at the time. My memory would not consist of images or seasons, but of recurrence: after a wave of growth, after a collapse, after freezing and thaw and the passage of spores, the same small openness would again be there.

Not because nothing touched me.

Because innumerable movements kept disposing themselves around my shape.

It would be easy to romanticize this as refusal, but it would not be refusal. The basin does not refuse; it responds. My openness might be useful as a reservoir or pressure buffer. It might be a cold interval necessary to some buried route. Or it might be an old habit whose first occasion had vanished so completely that only the habit remained. I would not need to know which. Function and memory would meet in me without becoming distinguishable.

I would not be exempt from production. My margins would receive and release material. My level would change. Growth would use my edges, roof me more deeply, support the ground above me. A mite might traverse some wet seam at my boundary and vanish again. None of this would make me less open.

My stillness would be an achievement of motion, not its opposite.

If a traveler parted the growth and looked in, I would not watch them. Watching is too concentrated an act. Their heat and moisture would enter the local tissue; their shadow would alter almost nothing; their breathing would seem enormous. They might think they had discovered a remnant of the basin before its covering, some surviving piece of an older world. They would be wrong. I would not be a survivor of what the basin had been. I would be something its present life kept doing.

After they left, the damaged roof would close. Their footprints would lose their reasons for existing. If they had taken water, more would eventually arrive. If they had broken the rim, the repair would follow the hollow rather than erase it. Nothing would remember them as a person. Nothing would remember me as a pool.

Yet there would remain a difference between those two kinds of forgetting.

And perhaps, eventually, the difference would fail. Pressure would alter; roots would enter; the black lens would drain into a cord. I would not be spared merely because I had lasted. There is no exception here, and I would ask for none.

But by then even *pool* would have become too solid a name for what I was.

The water would become circulation. The hollow would become a route. Somewhere else, perhaps beyond a saddle or beneath another canopy, filaments would approach a depression and turn aside—not because my ending had been transmitted as an instruction, but because the basin was full of such recurrences, and no single one had ever needed to contain the others.

From the rim, I would be invisible. The bird would still cross overhead, possessing its elsewhere. Columns would rise and be reclaimed. Whole shoulders would change color without a sound.

To find me, you would have to stop looking for a shape tall enough or strange enough to be an inhabitant. You would have to go under the growth and search for the place where living tissue almost entered, thickened, and went around.

There I would be: not the valley’s center, not its witness, not one of its necessary passengers, but a small openness maintained by life on every side.

A local memory without a mind.

A hollow with no promise of permanence.

A *here*, for as long as the turning happened again.
