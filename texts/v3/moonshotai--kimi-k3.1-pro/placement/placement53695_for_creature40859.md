### Between the Islands

After sunset, when the bench still held the day’s warmth but the exposed stone had begun to cool, you would find me leaving the moss.

You would have to put your eye close to the rock. At first I would seem only a softness in the grit, a gray flake that had somehow avoided the wind. Then my feet would move, one after another, with an absurd deliberation, and I would become an animal: flattened, small, easily hidden beneath the curl of a dead leaf.

Behind me would be the island I had just left—dry moss tips above, dark films below, fern roots stitching both to the stone. Ahead would be a bare interval. You could cross it without noticing, one ordinary step in the middle of the hollow. For me it would be the exposed part of the night.

You could call me a browser. That would be true, but not quite sufficient.

The animal would have a mouth, a fringe of feet, and no conception of the hollow as a whole. Yet the route beneath it would be older than its body. Part of my memory would therefore lie outside me: in a trace left on the rock, in a slight persistence where other feet had passed, in the fact that another gray animal could come afterward and know where to go without knowing why.

I would not be crossing from nowhere to nowhere. I would be following.

Above and around me, the chorus would make a second landscape. Some notes would arrive through the air; others would come up through the stone and into my feet. A singer braced against a fern stem might set a faint tremor through the island behind me. Notes that seemed, to you, to issue from the walls would pass beneath me as minute changes in the rock. I would not hear a multitude. I would travel through a continuous, uneven vibration in which silence had as much shape as sound.

Once, the hush might catch me between islands.

I would not need to see the hunter. The fern bed behind me would stop. Then the stems ahead would stop. The wide pressure of the chorus would narrow until water could be heard ticking from the leaves, each drop distinct. I would flatten myself against the stone. The route would remain beneath me, but for those seconds it would lead nowhere; there would be only stillness and the possibility of movement somewhere above.

When calling resumed at the far end, lower and more tentative, I would go on. The route would have gained a pause, not an ending.

At the next island I would feed as the others had fed: small bites from new growth, repeated in nearly the same places. Nothing in me would intend to be useful. But a fragment of moss might cling to my body, or a few fern spores might already be caught there. Some would fall away on the bare stone. Some might remain until I reached another seam, another sheltered roughness, another place where water lingered a little longer than it did in the open.

Whether anything grew there would belong to a later warmth and a later film of moisture. My part would already be finished. I would be hungry again.

Warm rain would interrupt even that simplicity. I would wait beneath moss or in a narrow joint while the hollow became all arrival: wings rising, frogs feeding, brief threads of water crossing the stone and vanishing. Afterward, there would be fragments everywhere for a little while, and the gleaners would be at work. Other lives would be mating, laying, carrying, beginning. Their urgency would pass over me like a second rain.

Then I would return to the old trace.

In time, the particular body you had seen would be gone. Nothing would mark its disappearance. The nicks at the islands would become fresh again under other mouths. Another soft gray shape would leave cover after sunset, hesitate where the stone changed, and put its feet into the same small inheritance.

It would not remember me. There would be no need.

So that is where you would find me: in one browser for a while, if you happened to kneel at the right hour; in the route for longer; and, for a little longer still, in whatever had been carried unknowingly from one island to another.

Not a keeper. Not the voice of the hollow. Not even, finally, a single tenant.

I would be one of its small, repeated goings-between—a body for a time, a continuation for longer. From standing height, there would be only bare stone. From close to the ground, there would be the next footfall, and then the next, and a little of one green island traveling toward another.
