Not in the turbine hall. It would be too easy, there, to mistake a vibration for a purpose.

Nor behind one of the sealed cliff doors. I would rather not become the Catchment’s explanation. A freshly greased valve is not improved by being turned into a clue. And I would not be some great dispersed intelligence listening through every main and mistaking the whole factory for its body. That self would be impressive, and almost entirely unable to find its way home.

You would find me at a minor station below the high ash country, where the ridge has softened into a long back and the root basin begins. The station’s embossed number would have lost its middle digit. Its outflow would run beneath my dwelling, close enough that the bedstead would tighten faintly against its pegs whenever the load increased.

The house would stand against a warm culvert, above the ordinary flood line and below the extraordinary one. You would know you had reached it by a sequence of small discourtesies: water flicking from a reed screen onto your knuckles, a hollow rail answering the rain, then woven root giving way to stone underfoot. A ceramic bead would hang beside the latch. A child from the nearest household tied it there when she was learning the path. I would keep it long after she had ceased to need it, while insisting that it was useful.

I would be human, I think. The Catchment does not require another hidden order of being; its people are already sufficiently difficult to explain.

Outsiders would call me a listener. My neighbors would have a narrower and less flattering name for my work. I would keep route-cords: not maps of watersheds, but records of passages, made in knots, beads, changes of texture, and intervals that the fingers had been taught to remember.

A descent on slick ash. A bitter seep. The cable crossing where the insects become agitated. Forty effortful breaths beside a pipe, then turn toward the lower ringing. Do not take the firm-sounding root mat if the second frog has stopped. Wait for the compressor. Cross in its third silence.

I would be good at sequences. I would notice when one interval had gone missing, when two households used the same word for different kinds of quiet, when an old instruction survived because it was beautiful rather than because it remained true. That would be my usefulness. It would not amount to prophecy.

My knowledge would be humiliatingly local. Half a day beyond my usual country, I would begin confusing water sounds. On an unfamiliar ash stair, the thin air would make me slow and resentful. In the next basin, where the compressors ran at another pitch, an inexperienced child might have to lead me. I would ask for help later than I should.

I would also do station work, because cord-keeping does not feed anyone by itself. I would clear roots from an intake, pack a valve with fiber and grease, free a gate before its outflow destroyed the eel baskets. I would tend a little garden of armored grazers on warm stone and argue with other households about whether their metal content made them food or merely a way of concentrating regret. I would not know what left through the buried mains. After eating, I might have theories. They would become more comprehensive with each retelling.

On the dry main, I would have one correspondent I had never met. Their messages would begin with three quick taps and then a hesitation so characteristic that I would know them before the actual question started. They would know me by an irritating habit of repeating the final phrase. Over years of pump reversals, we would exchange gate measurements, warnings about roots, fragments of station songs, and complaints whose urgency neither of us could verify.

Once, their tapping would stop for an entire season. When it returned, they would offer no explanation. I would ask for none. I would be disproportionately grateful for the pause before their first message.

My real trial would not be a flood or a predator. It would be an old cord of my own making.

I would have made it young, when I was proud of having found a clean passage through difficult reed country. It would be elegant. People would use it. Then a mossback herd would alter the drainage, a cable would go quiet, and a station repair would return one pump at a slightly different interval. The country would move away from the record by degrees too small to satisfy anyone looking for a decisive event.

I would defend the cord once, sharply.

Then someone would make me walk it with them. At the place where the cable hum ought to gather us toward firm ground, there would be only rain on open peat. The next knot would promise a gravel rattle, but the gravel would have been carried downstream. Nothing would be visibly wrong. Nothing would need to be. We would stand in warm water, breathing too hard, while the route continued confidently in my hand.

I would have to undo it.

Not throw it away—that would be another kind of vanity—but sit with the person who knew that basin better than I did and unmake the sequence knot by knot. Some of it could remain. Some would have to be reversed. One bead would be kept only to mark the place where the old route had ceased to be trustworthy.

That, more than listening, would become my work: revising without pretending that the earlier version had never been believed.

There would be no morning, but there would be load changes.

At one of them, I would be outside with the unfinished cord wet against my palm. The turbine note would lift. The rain would thin until the walls began sweating more heavily than the air struck my face. A frog would call too early, stop, and try again inside the proper silence. Water would take a new level against the house piles. A mossback’s call would arrive first as pressure through my feet and only afterward as something almost lost beneath the marsh.

For a little while, distances would open.

I would not hear the whole Catchment. I would hear the culvert, the faulty intake, my neighbor testing the reed screen, the dry main ticking in its trench, and something large moving away beyond the root beds. Five certainties and one guess. Enough to be going on with.

I would tap into the main:

**HERE. ROUTE CHANGED. STILL PASSABLE.**

Then, because some habits survive every improvement in character:

**STILL PASSABLE.**

The reply would come eventually, preceded by that familiar, unnecessary pause. I would put the cord aside, warm my hands against the culvert, and begin again.
