### The pause in the shaft

I would look for myself first among the durable things, and be disappointed.

The quartz endures, but only by becoming dangerous. The central shaft endures, but not its cables, landings, or walls. Even a route preserved through three surges is less an object than an agreement repeatedly remade under pressure. I would not want to be a monument in a city that has learned, so thoroughly, to make monuments into feedstock.

You would find me instead in a maintenance clearance halfway up the shaft, between a counterweight rail and the warm housing of a lift motor. The gap is too narrow for freight. It has therefore acquired three uses: a cradle shelf, a cache of tested enamel, and me.

At rest, you would mistake me for insulation scrap.

I would be a mender, though not a shapely one. Eight limbs, two of them acquired from bodies with very different ideas of work. One long shaft-running limb; one short, brutally braced joint suited to a mill. My back would carry seven smoky plates, none quite flush, with amber showing between them only after a generous feed. The oldest plate came to me with a map etched on its inner surface. The map is accurate to a district that no longer exists. I keep the plate because it insulates well.

When I walk, there are two firm footfalls, then a close pair, then a pause just long enough to suggest damage. That pause is the part of me others use as a name.

By occupation, I would listen to routes.

Not map them. A map says where a corridor is; I would be more interested in how the corridor announces that it is leaving. The dry catch in a lift before its cable slips. The double answer of a floor under which a seam is opening. The faint warmth conducted into a handrail from a fault three galleries away. The moment seam-fleas stop glittering and contract against the quartz.

I would know the practical things, too: how to bridge a contact without becoming part of the circuit, how to brace a nursery wall, how to recover a limb whose crystal layers have begun to chatter out of phase. But other menders could do those things as well or better. My particular usefulness would begin when a district’s instructions and its machinery ceased to agree.

I would teach routes as sequences of bodily caution.

*Grip here, but not during the lift’s recovery stroke.*

*The amber indicator is lying. Trust the heat in your rear plate.*

*At the fourth anchor, insulate yourself before extending a palp.*

*If the fleas leave in bands, do not wait for the controller.*

Some of my lessons would outlast their destinations. That would not make them useless. A route can vanish and still teach you how a certain kind of failure feels through the feet.

My home would be the arrangement around the cradle shelf, not the clearance containing it. There would be others to groom conductive dust from my joints, others whose load I could take when they fed too quickly, others who knew without looking that my pause did not mean I had stopped listening. I would stop work for wiremouse egg-lines when the local compact allowed it. Anything that notices electrical trouble before the instruments do deserves a little inconvenience.

And there would be one cradle that had not quickened.

We would feed it carefully. We would keep it near the motor housing, warm enough that its young lattice could still be reworked. Sometimes I would touch its conductive threads and give it the simplest part of my cadence: two pulses, the close pair, the pause.

Then I would withdraw.

It would be important not to answer for it.

If it ever quickened, it might keep the pause, alter it, or discard it entirely. Kinship would not require resemblance. Still, I think I would like to encounter that hesitation someday in another body, crossing a gallery I had never seen, and know that something had been carried without being preserved.

Then, one production cycle, the vibration underfoot would change by almost nothing.

I would stop in mid-cadence.

Above me, the wiremice would turn toward their nests. On the quartz face below, the fleas would begin leaving. The lift would arrive with its familiar hesitation, and for the first time I might not know whether the hesitation belonged to the worn machinery or to the mountain.

I would not try to save the maintenance gap. It was never mine in that sense. The enamel cache could become feedstock. The cradle shelf could be cut loose. Even my mapped plate could be crushed if its ceramic were needed elsewhere. I would help carry the cradle and its charged core toward a motor housing still firmly anchored. Then I would go to the shaft crossing, because others would be coming toward it through routes already coming apart.

Not because the crossing was sacred.

Not because my body was expendable, either. A depleted mender is only another leakage trail through the dark, and a corpse repeating its last reflex helps no one.

I would hold the route while holding it remained useful. I would know that abandoning it too late could be as harmful as abandoning it too soon. That judgement—the unglamorous interval between loyalty and obstruction—would also be part of my work.

Afterward, I might be missing a limb. I might have a broader back, a shorter gait, a new irregularity others would have to learn. Or there might be little left of me but sound material and a cadence incorporated into somebody else’s route lesson. The city would make no great distinction between those outcomes. It would be busy becoming somewhere else.

But if I were still able, I would test the new floor.

Two taps. The close pair. The pause.

Then I would begin the lesson again—not *this is the way*, not even *this way will remain*.

Only: *here is what the ground is doing now*.

That is where you would find me: in the pause between what the city was and what its conditions would permit next, trying to make the pause just wide enough for someone else to cross.
