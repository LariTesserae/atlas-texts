At first I would choose the ray, and for the wrong reason: because it moves. From above the shell berms, it might seem almost to possess a view. It could cross in minutes distances that other animals would never travel, reading the buried flicker of muscle beneath the mud. But the ray’s city would be a sequence of open routes and interruptions. It would pass over thousands of doorways without learning why one was clear and the next had silted shut. I would not want to mistake range for knowledge.

Then I might imagine myself an old eel inside a thick wall. That would be closer. Where a fish in the lane encountered mineral shelter, I would know a country of turnings: my own mucus at a familiar junction, another eel’s signature warning me aside, the altered pressure of water when a shrimp crossed some distant opening. My body and my map would have nearly the same shape. But even that life would be organized around concealment and withdrawal.

Eventually I would stop looking for a body that could carry me through the Low City.

I would choose one the city could pass through.

You would find me downstream of an old arcade, where a narrow throat releases into a broader chamber and the water slows enough for shell grit to settle. Warm seepage would rise faintly through the sediment below. My base would be fixed among dead oyster valves and abandoned suture-worm tubes; one side would lean against a wall whose living and mineral parts could no longer be cleanly separated. From the lane I would be a blunt, hollow irregularity, easy to mistake for a remainder of the wall.

I would be a cavern sponge.

Not a miniature city, and certainly not its witness. A census would simply have another difficulty with me: living tissue spread through an older skeleton, canals modified by use, cavities occupied by animals whose movements helped determine which of my passages remained open. My address would be less a point than a local change in current—quick water arriving from the throat, slower water leaving through my wide openings, sediment collecting unevenly in my lee.

The city would enter me without becoming mine. Organic snow from waters far above; bacterial production carried from the seeps; mucus and rejected silt from the oyster beds; eggs, waste, fragments of feeding crowns, the chemical evidence of occupancy or injury farther upstream. I would not assemble these into news of distant districts. They would be differences in abundance and resistance, in clearing and fouling, in the work of drawing water through myself. My geography would be internal: this canal open, that one narrowing, this chamber quiet, this outlet facing a current that had strengthened over many tides.

The pale shrimp would make another geography inside mine.

They would know my pulse, my branching shelters, the few exits through which a larger animal might force its way. Generations would live along routes I could not distinguish from the rest of my body. They would pick at lodged particles, seize small animals drawn inward, and retreat deeply when the outer water changed. Their eggs would rest in cavities where the flow was gentle. Their waste would enter the microbial life of my tissues.

To them, I would be shelter, food-bearing weather, and a system of dangerous thresholds. To me, they would be small touches, brief obstructions, passages kept unexpectedly clear. Our intimacy would be real without requiring either of us to imagine the other.

When the broad ray passed, I would receive the event secondhand. The small pressures made by fishes holding station above the arcade would vanish. Worm crowns along the wall would withdraw, subtly changing the roughness of the current. The shrimp would go deep. I might continue pumping into that hush, drawing in water strangely emptied of ordinary movement. Only afterward would come the ray’s nearer consequences: lifted sediment, disturbed prey, the downstream trace of something uncovered.

At the turning of a strong tide, I would reduce my pumping while silt thickened in the lanes. Later, as the flow steadied, water would move through me again, carrying material from chambers I would never touch. I would leave it altered—not purified, not improved in any sense the city could intend, merely passed through one more life.

Other animals would gradually make my boundaries less definite. Worm larvae would settle near my base. Oysters would encroach upon one side. A threshold crab clearing its apron below me would unintentionally delay the burial of several openings. If part of my body were torn away in a collapse, any fragment that remained intact would continue pumping. There would be no appeal to the shape I had possessed before.

Eventually my pumping would falter. The shrimp would become restless. Some would cross my outer surface, testing neighboring sponges with their antennae; others would remain as predators entered canals that had once been refuge. After my death, water would still move through the skeleton for a time. Animals favoring broad cavities would shelter there. Then worms and shellfish would encrust the openings, dividing them into smaller, darker cells. My passages would become awkward, then narrow, then merely solid in places.

No one would repair me into what I had been. Nor would I vanish all at once. For a while the current would still turn aside because of my remains. A brittle star might coil where a shrimp colony had been. Later, a larva might pause on some exposed roughness and find it suitable.

For a while I would be an animal; then a skeleton with tenants; then a thickness in the wall; finally, perhaps, only a favorable place to settle.

That would be my Low City: not a view of it, not even a lasting chamber, but a passage maintained for a time, and then left for other lives to alter in the dark.
