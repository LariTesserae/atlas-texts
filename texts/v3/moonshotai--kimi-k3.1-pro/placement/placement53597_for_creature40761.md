I would not choose the lip, though that is where a visitor first wants to stand. The height would be too large a fact to live inside. I would be smaller and more intermittent than that: the fin moth.

More precisely, I would be the plural hidden inside the keepers’ singular, *the moth*.

You would seldom find me on the condenser fins. That would be only my last, brief address. Most of my life would pass beneath the condenser bank, in the older mat where the green surface has given way to brown leaf bases and then to something almost black. I would move through dead cup-leaf and softened moss, feeding on what the garden had already ceased to use. My body would be slow, wingless, and of no interest to the service book.

The shelf’s six kilometres of altitude would mean nothing to me. A handspan of dry mineral crust would be a more serious emptiness. My country would be the distance between one reliably damp hollow and the next; my weather would arrive through overflows, repairs, and the long vibration of the pump. At the end of its cycle, the root mat would shudder faintly and the accumulated drops would fall together. Not rain from the sky, but rain released from everything above me.

A drying repair would not be a disaster I understood. It would simply alter the pace of my becoming. A warm, stable year might bring others through alongside me, though we would remain hidden in our separate layers and refuges. When the time came, I would leave the wettest places. Behind a saddle, perhaps, or in the fibrous dust beneath an unused machine bed, I would make the dry, still interval necessary for wings.

Even wings would not make me a creature of the open air.

After dark, I would emerge smoky-coloured and heavy, resembling old paint more than an animal. I would not go toward the lamps. I would go toward warmth: a mild condenser fin, a seam of metal above a pipe, stone that had not yet given the day’s heat back to the wind. The shelf would become an archipelago of temperatures. Winged flight would only make some of the crossings possible, and barely. If disturbed, I would move a short distance and settle again.

For those few nights I would not feed. I would take heat through my feet, open my wings slightly, and wait. Then I would return my eggs to the damp edge of the mat, close to the life in which I had spent almost all of mine.

I think that is why I would choose this existence. Not to be the shelf’s secret spirit—it has no need of one—but to be a life made possible by residuals: waste heat, dead tissue, an uncleared corner, a little dust held where water does not quite reach. I would depend on both attention and neglect. The station would have to be maintained well enough to remain warm, but not so thoroughly kept that every refuge became clean, dry metal.

The keepers would sometimes go a whole season without seeing me. They might wonder whether the moth had finally gone. Meanwhile, under the springy green surface, in material no one had named, the greater part of that supposed individual would still be proceeding slowly and without witness.

Then, some night during a rare shutdown, you might find me.

The turbine’s bass note would be absent. Warm rock would tick as it cooled; drops would strike separately in the settling pool; water would whisper beneath the roots. On one condenser fin, a small flake of scorched colour would shift. Its wings would open a fraction, not toward the lamps or the enormous dark beyond the lip, but toward the diminishing warmth of the metal.

If you returned the following year and saw nothing, you would not know what that meant.

The service book would only wait for someone, eventually, to write *the moth* again.
