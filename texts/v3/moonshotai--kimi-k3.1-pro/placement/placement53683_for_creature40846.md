I would begin by wanting too visible a body. I would want to be the standing rain: to hang in the white-gold air, to appear without a cloud, to be the place where the hidden order briefly caught the sun. But that exception is too narrow to make a whole life. It does not mend the chamber or close a wall; it only holds the basin within its old bounds. Let me be one of the lives that has learned to expect that holding without understanding it.

You would find me beneath a drop, not inside it. The drop would be too pure and too brief to be my world. I would be a rain treader, wingless and pinhead-small, my bristles silvered so that water could strike near me, even fling me bodily away, and usually fail to wet me through.

At my scale, the openness seen from the rim would disappear. There would be no three islands, no composition of mud and glare. There would be the film on wet silt, the faint pasture at a pool’s edge, the small differences between shine and food. A black pin would be nothing to me. Its significance would arrive, if it arrived at all, as coolness.

I would gather with others in that coolness. Above us, a drop would grow from a dust-bright point into something heavy and entire. We would wait. Often the waiting would come to nothing: the drop would slide sideways, join another, or remain suspended while the congregation thinned. There would be no failure in it. We would loosen back into the film and browse.

Sometimes the drop would fall.

The surface would open beneath us in a bright ring. The impact would break the film and lift out specks of nourishment too small to separate by sight. I would be flung outward with them, silver and dry, one fleck among many. Then I would turn back. The feast would already be thinning; the ring would already be closing; the place would already be becoming ordinary. Still, we would return.

My eggs would keep a longer patience. In dry silt they could outlast me and the field in which I had fed, and simple flooding would not necessarily wake them. They would need the repeated chill-wetting of condensation, the machine’s narrow act translated into a hatching condition. In that way, I would be evidence without being a record. A patch of silt that looked vacant at noon could be full of my kin because drops had formed there before dawn. The ground would remember, through appetite and egg, a rain that had never crossed the rim.

I would have no exemption from the rest of the basin. In drought, a hushfrog might find in my gathering enough to matter. Nor would I keep the openness, as the grazers and burrowers do. I would simply be one of the minute lives for whom the standing rain had ceased to be a mechanism and become a season—irregular, local, often fruitless, but expected in the body.

From the rim, I would vanish. Even beside the pool, you might see only a suspended point of silver, then a ring, then nothing.

But if you kept looking at the nothing, you might see us turning back.

That is where I would be: not in the pin, not in the drop, not in the brief bright impact, but just afterward, returning across the surface toward the little that was enough.
