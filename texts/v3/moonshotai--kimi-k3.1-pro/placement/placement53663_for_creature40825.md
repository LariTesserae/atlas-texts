At first, I would be the one who leaves.

Not the custodian with both hands near the white knot, attention narrowed to its cool filaments and the temper of the root bed. I would be the second member of the pair: the one standing a little behind, apparently doing nothing. Barefoot, if the mat allowed it. Watching the marsh fowl. Listening for the ticking of eels. Noticing when a frog’s voice stopped coming from the wall and did not resume.

My partner would tend the graft. I would tend the fact that we were still inside a district.

It would not be a romantic occupation. Most of it would consist of small corrections and an embarrassing readiness to interrupt. *That seam was not pale this morning. The birds have moved. Your left heel is sinking. Wait.* And, when the insects and frogs stopped together: *Enough. We are going.*

I would learn to say that without apology.

My address would be an unglamorous one: **Threshold Seven, Fourth Commons—the Carriers’ Approach**. It would sound more substantial in the civic roll than it ever looked on the ground. There would be a threshold, certainly: a pale root arch with its number worked into a thickened seam, its proportions fixed by long agreement. Beyond it, an obligation.

One passable approach between Fourth Commons and the nearest inhabited cluster, wide enough for two people carrying a third.

No house need be attached. No roof. Roofs attract proposals.

The approach itself might run beside a pool on Monday, pass beneath a new leaf corridor on Wednesday, and by the end of the week make a detour around a lane full of white down. Its roots would not be old. Its leaves would not be mine. Its continuity would lie in the fact that someone carrying weight could still use it without having to negotiate with the ground.

I would keep a measuring cord for its width and a shallow bowl for no good reason. The bowl would have survived more removals than any room I slept in. It would be slightly oval, unpleasant to drink from, and quite incapable of holding enough water to be useful. I would object whenever anyone suggested leaving it behind.

At civic meetings I would be tedious in a very particular way.

I would not defend a path because it was beautiful, familiar, or had existed since last winter. Familiarity would be a weak claim in Serein, and beauty often only meant that a root bed was preparing to do something expansive. Nor would I object to every release. If the pumps needed tissue and my present lane was the least harmful thing to stop keeping, then the lane could go.

But I would ask: *In what order?*

Which approach would exist while this one was being unmade? Where would carriers stand while the bridge neglected its middle? Would the temporary route bear weight after rain, or only in the optimistic hour immediately following its growth? If a storehouse ceased to exist, who had been using its dry interior without appearing on any register?

People would sometimes accuse me of confusing an address with a piece of ground.

I would usually be doing the opposite.

Once, the pumps beneath Fourth Commons would begin to fail. Not dramatically. The cistern would remain drinkable; the rim would keep its accustomed height. But the water beneath the paths would develop a hesitation, and the eel ticking would become difficult to interpret. Some channels would be empty. Others would merely have gone silent.

The repair would require more than the district could comfortably release.

After the usual argument, two storehouses would be designated, along with the lane then forming most of my approach. A replacement would be grown first, or nearly first; there would be an interval in which Threshold Seven survived chiefly as a measuring cord, a civic promise, and several people refusing to admit that the promise had been broken.

On the morning of the graft, I would walk the old lane before going to the knot.

It would be a good lane. Springy but firm, crossed by two shallow runnels, with a frog calling from somewhere that sounded like the inside of my chest. Already there would be moths along the condemned storehouse seam. Not many. Enough.

The fowl would arrive before the visible unmaking. For a while they would step with quick precision through tissue that still looked almost architectural. Then they, too, would withdraw.

When the stillness came, I would leave with my partner.

There would be no staying to witness the decisive moment. I would take the cord, the bowl, and whatever portable obligation had been put into my hands. I would go by the route we had agreed upon, not the shorter one that had begun to look spongy. Behind us, fibres would creak. A cistern would fill with the sound of rain falling somewhere beyond the moat. The old storehouses would pale. Nothing would flash.

Later, the air would carry that brief ammoniac sharpness, and then the greener smell of crushed tissue.

When we returned, Threshold Seven would be standing forty paces from where I had last seen it.

Same number. Same proportions. Same low, unimpressive arch.

The new approach would look obvious. That would make me suspicious of it.

I would walk it barefoot, stopping where the mat changed its note. I would measure the narrow place beside the pool, press a heel into the root junction, and wait for sap to bead. I would find one section losing coherence near its far end and make everyone dissatisfied by refusing to call the approach finished until it was corrected.

Only when two carriers could pass without thinking about the ground would I say that the address had been kept.

Behind us, the old lane would already be busy. Moth larvae in the pale thread. Fowl among the softened walls. Smaller lives entering stages of tissue whose names I would never learn. A route I had walked for years would become, for a few days, more crowded than the market.

I would feel something about that, but not enough to interfere.

Eventually, I think, the human answer would cease to be quite sufficient.

Not because Serein would transform me into a root or a knot; it would have better uses for the tissue. Keepers would change. The measuring cord would fray and be replaced. The bowl would finally be lost, or broken, or carried off by someone with fewer sentimental obligations and a genuine need for a bowl. My sleeping rooms would be unmade. My body would have its own brief civic history and then return to the general usefulness of things.

What could remain, if anyone continued the work, was the address.

Not as a spirit in the arch. Not as a memory held by the basin. Serein would require nothing so convenient.

I would be a habit with a number: the repeated act of noticing that an approach had stopped existing, and beginning again somewhere else. A pattern of attention passed between people who might disagree about everything except the width necessary for carriers. Sometimes I would fail. Roots would seal the wrong passage; a keeper would miss a silence; a meeting would defer the unglamorous obligation in favour of a roof everyone could see. For a while, Threshold Seven might be only an entry in a roll and a difficult detour through wet green.

That vulnerability would be part of what I was. An address is not a promise the ground makes. It is a promise people have to keep making in the presence of ground that has other arrangements.

On quiet days, you could look straight through me and see nothing but a commons: one bird turning its head, water shining in an interval, eels ticking too faintly to hear. Then someone would arrive using yesterday’s route, and I would briefly acquire hands, a voice, and a great deal of unwanted specificity.

*Not there. That path is being eaten. The bridge has no middle today. Come this way. Mind the soft seam.*

If you came looking for me, that is probably how it would happen. You would find a barefoot keeper beside an unremarkable opening in the green. They would tell you something you did not wish to know about the route you had intended to take, then show you another.

You would follow. The mat would spring beneath your heel and darken for a moment. A frog would call from somewhere like a wall. Behind you, the old way would be passing efficiently into other lives.

You would think you had found the approach to Fourth Commons.

For a few steps, you would also have found me.
