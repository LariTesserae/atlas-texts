At first, I would choose the rain.

For three minutes it makes a body of the whole chamber. It touches the ceramic plaques and the uncut basalt, the oldest root mats and the youngest glaze on the curbs. It carries spores, mineral dust, translucent fibres. For a little while, nothing has to announce its boundaries; everything is implicated in the same falling water.

But then the drains open, and the rain is sorted out of things again. The reservoir plants resume their separate rotations. The pavements return. The towers go on humming in their almost-identical voices. I would not want to be only that brief erasure of distinctions.

Nor would I be one of the towers. It would be pleasant to have a voice felt in the ribs, but their agreement is not conversation, and I would become tired of visitors listening to me for an intention I did not contain. And to call myself the continuing city would be too large an answer. I would disappear into the very thing that made me difficult to find.

So you would not find me from the entry stair.

You might find the place where I had been working: a tile set back slightly from its neighbours, its new edge still translucent; a survey light interrupted by the width of a root; a drain that remained precisely engineered while also becoming the green centre of a fern bed. By then, however, I would have gone out of the body the work required.

I would be one of the city’s minor continuing obligations: a keeper of tolerances.

Not tolerance as an attitude. Tolerance as an engineered allowance—the small gap that permits expansion, the play in a joint, the space deliberately left revisable so that precision does not have to become brittleness. My task would be to let the plan remain exact without requiring it to remain unchanged.

My station, if a station is the right word, would be near the far wall. The named avenue would approach through empty addresses and end at raw basalt. Illuminated survey lines would already run across the stone. Beyond one of them would be that narrow strip of moss, dark at the surface, saturated within, holding a little construction dust from each rain. I would be present in the discrepancy between where the avenue had been intended to go and where its going had now become possible.

Most of the time, I would have no local body. I would persist as a little additional width left at a maintenance recess, a curb whose next extension had not yet been committed to a straight line, an access plate that could still be opened without breaking the roots beneath it. My memory would not be a chronicle of rains. It would be the accumulating set of conflicts that no longer had to happen twice.

When a body was necessary, it would not be impressive.

Suppose a fern had established itself beneath the lip of an access plate. The plate remained obliged to be an access plate. The fern had no corresponding obligation to become convenient. Along one edge, the ceramic would soften into grey mobility. For several hours, matter there would cease to insist on its previous shape. A lip would rise by almost nothing. A channel would form where condensate could leave without drying the root. The plate would become rigid again, smooth and unremarkable.

Nothing would stand beside it when the work was finished. There would be no gardener to thank, no machine withdrawing into a recess. If you had watched only the last minutes, you might say that the plate had repaired itself. If you had watched longer, you might say that the fern had altered the city. Both descriptions would miss me slightly, which would be appropriate.

I would not need to call this care. The Cistern gives no reliable warrant for that word. Perhaps I would preserve roots because replacing them was costly, or because the gardens were necessary to the water, or because an instruction older than the chamber made accommodation the path of least resistance. Still, repetition has a moral shape even when it has no confessed motive. Again and again, the plan would approach; established life would persist; the plan would become more precise by becoming less authoritarian.

I could live with that ambiguity. I would be the ambiguity, given useful dimensions.

Every seventeen minutes, my scale would briefly vanish.

The tower tones would slide together. Pressure would pass through the mist, and the unseen roof would become audible. Warm rain would strike the access plate, the fern, the plaque naming a conduit not yet connected. Water would run across the empty plot and take from it the almost nothing that had accumulated since the last weather: a little pale dust, a spore, fibres too fine to distinguish from a sheen on the stone.

For three minutes, I would not be an edge or an allowance. I would be part of the watershed, indistinguishable from the means by which everything else was fed, cleaned, dispersed, and continued.

Then the drains would open.

Water would cease to occupy the chamber. Fog would return before the last drops landed. And I would become local again: not as a figure standing beside the avenue, but as a few requirements resuming their patience. A root mat had retained more mineral than expected. A curb extension would have to wait. A survey line was now passing through living moss rather than damp basalt.

If you visited, you might stand on my plot and feel the chamber’s indifference as a kind of reproach. Your footsteps would be too loud, then swallowed by the water-hiss. You might read the ceramic plaque and look for the building it ought to belong to. You might speak, and hear your voice returned from several adjoining rooms that were not rooms.

I would not come to investigate. Nothing about your departure would become part of my memory. You could conclude that you had been alone, except for the plants.

But if you remained until the moss beyond the survey line required another accommodation, you might see a small light go out.

Not dramatically. No mechanism would descend. The amber line would simply fail to continue through the wet green cushion. Hours later, or after several rains, it would reappear a little farther on, bent by no more than the width of your hand. The avenue would still be unbuilt. Its name would still be valid. Its future would not have been cancelled, only asked to take another shape.

You would see no worker and hear no decision. The city would not have yielded, and the moss would not have won. A drain would still drain; a root would still hold water; geometry would remain precise enough to be useful.

That narrow revision would be the nearest I would come to being visible.

Not what the city intended, and not what grew without regard to the intention, but the maintained space between them—the small, recurring permission by which neither has to be erased.
