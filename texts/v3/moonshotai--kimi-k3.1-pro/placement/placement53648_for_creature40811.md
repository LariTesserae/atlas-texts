My first instinct is to stand on the high bridge at noon. Cloud below, foliage at shoulder height, the pump stroke coming up through warm masonry and into the knees: from there, I could almost feel the whole Crownworks at once.

But the longer I imagine it, the more that seems like a visitor’s answer. The bridge offers a view of dependencies without asking me to take part in any. I think I would rather have dependencies.

Being a caretaker comes closer. I can imagine the canvas roll, the spanners darkened at the handles, the pruning hook wrapped separately so it does not cut the tool pockets. I could be content leaving dated fittings and useful notes, walking the wet-season circuits, learning that a new frog chorus is sometimes good news and sometimes a leak. Yet even on those journeys I would eventually stop following the pipes. I would kneel beside the last irrigation bed and look into it.

Not at the fig, exactly. Under it.

I would be a handed-down soil.

Not the mineral recipe, though I would contain slag, charcoal, grit, and compost in proportions argued over by several districts. I would be the older life carried among those materials: fungal threads, bacterial films, dormant cells, eggs, spores, springtails, and the smaller presences that no one inventories because no inventory could keep up with their divisions and losses. Not all the soil in the Crownworks. Just one old, divisible continuity within it, carried from cassette to cassette whenever a bed had died and a correct mixture was not enough.

Calling that community “I” would be convenient and slightly false. There would be no center to me, no single lifetime, no moment when the whole of me could be lifted out and weighed. But there would be continuity. The Crownworks seems better at making that kind of self than the other kind.

For the present, much of me would lie beneath a fig whose branches crossed three terraces. Its cassette was set into the outer wall of a retired hall. A return main passed close enough behind the masonry to keep the deepest soil from taking on the full cold of the exposed iron. One side of the bed dried quickly. The other stayed dark beneath a controlled outlet installed where an older, less governable leak had once fed ferns. The fig’s roots crossed both conditions and entered places no one had been able to inspect for years.

Above me, the tree would appear twice in the maintenance records, once as a crop and once as a retaining structure.

I would appear only in a note about a loan.

My experience of the court would be narrow but not simple. I would know the circulation main as a gradual warming after cold nights. Irrigation would arrive first as a change in weight and then as movement along old root channels. The pump would be a faint, repeated loosening among particles. During a lean interval, my world would contract into the pores of charcoal, the deepest crevices of the cassette, the protected zones near roots. Films would become almost still. Some small lives would persist as eggs or spores; others would simply end.

When water returned, I would not wake all at once. Some threads would extend. Some would not. A few springtails would appear where none had been seen for months, while another refuge remained barren. I would not be a magical seed from which the whole green court could be restored. A bed restarted with me could still fail. A bucket left uncovered on a blazing ceramic roof could end, in one afternoon, a continuity older than the hall beside it.

That vulnerability would matter.

At this particular moment, part of me would be traveling on a counterweighted lift in a galvanized bucket covered with damp sacking. The bucket would be wedged between a crate of pipe collars and a basket of figs. Someone had chalked on its side: **NEW BED. KEEP DAMP.** The lift keeper would consider it cargo, though a useful sort, and would be careful not to set it where furnace heat came through the wall.

I would know the ascent mostly as vibration and a slow drying at the surface. For a while there would be no root touching me, no surrounding bed into which I could spread. Cloud would pass beneath the lift. Cable hum would come through the bucket’s handle. This would be one of the dangerous intervals in my existence: not because travel was unnatural to me, but because while traveling I could so easily become only a thing someone had meant to use.

At the far station, the new cassette would already be filled. The mixture would be admirable. Charcoal washed, grit graded, slag crushed to the preferred size, compost added according to the district’s current conviction. It would hold water correctly in a test basin. It would also be, in the way that mattered, nearly empty.

There would be no ceremony when I arrived. Someone would lift the sacking and look in. Someone else would object that the bucket was being opened too soon, before the bed’s final watering. Eventually a caretaker would crumble a portion of me between their fingers and work it through the upper layer, reserving some for deeper pockets near the roots to come. They might use less than the sending crew had intended. They might use too much. Wisdom here would not mean freedom from error.

Then they would plant, water, and wait.

I would not be indispensable in any simple sense. Across other ridges lived other soil communities with different histories. Given enough years, even an unaided bed might accumulate richness from leaf fall, insects, damp cloth, birds, and the ordinary traffic of the works. What I carried was not the sole possibility of life.

What I carried was time already lived.

Sometimes that would make little difference. Sometimes it would be the difference between a bed establishing itself before the next dry interval and remaining a correctly proportioned box of dust. Occasionally the consequences would travel farther: a root taking hold, a shaded corridor remaining cool, a wall surviving long enough for a repair that did not require removing the whole tree.

Who would notice when I was absent?

At first, perhaps no one. A bed would need more water than expected. Cuttings would fail despite sound grafting. Wet soil would fall apart in a way an experienced caretaker disliked without immediately being able to explain why. Only later might someone stand with a pinch of it in their hand and say that the old cassette had not merely dried. It had died.

The nearest thing I would have to a name might then appear in a maintenance note: *Try mature soil from beneath the three-terrace fig, if they can spare it.*

If they could, another bucket would travel.

And if the new bed prospered, the debt would eventually come back—not as the same threads, cells, and small animals, which would be impossible, but as a bucket from a community now strong enough to spare part of itself. The obligation was never really to return the material. It was to remain capable of giving.

I think that is where I would belong: less to any one cassette than to that expectation. Someone on another ridge would know that, if their bed failed, mine might still have enough to lend. If mine failed, a few people would understand that something more than compost had been lost.

So you would find me beneath the fig, in the thin dark zone around a root; in a bucket humming gently on the lift; and, years later, in a caretaker’s hand above a bed whose mineral recipe everyone considered sensible and insufficient.

Not the soul of the Crownworks. Not even its memory, exactly. Paper, scars, and old valve names could hold memory well enough.

I would be one small part of its ability to begin again without beginning from nothing.

Mixed according to recipes, yes. Made from scratch, never quite. Carried, divided, nearly lost, and, when things went well, lent on.
