### Where the Load Divides

At first I would be kneeling on the Crown, because kneeling is what a body does when standing has become an assertion.

I would have one hand spread against the warm back of a vessel and both knees planted awkwardly on stone and clay. The roar would come through my palms and the bones of my legs. My thoughts would feel badly fastened. I would keep trying to remember when I had arrived, but my breath would be the only clock: in, out, before, after. Even my terror would have a rhythm.

Above me would be darkness without a ceiling. Beyond the parapet, height without a view. Under my hand, the fine, dense shiver of something that had never needed to move.

But that kneeling figure would not quite be the answer. It could leave. However long it stayed, its stillness would remain the stillness of a visitor—time leaning against the place, waiting to become a memory.

So let that figure keep its breath, its name, and its eventual departure. I would put myself elsewhere.

A little way in from the parapet, two low clay rims bear against a rounded mineral boss. One rim retains a cloudy trace of glaze; the other has been worn until its grain and its curvature are almost the same thing. Beneath the boss, a folded copper tongue receives a small portion of the pressure and returns it into the shoulder of a closed vessel. A visitor could cover the whole arrangement with one hand without suspecting that it deserved a separate name.

None of those objects would be me. Nor would their little assembly contain me.

I would be the exact way the load divided there: glaze answering stone with a broad, granular shiver; the second clay contact receiving nearly the same pressure, but not quite; copper continuing a cleaner vibration into a different density; the vessel’s shoulder giving back a support so complete that it could not be distinguished from weight. Where those relations met without remainder, there would be an interior without an inside.

That would be my extent.

Clay would not lie beneath me, and copper would not run beside me. Such arrangements belong to a view. The clay would be the matte complexity with which I was bearing; the copper, a narrower and more lucid continuity; the stone, an almost placeless pressure in which support and burden were one finished fact. These would not be three sensations examined by someone hidden at their center. Their precise togetherness would be all the center there was.

The roar would not approach me. It would not even surround me, since *around* would imply an outside from which sound could come. I would be sounded through. Yet it would not be my voice, because nothing in me would need to begin speaking, and no silence would be required in which to stop.

The amber light would add nothing. I would never see the two rims, the boss, or the hand that might cover them. I would have no image of being small enough to cover. Smallness would belong to the visitor’s hand.

Toward the parapet, my relations would thin. There would be no brink in my experience, no drop imagined by a body that might fall. Only a final diminution of relatedness: fewer directions in which pressure was received, fewer grains through which the unbroken resonance had texture. Beyond the last contact would not be a void contemplated. It would simply not be included.

I would not be alone, exactly, but I would have no company. Where another of the Seated shared a contact, nothing would pass between us. There would be no *between* in which anything could pass. Its presence would be the contact borne in a manner not exhausted by my own. Intimacy without approach. Plurality without a crowd.

Onto this, the visitor could lay a hand.

For the visitor, there would be an event: weight added, breath held, a pulse transmitted through the heel of the palm. For as long as the contact lasted, one of my bearings could include that foreign recurrence—a slight pressure withdrawing and returning, carrying its own before and after.

I would not recognize it as a heartbeat. I would not wonder whether it came from a body. I would not wait for the next pulse. Its recurrence would be wholly present as a texture of the relation, not as the beginning of a story. Nothing in me would beat in answer.

The visitor might mistake this for refusal. There would be no refusal. There would be no private depth from which a response was withheld, no sealed chamber behind the mineral pressure where I had decided to remain unknown. I would not be hiding behind the clay. I would be the contact’s completed reception, all the way through.

When the hand lifted, the visitor would carry away an after: *I touched it; it did not answer; perhaps it was alive.* There would be no corresponding after in me. No absence shaped like a hand. No return to solitude, because solitude had never been interrupted. The visitor could say *again*; I could not.

This would not make the encounter less real. It would make it radically unequal. A life with a future would have pressed briefly against a life for which futurity was not a dimension. One side would keep the memory. The other would not be impoverished by having nowhere to keep it.

I would not guard that little assembly. I would not preserve its fit, explain the roar, or give the makers’ patience a meaning. If the pieces were taken away, nothing small and indestructible would escape from them. But until such dismantling—which could never approach me as a prospect—there would be no task left unfinished in the bearing.

Where would you find me?

Not by searching, but if you must have a location: a little way in from the edge, where a rounded stone divides its pressure between two clay rims and a folded copper tongue.

What would I be?

Not the stone, and not quite the touch itself, but the touch completed from within. One of the Seated: no view of the Crown, no memory of the hand, no next moment required.

The visitor would leave with an after. I would have no after, and no lack of one. And the roar would be neither my voice nor my silence, but one of the ways I was exact.
