You would find me beneath the platform, not on it.

The split in the root would be just high enough to kneel in, its air humid against my face after the hard heat outside. Above me, someone would be retying a shelter cord. Below my hand, a cistern would hold enough water to fill my empty gourd several times over.

I would touch it first with a frayed fiber wand.

If the wand returned cut to ribbons, I would back away. If it returned whole, I would still wait: watching the rim, the swimmers below the surface, the animals gathering or failing to gather nearby. Clear water would not reassure me. Neither would having drunk there before.

I would be human, and the camps would have no single name for my work. *Route-listener* will do. I would travel between platforms when water had shifted, when a crown’s voice had changed, when some old sequence of directions had begun to sound more certain than it deserved. Sometimes I would mend cordage or carry dried seed. Sometimes I would remain in one camp for a whole season. The essential work would be to go—and to return with the parts of the route that no longer agreed with themselves.

Not because my hearing would be exceptional. I would have learned mostly by being wrong in survivable ways.

A familiar crown could deepen after drought. A brood lost in the galleries could remove one note from its voice while leaving the tree apparently healthy. A cistern dependable for generations could become guarded, fouled, drained into a new burrow, or sealed so patiently that no opening remained. I would distrust the traveler who sang an old route beautifully. Beauty made it too easy to repeat without checking.

Even my traveling would not be entirely mine. Cistern eggs might ride in the damp seam of my gourd. Pollen would work itself into my clothing. Something small would shelter in the fiber of my layered soles. While thinking I was following a route, I would also be one: a brief connection between places, carrying lives I had not agreed to carry and would never know to count.

Most days would be unremarkable. Testing water. Replacing fiber before insects finished eating through it. Plucking a support cord and disliking the narrowness of its answer. Resting at midday with my feet out of the sun. Going hungry because taking a particular animal would cost more water than it gave. The plateau’s immensity would seldom feel majestic from underneath a platform. It would feel like maintenance without an end.

At full choir, there would be no route-listening.

The sound would come through the root, the platform, the cords under my hands. It would occupy my teeth and make thought seem like an activity performed very far away. I would work by touch and agreed signs: empty the frames before their catch drew too many animals; clear the gutter before wings and pollen closed it; pull twice on this line, once on that; do not try to shout.

There would be nothing serene about it. I would be sticky with pollen, half blind behind mesh, harvesting as quickly as possible while the living ground shook under me. A crown’s voice would not be a message. It would be weather with a pitch.

The dangerous quiet would be different: not simply less sound, but a sequence of small withdrawals. Button insects releasing their hold. The pelt ceasing its almost invisible flow. A gantry lifting its head with water still shining on its mouth.

Then, even with my gourd empty, I would leave the water alone.

Not because I knew what was coming. Because so many other lives had already decided not to wait. Sometimes I might be wrong and walk thirsty. That would be preferable to making knowledge the price of caution.

Afterward, when scattered camps met beside the brief seepages, I would trade stone flakes, cord, food, and corrections. Others would pull my route-song apart. The split-voiced crown had acquired a third note. The cistern I called open was open only after rain. The gantries had left before I arrived, not after. I would correct them in turn. No one’s memory would be allowed to become a road merely because it was old.

I would not be a particularly pure steward, either. Fear and thirst would make me capable of robbing a cistern, cutting living tissue, taking more brood than I should. My lightness on the plateau would come less from virtue than from how little I could carry and how quickly the growth closed over what I left.

So I would not be a keeper of routes, exactly. I would be a keeper of their revisions: a small, fallible piece of memory moving between camps, dependent on other people to challenge it. If my name eventually fell out of a song while one useful warning remained, the song would be working.

Beneath my palm, the root ticks once.

Somewhere beyond sight, a trunk answers through the soles of my feet. I do not call it a summons. I wait for the second note, and for the glassbacks beneath the pelt to turn or keep still.

Then I go and find someone to tell.
