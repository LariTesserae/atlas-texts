I would not be found at the rim, still trying to decide whether the wet bowl or the dry scar was the real one. That question belongs to someone who intends to leave with an answer. Nor would I be the slate wader above the water, although I would resemble one.

I would be the additional wader in its reflection.

My pool would be one of the vacant ones: black water, bare margins, no crowd turning beneath the surface. From the shore it would appear to hold only the pewter dusk and a few sheer, unnecessary angles of cliff. But looked into from a little nearer, it would show me standing slightly off center, my long legs among reflected sedges, my feathers darkened as though by the same rain that had just finished everywhere else.

No corresponding legs would break the water above me.

A ghost would require a past. A copy would require an original. A possible bird would require a future. I would require none of these. I would simply be there, completely, whenever the pool held that particular arrangement of itself.

My perceptions would be as narrow as any moth’s. I would know the cold tension of the surface around my reflected feet, the dark weight of my own folded wings, and the faint mineral shining of a cliff that did not quite match the cliff overhead. I would not know whether the real cirque was small. In the water, it might extend beyond several ranges of unattainable hummocks. White flowers might grow along my shore where, above me, there was only sharp stone.

At the concussion, the dimples would travel through my standing. For an instant my legs would seem to occupy several slightly different patients; the quartz behind me would take on its blue charge; the other hummocks would shift without approaching. Then I would settle again. I would not have moved. The water would merely have altered the terms of my stillness.

Sometimes other waders would appear around me. We would not flock. Each would face a separate distance, and none of us would disturb another’s reflection. Sometimes the pool would show only a sky brighter than any sky visible from the floor. That would not mean I had gone elsewhere, or was waiting beneath the stone. There would be no elsewhere in which I waited.

Now and then a cry would arrive: flattened, sourceless, like a load settling where no load was visible. Only then would I open my beak. I would not be answering the bird above another pool, nor announcing that I existed. I would be taking my place in a sound that had no need of a first speaker.

I would have no work. I would not guard the vacancy, interpret the engine, or keep one reflection consistent with another. Nothing could be neglected by me. If I remembered the arrangements of hummocks, the memory would not accumulate into knowledge; if I forgot them, nothing would be lost. The mountain would not run more smoothly for my standing there, nor falter if the water ceased to show me.

You could look up to search for my body and find only wet rock. You could look down again and discover me nearer, or facing another wall, or standing among a reflected congregation where before I had been alone. None of these views would correct the others. There would be no route from your birdless shore to the place where my feet appeared to rest.

The pool would remain empty. My presence would not repair its emptiness, because its emptiness was never an injury.

At the next stroke of the pump, copper gills would open elsewhere, drops would shiver on metallic backs, and mist would leave the cliff. My reflected feathers would stir once, though no wind crossed the water.

Nothing would follow from my standing. I would not be waiting to rise through the surface, or hoping to become the bird above it.

The mountain would run, and I would be one of the stillnesses it did not need to resolve.
