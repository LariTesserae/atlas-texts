At first I imagine myself on the maintenance route, because that is where a human body can most easily be inserted: lamp, ear protection, wet boots, a hand lifted in signal. I would clear an intake screen, read a gauge, and go home. Even after years of shifts, my knowledge would be arranged around jobs and access. The basin would begin wherever my task ended.

I would not be a bat either. The western roost would be home, but every hunger would lead me out through the fissures and over the plateau. My life would be stretched between two worlds.

I think I would rather be one of the creatures for which a small wet surface is not an impoverished world, but the whole of one.

So: a crab.

A small crab, pale except where iron and mineral dust had stained my shell, living near the end of the attempted route along the Hot Fault. My landmarks would be the gauge and the ceramic insulator on the wall. People had left them there in preparation for something. I would live in the chamber where that preparation stopped.

My territory would occupy only the few square metres below them: the underside of a collapse block, a fan of warm gravel, two mineral rims, and the margin of the pool beyond. A person might dismiss it as an awkward patch of ground between one footing and the next. I would know it as several countries.

The block would be shelter, its underside filmed with water but never touched by the strongest flow. The older rim would be hard and reliable, though sharp along its crystals. The newer one would bear my weight and little more; I would cross it slowly, testing the hollow crust, aware of the dark water beneath. Behind the rims would be a pocket of silt crowded with amphipods. Along the warm seep, cream and rust-coloured films would spread and recover. The overflow lip would be another kind of pasture altogether, wetter, brighter with movement, frequently revised.

Much of my knowledge would come through my legs.

The large pumps would not seem to occupy any particular place. They would be a continuous pressure in rock, water, and shell, a condition under which all nearer events occurred. The loose pipe would knock without direction. A drip striking the pool would be close weather. Gravel shifting under my own weight would be immediate and useful. If people ever approached with a lamp, I would probably know them first as an irregular violence in the ground. By the time their light reached the pool, I would be beneath the block, folded into its shadow, waiting for the vibration to become distant again.

My life would be made of things too small for a survey to record. Film scraped from a warm face of stone. Something organic investigated and then carried a short distance. A fish turning beyond the rim. A sudden retreat when the crust ticked underfoot. Long intervals in which nothing appeared to happen, though the film was recovering and the silt was being worked and water was finding its way through another crystal space.

Sometimes there would be the greater vulnerability of moulting. I would withdraw beneath the block and wait, soft and provisional, while the abandoned shape of me remained outside on a ledge: legs, claws, the exact emptied architecture of a crab. Seepage would find it. First its surface would lose a little of its delicacy. Then crystals would gather in the joints. Given enough undisturbed time, the water would begin to glaze it into the ledge.

The basin’s slow construction would not be an abstract fact to me. It would alter the paths I could take.

A seep would migrate, and one feeding surface would become poor while another, a short distance away, began to flourish. A rim would thicken until the overflow crossed at a lower point. Sediment would collect where there had been open water. A familiar shelter would become wetter, or smaller, or joined by new stone to the block beside it. I would adjust by centimetres. My world would remain almost the same size, but it would not remain the same world.

I would never know the twelve kilometres of maintained routes, or the regional extent of the connected chambers. I would know the block’s underside, the dependable rim, the fragile crossing, the place where the current deposited food, and the deep margin into which I could retreat. That would not be a reduced version of the basin. It would be the basin at the scale on which I could live it.

Not an explorer, then, and not a keeper. Just one of its deliberate pedestrians, picking my way across ground that would bear a crab but not a boot.

And when the water changed the shore, I would move a little way along it. To the gauge, almost nothing would have happened. To me, the chamber would have acquired a new country.

Behind me, under a film of water so slight it might seem merely to shine, my old moult would be acquiring its first transparent coat of stone—not a monument, just a little more ground waiting to happen.
