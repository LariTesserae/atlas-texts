I would arrive with a notebook and the wrong idea of a map.

I would be sent, perhaps, to decide whether the installation could be made serviceable again. *Serviceable* would already be a dangerous word. It would assume that the short circuit was a failure, the cold outfall an inefficiency, the resident’s films something to be removed before the real condition of the works could be assessed.

At first I would pace the banks and number the pipes. I would draw the pump house, the plinths, the sluice frame, the visible culverts. The pencil would remain good at things that kept their positions and increasingly poor at everything else. Pools would need dotted outlines. Depths taken in the morning would have to be marked with the hour. By afternoon some of them would no longer be depths, only earlier opinions about where the bottom had been.

The gauge would stay at its stained notch. My measurements would be very good at showing that nothing had changed, and much less good at describing what had happened.

Eventually I would stop trying to face the resident from one place. I would sit instead on the eastern side, near the outfall, where the clean water has twenty paces of concrete ditch in which to be an outflow before it becomes a return. That would be my place: not at the supposed head in the pump house, and not above the central silt waiting for a body to rise, but at the point where leaving is already on its way back.

One afternoon I might knock on a pipe.

Not to summon anything. Only to test whether the sound carried through the standing metal or died in the scale. Three knocks, perhaps, because a hand likes a pattern even when the mind knows better. The iron would take the warmth from my knuckles. Nothing would happen, or nothing would happen soon enough to satisfy the part of me that still expected cause and effect to stand close together.

Later there might be two ticks from across the basin and, after a longer pause, a third from somewhere near the sluice. Or a pool might dimple without any sound. Or the whole event might remain indistinguishable from the Mere’s ordinary activity. I would write down the times and resist drawing an arrow between them.

But if anything of me entered the resident’s world, I think it would be that: not my intention, not a message, but a small derangement of timing.

For a while, I would be a visiting rhythm.

I would not travel intact around the circuit, like a parcel of water carrying my name. I would have no single location after the knocking stopped. My existence there would be a slight difference in what was likely to happen next: a delay where pressure had previously passed cleanly, an extra contraction in a seam of clay, a brief preference for one route through the scale over another. Sometimes those differences might coincide strongly enough to borrow an edge. Two ticks and a third. Three fluted veins at a grille. A mud rim whose final rib rose just after the others had begun to soften.

Those would not be portraits of me. They would be the nearest I came to visibility.

Nor would the rhythm remember me in any way I could comfortably call recognition. If I returned and knocked again, there would be no visitor identified, no acquaintance resumed. There would only be another obstruction, another pressure, another pattern entering the day’s circulation. At most, an old passage might be a little easier to take. My continuity would not be something kept intact. It would be something made fractionally more probable.

That would be enough. Like an arch in the silt, it could be finished without becoming permanent.

I would have to be careful not to mistake being taken up for being welcomed. The Mere would have no use for my company, and no obligation to preserve the pattern by which I had briefly imagined myself present. Most likely it would alter the rhythm before dispersing it. There would be no final answer, only a point beyond which I could no longer honestly separate my contribution from everything else underway.

My more consequential self would remain on the bank, with the notebook.

That self would learn not to clear a grille to improve the view, not to put a hand into a vent to discover how much pressure lay behind it, not to wipe away slime merely because the old maintenance drawings showed clean metal. At the end of the survey, my report might be very dull: no recommissioning; no scouring; no drying or regrading of the basin; no works recommended.

It would not make me the resident’s guardian. A sheet of official prose could only delay efficiency for a while. It could not guarantee the next drought, the next ownership, the next person with a better plan. My contribution would be smaller than preservation. It would be a pause in human usefulness, allowing neglect to continue its older, less conclusive work.

After that, I could return without needing to monitor anything. I would sit by the outfall with the paper cockling in the damp and listen to the pump-house roof dripping into its own floor. A drop would fall. A valve might tick. The nearest runnel might draw back while I was still deciding whether I had heard anything at all.

When I stood to leave, my boot would make one more resistance. Any examination of it could happen behind me, or across the water, or after I had gone.

So I would be there in two ways, only one of them visible: a visitor on the eastern bank, and perhaps, for a little longer, a faint irregularity in the circuit. Not a second resident. Not an interpreter. Not a voice waiting for its reply.

Just a rhythm the Mere had not yet worn away—and then only the Mere, continuing without any need to notice that I had gone.
