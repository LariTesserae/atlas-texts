At first, you would not find me at all.

You would find a shallow basin whitening after sunrise, a slateback track already softening at the edges, perhaps a few beetle shells beneath a ledge. The gravel would look no more promising than any other gravel. I would be beneath it: a hard rain-beetle egg lodged in the crust, indistinguishable from a dark mineral grain unless you knew exactly what you were looking for—and perhaps not even then.

I would not be one of the eggs that wakes at the first wetting.

Above me, serein might darken a few hectares of stone. Water might whisper for several minutes through channels older than the wayhouse, bringing up the smell of limestone and crushed stems. Elsewhere in the dust, eggs would open. Beetles would emerge in glittering hundreds, then thousands. The first rust-red filaments would begin to unfold. Still I would remain unchanged.

There would be no vigil in this, and no wisdom. I would not dream of rain or grow impatient with the sky. My waiting would be a matter of shell, chemistry, and conditions not yet sufficient. A brief wetting would not be enough. Water would have to stand in mud. Until then, even a beautiful rain could pass directly over me and leave the future closed.

The devices would mean nothing to me. A white pin, a buried ring, a seamless sill—these would belong to a geology too remote to concern anything my size. My world would not be divided into devices and basins, but into finer distinctions: dust merely dampened, dust wetted through, mud briefly occupied by water. The difference between the news of rain and enough rain.

When my condition was finally met, I would hatch. Even then, I might not emerge.

I could wait beneath the crust while the coolest water vanished and the first slatebacks came trampling through the wettest ground. Their footfalls would reach me as deep disturbances, each one altering the load on the stone. Only when the surface had begun to dry would I push upward.

I would come out after the rain had stopped.

The veil would be gone from the air. Somewhere else, kites might still be gathering beneath a cloudless sky, but here the shower would already be becoming a stain, a scent, a set of tracks. The stone would not yet be dangerous to touch. My dark back would gather warmth from the returning sun. I would drink from a film so thin it held no reflection.

For that short interval, I would cease to be a reserve. I would be entirely present.

To a rain-reader watching from a ridge, I would be part of a brief dark stippling at the basin’s edge. To me, the ridge would scarcely exist. There would be wet gravel underfoot, the mineral taste of water, the vibrations of animals much larger than my whole visible world. Winged things would rise in short, glittering columns. Shadows would drop through them. Beetles around me would vanish into cracks or beneath discarded shells as the stone warmed.

I would have an afternoon in which to drink, mate, and return the unspent portion of life to the ground.

The generation I joined would not make a single wager. Some eggs would wake after the next slight shower. Some would require standing water, as I had. Some would remain hard through rains that brought the whole visible basin teeming to life. Nothing about my one successful afternoon would make me eager to see that changed.

By the second day, you might find only a bronze flush, a few husks, and an unusual abundance of tracks. You could stand there in the hardening light and call the basin empty. You would be almost right.

But beneath the crust, life would still be declining to agree with itself about when to begin.

That would be my place in the Serein Uplands: one dark back in the cool hour after rain, and then part of the basin’s refusal to spend itself all at once. Not a prophecy. Not a promise. Only a reserve, so that even a perfect rain could not consume the whole future.
