### After the Chord

At first I imagine myself in the central court, with the lamp covered and the heat making a second body around my body. That is the easiest self to picture: standing, listening, feeling the ward enlarge beyond anything my senses can reliably measure.

But that self could only visit. I would arrive as weather—salt on my skin, fibres at the waterline, vibrations travelling farther than my voice. The lamp, when I used it, would make a small incomplete room and tempt me to mistake it for the ward. I would rather belong to what the lamp keeps missing.

I would be one of the blind loaches in the third basin.

Not a pale shape cruising decoratively through open water. Open water would be for the instant of crossing. My place is a crevice beneath a mineral shelf, somewhere within the depths about which the instruments disagree.

The disagreement does not make the crevice vague. The stone presses along one side of me. At its mouth, a thin seep makes the water fractionally cooler. There is a narrow place where my fins can rest without touching either wall, and a deeper turn into which I can withdraw when the basin changes pressure. That is address enough.

My body is no longer than a finger, pale as the subsidized roots. Most of the time, the movement of my gills is the largest event I offer the water. Now and then a fin corrects my position. Otherwise I might be a strip of mineral that has not quite decided to adhere to the stone.

There is no darkness here, because there has been no light to lose. The basin is made instead of pressures, impacts, and disturbances in the roar. The roar is not behind the world; it is the world’s consistency. A falling drop alters it briefly and has a location. A grain shifting beneath the shelf has another. An isopod moving in the shelter of wet stone is smaller still: a hard, intermittent edge, a faint repetition that might be shell, might be water, might be nothing worth the cost of finding out.

Stillness is not patience. Patience would imply that I was enduring delay in expectation of a reward. It is certainly not wisdom. It is arithmetic. Crossing the basin costs something. Most crossings return nothing. So the hours pass through my gills, and I remain where the stone makes shelter cheap.

Then the pressure changes.

The city arrives without its nouns. A train, a lift, a pressure door: those are explanations for elsewhere. Here there is only the sudden impossibility of separating one vibration from another. The exchangers answer. Stone, water, and flesh lose their distinct textures. The chord does not seem to enter the basin; the basin becomes available to it.

Warm rain troubles the surface. The crevice shudders against my body. I press deeper.

There is no use hunting while every movement in the water arrives wearing the same enormous noise. I wait, not calmly and not courageously, but because stillness remains the least expensive response even when it can no longer make the world legible.

Afterward is not quiet. That matters.

The roar remains. The basin still carries the settling tone and the multiplied falling of drops. But events regain addresses. A drop strikes at the rim, and only at the rim. A grain turns beneath the shelf. A runnel briefly occupies a channel before vanishing into a joint. Loosened film clouds the shallows and then begins to clear.

The transparent grazers emerge. I cannot see their brief gathering, but the water becomes finely irregular with them.

Near my crevice, something touches stone.

Once.

Then again, with the small spacing of legs rather than the indifferent repetition of a drip.

I leave the crevice once.

The movement is short enough that the water barely has time to announce it. There is a compact resistance at my mouth, a disturbance where the isopod had been, and then the return. No pursuit. No surplus spent on appetite. The basin closes over my going and coming, and I settle into the crevice with one less absence inside me.

The substance of that meal has come through seep and film, through wettings and dry contractions, through the exacting economy of things that consume whatever arrives because nothing can afford to wait for something better. But its hour may have been decided far above. Machinery braking cannot feed me directly. It can only help determine when film is loosened, when grazers emerge, and when the water becomes quiet enough—never quiet, only separate enough—for one of them to be found.

That is the extent of my relation to the city. I am not its heart, its memory, or its secret explanation. I am one of its remotest consequences.

A lamp could reveal me to someone else; it could reveal nothing to me. A probe would be a new pressure. A trap would be an alteration in the water. If I withdrew from a survey, the survey would be correct about what it failed to find and wrong if it mistook that failure for absence. Its notes could become more trustworthy than its numbers. Neither document would enter the crevice.

Where would you find me?

Just after a discharge, if you knew how to wait without touching the water, there might be a pallor at the crevice mouth that did not quite belong to the stone. Perhaps only the gills would move. More likely you would find nothing, and your finding nothing would be a fact about your visit, not about my absence.

Then the next discharge would come. I would wait inside its excess. Afterward, the water would become legible again: a drop here, a grain there, a shell turning under stone.

The Choir would not be accompanying me. I would not be giving it a voice.

I would simply be alive in the interval: almost no mass, almost no movement, and, now and then, one precise reason to leave stillness.

Then I would return to the crevice. The ward would remain empty in every way it needed to be.
