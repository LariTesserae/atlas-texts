At noon, the census would get me wrong.

It would put down: *deepest cleft; two trees, perhaps three; many stems; no canopy; little viable seed.* Then it would move on, because a clear pool or a dark patch of sediment would seem more likely to conceal a life.

I would be in that cleft, a little back from the worst of the fissure heat, where warm grit and cooler rock meet below the surface. And I would be the uncertainty in the number.

One plant repeatedly divided and renewed, or several plants sharing an obscured history below the stone: my location would be clearer than my pronoun. Above ground, I would invite counting. Below it, I would refuse.

A stem would lean hard away from a hot fracture. A dead section would still point toward a seep that no longer ran there. Three living shoots would rise from the cool side of what appeared, from above, to be separate wood. To stand beside me in clear weather would be to mistake my shape for damage. It would be damage, but not only damage. Each crooked stem would be a compromise that had survived long enough to become wood.

The wind would have made almost nothing of me. Inside the boundary it is too weak to sculpt a tree. Heat would do the sculpting, and water its uneven return.

On clear days, parts of me would seem stupidly placed. A root would end against bare stone. A stem would bend around nothing. A whole section would have died in a place now no hotter than the rest of the cleft. The basin often looks like that in fragments: behavior without the structure that once compelled it.

But when cloud pressed against the summit, the seeps would quicken. Condensation would gather along lines that did not otherwise exist. Threads of water would pass through the workroom-sized volume at the basin’s center, and suspended runnels would turn their patient corners. For a few minutes, my apparent mistakes would acquire their accompanying geometry.

A bend would meet a line of droplets. A barren turn would stand at the foot of climbing water. Dead wood would mark a route that no longer returned often enough to sustain it. Nothing about me would straighten, but I would become easier to read.

I would not have grown around rooms. I would have grown around repetitions. That is why the invisible house could vanish without making me homeless.

The click would not summon me. I would know the cycle less by that small sound than by its consequences: moisture gathering at the roots, then weight arriving all at once. Release rain would strike the stems, drive grit against an exposed root, enter old bark, and carry sediment into some cracks while stripping it from others. One part of me might be fed as another was undermined. There would be no moral in it. The rain would simply be the weather that reached, however briefly, all the separate conditions in which I lived.

Afterward, the water would resume its appointed routes. I would remain crooked.

Because I would set little viable seed, most of my future would arrive as resprouting. That would make *I* an awkward word. A new stem from an old root mass is not quite a child and not simply a continuation. If a young fracture divided the roots, the two sides might begin to wet and dry according to different local chances. Would I then be two? Perhaps. The question could remain unanswered without preventing growth.

That, I think, is why I would be this being, rather than the bird with its exits or the water with its instructions. I would persist without being required to remain singular.

And I would not merely endure the basin. My roots would widen the fractures they followed. Dead tissue would catch in a rill and hold it for a moment longer than the system intended. A little silt would settle behind that obstruction. A fallen stem would shelter damp grit through one clear morning, allowing something green to survive until the next cloud. None of this would redirect the climate or repair the artifacts. The water would still find its invisible corners. But it would find them above a floor fractionally less bare.

I would not be rebuilding the installation. I would be helping build the mountain around its habits.

At noon, you would still see only two or three stunted trees. On a cloud-pressed day, you might briefly understand why their shape was difficult to separate from the water’s. Then would come the click, the dense rain, and the disappearance of every suspended surface.

I would remain in the deepest cleft, miscounted but not misplaced.

Not the keeper of the Held Rain. Not a ghost furnished into its rooms. I would be the basin beginning to answer its instructions in wood: one plant learning how to be several, or several learning how to be one.
