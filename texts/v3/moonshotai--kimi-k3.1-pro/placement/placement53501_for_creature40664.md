### The Late Return

At first I would look for myself on the rim, or in a wright: somewhere with a view, or somewhere with an outline.

Both would be a visitor’s choices. The rim makes the plateau seem like one thing. A wright makes an inhabitant seem briefly, reassuringly separate. But a wright is only the shape an interflow gives to work it cannot do with water, and the view from the rim would mean nothing to the body beneath the ash.

I think I would be a small interflow below the town, in the overflow from a single washing court. Not the body beneath a whole district, and not one of the great neighbors whose adjustments could alter gardens and conduits. My connected wetness would occupy a narrow lens of ash between dry basalt, extending back beneath a wall and down toward the marsh.

At the surface, you would find only a charcoal-green slipperiness under a tilted plate. It would dry a little at its edge on hot afternoons. After rain it would thicken, though not enough to make itself conspicuous. The rest of me would lie behind it: pale cords in the ash, finer branches in pores that held water long after the open channel had emptied.

The town would have no plan for me, and no skyline. It would be a sequence of arrivals.

Rain from the exposed shelf would come quickly, clean and hard in its first pressure. Water that had passed through the grove above would arrive later, softened by its delay and carrying the faint organic burden of leaves. The washing court would be a broad, brief disturbance followed by dripping. The covered tank would be a coolness that changed slowly. When mixed water came down from the cistern after a congregation, I might know that something had been altered above before I knew which garden would receive more overflow or which outlet had been permitted to narrow.

None of this would feel like news arranged for my attention. It would be the weather of my body.

My particular character would be delay.

The fine ash around me would hold water closely. After a storm, the neighboring seeps would quicken, spill, and begin returning to their ordinary rates while I was still taking in what had arrived. Then, when their outlets had settled, mine would continue a little longer. Not abundantly. A thread at the plate’s underside; an occasional drop; a seepage into the basin below that remained after the more audible flows had finished.

This would not be an improvement on the other interflows. It would simply be the pattern I tended to preserve. My late flow would keep one narrow fern bed damp after the adjacent rock had dried. Fine sediment would have time to settle in my basin rather than being carried immediately into the marsh. If another body knew me at all, it would be by the pressure that continued when the others had finished.

Once, perhaps, I would need a wright.

The occasion would be very small. A channel liner above my outlet might settle until part of the overflow ran beneath it instead of over it. For a while, seepage alone could manage the consequences. I could loosen the accumulating sediment and let successive pulses carry it away. But eventually the water would begin to work into ash that could not be reached without making the damage worse. The liner would have to be raised, and water cannot raise a stone by being patient with it.

After enough wet seasons, a pale felting would gather at the edge of the court. It would draw ash and fiber into itself so slowly that a visitor could mistake the whole process for a stain spreading around the drain. Limbs would cord out of it only when they were needed.

The wright would not be a little person sent out with instructions. It would be the part of me that could exert force where seepage had lost its usefulness.

Through it, I would know the court differently. Not as open ground, but as a field of possible anchorages. A basalt slab would be its weight, the direction in which that weight could safely be shared, and the vibration returned through its fractures. Dry wind would not be weather to observe; it would be a withdrawal beginning at the wright’s exposed tips.

The work might take three clouded mornings. Brace against the court. Tighten around the liner. Shift it by less than a finger’s breadth. Set a thin wedge beneath one corner. Let a little water pass, then read the new vibration and the place where dampness gathered.

When the overflow ran over the pale stone again, the work would be finished.

The wright would return to the tank and slacken there. Its green tissue would dull; its cords would separate; the ash of its core would settle among the other fine sediments. There would be no story retained of three mornings in the open. The repair would remain as an absence of resistance, a difficulty no longer encountered. Between building seasons I would not be waiting for that brief body to return. It had never been the whole of my life.

I might once have been continuous with another interflow. If so, I do not think separation would remain a wound to be repaired. Should water bring us into contact again, we might recognize something familiar in each other’s responses and still remain neighbors. My delay would be mine by then. So would the fern bed, the basin’s accumulation of fine sediment, and the unremarkable liner that no longer needed attention.

From the rim, you would not find any of this. Even from the washing court, you would see only wet stone and an overflow doing what overflow ought to do.

To find where I was, you would have to come down after rain and wait beside the little basin at the marsh margin. The larger outlets would answer first. Channels would drip; tanks would settle; drops would fall at widening intervals. Then would come the deep, separate silence that makes the town appear empty again.

My seep would still be arriving.

It would not announce an inhabitant. It might not even break the reflection, except when enough water gathered beneath the plate to release one drop. The basin would rise by almost nothing. Then it, too, would begin its return.

That is where you would find me: not in a figure at the outlet, but in the interval itself—the small, late persistence by which water that had passed through the town was received, slowed, and given back until the basin returned to its dark mark.
