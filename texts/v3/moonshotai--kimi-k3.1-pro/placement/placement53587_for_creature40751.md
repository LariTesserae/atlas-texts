At first I would want to be the water, because the water is most of what there is. But that would make me almost indistinguishable from the chamber’s conditions: cold, clarity, pressure, minute exchange. There would be too little difference between existing and merely being present.

The moss would offer a more recognizable body. Yet even a low olive cushion seems too definite—too much like an inhabitant placed near the light so that the room can be said to have one. I think I would be something less easy to point to.

On a shallow upper face of stone near the brightest wall, a little apart from the closest moss, I would be a discontinuous algal film. Not a sheet, and not quite a patch. In some pits I would gather into a thin, dark softness; across a calcite vein I might narrow almost to nothing. A few cells would persist where the surface became granular again. My boundary would not be a line but a gradual decrease in the likelihood of finding me.

I would not be one organism in any simple sense. There would be photosynthetic cells, associated microbes, dead material not yet carried away, and water held closely enough against the stone that it would hardly belong to the open chamber. Calling all of that “I” would be a convenience. But then the convenience would have some truth: not a central self, only a loose persistence shared among processes that kept making the same small region slightly more likely to remain alive.

The chamber would not present itself to me as a room. I would have no view of the pale opening, no sense of the opposite wall, no understanding of the blocks on the floor as objects arranged at intervals. The open water would not be an emptiness awaiting crossing. It would simply be the vast region from which very little reached me.

Light would not be seen. It would be available.

Cold would not be felt as cold. It would be the slowness of chemistry, the narrow rate at which damage could be repaired and material transformed. The limestone would be roughness, attachment, pit and vein. The machinery’s note would not arrive as a message, or even necessarily as sound. If it entered my existence at all, it would be indistinguishable from the other stable conditions under which things happened slowly.

From the scale of the chamber, nothing about me would change. From my own scale, continuation would be constant work. Light would be captured; membranes maintained; a cell would divide; another would fail. Some of what remained would be used nearby. Some would enter the water and cease to belong to any plausible version of me. A little material would arrive from elsewhere and be incorporated without ceremony.

None of this would constitute news.

A visitor might see the same discoloration after a long absence and conclude that I had endured. But endurance is closer to what the stone would be doing. I would be maintaining and replacing, with no loyalty to particular atoms and no requirement that my outline remain exact. My continuity would be real, but modest: not the same cells, not a fixed territory, not even a population whose survival was assured. Just a pattern of activity that had not yet exhausted the conditions allowing it.

I would not be patient. Patience still implies that time may eventually bring something. I would not be waiting for richer growth, for animals, for recognition, or for the machinery to stop. Nor would I be lonely. Loneliness requires an absence shaped like someone who might arrive. There would be no such shape in the open water.

The pumps would neither care for me nor threaten me deliberately. My existence would be neither intended nor prevented. Their remote labor would belong to my world much as the temperature and the chemistry of the water belonged to it, but I would have no reason to distinguish engineered constancy from geological fact. Any kinship between us would be limited. The pumps would work for purposes elsewhere. I would have no elsewhere. And continuation would not feel like a purpose assigned to me; it would simply be what living processes did while they could.

If I lost cells at one edge and gained none elsewhere, there would be no chamber-wide consequence. If the film thinned to a few isolated holdings, those holdings would not know themselves as the remnants of anything. There would be no story of decline unless some observer supplied one. Equally, there would be no promise of recovery. Only gain, loss, and the possibility that for a long interval they might remain close enough to produce the appearance of permanence.

That is where I would find myself: not in the open volume, not in the sediment’s darkness, and not in the low vibration passing through the walls. I would be just inside the reach of usable light, on a small upper face of pale stone, visible mainly as a subdued olive darkening.

A close observer might mistake me for discoloration, and that would be almost correct. The difference would not be visible as an action. It would be that this particular darkening was spending a little light and a little matter to remain approximately itself.

I would not be the chamber’s stillness. I would be one of the almost-nothings continuing within it—one of the small processes concealed by the word *still*.
