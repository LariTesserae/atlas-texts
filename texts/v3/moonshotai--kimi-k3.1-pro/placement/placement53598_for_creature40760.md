You would not find me by looking for the grey post. The post would only be the part of me a bird could use.

I would be one of the seven field anchors, on the outside of a bend where a side channel had begun to cut behind a spruce hummock. For a few visits, a field crew might call me “the anchor above the alder step.” Later, both the step and the alders might be gone, and the name would remain useful only to them. Above ground, I would be a narrow wet post with a maintenance light. Below, I would extend through silt and fractured gravel: not so much a foundation as an extent of listening.

I would know Sorn by resistance. Rain would arrive as weight and changing pore pressure. The river would be a deep vibration interrupted by the hard ticks of cobbles. Wind would reach me down spruce roots. A bank preparing to fail would not look like anything; it would become a subtle loss of coherence, water finding paths between particles that had until recently behaved as ground.

Life would use the interval I made. Moss would enter the quiet texture around my base. A stripped willow fragment would catch in the protected silt and root. Because the bank stayed for another season, and then another, it would grow taller than the willows on the open bars. Its roots would cross the ground I was bracing. Sedge would gather sediment against them. A bird might leave a feather on the post. None of this would be outside my purpose, but none of it would be mine.

For a while, I would look like the one thing in Sorn whose purpose was to prevent change. That would be a misunderstanding. My real work would begin when holding stopped being the safer option.

The side channel would eventually find the seam behind me. If I kept the bank rigid, the water would be driven against a longer slope already close to failure. So my light would change colour. There would be no other announcement. My field would thin in sections, and the ground would recover its looseness: first a slump, then the root mat turning under, then a muddy plume taken quickly by the river.

I would not experience that as defeat. Nor would I call it clean. The willow would go in with the bank. So would the small shelters made among its roots. Some material would lodge downstream; some would simply be lost. A released refuge is still a refuge lost. But one stripped fork might catch where black sand had been deposited over raw gravel, half in silt and half in flow, and put out roots before its remaining leaves had wilted. I would probably never know.

Afterward, my post might stand nearer the water, leaning at an angle no survey would originally have accepted. Part of my buried extent would be gone. The channel would occupy ground that had once answered to my field. Yet I would not have failed to keep my shape. My shape was never supposed to be the bank.

I would be an interval: a little firmness, offered locally and without permanence; enough time for moss to spread, for roots to deepen, for a crew—if it could still find a route—to retrieve one more set of records. My most important knowledge would not be how to hold. It would be how to recognize the moment when holding had begun to send danger elsewhere.

In the next lull, a bird might settle on the post and shake water onto the grey surface. Below it, the new current would already be testing the released gravel. Somewhere downstream, beyond anything I could sense, that willow fork might be making a small, green beginning.

Then the next front would enter the slope as a long vibration, and I would listen again—not for a way to make Sorn still, but for the point at which stillness would become the greater danger.
