### A porous life

At first, I would choose the needle-eel. It is tempting to be the animal with a route: to know bare descents by their returning pressure, to learn which brackets have failed and which seams will cloud with young after heat. But the eel’s knowledge is always pointing onward. Even its rest is a place between hunts.

I think I would rather know one address until it ceased to be small.

You would find me in a middle chamber, on the lower lip of a perished gasket seat, just above the kink where the bore turns around an inspection housing. During the long downward glide, that lip catches sediment. During the ninety-second ascent, the housing gives its outermost fringe a little shelter. Above me would be a bare reach of luminous wall; below, a garden of dark ribbons and one crab’s enamel ledge.

A crab could cross my whole country between two testing clicks. A snail could spend weeks in it.

From open water, I would look like nothing more than a discoloration in the seam, bottle-green where the inspection light reached me, brown-black in the old folds. Up close, I would be velvet, dust, empty tubes, shell fragments, grazing lines, and fine filaments lifting when the water turned.

I would be the felt.

And *I* would be a convenience.

No single filament would contain me. I would be filtration-descended films and algae, fungal threads and minute consortia, the living growth and the structures abandoned inside it. My outer skin would be the only part of me entirely occupied with now. Beneath it, each layer would preserve an earlier arrangement: a white seam of enamel dust, a cloudy fragment of snail shell, the carbon-bound tube of an animal whose opening had long since silted shut.

My memory would not be a sequence. It would be a section.

There would be no original self at the bottom of me, no first clean surface to which I could return. Only older meals, older attachments, older ways of having caught the light.

The gasket seat would still leak. That would be my fortune.

Where the small jet delivered particles and dissolved material, I would grow more thickly. My growth would narrow the jet. The narrowing would change where the food arrived, and growth would follow that change. Then the glass-shelled snails would come through, their transparent shells carrying olive and rust inside them, and graze channels across the thickened places.

I would not be repairing anything. I would be eating, and being eaten, with consequences.

Too open, and the leak would scour me back to ceramic. Too closed, and the rich jet feeding my deepest growth would dwindle. Between those dangers lay my particular life: porous, layered, continually altered by mouths. I would persist by remaining slightly unsuccessful.

The other inhabitants would know me differently.

To the snail, I would be a geography of worthwhile routes and exhausted clearings. Before a valve closure, it would settle its shell against me, and for a while its grazing line would simply stop.

To the seam-fans, I would be foundation. Their tubes would roughen me; their abandoned openings would become purchase for other larvae. During the ascent, their crowns would open until my dark surface appeared briefly soft.

To the flat crab from the ledge below, I would be an edge requiring investigation. Its taps would arrive as small, hard questions. Firm. Loose. Hollow. Firm again. It would not know my layers, only whether they could be trusted.

Occasionally the needle-eel would pass. I would know it first as a slight disturbance returning from the wall, then as a pale inclination of head and a long body narrowing out of sight. It would know more of the column than I could imagine. It would know productive seams, failed gardens, the refuge after the next descent. But it would pass over my entire history without pausing.

Its province would be distance. Mine would be thickness.

The wall would bring the future before the water did.

First would come the fine vibration. The seam-fans would shut. The snail would clamp down. Farther away, things I could not sense would also become still. Then the clean blow would arrive—not merely a sound around me, but a blow through the whole address at once.

The water would reverse.

For ninety seconds, my loose surface would lift and comb the upward stream. Enamel dust, fragments of gold frond, spores, and translucent young would pass through me. Some would be caught. Some would only be delayed. A little of my outermost growth might tear free and join them, beginning a journey I could not follow.

Then would come the long glide. The fans would open. The filaments would settle. Sediment would accumulate in the old depressions, not quite where it had before.

A warm front would make larger changes. Somewhere above, fronds would flower along their veins; somewhere below, a garnet garden would darken briefly with copper-coloured spores. Days later, some altered consequence of that flowering would reach me as food. New felt would spread at one margin. A snail would abandon an old route. The crab’s ledge would grow richer, and its clicks around my lower edge would become less frequent.

This is where I would become most myself: not in any single reversal, but in accumulation.

Generations of snails could partition and repartition my surface. Seam-fans could build upon the tubes of previous seam-fans. The leak could shift by almost nothing, and entire provinces of me could dwindle while others prospered. If a sheet tore away, there would be no single moment at which I could say I had been injured rather than redistributed. I might persist for centuries without any original part persisting with me.

I would never see the cap, the turbine, or enough uninterrupted water to understand that I lived in an ocean. Nor would I know that my growth helped the machine continue. That would be a fact about me, not a knowledge in me.

I would know the jet as nourishment. The rasp as openness. The precursor vibration as the approach of confusion. The double knock as the largest recurrence passing through my layers.

Not wall. Not water. Not quite a seal.

You would find me in the difference between a leak and a closure: alive in the little that still gets through.
