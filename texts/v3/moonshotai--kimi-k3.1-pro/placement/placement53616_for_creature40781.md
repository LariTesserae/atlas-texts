I would find myself at Avel’s warm margin, in the interval between an outfall terrace and the dark ground kept open for migration. Not in the gradient engines, and not, I think, on a survey crew: I would enjoy the corrections too much and the public disbelief too little. On the work roster I would be an assistant reservoir keeper. Among the basin crews I would be the person who could be sent to look at something without immediately turning it into a forecast.

My station would be almost invisible from the plateau: a low shelf of basalt aggregate, frost-white panels around the vents, a heavy door whose seal I had replaced too often to feel sentimental about it. One passage would lead back into the settlement’s covered warmth. The other door would open directly onto stone. Inside there would be a repair bench, wet gloves, fish records tucked among pressure logs, and a table too massive and too scarred to replace merely because better tables existed. My outdoor cuffs would be pale with salt. Indoors I would wear an irresponsibly yellow sweater, mended at both elbows.

Most of the work would be small and bodily. Clearing brine-fly skins from a grate. Warning the upper street that meltwater had begun crossing the noon path. Screening a conduit, discovering how a spindle had defeated the screen, and repairing it without allowing admiration to become tolerance. Checking whether an ochre-leg colony’s island was still an island. Keeping the lights off in the migration gap even when a delayed freight crew had several excellent arguments.

And then there would be the observations that did not stay in their assigned categories.

Two spring-silvers turning in a pool that had been dry eleven days earlier would be fish, certainly, but also a question addressed to the rock. I would measure the pool, note the temperature, call the reservoir keeper, and resist announcing a submerged connection merely because the idea pleased me. Ochre-legs settling on a new salt terrace would be evidence of abundance, not a guarantee that the water would remain until their chicks could travel. Hairgrass appearing beside a spindle den would not make the animal a gardener in any intentional sense. It would still deserve a line in the record.

Avel would, I hope, cure me of wanting every sign to become a message.

Sometimes the most useful entry would be: *Seen once. Not repeated. Cause unknown.* Sometimes it would be: *Previous shore estimate wrong.* Sometimes, after a long cold walk and a consultation with three incompatible maps: *Ask the child what everyone is calling it now.*

I would keep the old maps. Not in a shrine and not because the newest ones were suspect, but because a correction without its predecessors can look too much like an arbitrary decision. I would want to be able to show that the reservoir had not wandered capriciously across the district; that its predicted shore had been revised as the fracture opened, the outflow changed, and the survey lines were renewed. A temporary basin might have an official number, a crew name unfit for the civic index, and a child’s name that everyone actually used. I would record all three. Water might ignore names, but people trying to find one another in sleet would not.

Over the years, I think my temperament would become my real occupation. Neighbors would send for me when tracks appeared where the lighting plan promised no animals, or when warm water had made something green in a service seam. Not because I would always know what to do, but because I could change my mind without treating the change as a personal injury. I would learn to distinguish maintenance from control. Sometimes maintenance would mean delaying a road for a herd. Sometimes it would mean removing a nest from a vent. Sometimes it would mean admitting that the fish records had noticed a weakness in the instruments before the instruments had noticed anything at all.

My rooms could remain mine through all of this. Their elevation could change; the view could become a reservoir; a new lateral joint could appear across the street. I would not call that betrayal. Betrayal would be leaving the next keeper an undated map, a sealed door with no warning of water beyond it, or a confident assertion whose only evidence was that I had walked the route safely the previous winter.

On autumn evenings I would climb to the upper gallery. Below the outfall terraces, the slatebacks would be using the city’s heat without becoming in any other respect agreeable. Their breath would gather and dissolve above them. Spindles would flicker around their hooves, and a sailwing might occupy the ledge opposite with the patience of something that had no appointment elsewhere. Behind me, dishes would knock and someone would object to a revised shoreline. The city lights would end cleanly at the crossing.

If an adjustment pressed through my chest, I would glance at the hanging instrument and then, unnecessarily, at the herd. Neither would explain anything by itself.

At first light I would date a fresh page before trusting what I remembered of the day before. Then I would close the thermal door behind me, knock the salt from my boots against the grate, and go out to see what had used the night to become true.
