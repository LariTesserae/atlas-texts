### A Room in the Dead Candle

On the rise east of the central meres, the old flower stalk makes a small, dry room.

Dry is relative. Rain enters through the broken crown and runs down the inner flutes. At the base, where the stem has begun to soften, water stands among brown fibres. But just above the rot there is a chamber sheltered by a curl of dead tissue. The mites keep to the outer bracts. I would keep to the chamber, where three silk lines cross the hollow.

When the wind bends the stalk, the lines tighten and slacken. When a drop falls inside, the whole room ticks.

I would be the spider living there.

You could pass within a metre and see only what the crews see: a silvered stalk on a useful rise. The gauge posts have numbers; the candle has a use. In luminous fog, when the distances whiten and familiar shapes become unreliable, it is one of the points by which a person can cross from the track to the saddles without trusting appearances. No one needs to look closely at it. No one would notice that it is occupied.

The raven could cross my whole country without lowering its wings. A worker could step over it. My country would be the dead plant, the cotton grass immediately below, and, on still evenings, the wet leaves between them.

The steam field’s roar would not come to me as a voice. It would be a continuous coarseness in the world, carried through the root mat and into the stalk, too constant to signify danger. Meaning would lie in smaller disturbances: the dry fumbling of a mite, an insect’s wing touching silk, the slight lean of the stem when a boot compressed the hummock below.

Most days would require stillness. I would fold myself close to the wall and spend almost nothing. At noon, the split in the stalk might admit a narrow blade of white light while the fibres at its base remained cold. Rain would seal me in. Frost would make the silk brittle. There would be nothing to do about either.

Then, for a few sheltered days in the warmest part of the year, the midges would rise.

They would not fill the air in any way a person standing upright would notice. They would remain close to the mat, among the cotton-grass heads and crossed stems. I would leave by the lower split and hunt among the dead rosette leaves. There would be no broad web cast into the wind, no shining net stretched across the open basin. A few lines. A pause. The small collision of an insect with a surface it had not understood. One good hatch might constitute abundance.

The plant would have spent decades making my shelter. Its flowering might have produced seed, or it might have happened in a cold week when almost nothing was flying. Either outcome would belong to a history too long and too large for me. The standing stalk would simply be the condition in which I found myself: walls, shade, a place for water to gather below, a structure that loosened by almost imperceptible degrees.

Eventually it would fall.

Perhaps I would be among the cotton grass and feel only the blow through the mat. Perhaps I would be inside, and the dry room would become a horizontal, splintered passage taking in water. There would be no special provision for my survival. If I crossed to another shelter, the journey might be shorter than a worker’s stride. For me it would be exposed country: wet leaves throwing back the severe light, hollows deep enough to disappear into, every stem moving separately in the wind.

And if I did not cross, the mites would still shelter in the bracts. Water would still collect in the rotting base. The mat would begin, over years, to close around the stalk.

That is where I would find myself in the High Moss: a brief occupant of a structure already becoming ground. My address would not be a territory but a condition—shelter above the rot, close to the wet mat, dry enough for now. The engines could speak at the top of their voice without saying anything I needed to answer.

When the silk tightened under some small, living weight, I would come to the opening.
