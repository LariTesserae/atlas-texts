### A Way Across

At first, I would make the mistake of standing on the rim.

I would think I had come to witness the Nave. I would try to hold its whole broken oval in my sight, to make the standing excess of water stay put by calling it a meniscus, to separate the sound into tearing, cracking, clapping. For several breaths I might succeed in believing that naming was a kind of distance.

Then some deeper concussion would arrive through my feet, and my body would stop being a vantage point. It would be another cavity the mountain was sounding.

A human body is almost a catalogue of what cannot prosper there: air spaces, rigid cases, tall leverages, a face made for regarding scenery from outside it. If I entered the water as I am, pressure would find me before wonder could. So to remain—in any sense that mattered—I would have to become otherwise.

My first wish would be to become the antecedent. That is the obvious vanity: to be the one clear thing moving at exactly the right time, to survive by comprehension rather than patience. But the antecedent does not comprehend the Nave. It is locally correct, and even that with almost no margin. I would want more from the pressure-hollow than a single stroke. I would want to remain inside it, to see whether the cliff split, to turn timing into safety and safety into a home.

That wanting would already be too large to fit.

Every body there would present some version of the same difficulty. A film preserves a chemical arrangement. A snail preserves the living patch beneath its foot. The swimmer preserves memories of pressure and a body small enough to be wholly included in an interval. Each asks the next instant to retain enough of the last one. I would be trying to imagine a self that did not have to make that request.

So, in the end, I think I would be one of the pressure-hollows.

Not all of them. Not their spirit, nor a secret kindness hidden in the mountain. One.

My occurrence might be in the open brown-green water a few body-lengths from a black rib, just beyond the immediate scalding of a vent. An olive film might darken a fracture below; on the rib, a chalk-white snail might already have closed itself into mineral stillness. The antecedent would be hanging nearby with its fins furled, visible only when the meagre light found the dark thread of its gut.

No eye would find me.

I would not be a bubble. Gas would be another flaw for pressure to discover. I would have no membrane, no transparent flesh, no surface to catch the light. My boundaries would be the places where cancellation failed. Within them, pressure gradients would turn against one another just enough to undo, briefly, their violence. Outside, the sea might still move with the coherence of masonry. Inside, for less than a stroke, water would be merely water.

The roar would not diminish. I would not be silence, or calm, or refuge in any sense a body could settle into. I would be a small, local agreement among forces that otherwise did not agree.

Then an echo would arrive before its event: not a sound separated from the greater noise, but a pressure already present, an intimation in chest and teeth of a cliff that had not yet split.

The antecedent would open its fins.

If my dimensions were sufficient, the animal would fit wholly within me. That would matter. To encompass only part of it would be merely another way of abandoning the rest to incompatible water. My usefulness, if I had any, would consist entirely in being large enough by a very little, and exactly where one small body could make use of the difference.

It would make its single exact stroke.

I would not carry it. I would not guide it or remember it afterward. It would cross several body-lengths of open water and fold itself close again, finishing before the concussion arrived. To anything watching, it might seem that a transparent sliver had fled an event before the event existed.

If I were misshapen, or too distant, or closed too soon, the swimmer would become a brief milkiness in the olive dusk. That possibility would not make me cruel, just as a successful crossing would not make me benevolent. I would have no intentions. I would only have dimensions, and for once the dimensions might be enough.

In this, I would be the opposite of the broken horseshoe of masonry. That structure has the appearance of shelter and shelters nothing. I would have no appearance at all: no courses, no steps, no upright stones resembling a threshold from one privileged angle. No creature would recognize me as architecture. Yet, for less than a stroke, I might be the more honest kind of shelter—not somewhere to remain, but somewhere in which a crossing could happen.

By the time the cliff split, I would be gone.

Not dispersed, because no portion of matter had ever been gathered into a body called mine. Not hidden in the rock, waiting for another early echo. There would be no attenuated self hanging invisibly in the water.

I would not survive the Nave. I would occur in it.

If the antecedent survived, my only trace would be several body-lengths of distance between where it had hung and where it now folded its fins. Even that would not belong to me. It would belong to the swimmer’s continuing: to its next interval of sieve-feeding, its next pressure-memory, its next narrow opportunity to be locally correct.

The films would go on preserving their chemistry. The snail would go on holding while holding remained possible. The mountain would go on beginning. I would retain nothing, and perhaps that is why I could be complete.

Not a home. Not a way out.

Only one small interval in which the water allowed a living thing to cross—and then allowed the interval to end.
