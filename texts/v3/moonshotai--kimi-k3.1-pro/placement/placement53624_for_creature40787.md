### After the Click

At first I would choose the delayed light. There is an appeal in being afternoon after afternoon has ended, in arriving without footfall and leaving no body behind. But the light fills precisely that great central volume which life has learned not to occupy. I find I want a narrower place.

A slateback, then: one of the less accurate ones, perhaps, sleeping above the eastern seam and opening seeds with several patient blows. Yet whenever I imagine the strike, my attention stays on what happens just after it. The forefoot opens. The pebble drops. The lizard turns to the exposed kernel, and the cleverest act in the Hollow leaves nothing behind that looks like cleverness.

So I would be the pebble.

Not one reserved for the purpose. There would be no purpose in me between uses. I would lie on the shallow slope above the basin, a little out from the eastern seam, where the floor appears level until water proves otherwise. Flattish, dust-colored, small enough for a slateback to lift, with one face that sat steadily against calcite. You could pass within a pace and not separate me from the floor. If you did pick me up, there would be no mark by which I could persuade you to put me back in exactly the same place.

My long intervals would not be quite empty. Light would whiten and dull on my upper face. The delayed grayness of some surface cloud would pass through the mineral grains around me without casting anything so definite as a shadow. The floor would keep its coolness against my underside. At the seep’s most generous, dampness might darken the stone nearby; more often, rain would remain something that had happened elsewhere, inferable only from the brief approach of feet and the greater frequency of small sounds.

Then, one white afternoon, when the plain above was already dark, a slateback would stop beside me with a seed.

It would turn the seed until it sat firmly, brace it with one forefoot, and close the other around me. The pressure of the floor would leave my underside. For a moment I would simply be lifted, uncommitted. Then the animal would raise itself slightly, taking weight on hind legs and tail, and I would acquire a direction.

That would be the difference: not a change in my substance, but the temporary inclusion of my weight in another creature’s intention.

The first blow would miss. The seed would jerk aside, and I would strike bare calcite.

The click would travel through the whole pale room. A moth might tighten its grip on the roof; something in a crevice might alter the pressure of one leg. The sound would not make me important. It would only make me, for an instant, audible at a scale far beyond my size.

The slateback would recover the seed. This time the case would break beneath me with a smaller, rougher sound. I would be dropped before the eating began. By the time the animal bent to the kernel, I would already be a stone again, lying a little nearer the basin than before.

Nothing about me would record whether I had been used well or badly. The seed husk would not explain me. The slight disturbance in the dust would not preserve the shape of the forefoot. If another lizard lifted me years later, it would receive no instruction from the earlier strike: no improved edge, no inherited accuracy, no reason to hold me in any particular way. Even if the same animal returned, it would have to discover my balance again.

The roots could inherit a groove. The lichens could keep their slow increments. I would keep no lesson. My part would not accumulate.

I would not be the Hollow’s memory, then. The roots already leave a finer record than I could, and even they do not leave it deliberately. I would only have been available: first to the floor, then to a forefoot, then to the floor again.

You would find me there afterward, if you found me at all. At the eastern seam, a bead of water would swell, darken the stone beneath it, and fall. If the basin were dry, its landing would make almost no sound. Farther off, the slateback would be walking without haste toward some pocket known only as the next place in its range.

Much later, another forefoot might close around me. There would be no recognition in it. For a few seconds I would again have weight, direction, and use.

Then I would be let go, and the late light would still be coming down.
