The first wrong thing is under my left heel.

Not silence. The roof above me is still harsh with wings and cries; the overflow basins knock and bellow; something small scratches in the vault. What is missing is so slight that I would hesitate to call it a sound. It is a faint pressure that usually answers through the cover stone when the inlet frog calls.

I keep my weight on that foot until my sister’s child notices I have stopped.

They are still quick to lift covers, as though looking were the same thing as finding out. They touch my wrist. I sign against their palm: *Level?*

They raise the inspection slab, peer in, and answer: *Full.*

I can see the satisfaction in their shoulders. A full cistern is a reassuring thing.

I add: *Voice gone.*

I am one of the listeners for the middle of the run called Seven Covers. My portion of it is small: three cisterns, two frog basins, an open feeding shelf, and the water beneath two blunt spans. Before the spring mouth and beyond the lower overflow, other people attend to other absences. None of us could explain the whole basin, and none of us needs to.

My hearing has thinned at the top. The smaller roof birds no longer separate for me; their cries have become a continuous abrasion. My sister’s child can still distinguish the high frogs and certain sounds in the roof voids. They hear more than I do. I remember more repairs. Between us, we do tolerably well.

At the third cover, the hidefish rises.

It is longer than I am, though I have only once seen enough of it at once to be certain. A scar buckles the thick skin behind its head. When I was first learning the run, that scar was already pale. The animal takes air with a slow gulp, turns beneath my reflected silhouette, and carries down a carcass I had not noticed.

I am glad of it. That does not require me to imagine it is glad of me.

Its presence tells me the deep water remains tolerable. It tells me nothing useful about the inlet.

The water in my stone scoop is clear, with a faint cream seam forming at the rim. Clear is not good by itself. I crouch at the feeding shelf and put two fingers into the flow. Gray film should gather grainily against them. Instead, the stone is nearly bare. A few sievebacks cling beneath the downstream slab, their legs combing and combing at very little.

My companion signs: *Crabs?*

They mean waste, a carcass, richness collecting where it should be moving on. But there is no multitude at the waste inlet, and the overflow does not smell foul.

We go upstream.

Beneath the second span, the spring has been writing while no one watched. A cream lip of new stone has narrowed the aerated inlet. At one edge it is thin enough to flake beneath a fingernail; at the other, it is thicker than my thumb. Water still passes, but most of it has found a lower seam. It reaches the cistern deep and warm, without spreading across the feeding shelf.

That is why the level has held.

The spring has not failed. The arrangement has.

On the wall above us, an old instruction has been cut into new stone. The wall was refaced since I last had cause to read it, but the message is older than either of us:

*When the level holds and the film fails, look beneath the span before opening the cistern.*

The border is a row of short, deep cuts. A hand can follow it in dim light without troubling over the smaller signs. My companion traces it once, then looks at the cover behind us with the slightly offended expression of someone who has been given good advice too late to avoid a mistake.

They reach for the basalt point. I close my hand lightly around their wrist. Not refusal. Sequence.

We set the downstream grate. We open the side pocket that will take the first cloudy pulse. Only then do I score the mineral lip and show them where to strike. A blow at the wrong angle would crack the seat of the span; a harder blow is not always a more useful one.

The mallet taps vanish almost at once into the rookery noise. I feel them through my wrist.

The lip breaks in three pieces, one larger than I intended. My companion looks at me quickly. I touch the unharmed span seat, then the broken crust, and sign: *Mess. Not damage.*

There is a difference, and it is worth learning early.

We clear the fragments, reopen the side pocket by degrees, and wait with our feet in the water. The flow returns first as a thread, then as a rough, shallow sheet over the shelf. It looks busy immediately. It carries almost nothing. We do not mistake that for recovery.

Before we leave, we add what was different this time: the cistern remained full; the lower seep supplied it; the deep fish still fed; the feeder ran clear. My companion does the cutting. Their hand is steadier than mine now, though they pause over the sign for the lower seam and I have to correct it.

They leave a little space at the end, perhaps for one of our marks. I move the point back toward the measurements. Let the next keeper copy the size of the crust and the place it formed. My name would be less use to them.

The frog does not return while we stand there. Repairs do not summon frogs.

At the next inspection, the film is still thin. At another, it has begun to collect in the roughness beside the slab. Sievebacks appear beneath the downstream face. My companion reports a movement at the inlet that may be a fish and may only be their wish to finish the matter. We keep checking.

Much later, beside the third cover, they touch the back of my hand.

*Low knock,* they sign. *Returned.*

I put my left heel where I put it before. Perhaps I feel the answer through the stone. Perhaps I only feel the water, the birds above, the small impacts of the city arriving through my bones. I have learned not to make certainty out of a wish.

So I believe their hands.

That is who I would be: not an ear for all Sinter, and not, for much longer, the best ear in my own run. A person with wet feet and stiffening fingers, responsible for a short length of water. Someone who remembers where the crust forms, who is glad of an old fish that does not know my name, and whose best work may eventually be a correction another person copies without knowing whom it came from.

The cover will be replaced. The wall will be refaced. My sister’s child will become someone else’s source of old advice. The hidefish may outlast us both.

If you came looking for me, you would have to come close. I might not hear you call.

You would find me beside the cistern with my palm on the rim, keeping company with a voice I could no longer hear.
