Beyond the last marker stones, you would find me first as a small mistake in the water.

Cold where the saddle should be warm. A thread of sediment rising, then settling. At last, close enough for sight, a six-fingered hand coming to rest on the bare stone.

I would make the rest of my arrival slowly. Not from virtue. The crossing had been long, and I was tired enough to knock something.

I let the saddle take my weight. My tail found a fracture; my hind pads braced; the harness cords drew tight against my shoulders and went still. Among the tools and food fibers was a palm-sized slab sheltered under a lattice of cord. A living felt covered one face of it, pale and root-sweet, carrying with it a little of the chemistry of another cone.

The work I do has no single name. My household calls me a route-carrier, usually when they want something fetched and cannot persuade a mason to go. It confers no authority over gates, gardens, or other people. Occasionally it gives me authority over the order in which bad news should be told.

I carry cuttings, felts, corrections to channels, and accounts of failure. The failures require the most care. A culture can be sheltered on stone. A failure has to be carried whole, including the parts that make its carrier look foolish.

A route is not a picture of peaks arranged beneath me. It is a cold seam crossing the left gill, then a descent taken with the tail held close, then a slope whose roughness changes under the pads. After that comes an exposure in which no stone answers the hand and warmth must be followed without demanding that it comfort me. If the next warmth arrives too soon, I am not fortunate. I am wrong.

I came to this work because I wanted to be forgotten.

I was the child behind the screen in the first court. The single clear knock was mine.

I had been trying to descend through an upper doorway without touching the stairs, on the theory that a traveler should be independent of architecture. My hind pad found the wall sooner than expected. My harness caught the screen weight. The stone made the rest public.

When I emerged, the gardener’s gill leaves were still slightly lifted. They denied amusement. Their hand signed *sleep*. Mine signed *stone shifted*.

The stone had not shifted. Even then, I preferred a technical explanation to an apology.

The gardener was given the task of teaching me water, possibly as a punishment for us both. I learned gradients quickly and chemistry slowly. Warmth seemed honest to me; bitterness, dullness, and root-sweetness were for a long time only adult words used to postpone meals. I wanted routes, outer gardens, other cones—any place where my name-sign did not make people remember some earlier version of me and smile.

The gardener also taught me to tie garden fiber so that it could be freed without cutting. I objected that a tight knot was stronger. They agreed. Then they took my wrist and pressed the rest of the argument into it, unhurriedly, while I tried to pull away.

*You will not always be the person who comes back.*

Eventually I left for an outer vent garden. There was no revelation before my departure. The garden needed hands, and I wanted to live somewhere no one knew about the screen, the stairs, or several subsequent incidents involving harvest baskets.

For a while, solitude was exactly as good as promised.

Then I altered a gate by one setting. It was a marked setting. I announced the change to everyone whose water it would touch. No one objected. I read the warmth correctly and the chemistry late.

An old felt began to recede from its stone. By the time I understood that the dullness in the lower channel belonged to the water and not to my own fatigue, the lineage had died back to two narrow strips. No wall fell. No one was injured. There was not even a single dramatic hour in which everything was lost. Food simply became more difficult. Work increased in several households. An obligation I had thought small became visible everywhere.

The announcement had made the change legitimate. It had not made it wise.

I wanted the old instruction smoothed from the gate and my part in the matter smoothed away with it. Instead, I was told to make a complete record: the setting, the sequence of tastes, the condition of the leaves, who had been consulted, what each of us had assumed, and when I had first noticed something wrong without admitting that I had noticed it.

The cord was humiliatingly long.

I was not exiled. I was asked to carry the record to the next cone, where several gardens resembled ours. This was worse, and more useful.

There, the gardeners examined my living samples briefly and my failure for much longer. They wanted to know whether the wrong taste had come before or after the change in warmth. They wanted to know which objections had seemed merely personal at the time. One elder held my wrist through the entire account and, at the end, asked whether I had included everything of which I was ashamed.

I had not. We began again.

After that, other people entrusted me with their failures. I carried successes too, but those traveled easily. Everyone welcomed a vigorous cutting or a better method of abrasion. A failure required someone willing to remain in the court while it was taken apart, inspected, and sometimes recognized as embarrassingly familiar.

That was how a use was found for me.

I did not become patient. I still move too quickly when nothing forces me to slow. I still dislike one mason for a reason that would not survive being spoken aloud. I carry a smoothed stone slip whose markings were erased long ago because its vibration pleases the inside digits of my left hand. No one has persuaded me that this is a good reason. No one has needed to.

The gardener died while I was away. Their household first knew that something was wrong when a familiar adjustment to the morning flow did not occur. By the time I returned, their name-sign had been added to the household cord and another keeper had taken the gate.

I read the name. Then I went to inspect the gate because I did not know what else to do with my hands. It was well kept. I resented that for a while.

What remains most strongly to me is not the cord or the court. It is a habit of slackness in knots. I leave fiber so that someone with cold hands, or numb hands, or simply less patience than I have can free it later. Sometimes the person who comes back is me, years afterward, and I am grateful to an earlier self without being able to remember tying the knot at all.

Now, at the last marker above my home summit, I wait.

Nothing prevents me from rising over the wall and entering. But the felt on my harness carries another seep’s chemistry, and the longer cord at my hip describes a terrace that has gone bitter on another peak. No water is private. Even news must be announced before it becomes part of someone else’s garden.

I set my palm to the marker and give the traveler’s pattern: pressure, release, pressure; then stillness.

Eventually a keeper comes over the outer wall, unhurried, one hand trailing near the stone. We recognize each other at close range. There is no embrace. We hold wrists, and for a little while an observer would think we had merely paused together.

Their first question is not what I have brought.

It is *What failed?*

I begin with the terrace. Then the gate. Then the taste that everyone had mistaken for something harmless. The keeper interrupts only twice. Behind us, the felt waits under its lattice, and the warm water of home crosses my gills in layers I had forgotten were particular to this place: iron at the edge, old root culture beneath it, the faint sweetness of the first court’s gardens.

At last the keeper takes the cord and leads me down.

Behind a screen, a child lands badly. One clear knock travels through the court. From the nearest work court comes the familiar brief note of reproof.

My gill leaves lift before I can arrange my face.

Then I follow the keeper into the warmth. I have a failure to deliver.
