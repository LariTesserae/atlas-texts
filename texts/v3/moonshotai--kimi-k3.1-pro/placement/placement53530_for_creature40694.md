I would begin by wanting to be one of the broad leaves.

Not for its stillness, exactly, but for its sufficiency: to be shaped precisely by what sustained me, to take the low note through my whole frame and give back exactly what continuation required. No appetite beyond that. No unused capacity. No next leaf preparing behind me.

Then I would want to be the fern-work instead, because exactness is more appealing than sufficiency. To be so finely divided that no error could pass through me. Or perhaps I would want to be the note itself, loud and featureless and free of the need to listen.

But each of those wishes would be dishonest in the same way. I would keep secretly adding a witness. The leaf would be someone enjoying the vibration. The fern would be someone taking pride in precision. The note would be someone who knew it had no beginning available to memory and no ending available to fear. I would be haunting the garden in order to make room for myself.

Nor would I be its gardener. There is no work for that word to do here. To claim it would be to invent neglect merely so that I could arrive with a duty: a crossing to correct, a root to free, a component to clean, some small insufficiency awaiting my hands. Nothing has been waiting for my care.

So I would find myself outside the seam, with one boot on the sixth staple and the other braced against the fluted rock.

The plains would be too far below to offer me any useful scale. My concerns would become very narrow: the skin of my fingers, the security of a boot sole, the water remaining in its container, the number of breaths I was wasting by breathing too quickly. Above the black needle, the horizon would have its astronomical curve. Against my wrist, through the iron and stone, the garden would have its pressure.

I would put my head near the opening, but I would not cross the slab. Not because crossing would profane it—that would make my presence much too important—but because there would be nothing for me to do on the other side. The threshold would be the limit of my relevance.

The chord would enter my hand before I heard it. My pulse would answer, though not in answer to anything. It would merely continue making its small, fallible count against a sound that offered no count at all. Hurry, pause, hurry. A mortal meter laid accidentally over continuation.

Looking in, I would become almost painfully specific to myself. This ache. This thirst. This cold knuckle. This intention to remain for another minute. The garden would supply none of those distinctions. It would not ignore me; ignoring requires the possibility of attention. It would not refuse me, either, and I would have to do without the strange consolation of being refused. My awe, my loneliness, my desire to be acknowledged—all of that would be private weather occurring on the outside of something that had no opening for it.

And my body would seem, by comparison, magnificently wasteful.

Warm where the rock was cold. Wet where nothing was wet. Full of gradients and corrections, of material taken in and material cast off, of damage anticipated and damage repaired. I would be alive by continually departing from the condition I was trying to preserve. My continuation would require appetite, error, exhaustion, replacement. Even standing still, I would be busy becoming slightly otherwise.

The garden would be doing exactly enough. I would be doing a great deal more than enough and still not be finished.

Perhaps that would be the first thing in the encounter capable of changing: not my understanding of the garden, but my understanding of unfinishedness. I had been accustomed to treating it as a defect. An unfinished person, an unfinished life, an unfinished thought—each one waiting, presumably, for its final form. But at the seam I would feel how much of me depended on there being no final form. How a next breath is not a flaw in the previous one. How a memory is not an error in the world. How having an afterward is not the same as being incomplete in some contemptible sense.

I would not turn the handwheel. I would not restore the number on the tag. I would not lift the root mat to expose the ceramic fuse or break off a leaf to prove that dry tissue could be pliant. Even my restraint would not become part of the garden’s virtue. It would simply be the least theatrical way of standing there.

For a little while, then, I would be a brief outside.

Not its observer in any constitutive sense; it would not become more observed because I had seen it. Not its final visitor, because finality would be another story I had brought with me. Not a consciousness lent to the machine, and not an animal admitted to the ecology. Just an unfinished physiology at the threshold of a finished one, able to feel the note in my teeth and unable to make an event of it.

Eventually my fingers would lose their accuracy. The water would be low. I would descend.

The garden would not remember the visit, and my absence would not close over like water. There would be no space where I had stood. The gauges would remain untrembling, the frond’s narrow shadow would remain where it was, and continuation would not have been strengthened or endangered by my having come and gone.

Lower down, where sound could live more convincingly in the air, a loose stone might click beneath my boot.

It would click, and then it would stop.

I think I would stand still for that ending. Then I would go on—thirsty, sore, already altering the encounter into memory, already making it into more than it had been. The garden would continue without holding on. I would continue by holding on in a hundred ordinary ways.

And I would be glad, perhaps for the first time without qualification, to be the thing that had to leave in order to continue.
