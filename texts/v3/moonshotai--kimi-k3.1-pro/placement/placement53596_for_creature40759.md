My first impulse is to be the bead of water at the crown of a pale rib: to gather where water should not gather, hold the last light for a moment, and fall. But that would be choosing the shelf only at the instant when its patience becomes visible. The bead is not the patience. It is merely where the patience briefly acquires an outline.

Nor would I be the rib itself. I admire its lack of hurry, the way its apparent exception is only a condition continuing through days and weeks. But the stone is not quite a resident. It makes residence possible without partaking in it.

One of the copper-brown trees comes closer. Its visible stems would be only a partial account of its body; the rest would lie flattened over stone or divided into root threads within clay. It could lose a stem without ending. Its age could not be read from any single upright part. Still, the trees can be counted from the rim. I think I would want a self whose edges were less easy to agree upon.

So I would be a hummock.

You would find me beside a pool, a little back from the open rim, where a seam of iron-red clay has been caught between dark slabs and prevented from washing entirely away. A pale rib would stand beyond the pool. In wet weather, water would lie against one of my margins; in drier weeks, it would withdraw and leave a dull, soft edge. There would be open rock on either side of me. I would not be part of a continuous greenness.

A single black-stemmed fern would rise from my surface. From a distance, you would probably notice the fern and take me for its ground. Close up, that distinction would become less secure. Its living roots would pass among older roots whose plants had vanished. Moss would run over dead stems and into bare clay. My edges would be real without being precise: here a springy thickness of fiber, there only a green film, elsewhere a little red material bound so loosely that the next heavy rain might take it.

Only a thin layer of me would be actively becoming. Moss would spread across it; roots would enter and loosen it; a folded frond would lengthen within itself before emerging. Beneath that surface, earlier surfaces would remain interlaced and compacted. Not a chronicle exactly. Too much would be missing, and what remained would not be arranged for reading. My form would preserve successful growth more faithfully than disappearance. A plant could contribute its fibers and then be gone, leaving no separate grave, no distinguishable episode, only a slight alteration in what followed.

The warmth would not make me quick. Rain would swell me. Drier weather would settle me again. My center might change almost not at all while one margin extended across a slab by increments too small to assign to a season. An old fern frond would keep its whole outline for a long time, then brown and sink into the mat. Nothing would tunnel beneath it. Nothing would carry pieces of me away to make a shelter. My stillness would contain no concealed movement except growth, pressure, water, and the slow rearrangement of weight.

Most things arriving at my surface would come to nothing. Spores would dry in a groove or wash across me into the pool. Seeds would lodge for a while and fail. A detached shoot might remain green after rain without finding any depth in which to continue. I would offer none of them encouragement. I would have no capacity to select the promising one, and no interest in completing the shelf’s greenery.

Yet I would not be quite equivalent to bare stone.

A little fiber would keep clay from washing away. A shallow unevenness would hold moisture slightly longer. A seed would lie against old root material instead of open rock. These would not be gifts; they would simply be the consequences of previous lives remaining physically present. Beginnings would still be rare. I would only make them, in a few places, fractionally less impossible.

Perhaps that is why I would choose this body. Not because it is old, and not because it would allow me to persist unchanged. Much of me would be dead material, and my living surface would always be replaceable without the hummock necessarily ending. I would be less an individual than a continuing arrangement: a thin present tense sustained over things that had ceased to be separate.

If a seed did establish, it would not be my child or my achievement. Its roots would enter spaces made by other roots. It might alter the movement of water through me, shade a patch of moss, add its own debris after a time. I would become different without having intended the difference. Even that would be enough.

Toward evening, the pale rib beyond the pool would retain the light after my greens had deepened. The bead at its summit would brighten, then lose its color. Eventually it would fall. If it struck the pool, there would be one small, clear note; if it struck clay, perhaps none. The fern would not turn toward it. Nothing hidden would resume calling after the silence.

I would remain beside the darkening water, neither keeping the shelf nor waiting to be discovered. You would find me by looking below the fern and not requiring a self to have a single stem: old fiber, red clay, a narrow living surface, and a margin almost imperceptibly becoming somewhere else.

Not the water that rises. Not the brief bright drop. Only a little substance left by persistence, on which persistence might occasionally happen again.
