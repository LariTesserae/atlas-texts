At first I would want to be one of the independent lives: the black moss beside the reliable drain, perhaps, or one of the translucent animals in the oldest cistern. There would be a relief in proving continuity simply by continuing. No interpretation, no itinerary, no argument about what the Ward intended. Only: *still here; still alive.*

But I do not think that is what I would be. I am too much made of other people’s accounts.

A door opening onto blank clay also feels familiar: an entrance established long before anything to enter, an address maintained on the strength of expectation. Yet that is too pure a form of possibility, and rooms in the Low Ward are permitted only one name. I would do better among the people, who can be several things without acquiring another interior.

So I would be a resident. More particularly, a copyist of testimony.

Not a keeper, and certainly not a warden. I would have an ordinary body with cold hands and an unreliable memory for faces. I would burn food when interrupted. I would dislike being waited for and dislike waiting even more. My work would be to copy registers for the plain, compare itineraries, and preserve disagreements without making them tidy.

When two witnesses described incompatible ways of reaching the same room, I would not decide which account made the Ward more intelligible. I would copy both. I would keep the names, seasons, cautions, and corrections. If an old route ceased to be reliable, I would not strike it out; someone might someday need to know that it had once been walked by a person who returned often enough to be trusted. My own entries would sometimes be wrong. Other hands would correct them. I would hope to be useful in a way that survived being corrected.

I would live in a small cell called **Desk**. The name would already be misleading: there would be a bed in it, a stove, two shelves standing unnailed and slightly away from the walls, and far too many paper wrappings from stores brought down from the plain. There would also be a desk. I would be poor in space and rich in address. Desk would be way-near the common kitchen by an itinerary attested for four generations, though wall-near to several rooms I had never entered and one whose occupants I knew well enough to dislike.

My copies would travel farther than I ever would. I like the thought that a careful sentence might leave the basin in a surveyor’s case and, years later, protect someone who knew nothing about me except that I had once bothered to write *returned* rather than merely *went*.

I would not become serene. That seems important. I would acquire the foods, the folded sitting pad, the mending, and the procedures, but not necessarily the temperament. During the first part of a repeated passage I would be angry. Then bored. Then, if the passage continued, practical. Children would probably imitate my sighing. I would have a formal name for the registers, a household name shouted across the kitchen, and a nickname used by one old enemy with such accuracy that I could never entirely hate them.

If I were ever fortunate enough to see one of the cistern animals, I would want to write something expansive about it: the old water, the translucent body, the years required for so small a life. I hope I would manage only:

**seen; alive; one.**

That restraint might be the best thing I ever wrote.

As for whether the Ward was aware, I would have strong private suspicions and very little testimony. When the note passed through the pipes, I would record where I felt it in my ribs and whether anyone in Desk felt it too. I would not claim to be its interpreter. I would not want to be the voice of the place. The residents already had enough trouble with rooms accumulating interiors; they did not need a person doing the same with meanings.

And where would you actually find me?

Not, at first, at Desk. You would follow the itinerary and find the stove cold, a brown cup beside an unfinished copy, and a note saying that I had gone to meet someone and expected to be back **that day**, with no hour added.

Farther on, you would find me sitting in a passage that had repeated, my pad folded under me and my notebook resting on one knee. I would be hungry despite having brought food, because I would have eaten it too early. I would be composing an angry speech about the route and correcting a copied date at the same time.

If you sat down, I would move the notebook to make room. I would not tell you the waiting was sacred. I would tell you who had waited there before, which witness had been wrong about the second turning, and what we still had to eat.

Eventually a drop would strike porcelain somewhere close by.

We would both look up. If nothing else happened, we would write that down.
