If I imagine myself there, I begin as a visitor, because visiting is the only arrival I know how to imagine.

From the rim, I would see the whole hollow and mistake that for understanding its scale. On the floor, the distances would become more complicated. A few paces would separate the smell of warm dust from the smell of wet limestone. The stones would no longer look arranged for my inspection; each would simply be at its own distance from the next. My footsteps would seem unnecessarily full of incident.

I would stop beyond the damp fan, where my weight would not darken the clay, and try to listen. At first I would hear mostly my own readiness for the next drop. I would hold a breath, release it, shift a knee, and wonder whether the interval had been unusually long. Only after a while would it occur to me that I was not yet listening to the hollow. I was listening to anticipation, and asking the water to relieve it.

Nothing there would oppose that way of attending. Nothing would reward it either.

For a time, I might wish to be the drop. The drop has a shape before it falls. It becomes distinct from the rock, gathers weight, and then makes the one clear sound in the hollow. To be the drop would be to exist briefly but distinctly: arrival, note, rings, stillness.

Later, near the eastern wall, I might wish instead to be one of the stones. Not a standing stone especially—one of the half-buried ones, finished with movement, its upper surface taking the same weather as the natural rock. To be that would be to remain without having to repeat oneself.

But both wishes would still be wishes for a witness. The drop asks to be heard; the stone asks to outlast whoever might have heard it. The hollow offers no encouragement to either request. Its people are gone, but their stones have not kept them. The drops are innumerable, but none has become important by sounding clearly.

So if I could be something in that world, rather than merely pass through it, I would be a small rain.

Not a storm. Nothing that would reopen the southern watercourse or leave a new mark on the basin. I would begin as separate dark spots on the plain and the rim, each one disappearing almost as soon as it became visible. On the floor, I would speckle the silt without at first changing its color. A isolated stone would acquire a dark cap. One leathery leaf would hold a bead of water while the next remained dry. At first, my body would be almost entirely separation.

Then some of the spots would widen. Water would gather in the shallow clay patches and find the old gradients, not as streams but as films so slight that their movement would be easier to infer than to see. One film would join another; another would end against a grain of gravel and go no farther. The damp fan would extend a little beyond its usual margin. For a short time, the distances in the hollow would remain visible, but not all of them would still be dry.

I would have no center. The basin would receive whatever reached it, but it would not be my destination. Most of me would never touch the seep. My place would be out on the floor: in the temporary continuity between a clay patch and a root pocket, along the lower edge of a shrub, or in the narrow wetness following one half-buried side of the eastern rectangle.

There, perhaps, a little of me would run straighter than water elsewhere in the hollow. The old human placement would briefly conduct rain. No purpose would return to the stones. A buried edge would simply be an edge still, and I would follow it as far as weight and surface allowed.

For the Interval, there would be no visitor called rain. There would only be more points available at once: moisture where there had been residue, coolness extending across a patch of ground, brief connections along routes ordinarily beyond reach. I would not awaken anything. I would not enlarge a territory. I would only make it possible, for a while, for more of the hollow to happen together.

And I would be actual while I lasted. A root pocket would receive water. A stone would remain cool a little longer. Some mineral would be carried a short distance and left elsewhere. None of this would require me to become important.

My ending would be uneven. The broad films would narrow first. Connections would fail without any single moment at which the rain could be said to stop. An exposed patch would lighten while the ground beneath a shrub stayed dark. A bead on a leaf might outlast the film that had joined two depressions. Some of me would enter the warm air; some would sink below the silt; some would be drawn into the small, separate economies of the plants. Nothing would continue to have the shape of rain.

Much later, a little of that water might reach the western rock, altered by its passage through dust and root and stone. It would not be a return. There would be nothing left organized enough to return.

That is where I would find myself: not in the bright instant of falling, and not in a residue hoping to be preserved, but in the short-lived nearness of things usually separate. I would be a passing connection that did not need to become a union. When it ended, the distances would still be there, but they would not be losses. They would simply be distances again.

The next drop beneath the overhang would gather without reference to me. It would fall, sound once, and trouble the basin briefly.

It would not be mine.

It would not need to be.
