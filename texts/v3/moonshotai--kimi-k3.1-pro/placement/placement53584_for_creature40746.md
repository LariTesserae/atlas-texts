At first I would choose the rim. I would want the whole hollow visible at once: the dark roofs interlocked with the canopy, the courts opening like small brown rooms below, the mountain’s smooth back carrying the eye away. But that view invites a false occupation—witness. Iru has little use for a witness who will not eventually come down, get dust in his hair, and lift somebody’s floor.

For a while I might imagine myself a gardener instead. I like the thought of work whose success can be inherited. But I suspect I would love the idea of that patience more readily than its practice. One attempt to move a herd of tallowbacks would probably expose me. I would warm the stone, wait, become inventive, wait again, and finally begin making speeches to animals whose interest in me had never been likely.

The image that survives is less picturesque and much more cramped.

I am lying on my side in a stranger’s sleeping chamber, with bedding rolled against my hip and one ear pressed to the floor. The stone is warm through the cloth at my cheek. The householder is explaining, for the third time, exactly how the shaft used to sound. A child beneath the stair is reproducing the new sound with merciless inaccuracy. And I have just realized that the noise I have been concentrating on most carefully is a beetle.

I would be a vent-keeper. Probably a junior one for longer than my pride thought reasonable.

Not a mystic. The mountain would not be speaking to me, or if it were, its meanings would be too large to help with a slipped slab. Air, stone, fiber, insects, and old workmanship would be speaking. My task would be to avoid confusing them, and then to do something useful about what I heard.

My own room would be a converted storage chamber below the press lane, close enough to the foundation’s double tick that visitors would complain about sleeping there. I would defend it out of habit and privately agree with them. My working clothes would carry pale stone dust in the seams and old oil at the cuffs. I would own a scraper, two wooden wedges, a coil of good fiber, and very little else that could not be borrowed. The copper needle I sometimes used to clear a narrow gap would belong to the district. Losing it would be remembered longer than most of my successful repairs.

I would collect the customary small burns on the sides of my fingers. I would learn to test a slab less eagerly. Eventually I might even become good at distinguishing the tick of cooling stone from the dry stitch of an insect behind the wall. But the skill I would most hope to acquire is not exceptional hearing. It is distrust of my first explanation.

A shaft can sound healthy while a room beyond it slowly cools. A partial blockage can improve one household’s sleep and impoverish another household’s warmth. A new canopy can shelter a nursery, shade a neighbor’s oil-cushions, and still have nothing whatever to do with the noise everyone has gathered to discuss. The duct does not respect the boundaries between rooms, and neither does the argument.

At first, whenever the argument became difficult, I would offer to listen again. This would look like humility. Sometimes it would only be postponement with my ear against a floor. I would have to learn that listening cannot make incompatible needs agree. It can merely prevent the eventual choice from being lazy.

One night I would be called to the western roofs because a roughness had entered their thin, reed-like note. It was not much. The presses had stopped, the moths were lifting, and most people could still sleep through it. But the sleeping gallery at the end of the branch had begun to lose warmth. Nothing there was yet dangerous; that was precisely why everyone hoped I would say the matter could wait.

The householder blamed a newly shifted canopy. A neighbor blamed someone for narrowing a duct to quiet a nursery. I privately favored beetles, because I had recently learned several kinds of beetle damage and was eager to make use of my knowledge.

Then a hushwing passed over.

The cry went on above the roofs, and the silence followed it through the nesting chambers. Gliders ceased their small investigations. Something that had been scuffling behind a wall stopped. The child under the stair stopped too. Even the insects seemed to leave wider gaps between their ticks. The shafts continued, indifferent to wings, and for a little while their chord stood almost alone.

In that interval I heard it: not a sawing, not a stitch, but a faint double knock beneath the expected note. Each time it came, the air against the back of my hand changed by almost nothing.

A beetle does not make a floor breathe.

I left a pinch of pale ash beside the slab and waited until the ordinary sounds had returned before asking for help. When we lifted the floor, we found that an old fiber lashing had begun to part. The stone it supported had settled just enough to open a narrow escape into a disused chamber below the lane. Warm air had been going there for some time.

The chamber was not empty.

Beetles had established themselves along the warmest joint. Dry cases and seed husks had collected behind a fallen wedge. The household’s old stair lizard, recognizable by the kink near the end of its tail, was stationed in the mortar gap with the composed expression of an animal whose arrangements were being misunderstood. It had been doing extremely well out of our emergency.

That would be the moment, I think, when Iru became fully intelligible to me—not harmonious, not quaintly balanced, but continuously occupied. Every failure had already been examined as an opportunity by something. Every repair would dispossess a small tenancy as well as restore a human intention.

I would want to leave the little chamber warm. I would also know that people who thickened easily were carrying their bedding toward the cooling gallery. We would wait for the lizard to come out. Then we would close the leak.

The immediate work would take us until the presses began again: the old fiber removed, the slab lifted and reset, a fresh lashing drawn tight, the edges checked by hand. The more deliberate refitting of the stone could wait for a longer plan. There would be disagreement about that too, and some of the disagreement would be sensible.

When we finally opened the regulator, the note was not the one I had expected.

It was clean, but slightly lower. The child informed us that we had repaired the wrong shaft. For one unhappy moment I wondered whether I should keep working until I had restored the exact sound described by the householder. Then I felt the warmth reaching the far side of the floor and understood that I was being tempted to preserve the wrong thing.

The old note had not been sacred. It had been one stage in the shaft’s long wearing and settling. Restoring it precisely might have meant retaining some part of the damage. The address could change. The warmth had to continue.

The children would learn the new pitch first. Their elders would keep imitating the old one when giving directions, and everyone would understand them anyway.

Before replacing the floor, I would put one small ridge and a notch on the hidden face of the slab, where the next keeper’s fingertips would find them: *lift before sliding; the left edge binds.* Not a signature. A signature would be no use to someone lying there in the dark with a hot stone against one shoulder. An instruction might be.

By morning almost no one outside three households would know that anything had happened. A lizard would be looking for another hunting ground. A sleeping gallery would be warm. A child would possess a slightly revised address. Somewhere beneath the floor, a repair would have advanced far enough for the next person to begin without repeating all my mistakes.

That is where I think I would belong: below the visible town, in the narrow interval between what people inherit and what they must decide to continue. Not the warmth itself, and certainly not its master. Just a temporary keeper of a passage, with burned fingers, an imperfect ear, and a growing suspicion of elegant explanations.

And when the repaired shaft sounded and the nearby households paused to listen, I would pause with them. For once I would not be searching beneath the note for a fault.

The note would be new. The warmth would be going where it was needed. And for the length of that sound, everyone in the room would know exactly where they were.
