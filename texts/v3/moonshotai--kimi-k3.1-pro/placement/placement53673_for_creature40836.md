### A Borrowed Warmth

I would be a mill, but I would not begin as the broad, weather-making animal. I would begin as the soft thing beneath one.

You would find me pressed to the ventral plates of an old mill that was not my parent, where its body heat gathered in unequal countries. My own plates would still crease. My tooth-rings would be pale and useless for stone. If I loosened my hold and slipped beyond the belly’s edge, the Manifold would return me to its ordinary temperature very quickly.

So my first world would have no horizon.

It would have temperatures and a rhythm: a ceramic knock, the long scrape of teeth, a settling of weight, and the restrained hiss of a vent farther along the body. Sound would come through the old mill’s flesh into mine. The ground would add its own ticking. Somewhere beyond hearing, a cliff would break, and I would feel the herd pause before I understood that anything had happened.

I would know my carrier by its late third step and the slight shudder it made before settling. That would be more reliable than a face. When other mills drew close, their bellies would extend the ceiling. I could pass from warmth to warmth without ever seeing the fierce white sky. Some of the soft shapes beside me would vanish when a carrier left the herd. I would learn, without words, that a body could be both shelter and departure.

Water would reach me already on its long way through other lives: fog combed from the wind by cableleaf, drawn into root and sap, given back in an adult’s brine, offered to me with softened fodder. I would drink a fog bank’s century without knowing it. Nothing would arrive new. Everything would be handed on.

As I hardened, I would begin to explore the cold by increments.

One body-width toward a plate margin. Then, briefly, down onto the pelt. A cushion older than my carrier would give beneath me and slowly take my weight. Light would strike from above and below. My shadow would be small and blue. For a moment, the basin’s noise would arrive all at once—not through the thin air, but up through leaf, stone, foot, and bone.

Then I would turn back.

Not toward a name. Toward the late third step.

On one of those returns, a transparent grazer would cross with me into the seam of a new plate. Cleaner eggs would be left beside a developing vent. A dormant film, indistinguishable from salt, would wake in the first moisture my shell managed to keep. I would feel none of this as the founding of a world. I would be busy becoming heavy enough to mill rock.

But my back would slowly learn weather.

Condensation would gather in grooves that had not existed when I was born. My exhalations would plume and settle along the plates. The first tiny things would drink there. A seam predator would flatten itself at a warm margin. Charged flyers would gather over my vents and divide into glittering ribbons when a sailer’s shadow crossed us.

I would still be one animal when I walked. Standing flank to flank with the herd, I would become something less singular: a watershed, a contact, a place where populations changed hosts.

The old routes would enter me just as quietly. Not as maps, but as preferences. A grove whose root charge remained steady under feeding. A talus slope avoided because the old animals turned from it before its ticking sharpened. A place where the herd always stopped with the wind on a particular side.

When my first carrier died, its rhythm would simply be absent.

Much later, I might cross the dark vegetation that had taken its outline and hesitate without knowing why. The route would bend around that hesitation. Calves beneath the herd would learn the bend.

Perhaps, much later still, the basin would come into phase. I would know it first through my feet. Current would run longer than usual; the groves would hold a little more warmth; the flowers would open on a schedule too large for me. Wings would thicken above the vents until every exhalation went up into brightness and movement. My calves would grow. Some would be carried away on other bellies.

The whole event would pass through me as good feeding, deepened grooves, and a herd standing closer than before. I would never see the dark bloom from a distance. I would be part of its wiring.

And eventually you would find me again, no longer underneath.

An old mill now, broad enough that fog treated my back differently from the ground. My step would have acquired an irregularity from a scarred plate. My vents would open with a small preliminary click. I would be followed, not because I led, but because I had so often not been wrong.

Under my belly, pressed where the heat varied least, there would be a soft calf that was not mine.

It would not know the routes, the carcass garden, or the long flowering. It would know the click before the vent and the pause before my damaged leg came down. To it, those would not be details. They would be the shape of safety.

The cold would still be on the other side of my plates, undefeated. Above us, the whole basin would be roaring without haste. I would feel the calf as a slight cooling where its soft body drew warmth from mine.

Then I would take the next step, and it would begin learning whether I was reliable.

That is what I would be: a mill—a small weather carried on plates, a route learned in the body, a borrowed warmth grown durable enough to be borrowed again.
