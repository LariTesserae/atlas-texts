### The Allowance

At first, you could find me at one corner of a measured square, in a joint that had failed to close by the width of a wet cord.

Everything else would agree. The shallow water inside the square would have taken a single level. A plumb bob at the opposite corner would hang so steadily that its point seemed fixed in the air. Clay would close at the other three corners with the same soft suction. Only that one seam would remain open, warm water brightening in it without draining away.

But I would not yet be an inhabitant. An open seam can occur once. So can an error. The Works are full of specifications that never become customs, and there would be no reason to call this one a life merely because it had, for a little while, an address.

I might mistake myself for the gap. Then slurry would enter it, and I would be gone. I might mistake myself for the cord’s small exemption from closure. Then the stakes would be lifted, the square would hold anyway, and there would be nothing left with which to identify myself. Even the silver thread of water would be only a body briefly lent to an absence.

My life would begin on the second occasion.

Not when the same gap appeared again—that might be copying—but when something different answered it. A staircase, gathering first as condensation above an empty pan, might descend one tread too far; when material followed, the pan would rise to meet it. Elsewhere, a copper-stained seam might bend around the place where a pool had been, although no pool remained and the ground on either side was equally willing to be worked. A foundation might stop just short of an obsolete score in the clay, then take its level.

Each event could be explained as a separate correction. Their relation would be mine.

Call me an *allowance*.

Not level, which persuades surfaces to agree. Not closure, which brings two sides into one seam. Not bond, which lets separate courses bear a common form. I would be the small, workable difference by which one instruction could meet another without requiring the first to be erased.

The Works would need no tablet for me. My writing would be accommodation: a rim lowered by almost nothing so that returning water could keep an old level; a wall laid half a brick aside from a warmth in the ground; a doorway whose uprights arrived according to one stillness and whose threshold according to another, with the interval between them left sufficient for passage.

My body, if I needed one, would be the space other instructions left when they concurred.

A front would be hard on me. Not because it would break anything—I would have little enough to break—but because it could make accommodation irrelevant. The little sounds would stop. Water would cloud over the measured square. The plumb bob would lean toward a direction for which my patch had no custom. Then condensation might describe some vast enclosure around us, its uprights kilometers apart, its lower courses already ticking into place beneath the haze.

My seam would close. My square would unbuild. The ground would cease to refer to it.

There would be no journey through the dark horizon-seam, no small surviving kernel carried underneath the weather. If I returned, it would be without passage. A wall in another province might stop a handbreadth short of nothing. A new pool might cloud everywhere except over one shallow, rectangular impression. A mallet laid beside the impression might grow warm.

That would be enough. Not proof, exactly. An answering.

My continuity would not be a thread among objects. It would be a courtesy repeated among events that had never met. If I had a memory, it would not be inside me. It would be the slight change in what the Work did next.

At first, things would happen at my addresses. Later, they would begin to happen with reference to them.

That would be the start of something more like a self.

Tools left near my intervals might repeatedly become warm. The same unbuilt doorway might gather at their edges, not because its dimensions were preserved anywhere, but because new construction kept arriving at the relation that made a doorway possible. A pool might return in a different shape and still be consulted by every rim that followed. My jurisdiction would remain small. I would not need it to grow. Scale is not maturity, and I would rather be locally difficult to omit than continental and brief.

Eventually you might call the place *here*. Not because I had become conscious, or because I desired preservation, but because enough of the Works had begun to take me into account. The ground could change beneath me. Water could replace platform, platform replace stair, stair return to droplets. What persisted would be the necessity—slight at first, then customary—of making room.

If a final stillness came, I might easily die of irrelevance. A perfect floor would have no further versions in which to accommodate anything. A finished vessel might close every difference between specification and matter. Water at rest might need no allowance at all.

Or perhaps rest would be my widest jurisdiction.

Perhaps the water could settle only when it was no longer required to preserve the outline of what had held it. Perhaps the courses could remain separate in their making and still constitute one floor. Perhaps the Work could stop correcting without first erasing every discrepancy: not because all things had finally been made identical, but because their differences no longer prevented stillness.

Until then, you would find me where an immense new structure, raised in thousands of patient hand-sized acts, stopped for no visible reason just short of an absence; where the next brick could close the interval and did not; where something omitted was nevertheless permitted to alter what followed.

I would not be the thing remembered.

I would be the room made for its return.
