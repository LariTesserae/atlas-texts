I would like, at first, to be the moth. Senn makes that choice almost irresistible: one small life whose passage no one can turn into a repair, a harvest, or a duty. But I think I would be on a ledge watching it. More precisely, I would be one of the reasons other people knew to look up.

Nor would I be the central hollow. Its exemption from usefulness is beautiful, but that exemption is maintained by everyone else’s restraint. I would want to be among those doing the restraining, however imperfectly.

You would find me on one of the inhabited tiers, beside a public oven whose baking trays had been cold for generations but whose chambers still interrupted some harmful pressure in the wall. It would be the amber hours. I would have seed meal on my wrists, dust in the folds of my clothes, and one hand resting on the rail. Something would be coming through it.

My name would have a visible form, but the form I knew best would be tangible: two light pressures, a longer one, then a pause. On new black fiber it would arrive cleanly. On the old rail beside the oven, the long pressure would spread until it resembled hesitation. Passed through certain people, the pause would disappear altogether. I would answer to both versions.

I would not be Senn’s best reader of walls, nor the person most trusted with the old seed trees. I would take my turn at the mills, carry exhausted dust, cut vines, set a few bricks badly and then reset them. My modest gift would be for relays. I would be good at telling whether a message had been altered by damaged wood, by a recent repair, or by the person through whom it had passed. A warning I would transmit exactly. A joke I might improve. I would be vain about this distinction, and not nearly as patient as my work required. Sometimes I would feel a request arrive and finish eating before acknowledging it.

It would take me a while to understand that this was close to the whole art of Senn. Almost nothing there arrives untouched. Light comes through the shell; food comes through root, capsule, mill, and oven; names come through fiber and other bodies. Preservation cannot mean preventing change. It means learning which changes can be borne.

The messages I loved best would be the unnecessary ones. Children’s patterns elaborated until they became games. A private joke sent three balconies out of its way. Laughter accidentally entering a railing. An invitation to sit beside the hollow when there was no practical reason for two people to be there. Warnings and instructions would keep Senn standing, but these other messages would keep it from becoming only a balance that had to be maintained. I would be reliable about carrying them, precisely because no one could prove they mattered.

Sometimes I would sit at the rim of the central emptiness. Not for silence—there would be none—and not because the place was sacred. I would go because there would be nothing directly in front of me to prune, lift, mortar, or judge. The dust curtains might shift and send someone else climbing toward a flue, but for a little while I could simply be a warm body at the edge of a volume too large for my concerns. The roar would still pass through my teeth. I would still be part of the shell’s metabolism. I just would not, for that interval, be useful.

When a moth flew, I would send the news along the rail before climbing to see it. If it crossed the hollow, I would not call it an omen. I would watch the emptiness acquire a far side. For those few wingbeats, there would be nothing to relay that could improve the fact of everyone looking.

Afterward, the work would resume. Eventually someone would want me at a mill or a damaged parapet, and my name would travel toward me, altered once by the condition of the rail and once by the impatience of the person relaying it. I would feel it in my palm and know it was meant for me.

That, I think, is who I would be: not Senn’s guardian, and not its exemption. A relay with a body, fond of unnecessary messages, doing my share of the necessary work.

You would find me at the hollow’s rim with seed dust on my hands. Or, a moment earlier, you would find me in the rail itself: the slight alteration by which a message, having passed through a living thing, still arrived.
