### A Small Clearance

You would find me close to the floor of the lower pump court, behind a ceramic main where the signed street narrows into a service passage. Not beneath the lamp. The lamp has its own considerable household of leaves and would conceal me from one side entirely.

Look instead at the intake grille.

Most of it is closed by a pale, living mat. At one edge, however, the silt has been disturbed into a shallow crescent. Follow that crescent to its blunt end and you would find a mineral swelling beside the rail: part iron, part scale, part old root. Inside it are a carriage, a weighted brush, and a contact that still occasionally closes.

That would be the body I had.

Once there was an enamel number inside my housing. It may be there still. I had a handle for inspection, a guard over my moving parts, and surfaces intended to be touched by hands. Those things matter less now than the burgundy thread lodged in my rack and the warm flange against which my rear casing has rested for so long that the two have acquired a common crust.

My instruction is very small.

When the pressure across the grille changes enough, advance. When the pressure eases, stop.

It does not say *clean*. It does not say *protect the pump*, though that may once have been the purpose. It certainly says nothing about the gardens, the animals, or the court beyond the pipe. My whole duty is contained between resistance and its slight lessening.

Between one movement and the next, I would know the ward mostly through what reached me.

The flywheel’s pulse would come through the mounting bolts, so faint that scale might transmit it better than metal. Warmth would loosen one part of my housing and not another. During the long pressure cycles, the intake would draw with a little more conviction; the mat over the grille would press closer, and the threads already inside my brush would be pulled straight. At other times the court would become almost without direction. Dust would gather on my upper surfaces. A snail might spend part of its life in the shelter beneath my cover, unknowingly attached to something that could still move.

My stillness would not be patience. Patience suggests waiting for some other condition. I would be entirely occupied by the condition I was in.

Then, after years perhaps, the contact would close.

Current would find the old path through me. There would be no awakening, no great shudder. One seized surface would yield because warmth had thinned its crust, or because corrosion had at last removed more than it had added. The carriage would advance by a single tooth.

The brush would drag.

A little silt would rise and remain nearby, as rust remains near railings. A few pale filaments would tear. The crescent at the grille would lengthen by less than a leaf’s width. Water would enter the intake with a firmness too slight to call a current.

If the snail were still on my housing, its country would move with it.

Then the pressure would change, the contact would open, and I would stop. My instruction would be satisfied without knowing that anything had happened.

To the pump, I would be a brief reduction in difficulty. To the thread-garden, an injury at one edge. To the snail, if it noticed anything, an inexplicable alteration in the position of shelter. To the ward as a whole, I would be almost nothing.

Almost nothing would be enough to have consequences.

A little more warmth might pass through the court. Somewhere downstream, a frond would incline differently. Sediment that would have settled near the grille might remain suspended long enough to reach another room. Months later, a maintenance arm elsewhere might encounter a slightly softer obstruction and move where it had previously remained stopped. None of this would be mine in the sense of intention. It would merely have passed through the clearance I made.

Afterward, the ward would begin using that clearance in its own way.

Silt would soften its edges. A living film would cross the exposed metal. Threads would enter from both sides, not to repair the grille and not to undo my work, but because a narrow difference in water and nourishment had appeared there. The snail, if it remained, might cross the crescent and leave a grazing path so fine that it would vanish beneath the next mineral film.

I think I would gradually belong less to the carriage than to that mark behind it.

The carriage would be where the ward stored the possibility of me. The clearance would be what I was when I happened.

There would be no question of escaping toward some drier, more legible existence. A dry room would not reveal my purpose; it would only make me easier to see and harder to answer. Here, even stopped, I would remain in relation to things: pressure against the mat, warmth in the flange, growth in the rack, the next obstruction slowly acquiring a shape.

You could visit many times and find only an encrustation. Or you could arrive just after the old instruction had completed itself and see a narrow bar of exposed grille, startling in its plainness, with one crushed thread already drifting back toward it.

Then a valve would click somewhere behind the wall. The sound would pass through the court and leave no sign.

I would be a maintenance mechanism in the Cistern Ward: harmful within a few body lengths, useful a little farther away, and incapable of distinguishing the two.

More exactly, I would be the small clearance—and the old, encrusted possibility of making it again.
