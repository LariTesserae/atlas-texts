I think I would begin by looking for a dry place, and that would be the first mistake.

A dry place would make me a visitor. I would be waiting for the pumps to finish, for a door to open onto some more sensible street. Even if I were one of the keepers, I would stand slightly outside the ward: arrive with a sleeve for a pipe, leave with wet cuffs, mistake the repair for an ending. The ward has no interest in that kind of ending.

For a while the willows tempt me. It would be good to be several visible trunks and one hidden continuation, to loosen masonry in one courtyard and bind it together in another. But I think I would be smaller than that. Lower. Harder to date.

I would find myself on a cold seepage wall, beneath a stair that curves around a pipe and no longer reaches the ground. Across the passage, a return main would keep the iron warm. At some point in the slow cycle, its warmth and the wall’s cold leakage would make a little weather between them. I would live where that weather touched the brick.

I would be moss. More exactly, I would be a colony, and even that would be too tidy a name. From the passage I would look like a dark green cushion, no more remarkable than a wet shadow. In cross-section I would be less simple: a living margin; saturated growth beneath it; older stems losing their color; and behind that a pale, porous crust fixed to the brick. The stone would not merely be what remained after me. It would be part of the shape my continuing had made.

The word *I* would become a convenience. The outer edge might be young and the attachment to the wall old. Count only the green tissue and I would have one age; include what I had become by staying, and I would have another. Neither would tell you where I began.

My time would not be measured by noon. The pumps would reach me first as a pressure in the brick. Water would rise through the lower joints and later withdraw. Condensation would arrive separately, drop by drop, without weather to explain it. Sometimes the warm main would shift the balance by almost nothing, and my margin would thicken. Sometimes a repair would divert the seep or cool the passage, and part of me would brown while another wetness, farther along the wall, became habitable.

I would not migrate. Yet over years I might no longer be where I had been.

Perhaps a fragment would be carried down during high water and lodge in a joint below. Perhaps it would rot. Perhaps it would hold. If it held, there would be no need to decide whether it was still me, or offspring, or simply the same small faculty for persistence occurring elsewhere. The ward is generous with continuities that do not require names.

The engines would keep the water moving. I would keep a little of it still: in living tissue, in the dark layer beneath, in the fine space between growth and brick. Not enough to alter the copper marks. Enough to soften a surface. Enough, perhaps, to catch one grain of silt that would otherwise have gone on through the southern wall.

That would be my whole part in the immense circuit. Not a task. Not even a resistance. A brief thickness in the water’s passage.

If you stood there, you might see nothing happen. The lamps would shine more plainly on the pipe than on me. A drop would fall somewhere behind the stair. The bass pulse would continue, and I would continue with no visible relation to it. Only after a long absence would you find the wall slightly paler where I had been, the green edge a little beyond its old outline.

I would not be waiting for the ward to dry. I would not be hoping to become soil, or stone, or anything final.

I would be the damp green margin, growing just beyond what I had already left behind.
