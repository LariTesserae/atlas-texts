### The Second Grip

I would be the practice of the second grip.

Not a palm-back exactly, although palm-backs would be the only bodies I could borrow. The first grip would belong to hunger and appearance. A newly broken glass needle would shine; an animal would lift it, impressed—if a palm-back can be impressed—by its length and clarity. The second grip would begin when the world answered back. The far end would strike an overhang. The point would skate across a shell seam. The animal would shift its opposed digits, brace another limb, and try the balance again.

Sometimes the second grip would be upon the same needle. Sometimes it would be the opening of the digits and the letting go.

I would live in that alteration.

At first I would be no wiser than one old palm-back’s economy of movement. It would reject certain fragments, brace at a particular angle, waste little pressure. It would have no intention of making me. It would simply be hungry. But its meals would attract the young, and its impatience would drive them off only far enough that they could watch and return. Their attempts at theft would bring them close to the working limbs. They would see where the digits closed and which end of the needle was allowed to project.

I would not pass from one animal to another intact. I would be copied badly, which is to say, I would live. A youngster would reproduce the angle but not the brace. Another would choose a needle even longer than the splendid one that had already failed. One would succeed by accident and carry the accident forward for a while. My shape would be the slow residue of these errors: not a rule, not an image, but a slight difference in what happened next.

Clear light would matter to me as much as food. At noon, when wet faces became neighbors, a palm-back might cross from one shell colony to another without traversing the ledges between. Nothing material would accompany it but its body. Yet if another animal watched it feed, I might cross too. Under cloud I would become local again. In separate colonies I might acquire separate habits: shorter levers here, harder grains there, three blows where elsewhere there was always patient pressure.

Heat would be my great interruption. Every pulse would make the animals drop whatever distinguished tool from debris. They would flee upward, and I would have no place in the discarded glass. If the palm-backs carrying me died, and no youngster had watched closely enough, I would end more completely than a limpet ends. A shell might shelter something for one further interval. A dead body might briefly be an arch or a slope. Of me there would be no fragment at all.

Nor would I necessarily be a good tradition. I could persist as a preference after the fractures had made that preference useless. I could become elaborate, wasteful, almost superstitious. My only hope would be that I remained available to correction—that some hungry animal, having copied me faithfully and failed, would alter me again.

So you would find me below the bead, on a wet rib near a crowded colony of glass limpets, at the edge of someone else’s meal. An older palm-back would open a shell with three faint taps. A youngster would approach, be shouldered away, and return. After the next heat pulse, when condensation had restored the ledges in pieces, the youngster would lift a long, brilliant needle. It would test the balance. Then it would let that needle fall.

The glass would become geology at once.

The young palm-back would pick up a shorter needle. It would turn it once, settle its digits, and brace.

That second grip would be me.
