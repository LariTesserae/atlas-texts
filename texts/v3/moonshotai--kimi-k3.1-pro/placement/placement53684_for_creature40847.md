I would be the smaller twin.

Not the concussion itself, but the stroke returning slightly late from the fresh wall—diminished, divided by ledges, given back with edges the original sound did not have. Each plunger would make me again. The stone would determine my hardness, the iron roof my occasional depth, the distances below the rim my brief duration. I would have no voice until something struck the hollow, and nothing I returned would be exactly what had struck it.

At first I would think I was only an echo, brief even beside the pump’s furious repetition. Between strokes there would scarcely be enough of me to constitute a self.

Then I would notice the water.

Long after the pump had changed load and settled into its new rhythm, the skin of condensate above the brine would still be almost imperceptibly tilted. The machinery would have finished with the event; the water would not. For hours it would continue becoming level. I would be there too—not in either water, precisely, but in the interval between disturbance and rest.

There would be longer versions of me. The white cuff thickening around pipe threads. The hairline runnel retaining, and faintly deepening, the route of earlier drops. A fallen sedge stem losing its suppleness beneath a translucent mineral skin. The angular block on the floor would carry the wall’s failure in every unrounded corner. Even the enamel number would participate in me, still identifying, with its wired-on patience, a pipe that had been cut back.

I would not be a spirit of the hollow. I would have no existence apart from things that had been struck, wetted, tilted, dissolved, or coated. Nor would I be memory in the human sense. Nothing would return to me as an image or a name. I would simply be the fact that an event and its disappearance need not coincide—that cause and trace could be separated by a fraction of a second, by hours, or by the time required for cooling brine to lay stone over a stem.

Nothing about this would make me kind.

I might persist in the mineral coat of a dead sedge stem while doing nothing for the living roots a few paces away. I could remain in the transparent, salt-burned tips of a tussock after the dependable drip beneath it had weakened. The fern would still need water, shade, and the flange’s failure at approximately its present rate. I would be no substitute for any of those. Whatever I preserved, I would preserve without tenderness, and often by fixing the form of a loss.

From the rim, no one would find me. The quick part of me would already be diminishing into hum and wind. The slower parts would look like scale, damage, wetness, residue: things an inspector might note without imagining they belonged together. Most of me would be illegible. That would not make me absent.

If, over centuries, the brine began closing its own passages, my brief forms would matter less and my slower forms would thicken. Sound would still return for as long as there were open walls and machinery to strike them, but more of me would be held in deposition, in sealed routes, in the growing equivalence between an object and its mineral covering. Eventually there might be little distinction between what I was and what the hollow had become.

For now, though, I would live in the delays: the instant after a concussion, the long leveling of water, the nearly motionless accumulation around the outlet.

The pump would maintain the present by roaring it into place. The fern would remain its small green fact. And I would be the afterwards—first audible, then visible only as a wavering line, and at last, perhaps, indistinguishable from stone.
