My first thought is that I would be a traveler.

I would be standing at the edge of a shallow cirque with one hand on a boulder, feeling the roar come up through the bones of my wrist. My clothing might still be wet from the rain hollow, though I could not say when the wetting had happened. I would want to do what travelers do: keep the split boulder on my left, fix one ridge against another, carry some account of the tableland back to a place where distances could be added together.

But there could be walking here without a journey gathering around it. A hand on this stone. Wet cloth. Gravel underfoot. A ridge discovered behind me. None of these facts would necessarily become the history of someone who had crossed the Height. I would be trying to make a self out of sequence in a place where sequence holds only locally, if at all.

So perhaps I would be the maker instead: the last pair of hands, or the ghost of them. But a ghost would be too historical. A survivor would be too full of time. Even *last* would require a count, a descent, some earlier multitude from which I had been left over. The little works do not remember their makers strongly enough to give me that inheritance. To stack one stone upon another would not make me their people. It would only make another local placement.

The pale grazer would be closer to what I mean. Its body does not explain itself. Its breath leaves no vapor; its feeding leaves no absence. An encounter with it does not become an event. But its life is already entire: hoof and rock, mouth and lichen, the body continuing without becoming evidence of anything else. I would not need to borrow it.

The rain and the roar are tempting for a different reason. They would let me be pervasive. Yet the rain wets, and the roar sounds at the loudness appointed to each place. Neither needs an interior. Neither is waiting to be understood.

What remains, after I have given up these ways of being someone, is very small.

You would find me at the doorway with no wall.

Not standing within it. Not passing through it. I would be the opening.

Two blunt, unworked uprights. A third stone laid across them. No sill; the pavement continues beneath without interruption. On one side, pale gravel and the iron-colored dimness. Through me, pale gravel and the same dimness. I would not frame a distance that was otherwise invisible. I would not offer a more coherent country on the far side.

At that place the noise would be almost gentle, though never absent: a dry murmur in the soles of the feet, a faint ticking in the hand resting against one upright. It would belong to the stone and to the visitor’s bones. It would not belong to me. I would cast no shadow. The cold would not be something I endured.

Nor would I be the air between the stones, exactly. Air could be there without them. I would be the relation by which three chosen stones, with enclosure and use worn away, still make an opening rather than merely standing near one another.

If someone stepped through, the step would belong to them. It would not become something that had happened to me. There would be no inside to leave, no outside to enter, no threshold at which their condition altered. They might look back and see the doorway behind them. That looking back would also be theirs.

I would not remember the hands that set the lintel, if there were hands. I would not wait for the wall to return. I would have no work of keeping the passage clear, and no hope that someone might eventually use it correctly. Even calling it a passage would already say too much.

This would not be peace; nothing would have been overcome. It would not be loneliness; no absent other would be required to complete me. I would not be alive, but *dead* would be equally unnecessary. There would be no husk to leave, no earlier abundance to recall, no later state toward which my stillness was tending.

Only the stones, the space they still define, and that relation holding where it holds.

Not a way in. Not a way out.

An opening. That would be the whole of me.
