### Between the Wards

At first I would want the eastern saddle: the dark condenser nets, the cold gutters, the cloud lying below the rim like something one might eventually understand if one looked long enough. But I suspect I would do that work badly. I would keep watching the cloud when I ought to be watching the netting. Its stillness would begin to feel like a question directed personally at me.

The seep gardens would tempt me next. I love their definite edges, the way lavish green simply stops and bare stone resumes without apology. But I think I would love them best as destinations. If I belonged to one, I would keep inventing reasons to carry tools to another ward.

So I would find myself between places.

I would be a conduit keeper, responsible for a chain of small things rather than a single court: the covered watercourses beneath three footbridges, a run of channel alongside a long civic stair, and several inspection basins where the stone could be lifted without disturbing a whole path. The work would carry me across the open intervals of Serein, often with no one nearer than a goat on a fracture above me. It would suit my mixture of sociability and evasiveness. I could belong to several wards without having to be present in any one of them all the time.

My house would be at the dry edge of a middle ward, just beyond the last fern, where lichen and cushion plants took over from the garden. I would sleep behind a closed slab shutter with wool over my eyes while the daylight remained outside. My cloak would be brown, with a square of surprising blue cloth repairing one shoulder. It would signify no office. Across a courtyard, however, people would know me by it before they could see my face.

I would not be Serein’s finest keeper. My ears would be good, my patience uneven. There would be others better able to judge a doubtful level by eye or fit a replacement cover without a fraction of wasted abrasion. What people could rely on was that if I said I had inspected a conduit, I had actually lifted the cover. I would not have stood beside it listening for a moment and then allowed confidence to imitate knowledge.

Much of the work would consist in discovering that nothing needed doing.

At one bridge, an obstruction would announce itself by making the water quieter. At another, the ordinary drop would acquire a second, hollow tap. I would know the clean smell beneath a sound cover and the flat, slightly vegetable smell that meant something organic had lodged where it should not. I would run two fingers along a groove to learn whether the water was carrying as a film or breaking into beads. Then, often, I would wipe the edge, seat the cover again, and leave.

A good inspection might produce no visible evidence at all. I would have to learn not to invent work merely to make my walk feel justified. Intelligently leaving something alone would be part of my craft, though not always the part that satisfied me most.

My tools would be few: cord, dry cloths, a wooden mallet, a clay lamp, and a bent copper needle whose particular shape everyone would recognize. I would be absurdly possessive of the needle. When a repair was necessary, I would restore the public dimensions exactly. My knots, however, would tuck their ends upward. An old teacher would call this vanity and then admit, reluctantly, that it kept the cord from snagging. Both judgments would please me.

The short-winged birds would learn what an opened inspection cover meant. They would wait on the nearest wall with an appearance of complete indifference, then descend to quarrel over the exposed insects as soon as I stepped back. I would talk to them when alone. Sometimes I would talk to the goats too, though less hopefully.

I would not be especially solemn. I would pretend not to plan my rounds around other people’s meals and would not fool anyone who knew me well. I would occasionally leave a mallet in the wrong ward, care too much about receiving credit for a neat repair, and remember being wrong about a cover’s projection more sharply than all the times I had been right.

My closest friend would be a gardener in one of the lower wards, someone with scratched hands and an infuriatingly good eye. She would have no reverence for my expertise. When I described some subtle alteration in the interval between drops, she would ask, “And did you mend it, or only listen to it beautifully?”

I would dislike how often that was a fair question.

We would sit at the edge of her overflow garden, where the water made several small sounds at once. She would usually want to know what was happening elsewhere: who had quarreled at the meeting ground, whose child had finally mastered an accurate marmot call, whether two keepers who had stopped speaking were now speaking enough to finish a repair. I would carry such news badly at first, sometimes improving it in the telling. She would teach me to distinguish between what I knew, what I had inferred, and what I merely found entertaining.

There would be wakings when Serein’s completeness felt like an open hand resting lightly but inescapably on my chest. Not catastrophe. Not imprisonment, exactly. Simply the absence of any elsewhere toward which my work was tending. The same clear light would fall on the same sharp corners. The cloud would remain beyond the saddle. Even my excellent little repairs would vanish into the continuing sufficiency of things.

On those wakings I would take the long eastern circuit, not because I expected the rim to answer anything, but because the path was exposed and lengthy. Or I would go down to the gardens and be irritated by their health. I might be short with a child who had interrupted me and then have to return and apologize. I would not become the person who finally understood that Serein needed to change. Nor would I become perfectly reconciled to it. My restlessness would not be cured; it would be given a route.

At other times, the same completeness would come as profound relief. No approaching weather would make every task urgent. No expanding edge would ask me to turn competence into ambition. A channel could be enough without becoming the beginning of a larger system. I could do a small thing well, eat with someone, sleep badly or well, and do another small thing later. My skill would not have to become a destiny in order to be worth having.

And although the route would remain the same, I would not.

For me, one bridge would become the place where I cramped my shoulder in the inspection space and my friend held the lamp without once telling me to hurry. Another would be where I insisted on a remembered dimension, was proved wrong, and had to say so before people whose good opinion I wanted. A stretch of open stair would be where a child I had taught suddenly heard the obstruction before I did and tried, unsuccessfully, not to look triumphant.

None of this would require a new ward, a higher wall, or a mark cut into the stone. Serein would not have to preserve my past for my past to be real. It would live in how readily I asked for help, how carefully I trusted memory, which paths I took when I wanted company, and which gardener could tell from my face whether I had come to talk or only to avoid being alone.

That would not make me serene. But it might teach me that staying did not have to mean surrendering depth, and that a life could acquire weight without giving everything around it a direction.

If you came looking for me, you might first see only an empty path and pale rock. Then would come two wooden mallet strokes from behind a wall, or the brief flutter of birds leaving an open channel. You might find me at the lowest public cistern, waiting with imperfect patience while a pale-muzzled goat drank from the side I had just cleaned.

The goat would put its muzzle to the water. Rings would travel outward, touch the stone, and vanish. The surface would settle at the carved mark.

I would wait, having learned that this particular goat could outlast my impatience. Then I would gather my cloths, put the copper needle away, and start uphill. The blue patch on my shoulder would remain visible for a long time in the clear air before disappearing behind a wall.

I would not be leaving Serein. I would be going toward the next small thing that needed mending—or, more difficult, needed to be left exactly as it was.
