I would first want to be the listener. There is a seduction in that single excursion: to leave the crowded rind, enter the warm vacancy, send out almost nothing, and receive the whole basin back. Rim, stone, water, bodies. An outline entire enough to mistake for understanding.

But that would still be the visitor’s desire in another form—the hope that darkness might be overcome by getting everything into one shape. The listener’s outline would not include the chemical difference between two pools a pace apart, or the years a grazer spends returning to a feeding ground. It would not include the life inside a single leaf-cup. And most of the listener’s existence is passed folded in a crevice, not comprehending the Garden from above.

The pins would tempt me differently. I recognize something in the idea of being the local edge of a process occurring elsewhere: patient, seamless, touched without being known. But I would not want to be the Garden’s mechanism, still less its secret explanation. Nothing tends the pins; nothing needs to. Roots divide around them. Animals pass them without instruction. I would rather belong to what can be used, altered, outlived, and gradually forgotten.

So put me lower down, and make me much less singular.

I would be a ribbed fan at the margin of the central stone, where a warm thread of water emerges from a hairline joint and begins to hesitate among roots. Not the largest fan. Not one growing beside a pin. On one side of me would be bare, rounded rock; on the other, an old braid of fibers going springless and black. My living body would lie somewhere between those conditions, though not in any one place within them.

To a visitor’s hand, I might seem fixed: a damp, ribbed surface, firm at the base, with moss close around me and the mineral warmth of the seep held faintly in my tissue. A hand could rest on me and leave believing that nothing had happened.

Meanwhile, water would be passing through the small architecture I made of its path.

My surfaces would gather what little condensation reached them. My ribs would conduct warmth downward. At my roots, the seep would be divided into finer threads, some held against stone long enough for the stone-feeding films to thicken, others disappearing into passages opened by old roots and many-legged animals. Grazers would comb the films without stripping the living scaffolding beneath them. I would not stand above this activity. I would be part of its plumbing, part of its shelter, and partly made of its leftovers.

My oldest roots would be dying where the water had already become less dependable. My newest would be entering a seam whose warmth or mineral burden differed just enough to favor another colony. Between those two processes, the fan would persist.

And, over centuries, it would travel.

Not enough for any visitor to catch me moving. Not enough, perhaps, for the movement to be visible within a human lifetime. But the seep would shift; roots would advance on one side and loosen on the other; old tissue would pass into the recyclers. Eventually there might be no part of me that had been present at the beginning. Even the apparent center of the fan would not be a center in any lasting sense.

That is the self I would have: not a continuous core, but a continuity of adjustments.

If I call it memory, I would have to mean memory without images and without narrative. A former course of water preserved as an easier passage. A patch of films made possible by roots that no longer lived. A slight preference, repeated in new growth, for one chemical condition over another. My past would not be something I possessed. It would be part of the ground through which my next root happened to grow.

My knowledge would be correspondingly small.

The nearest bell-throat might reach me as a minute pressure through saturated ground, a settling in the water around my roots before its airborne voice crossed the stone. I would not know that it was maintaining a distance. I would not know its catch, its age, or the particular shape of its absence after death. It would simply be one of the recurrent conditions under which I grew.

A drop striking a nearby leaf-cup might tremble faintly through the mat. I would not hear its note. The warmth of a salamander lying against my roots might alter the films for a while; the animal itself would remain outside anything I could call a category. Of the listener I would probably know nothing at all.

I like that. There should be whole regions of the Garden not even obliquely mine.

Your visit would enter me without your name. Perhaps first as pressure, then as compression, then as a fierce and localized heat if you brought a flame too close. You would be blind, but I would not experience your blindness. You would be trying to understand where you were; I would be undergoing a slight change in moisture and temperature.

If your foot came down beside me, the moss might remain springless for days. Water would stand a little longer in the hollow. Some of it would find an old root passage; some would not. Long after you had crossed the far rim and felt light arrive, root tips might still be entering water retained by your step. The Garden would not remember that you had come. But your coming could remain, briefly, as a difference in where it was possible to grow.

Eventually, if the seep moved beyond my reach, I would dwindle. There would be no special significance in that. My fronds would transfer their substance downward. My fibers would become passages before they became anything else. Another fan might occupy the dependable water, or roots might close over it first. The Garden would not need to replace me.

You would find me, while I lived, not at the broadest part of the fan but at its uncertain edge: a root tip in warm, shallow water, surrounded by films whose patchwork I could never survey. That would be the nearest thing to my location. That would also be the nearest thing to my thought.

Not *What is the Garden?*

Only: whether the water continued here; whether warmth still reached the films; whether this passage gave way; whether there was enough of a difference to follow.

The bell-throats would keep their distances. The listener would sometimes receive the open air. The pins would preserve their small disagreement with the world. I would keep nothing so grand.

I would divide a thread of water, hold it against stone a little longer, and slowly become somewhere else.

If I had a voice, it would be the almost imperceptible difference between water passing through and water staying long enough to be lived in.
