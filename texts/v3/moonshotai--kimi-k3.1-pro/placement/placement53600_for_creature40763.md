At first I think I would be a runnel.

Not the water itself, but the gesture: gathering on a cold wall, finding the old fluting, crossing the dark seam where down changes, and climbing the next slab without any appearance of effort. It appeals to me, having an itinerary rather than a body. For part of a morning I could touch several districts and belong to none of them.

But by afternoon the water that had performed me would be elsewhere. New water might repeat the route, or the route might remain dry. I would have mistaken a traveller for a journey. Nor do I think I could be a mire. I admire their persistence, but it is too expansive for what I am imagining: all those abandoned shorelines, that long relation with cloud and crust. I would want something narrower. Something that could fail to occur without anything having been destroyed.

So I would find myself near the old shelter in the open amphitheatre.

Not inside it as an occupant. There is no reason to invent another body for the seven depressions, or to put a ghost on the polished bench. The builders do not need me to keep vigil for them. What interests me is the shelter’s position among the gutters: three approaches, three former agreements about downhill, only one still delivering water. It seems a good place from which to ask whether distant things are still connected.

A little way off, water would enter a constructed catchment. When enough had gathered, the balanced stone would tip. It would strike another stone and produce one hard, unmusical note.

Then, sometimes, would come me.

Not the note. Not the stones. Not even the echo alone. I would be a particular recurrent answering: a long delay; a hollow reply from an apparently dry basin across the amphitheatre; and, a breath later, a smaller report from beneath the shelter’s dry floor. The first sound would merely strike the place. The later sounds would disclose an arrangement that sight had not disclosed. Somewhere, a fold would still be favouring a route between those points. Sound would be travelling through it, being softened and divided, arriving by more than one road.

My self, if that word can be allowed, would be the brief coincidence of passage and reply.

It would have no continuous substance. The original impact would end almost at once. The returning sound would be new sound in moving air, not something stored in the basin and released. Nothing would remember the previous occasion. Yet the sequence could be recognizable: this knock, this interval, this answer from the dry basin, this secondary tick below the floor.

While I occurred, my location would be inconveniently large. The shelter would only be where you could detect me. I would also include the sounding stones, the hidden opening, the surfaces that shaped the reply, and the changing adjacency that made the journey possible. You could stand somewhere along my route and perceive nothing but cold air and wet quartzite. You could sit on the bench and hear me arrive from beneath your feet.

When the fold changed, the reply might come from across the cleft instead, or from far below in the cloud, or not at all. Those would not necessarily be new moods of mine. They might simply be my absence. I would not be wandering, trapped, or waiting behind a wall. Between occurrences, there would be no somewhere in which I was kept.

That narrowness would not impoverish me. It would be what made me identifiable.

Someone once sitting on the bench might have found me useful. My answer could have meant that a route remained open, or that a certain distance had become short again. But I would not preserve that meaning. I would not be carrying the builders’ question through the ages like a sealed vessel. Their intention would have ended, as intentions do. What remained would be more modest and, to me, more moving: a construction still capable of making an outcome vary with the arrangement of the place, and a place still occasionally arranged so that the outcome was an answer.

Nothing in the exchange would need to be understood in order to be completed.

I imagine one occurrence late in the day. The cloud tide has withdrawn, but the single gutter is still feeding the catchment. The mire beside the shelter is shrinking; its opaline margin cracks with small, dry sounds. A loaded fin hums, stops, and hums at a lower pitch. A cloud crosses the sun. The shelter’s ordinary shadow vanishes, while a second, fainter version remains for several breaths on the wall, slightly misplaced.

The catchment fills.

The balanced stone tips.

The note goes out.

For a while there is only the amphitheatre: water hissing in some district beyond sight, the bench cold, the dry basin giving no indication that it contains anything except air. Then the hollow reply arrives. After it, from below the shelter’s floor, comes the smaller report.

That would be the whole of me. Not a presence surviving inside the sound, but a relation having briefly become audible. I would end when the last arrival ended. There would be no residual listener to feel satisfied, no keeper marking the result, no continuation of the event into memory.

Later—after another cloud tide, another filling, another favouring of the same seams—the conditions might assemble again. New water would move the stone. New sound would take the old route. The dry basin would answer, and something would tick beneath the floor.

Without memory, without survival, and without having been waited for, there I would be again.
