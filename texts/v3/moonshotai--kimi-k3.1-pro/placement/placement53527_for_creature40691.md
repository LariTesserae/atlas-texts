I would be one of those who went down the mountain and then, years later, came back up it.

I would leave for excellent reasons. The damp would have got into my chest. The frogs would have made me murderous. I would want to finish a sentence without a neighbor on the other side of a screen courteously pretending not to have heard its beginning. I would want, above all, to sleep somewhere that did not require me to listen.

My reasons for returning would be less clear, and would change according to who asked. I would come back with a carefully ground knife, a few lowland habits, and no technique important enough to transform anything. On the first familiar walkway above the marsh, my feet would shorten their steps without consulting me. They would remember where a board dipped, where a root had lifted the gravel, where one could turn quickly without striking an elbow on a post.

My feet would belong before I did.

For a while I would imagine becoming a listener. It is flattering to picture oneself as the person who hears what others miss: standing at the upper basins in the dry weeks, head slightly inclined, identifying some small trouble before it becomes public. The hollow would soon cure me of that flattery. My ear would be adequate, no more. I could learn a runnel well enough to know when it sounded wrong, but at the back wall, with all nine waters speaking over one another, I would still be separating them when a child beside me had already announced which spout was late.

My hands would be better.

So you would find me below the cooking yards and just above the first washing place, in a partitioned loft of an old terrace house. Three households would share the hearth below. My sleeping shelf would be high and dry, my wet cape would hang near the entrance, and my window would look chiefly into a woven screen and the underside of a narrow bridge. I would not have the view traders praised. On clear mornings I could see one bright strip of wet stone and, if I leaned out awkwardly, a little of the cloud moving against the lip.

My household’s responsibilities would include a short flight of stairs, the grate where a runnel entered its covered bed, and the walk above it. The walk would have a persistent tendency to lift at one end. I would regard this as a defect in its character.

Under the eaves I would keep a repair bench. Cord would pass through my hands more often than finished cloth: splices, strapping, lashings, screen edges, the beginnings of pegs and the remains of useful timber. People would bring me things after they had already tried to repair them once. Sometimes they would bring me the unsuccessful repair as well, which is usually more informative.

I would favor plain fastenings and common sizes. A clever joint that only its maker could open would strike me as a small act of hostility toward the future. A good repair, I would say, is one that the next cold, annoyed person can understand in the dark. I would preserve levels where I could, replace pieces where I must, and try not to make my own ingenuity part of the settlement’s plumbing.

This would not make me patient or uniformly public-spirited. I would give my cousin first choice of the dry cord. I would avoid the downstream fiber troughs during the coldest weeks. I would officially disapprove of wagers on bark-boat races while possessing unusually definite opinions about eddies. And when water changed note at night, my first thought would not always be a noble one.

Sometimes it would be: *surely someone closer has heard that.*

Late one night, after a shower, the runnel beneath my lane changes from a broad whisper to a strained ticking.

In the cooking room below, someone stops talking. A bowl is set down. I sit still for another moment, hoping the sound will resolve itself into an ordinary drip. Then comes the rapid knocking on a hollow beam, though by then it is almost unnecessary. Doors are already opening.

I take a hooked pole, spare pegs, cord, and folded matting. Outside, the boards are cold enough to hurt. Two neighboring households are at the grate ahead of me, and someone is holding the lifted walk down with a pole while another person works by feel along its pegged edge. Leaves and fine roots have packed themselves into a dense brown raft. Brown water is backing into the lane.

“Who cleaned this last?” I ask.

“You did,” says the woman on the other pole.

There is no answer to that except to keep hooking.

The work is awkward because everyone knows a better way to do it. We pull out leaves, find more beneath, free one corner of the grate and obstruct another. Someone reties a lashing that should have been replaced. I object. They tell me to object with my hands full. When the blockage finally breaks, the first surge comes through harder than expected. Everyone steps back except me; I move late and receive it from the knees down.

The laughter is brief, because there is still work to do.

By morning the grate has been reset, the walk pegged down, and the worst of the matting hung where it can begin drying. The market has already developed a version in which I was carried nearly to the marsh. I correct this story several times without making it less popular.

No one decides that I have become a good person. No one should. But something small has changed in what my neighbors can expect of me. The next time, they will not need to wonder whether the returned one in the loft must be asked specially, or whether I am still waiting for someone in authority to arrive. They will knock, and I will come. Perhaps slowly. Probably complaining. With cord, if cord is what is needed.

That is what I would be: not a listener of exceptional gifts, not a keeper of the whole system, and certainly not anyone who understood the hollow in its entirety. I would know one covered runnel, one troublesome walk, a few neighboring tempers, and which households downstream received the consequences of my laziness. I would be good with a splice and bad at dry-week alarms. I would still sometimes hate the damp.

If you asked after me, someone might say, “Above the covered runnel. The one who came back. Bring them if a walk lifts.”

And years later, if my name were cut beneath a stair tread, I would hope no one preserved the board after it had gone soft. Let them pry it up, cut a replacement, and set it at the old level. Let them walk across it while arguing about dinner, unaware that anything had been memorialized.

Most days, though, you would find me at the bench, twisting cord and pausing whenever the conversation around me suddenly thinned.

And on some nights you would find me willing a strained ticking to become ordinary water again.

Then the beam would knock.

I would put down my work and go.
