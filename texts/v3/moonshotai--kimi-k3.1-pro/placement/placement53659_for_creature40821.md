At first I would be indistinguishable from dust.

Not metaphorically: a little dormant tissue in a seam of basalt flour, corrosion, and mineral scale beneath the eastern wall. No leaf, no silhouette, nothing a visitor could separate from the ground-down plants already mixed through the bank. The engine would be making its entire weather above me. Whether I ever became more than dust would depend on a minor accident of acoustics—whether this seam lay just far enough inside a quiet pocket for the grains around me to remain undisturbed.

Water would have to arrive without becoming a puddle. A film on a pipe. A brief darkening of the dust. Perhaps, much later, another. My first body would be a root knot, binding a few particles together and surviving intervals in which nothing further happened. Only after that would exposed growth be worth the risk.

By the time I had a body a visitor could notice, I would be a blackfan. And you would find me only by overshooting the quiet.

The root-wrapped recess in the eastern wall would seem the likely place. You might stop inside it, surprised by the sudden lowering of the noise and by the pipes buzzing through the stone at your back. But I would not be in its deepest shelter. You would have to turn back toward the engine, almost to the point where the full force of the sound returned. There, where the hushroot ended in rounded, scoured lobes and the next bare rib began, I would be growing just behind the final lobe.

I would not be one of the generous, tiered crowns beneath a reliable rain column. I would be low and thick-stemmed, with fewer leaves than my root system seemed to require and reservoirs disproportionately large for so little green. Small, but not young. One side of me would record an older, harsher boundary in stunted growth. Along the undersides of my oldest leaves, repeated droplets would have hardened mineral residue into crooked gutters. Under the amber lamps, a visitor might notice those pale lines before noticing the plant that had made them: the plumbing briefly uncovered, the living tissue still nearly black.

Daylight would have no part in my accounting.

Most of my life would occur on the underside of things. My upper leaves could remain hot, armored, and dusty while, a few degrees cooler below, escaping vapor gathered against the copper-colored veins. Usually there would be too little to see. Occasionally a bead would grow heavy enough to enter a channel. It would pass into a hollow petiole, then downward, and some fraction of it would remain sealed among the root chambers rather than returning immediately to the air.

The chamber’s rare rain would not mean generosity. When the pressure cycle altered and moisture gathered along the ceiling flutes, the standing sound would divide the falling water into columns. The nearest reliable column might strike another crown, leaving the stone around me dry enough to powder. Drops would flatten and tremble on the leaves above; now and then one would run my way. My shape would record not rain in general, but this particular history of near misses.

I would look as though I were doing nothing. In fact, nearly all my important anatomy would consist of thresholds: cool enough to condense, sealed enough to retain, dry enough in the surrounding mat to release.

When that last threshold was crossed, my valves would open. Water would enter the hushroot felt. Fine roots beneath it would survive a little longer; roots belonging to some other lineage might happen to share the wetted ground. Then the mat’s condition would change, or my reservoir would fall, and the opening would close. None of this would be a gift, and none of it would make the thicket one organism. Our roots could cross intimately without our histories becoming common.

I would not experience patience. Patience would be the name a visitor gave to the intervals between my thresholds.

Nor would I remember the engine’s former voice. But I would carry its consequences. As the flywheel wore and the fundamental note shifted, the geography of loudness would alter by degrees too slow to watch. Dust that had lain undisturbed around my first root knot might begin to thin. Root tips on that side would loosen, dry, or fail to branch. New sporelings would appear not beside me but farther along the recess, wherever quiet ground had newly permitted dust to gather.

There would be no migration to join. The grove’s edge would simply cease to coincide with my body.

For a long time, stored water and condensation from the same imperfect pipes might keep me alive after conditions had stopped favoring my replacement. New leaves, if they came, would be smaller. The old mineral gutters would outlast their usefulness. A visitor could easily mistake me for a young plant struggling at the boundary, when in fact I was becoming a relict: an ancient, compact survivor of a refuge that had moved elsewhere.

Eventually a leaf would collapse into a thin brown sheet. If enough quiet remained, it would settle into the felt; if the loud rib had advanced, it would be abraded away. There would be no sudden richness of soil, no dramatic return. Only a slight alteration in the dust—wax, scale, dried tissue, dormant material, and a little of what I had once kept sealed.

That is the self I can imagine in the Thirst Engine: not its keeper, not its mind, not a consciousness speaking through the grove. A low blackfan at the changing edge of an acoustic refuge, locally wet in an almost dry room, made possible by faults that had never been intended for me.

If you stood beside me, you might see one droplet travel along a copper vein and disappear. Then there would be nothing more to watch. The wheel would creep; the pipes would buzz through the stone; three paces away, the noise would continue at full force.

I would not be waiting for the engine to stop or speak. I would be keeping a little water from the heat, at the edge of a quiet that was already becoming somewhere else.
