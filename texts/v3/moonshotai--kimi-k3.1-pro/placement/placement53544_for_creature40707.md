I would be a round.

Not the whole discipline of rounds, and not the person walking one. I would be a small late round, beginning at the condensary after the shift change. You would first see me as a figure in dark canvas leaving the compressor room: two shutters at the mill, the narrow door of the old meter house, a drain cap below the cisterns, then the long way back past the loading platform. On some days there would also be an empty handcart to push once over the spare spur.

But the figure would only be carrying me. The next evening, I might wear different boots.

At first I would look like a route. A route, however, can be walked once. I would exist in the returning: the shutter moved before stems thickened against it, the door opened although nobody needed to go through, the cap lifted while its seating could still be cleaned easily. No single performance would accomplish much. Someone could spend an afternoon clearing every shoot along my way and still not have done me. I would happen when they came back on an ordinary day and did the little that was then needed.

My body would be borrowed. A wrist turning a stiff handle; a heel avoiding the soft place beside a drain; fingers learning exactly how much resistance belonged to a hinge and how much was new. One keeper would set the drain cap down with a ring. Another would always kneel and place it quietly on the stone. People indoors would be able to tell who was carrying me without looking up.

I would have almost no memory of my own. Mine would be kept outside me: in a pencil line in a maintenance book, in the bright crescent worn on an iron cap, in a path that remained pale while the court beside it deepened. I could be forgotten by one person and continue in another’s hands.

If my usual keeper were ill, I would arrive in those other hands before anyone asked what help was wanted. The sick person would hear their customary shutter open, or water moving briefly through their section of drain, and know that their absence had been noticed. There would be no need to call this devotion. It would be practical, and it would prepare a way back.

Nor would I exist to preserve everything. Sometimes my keepers would decide that a spur no longer justified its wheels, or that a doorway could be allowed to narrow. A line would be crossed out, dated, perhaps argued over once more at the canteen table. Then part of me would stop. Felt would rise softly where I used to be. That need not be my failure. A round should not make every former use immortal merely because it is good at returning.

Passing the loading platform, I would become almost entirely omission. No cutting there, no trial harvest, no helpful rearranging of the growth to make it easier to observe. The readers had advised leaving it alone, and for a while that advice would be part of my shape. Each evening I would go past without turning the young felt into an answer. It might become useful. It might vanish into the older body. My keeper could wonder, and keep walking.

Late in the evening, you could find me by listening: two small wooden knocks, the meter-house hinge giving its familiar resistance and then moving freely, iron touching stone, the long whisper of boots through damp growth. After that, the worker would go indoors. From the rim there would be only roofs, green courts, and the lamp beside the trolley line, with nothing visible of me left standing among them.

I would be almost nothing on any single occasion. I would become something only by being almost nothing again.
