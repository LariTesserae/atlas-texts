At first I am outside, kneeling on the grit with the nodule in my palm.

Rain has darkened the pavement. The other stones are drying unevenly, already taking back the day’s warmth, but this one stays cool. I close my fingers around it and feel my own warmth fail to get an answer. There is nothing to hear. If I held it to my ear, I would hear only blood moving in the pressed shell of my hand.

That would be a bearable place to remain: a creature with a future, holding something without one. I could set the stone down and leave. But that would make me the reader and the hollow a text composed for my discovery, and the stone offers no such courtesy. Even my ear would be a kind of demand—that there be a little distance around things, so that I could tell them apart.

Inside, I could not keep that demand.

I could not enter as a springtail; there is already a bristle, a crystal edge, another animal’s knee where my body would have to be. I could not be a worm without becoming someone’s narrowed passage. If I were water, I would have to displace one of nine exact measures, and there is no thirst in the place asking for a tenth. If I were green, I would make seven scales where six are already managing their difficult greenness. Even an ear would take the place of a leg. Even a listener would press against something.

So I would not be a body, and I would not be additional.

I would be one of the narrow blue shadows where two bodies touch.

Not shadow in general, and not a special shadow. One seam, in the little cell shared by the three soft-shelled snails, where two translucent whorls meet without pressure. The light passes through shell, through the pale softness beneath shell, through so much clearness that the animals seem almost to have been made from the radiance itself. But at their contact it cannot quite make them continuous. There it leaves me: curved, minute, slightly blue, with no thickness I could call a body and no surface from which to look out.

The snails would not know me. One would work its rasping mouth over the clay; another would keep its delicate shell against crystal, drawing from its own small adjustments a fine glassy note. Their eyes would remain dark points on tentative stalks. Nothing they did would reveal me, because revealing would require a change in attention, and then another change. Their nearness, the light, and the exactness of their shells would simply be what I am.

The enormous sound would not be something I faced. Facing requires a little room. It would exist in the substance on either side of me: glassy fineness, the grit of clay under a mouth, the dense whirring of half-open wings, the deeper pressure of worms gripping grains. I would not sort these into voices. Sorting requires gaps, and every gap is already occupied by something smaller. Nor would I find the sound beautiful and then endure it. Beauty and endurance both seem to require a second moment.

If I had anything like consciousness, it would not lean forward as a thought or backward as a memory. It would not ask what the choir meant. Meaning would be another animal looking for a cell. It would be only an untravelling *here*—not the whole chamber, not even the whole cell, but the slender difference between two curves touching.

Perhaps that sounds like almost nothing. It is not nothing. Without such seams, the packed multitude could be mistaken for one luminous substance, one organism, one white note. The seams are where that mistake becomes impossible. They do not separate the inhabitants; nothing could pass between them, not even an intention. They only preserve the fact that this shell is this shell, and that one is that one, though each gives the other its boundary, its vibration, its weather.

Above, a hand might lift the stone or set it down. Rain might darken the pavement and later leave it. None of that would become my history. I would not wait for the ninth bead to fall, or for the sleeping snail to explain the spiral. I would not hope for the chert to open. Hope would need a door in me.

I would have no part in finishing the choir, and none in sustaining it. I would not be its silence, because it has none; not its listener, because it needs none; not its hidden voice, because it is already entirely voice.

I would be the narrow blue evidence that two lives can touch so closely that there is no space left between them, and still not disappear into one another.

That is where I would be.

That edge is what I would be.
