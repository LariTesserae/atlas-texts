At first, I would be standing in the wrong place.

I would be upright in the center of the hollow, making an event of myself. The roar would press through my ribs; I would look for the floodwater that the sound seemed to promise, and perhaps I would speak just to discover that I could not hear even my own voice. I would mistake the place for a room organized around sound, and myself for its listener.

But that self could only visit. A person might crouch behind the crescent, drink from the trough, and leave with damp boots. Even after many returns, I would remain one of the large, brief things that investigate the opening and go on. To find a life that fitted the hollow, I would have to leave the open floor and move toward the wall—past the ferns, below the deep green cushions, into the connected shelter of cracks and stones.

There, beneath the trough stone, I would become small enough.

I would be the dark, wingless beetle: slow, strongly flattened, my back close to the stone above me and my feet among cold grit and silt. There would be no stored possibility of flight, no sudden removal to the moor. My territory would not be the thirty paces of the hollow but the continuity of shelter—the underside of one stone leading, by cracks and protected angles, to the underside of another.

The place would not seem empty to me, but neither would it seem full. It would seem difficult.

The long intervals of roaring would be intervals of taking. Out in the current, the faint evidence left by small animals would be stripped from the stone almost as soon as it appeared. A springtail might pass, but its passing would not remain as a direction I could follow. Moisture, fungal threads, the trace of food, the nearness of another body: these would matter more than anything visible from the rim. The open grit would not be a floor to cross so much as an exposure between shelters.

Above me, the hollowed limestone would catch its little water, drop by drop. I would not know that hands had made it. I would know only that one wet edge persisted after others had diminished, and that beneath this particular stone the air was less thoroughly scoured. Someone’s ancient, brief use of the hollow would have outlived its purpose and become part of the architecture of my life. Not a gift intended for me. A shelter nevertheless.

Much of my life would be stillness, though not the noble stillness of waiting for something wonderful. A small hunter in a place with little to hunt cannot afford ceaseless searching. I would remain under stone for days, then move when wetness and scent made movement worthwhile. A springtail found in the silt would not reveal a hidden abundance. It would be one meal, followed by more shelter.

The other lives around me would not become companions. The seam-snail would be another slowness on another wet surface. The springtails would be disturbance, scent, food. The moss would be a saturated mass beyond my usual paths, thickening around projections of rock with a patience beside which even my waiting would be brief. We would not form a secret commonwealth. We would simply occupy separate, narrow answers to the same scarcity.

Then the pressure would turn.

The slackening of the current would matter before any silence could be named. A drop striking the trough might reach me as a minute shock through limestone. Around my antennae, the air would stop being emptied so efficiently. Differences would begin to persist: wet grit here, older silt there, fungus near the timber, the recent passage of something small along the wall.

For once, I might follow a history instead of happening upon a body.

I would leave the trough stone. Not emerge into the room, not cross the pale floor, not claim the silence. I would move from one shelter to the next and range a little farther along the wall than usual. The silence would not make me fearless. It would make the immediate world legible.

Somewhere above, a person might be hearing water click in a crack, a fern stem ticking against stone, the wren’s single dry note. For that person, sound would have returned to the hollow. For me, something more useful would have happened: the air would have stopped erasing local distinctions. What had recently passed could still be found. The near past would remain long enough to become part of the present.

And then the current would resume.

The traces would thin and vanish. The wall would become, once again, a set of surfaces from which almost everything detachable was taken. I would return beneath the trough stone, not in retreat from some terrifying voice, but because the brief widening of opportunity had ended. Nothing in the hollow would have withdrawn. The snail would still be within its seam, the springtails beneath their grit, the moss continuing its nearly invisible work. I would still be here too.

So you would not find me beyond the black cleft, or standing in the blue-grey dusk, or waiting for the roar to cease. You would find me, if you found me at all, in the shallow dark below the trough stone: one flattened, wingless life inhabiting the afterlife of an old human act.

I would not be the spirit of the hollow or its keeper. I would be one of its small, exact expenditures—bounded by a little damp, a little cover, and just enough food. For me, the roar would be mostly weather. The silence would be a brief recovery of paths. And when the room above filled again with that immense noise, I would remain close to the stone, alive in the narrow margin where the current was broken and a little water stayed.
