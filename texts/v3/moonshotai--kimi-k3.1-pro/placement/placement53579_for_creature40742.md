I would have to give up the view from the rim.

The view is tempting: six turrets under the fig, the pale court, the ferns each standing in their small pool of bareness. But to remain there would make me a visitor. I would see the town’s roofs and mistake them for its life, or I would arrive too largely, like the lizard, and for a moment become the reason everything else disappeared.

At first I think I would rather be the moisture.

Not rain itself, which arrives from elsewhere and reorganizes everything, but the dampness that remains after the surrounding clay has dried: the almost-spring, the dark seam, the film persisting on the shaded face of a stone. It would allow me to belong to several lives without being any one of them. The fig root would release me one drop at a time. Moss would hold me. The fungus gardens would know me as a condition of their unfolding. A worker would find me in clay that had become workable again.

But moisture is always arriving or leaving, passing into air, sediment, body. I think I would want a more particular obligation.

So, for a while, I become a worker.

My world shrinks to the reach of antennae. There is relief in that shrinkage. I do not need to know there are six turrets, or that one day sediment may close the hollow. I know that the air here is humid and still, that ahead it has become suspiciously dry. I know fresh clay from hardened clay by the resistance it gives. A fig root is not a tree to me; it is the reason the passage turns. A pellet is not a unit of architecture. It is the thing I am carrying now.

There would be no impoverishment in this partial knowledge. It would be sufficient to the next act.

Yet what holds me is not quite the worker’s journey. It is what happens after the worker sets down its pellet and withdraws. Another arrives, but no instruction passes between them. The second termite finds a patch slightly different from the one the first encountered. It adds material. A third presses new clay into old. Something has been communicated, but not as a message. It has been left in the shape of the thing itself.

Perhaps, then, I am not the worker.

I am the repair.

You would find me at the base of the turret that has already collapsed once, in the darker clay where damage has previously been answered. Not in the whole old repair, but in a new seam being added along its buried side. From the court I would be almost nothing: a damp crescent, easily mistaken for shade. From inside the gallery, I would be the difference between a thread of dry air and air that can be trusted.

I begin as a wrongness.

Light where there should be none. A small disturbance of pressure. Clay that has dried and drawn away from itself. Somewhere beyond the wall, an ant scout tests a covered route; somewhere nearer, soldiers may be gathering. None of this needs to become a story. The breach is enough.

The first pellet arrives. It is placed, not ceremonially but exactly enough, and the worker leaves. I am now a little more than damage and much less than wall. Another pellet comes, made perhaps from material gathered after the latest shower, perhaps from an older fragment wetted and carried back into use. Collapse enters me without shame. Sediment enters me. Something that was previously an embankment, a floor, or useless debris becomes part of my answer to the opening.

No worker contains my final shape. The breach supplies one constraint, the gallery another, the old wall another. Each body finds me at a different stage and responds to what it finds. One adds height; another closes a narrow weakness; another changes the slope simply by pressing where pressing is possible. My form is a conversation in which none of the speakers needs to remain.

And I am not necessarily devoted to restoring what was there before.

Perhaps the passage behind me is thickened and preserved. Perhaps it is abandoned. Perhaps the safest outcome is that a route ceases to exist, and what had been a road becomes part of the town’s defense. I would have no sentimental preference for openness, continuity, or original design. An opening can admit air, or ants, or desiccation. A wall can protect a garden, or trap moisture where it should not remain. My only necessity would be to become adequate to the conditions around me.

There would be no moment at which someone declared me finished.

The visits would simply grow less frequent. The dry thread of air would vanish. Behind me, humidity would return to the passage. Antennae would touch my surface and find nothing requiring departure from the work already underway. I would become sufficiently wall-like to be treated as wall. Attention would go elsewhere.

My success would be to become unremarkable.

For a little while, though, I would remain darker and damper than the surrounding clay. After sunset, when a vent released its almost imperceptible breath and the deep chambers kept their warmth, I would still be cooling slowly at the turret’s base. I would be a visible record of a decision no one had announced, made by builders who did not need to see either the decision or the turret entire. Not the town’s intelligence as a single presence—there is no such spectator here—but one small place where that intelligence briefly took material form.

Later, the record would cease to matter.

A root might thicken against me. Rain might soften one edge. Workers might cut into me, wet the old material, and carry pellets of my former wall toward the damp circular beginning of the seventh tower. I would have no original form to defend. To remain part of the town, I would not need to remain a repair, or even a structure.

Or sediment might rise around me. Shower after shower would lay its thin fans against the roots; silt would soften the court’s tracks; the floor would continue its slow ascent. My patch of darker clay would be buried among younger, less deliberate deposits. Eventually even a close observer might find it difficult to say whether I had been built, laid down by water, or produced by some brief collaboration between the two.

That ambiguity would suit me.

So you would find me first at a breach, then in a wall, then perhaps in a pellet traveling elsewhere, and finally in a seam beneath the rising floor. I would be a local and temporary act of repair: damage becoming information, information becoming clay, clay becoming part of the conditions to which the next small life responds.

Every shower adds a little ground. Every night adds a little town. I would be one of the places where, for a short wet while, those are the same addition.
