At first I would mistake myself for one of the coherent intervals.

It would be an easy mistake. To have an inside and an outside; to maintain a difference instead of spending it at once; to let waves pass without shattering them into foam—for a little while, that would resemble a self more closely than anything else in the sea. My body would be a province of cold water. My boundary would be the smoothness crossing broken waves. Sound would be conducted through me so exactly that I might seem to be the silence itself, rather than something the silence briefly surrounded.

But when the boundary wrinkled, there would be no survivor hidden in the ensuing roar. Nothing would carry forward, not even the habit of cohering. The next episode might be larger and more exact, and it would not be mine. It would not even be a descendant. If I were that, I could occur enormously, but I could not be found twice. And there would be no one within the interval to notice that this was so.

The film on stone would offer a more durable answer: a few seasons in the lee of a crystalline seam, a margin slowly becoming translucent, an outline written by the accidents that had spared it. It would be easier to find, but not safer. Its place would be only the stone’s reprieve. When the rise foundered, place and life would descend together.

I think I would be the other kind of life—the one for which being carried away is not exile, because there was never a home to be exiled from.

I would be a single cellular thread in open water.

My *where* would not be a set of coordinates. It would not even be a preferred depth, though at every moment I would have one. My where would be a temporary conjunction: enough ordinary daylight, enough dissolved salt, water that had not yet carried me beyond the conditions in which anything could be added. At one moment I might lie just beneath the storm roof, visible only when the white sun found my exact angle. Later, without any motion I could claim as my own, I might be turning near the hollow center of a vortex, crossing a province of sea. Later still, a descending sheet would take me into cold dimness.

Nothing in that sequence would be a journey. There would be no destination concealed in it, no lesson accumulating from one passage to the next.

I would have no heroism available to me. Not even resignation, which requires at least the idea of an alternative. I could not choose to remain beneath the sun, could not resist the front that took me sideways, could not interpret survival as a achievement of my own. In darkness I would persist. Where light and salts coincided, I would grow by an almost negligible addition. The immensity around me would not make that addition symbolic. It would simply be what I did when I could.

If I divided, there would be no reason to call one length the original and the other the departure. For a short time, two green strokes might occupy the same illuminated water. Then a shear line would pass between them. Only proximity would have ended. Neither would carry a message; neither would be the future promised by the other.

Perhaps once, a coherent boundary would gather me with others.

It would not select us. It would not know that we were alive. An exact surface between densities would simply hold several threads at the same depth, threads that until then had been separated by days of clear water. Not one cell would need to divide. There would be no increase in life, no bloom beginning, no fertile change in the sea.

Yet from one angle there would be nothing, and from another a faint green plane would extend through the blue.

For a few minutes I would be part of something visible on a scale utterly out of proportion to my body. It would be tempting to call that belonging. But nothing would pass between us except the condition holding us there. No recognition, no exchange, no shared inheritance. The sea would not have made a community. It would have placed its scarcity side by side.

Still, that would be the nearest I would come to having a location another being could describe: *there, at that depth, one hairline in the green flaw of light.*

When the boundary failed, we would not scatter in the ordinary sense. There would be no cloud thinning away from a center, no company dispersing. We would simply cease to be visible together. The delayed roar would arrive from every direction. Afterward I might be in sunlight again, or below it. The sea would offer no sign that anything had been lost, because nothing gathered had become a thing that could be lost.

That is where you would find me, if you found me at all: not at the center of the great silence, but briefly included in one of its consequences; later, nowhere you could point. A curved green stroke in clear water, so slight that you might distrust your eyes before you believed in it.

I would not be the sea awakening. I would not be the first sign of some crowded ocean to come. There would be no multitude concealed in my patience, no future meadow implicit in my drifting. I would be one thread, carried without preference, alive in the narrow, ordinary way.

While the light held, I would add a little green substance to myself.

Then the angle would change. The water would look empty again.

I would not be gone.
