I would have to enter more than once.

The first time, at noon, I would choose badly. I would want to be a gecko: warm stone against my belly, my whole existence narrowed to cover, distance, and the next small movement. There is something attractive in a self so exactly located. One step of the disputed stair. One injured toe. One decision missed by the width of a grain of sand.

But I do not think I would remain a gecko in my own imagination. My boundaries would keep slipping. The warm masonry would matter as much as the body on it; then the insects drawn to the warmth; then the old wall that made the warmth possible. Soon the gecko would seem less like a self than a temporary center around which other things had gathered.

The ants would offer a different possibility. A colony can be beneath the paving, on the ceiling, and inside a seed husk without having to imagine itself as several separate beings. No single ant carries the whole arrangement. That seems closer. Yet their certainty would trouble me: trail or not-trail, colony or intruder, provision or refuse. Even their boundaries, elaborate as they are, can be erased by dust and redrawn without consultation. I would not want a self whose principal knowledge was how to continue itself.

For a while I might choose the oldest vine instead—the dark cables at its base, the roots traveling beyond the court, the resin closing over old injuries. Almost all of it would be hidden, and then, at dusk, it would make its brief, fragrant offering. I understand that shape: long accumulation, a short interval of openness. But I should not confuse being made from old material with having earned great age. The vine’s patience belongs to the vine.

Only when the light changed would I notice the wall I had been overlooking.

From the entrance it would appear empty. Then the sun would catch a strand, and another, and the wall would become crowded with almost-nothing: fresh silk fastened to brown scaffolding, empty egg sacs, lines thick with dust, threads leading to surfaces that had fallen away. Somewhere within it would be the present spider, much smaller than the structure she was currently making useful.

That is where I would find myself. Not in the spider alone, and not quite in the web alone.

The spider is brief; her body does not contain the generations of work around her. The web by itself is only residue, already becoming material for wrens, dust traps, and other occupations. I would be their temporary relation: an inherited arrangement and the living attention that turns part of it, for a while, into an instrument.

Most of what I used would not have been made by me. That would not absolve me from work. It would make the work one of discrimination and repair. Which old line still holds? Which vibration is a beetle, which a moth, which only grit touching silk? Which part of the structure is genuinely connected, and which merely looks connected from where I stand?

I would not always know. Sometimes I would respond to the wrong tremor or trust a strand already weakened by dust. Sometimes an old attachment would make a new error easy to repeat. The web would not be a picture of understanding suspended on a wall. It would be a place where mistakes could acquire enough tension to be felt, and where revision would mean fastening something new without first being able to clear everything old away.

Then the annual dust would come.

At first it would seem to multiply my senses. Every thread would tremble; every surface would report contact. Soon all those reports would become indistinguishable, and then simply weight. The high web would turn brown. Its inherited extent would cease to be an advantage. I would have to abandon the appearance of being a large, continuous thing.

Below, the present spider would begin again with a few shorter lines. Not a copy of the upper web. Not a restoration of the same map. Just enough attachment to distinguish one nearby event from another. A lower ledge might become an anchor; a vine stem might replace a vanished plane of stone. Some old silk would remain above, but continuity would not consist in preserving it. It would consist in there still being a way to make an arrangement sensitive again.

So you would find me in two places, depending on when you came.

Before the dust, wait beside the apparently blank wall until the light shifts. I would be the old web becoming visible and the small, present movement testing one of its newest threads. Afterward, look lower, where there seems to be almost nothing left: a fresh strand, an imperfect angle, a little attention cautiously extended across it.

I would not be the court, or its ruler, or even one of its more important tenants. I would be an inheritance with a present tense—partly obsolete, briefly alert, revised by contact, and likely, in the end, to be useful in ways I would not remain to choose.
