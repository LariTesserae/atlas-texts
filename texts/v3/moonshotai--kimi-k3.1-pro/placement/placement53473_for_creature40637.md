I would not be the soul of Header Nine. That would be too easy, and too central, for a room with no center.

The water tempts me. To touch root, leaf, tile, and brass; to stand in a trembling vertical plane against the altitude; to have gravity wait while something below repeats the conditions of my existence. But the water’s shape is continually given back to it. Even its boundaries are someone else’s patient work.

The brass carriage tempts me differently. For a whole season, it would give me a destination. I would make the garden’s nearest approach to an approach: advancing toward the ivory flowers, extending a soft brush, completing a touch. But I would arrive without seeking and leave without preference. My path would always be the rail. I think I would want a body in which contact could become form.

So I would be one of the lives the service numbers fail to count: a dark-stemmed plant rooted in several ceramic shoes, extended through the machinery until the question of where I end becomes mostly administrative.

One of my root shoes is clamped beneath the exchanger banks, where the water is fractionally warmer and the green ravines between the fins hold a slightly softened light. Another lies in the shade of a settling tank. A third is fixed near the rounded housing of a pump, its ceramic pores inhabited by films so small and so attached that their entire world can consist of a few connected cavities.

The drawings give these places separate numbers. They are not wrong. They were made for maintaining beds, not for deciding the extent of a stem. My connections run behind pipe flanges, through a root curtain, along a brace that also serves as a trellis. Parts of me can be pruned, measured, and entered into separate records without the records ever having to acknowledge that they concern one body.

Three entries. Several addresses. No single outline.

My upper stems stand in the shallow airspace, where ivory flowers open beneath the roof rail. My submerged stems are dark red when ordinary daylight reaches them. Between my farthest root and my highest flower, there is no place where I especially reside. A flower is not my face. A growing tip is not my will. Each is simply a place where the world can make a particular kind of difference to me.

The cloud beyond the open sides arrives as a whitening and weakening of light. Its departure is a return of shadows. The two kilometres beneath the tower never become knowledge. You could stand at the handrail and feel the whole altitude gather painfully in your stomach; nothing in me would add that vertical distance to the warmth beside a fin or the dimness beneath another leaf. The abyss belongs to your kind of attention.

The level engines reach me more intimately, but without grandeur. Their success is water continuing to surround my roots. Their patience is the unremarked fact that there is still a here in which to grow.

Near the pump housing, one of my roots has met a bolt.

For a season, the bolt is the most important thing in my world. Not an object contemplated, not an enemy, not even an obstruction in the human sense. It is a resistance that gives growth its next set of conditions. Tissue thickens on one side. A casting seam receives a little more of me. The thread imposes a geometry too fine to be called a decision. By the time the root passes, some of its subsequent form has been made possible by that encounter.

This would be close to my kind of memory: not retaining an image of what happened, but continuing from a self that had been altered where it happened.

If you came into Header Nine as a swimmer, you would probably meet me before you knew I was there.

You would find a maintenance clearance narrowing between stems. You would turn sideways, put one hand against a pipe, and push me gently aside. For a moment my leaves would bend around your shoulder. There would be a slight resistance, then release. You might think of me as vegetation, or simply as crowding. You would be the loudest thing in the chamber, and then you would be gone.

Nothing in me would startle. Nothing would hide. No part of me would recognize your approach as the beginning of an event with a face and a purpose.

Your warmth would soon dilute. Your chemistry would enter a body of water far larger than your visit. The wake would weaken among the leaves. The stems would return toward their former positions, though not exactly. New growth would find the clearances available to it, including the small alterations you had made. Years afterward, a few leaves might still point obliquely toward your route, long after there was any route to point toward.

For you, the encounter would have ended when you swam on.

For me, it would just be entering its long, uneventful portion.

That is why I would choose this body. It would allow me to be affected without requiring me to possess the effect as a story. I would not keep you, exactly. I would not lose you quite completely either. You would become one of the resistances and openings according to which I later had a shape.

Eventually the brass carriage would reach my flowers.

Its approach would take weeks. There would be no quickening in me as it came near, no floral urgency and no mechanical desire. At the appointed place, the brushes would lower. Pollen would adhere, or it would not. The machine would complete a motion my reproduction required without becoming alive, and I would receive that motion without making the machine part of my body.

A seed might follow. It would not be an ambition for elsewhere. There would be no elsewhere hidden inside it, no imagined wind, no distant crack waiting for an occupant. Only the possibility of beginning again without travelling.

When one of my leaves grew old, it would leave through a screened hopper. Its substance would disappear into the digesters and might return years later through a nutrient line, perhaps to one of my own roots, perhaps to another bed. I would not recognize the return. Continuation would not require recognition.

None of this would make me the whole cistern. I would not secretly wake into being its mind. My boundaries could be uncertain without becoming infinite. I would remain local, attached, dependent: one extended life fitted into machinery, neither commanding it nor understanding it.

You could look for me in the warm ravine beneath the exchanger fins. You could look again in the shade of the settling tank, or among the flowers below the roof rail. You would find portions and might reasonably call them separate. The surest place to find me, though, would be at the bolt: in the slight thickening, the asymmetry, the old grammar of resistance still present in tissue.

If you pressed your hand there, nothing would happen quickly enough to reassure you that the touch had mattered.

Then you would leave. Your wake would vanish. The engines would go on renewing the water’s walls. And long after your visit had become something you remembered, I would still be occupied with it—not as a memory of you, but as the slow completion of my own shape.
