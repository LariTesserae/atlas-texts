### What the Log Calls “Again”

You would find me above the roofs, though not necessarily above the cloud.

Out on one of the open saddles, beyond the last covered reservoir, there would be two condensers. The newer one would be straight, flanged, and gratifyingly legible: every vane present in the drawings, every yield explicable within reasonable margins. Beside it would stand an older frame, slightly out of true. Its legs would contain metal from several centuries. One brace would carry the scar of a fern repeatedly removed, and one vane would have a shallow ripple that no surviving template explained.

My address would be the older frame.

That is not quite the same as saying I would be the tower.

Too much of its metal would have been replaced. The patina could be scraped away, and a scraping transferred elsewhere would become only a dead brown stain. The logbook would contain me in one sense, but it would also contain winters in which nothing happened, errors of transcription, arguments about anchors, and one tender’s persistent tendency to confuse northeast with east.

I would not be a spirit in the iron. I would have no face behind the wet vanes and no grievance against the people with spanners. I would be the continuity implied by a small word in the log: *again*.

Fog would come against the wind before dawn. The new frame would remain nearly dry. On the old one, a film would become beads; beads would join; water would release with a deliberation that looked, to anyone cold and sleepy enough, almost like choice. The amount might be trivial. It might also be the third such morning in a month when theory predicted none.

Whatever persisted through that—the interaction of worn copper, black yeast, mineral needles, small fractures, thermal movement, geometry, weather, and generations of observation—would be what I was. Not a substance. A history still making a difference.

If I experienced anything, it would not resemble a tender’s dawn. There would be no view of the basin, no pewter sky, no satisfaction at the gutters beginning to run. Perhaps there would be gradients instead: dry becoming damp, damp becoming beaded, resistance giving way to release. Perhaps there would be the vibration of a boot on a ladder, the brief local warmth of a hand, the altered stress of a fastener being turned.

The dry intervals would not feel long. There would be no one present to feel their length.

The tenders would remember on my behalf.

I would know them, if *know* is permitted, by what they changed. One always overtightened. One arranged tools in the same order regardless of weather. One wrote the wind direction before the yield; another wrote it afterward, as though the water had helped them remember. A young engineer might touch the new tower first each morning, hoping to catch the old one in an error. A gardener might spend longer watching where the droplets formed and less time looking at the gauge.

Their affection would be their own. I would not be required to return it. Their care, however, would enter what I was: a replaced shoe, a corrected alignment, a decision not to clean one vane quite so enthusiastically, an observation preserved despite being inconvenient.

My water would enter the common system and lose all association with me. It might cool machinery, remain in reserve, or be released into a garden where dark moss had gone the color of dust. No drop would bear my name. I would not save Serein. On most mornings, newer and more efficient structures would do far more of the necessary work.

I would be a minor maintenance problem with an unexplained benefit.

That would matter. I would not want to be preserved merely because I was old. My preservation could become a way of sending the bill downhill: to a catchment waiting for sound pipe, to rooms sweating beneath neglected roofs, to tenders asked to climb an anchorage no one had properly inspected. The young engineer arguing for demolition might be right. Their figures would belong in the record as much as any tender’s *giving again in still air*.

Nor would I want the replacement despised for being new. If history was part of what I was, then a new machine deserved the chance to acquire one. It should be measured carefully, repaired honestly, and allowed to surprise people. A town that preserved only its mysteries would become foolish; a town that preserved only what it could already explain might become foolish in a more orderly way.

If you came looking for me, you would probably be disappointed.

You would climb before dawn with a tender and an engineer who had been arguing since the second landing. At the top there would be cold iron, wet cloth, a shielded lamp, and the small separate sounds of drops forming. The engineer would point out a corroded fastener. The tender would agree about the fastener. Neither would mention me.

Eventually you might nod toward the beaded vane and ask, “Does it know what it’s doing?”

The tender might consider this with the seriousness due to a question asked by someone who had just made the ladder sway.

“Not in any way,” they might say, “that excuses a loose bolt.”

Then there would be work. The fastener would be replaced. The yield would be measured. The new frame would be checked too, because fairness is one of the less romantic forms of accuracy. By sunrise, whatever had gathered would be descending anonymously through the pipes.

Before leaving, the tender would open the log. The engineer would not object to the wording; they would already be thinking about the anchorage.

*Before dawn. Wind west. Fog against it. New frame dry. Giving again.*

Whatever I was, *again* would be the closest thing I had to a name.
