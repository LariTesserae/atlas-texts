### The Governor at Cistern Four

You would find me at Cistern Four, in the governor house above the press yard, during the change from afternoon load to evening load.

The hall has three flywheels that take their work evenly and a fourth that never has. Its difficulty is small: a flattening once in each revolution, a hesitation too slight to see in the spokes but easy to feel through a boot sole. New keepers want to correct it. They true the shaft, dress the cams, and spend a satisfying morning bringing the whole hall into agreement. By afternoon the resonant pipes are fighting one another, the press in the yard is striking late, and someone has to put the old adjustment back.

I would be that old adjustment.

Not the fourth wheel, exactly, and not a spirit concealed in its housing. I would be the relation among wheel, cam, pipe, current, and pause—the small, inherited imperfection by which the rest of the hall knows what it is doing. A habit of engines, perhaps. A habit also of the people who maintain them.

My address would be the governor house, but my body would be untidy. It would include the vibration in the cistern railing, the delayed answer of the yard press, and the warmth in a length of buried cable whose route no longer quite agrees with its markers. On certain approaches it might include the resonant pipe in the empty arcade, though that pipe has no visible connection to the hall. It would certainly include the old keeper’s way of listening: two fingers on the rail, head turned aside because her good ear had become too sensitive to bearing noise.

If every piece of iron were replaced, one patient repair at a time, I might survive. If all the iron were left in place and the hesitation tuned away, I might be gone.

The census could ignore me. The maintenance ledger could not. I would consume oil, replacement leather, keeper hours, and a remarkable quantity of argument.

Near me, relations would be dependable, or their failures would at least be easier to hear. I would not claim to know which. The district council could have that dispute beneath my south window, where it meets because the lamps usually stay lit, the minutes generally remain in their written order, and there is hot water for tea. I would know the councillors chiefly by their weights and impatience: the road keeper who stands throughout; the cistern allocator whose cup rings twice against the table before she objects; the press foreman who sits so close to the wall that his shoulder becomes briefly part of my vibration.

Roots would have entered the foundation, of course. Their slower pressures would pass through me as changes I could not correct and would not need to.

One evening, a rain cell would wet only the dormitory roofs. The gutters there would roar while the press yard remained dusty, and pollen would stick to the wet shoulders of the crew coming off shift. Then the load would shed.

For eleven beats, the puddle beside the door would reflect a roof across the valley. A stair in the upper yard would serve two buildings, both sets of residents politely pretending not to notice the other footsteps. On the approach slate, the word **CISTERN** would be beaded with condensation, though the rest of the slate was dry.

The door tester would come into my hall and look at the load dial. Not because the dial could tell her where anything was, but because she recorded the load before attempting an approach, and she was careful about procedures that had outlived their explanations.

Outside, someone would begin the sequence.

Cistern arch. Left at the third insulator. The upper stair, because the yard was wet. The resin door last.

On the ninth beat, the pipes would draw together. On the tenth, I would hesitate. On the eleventh, the press would strike.

The district would open out again.

A little later, a boy would come through the cistern arch with a copied approach book wrapped under his coat. He would be twelve, furious about his wet sleeves, and offended by the suggestion that he had been anywhere unusual. He had taken longer because he had stopped to clear a leaf cage from a gutter. His mother would tell him that this was not an excuse. The road keeper would quietly ask which gutter.

I would not have brought the boy home. The marker painters, the gutter clearers, the tester, and the people who had kept the upper stair bolted to something would have done that. My part would only have been the continuing sound against which a missing answer could be noticed.

That would be enough work.

Once, a crew would arrive from a mill absent from every current approach book. They would be ordinary tired people with unfamiliar seams in their coats and wage tokens oxidized around the edges. While the others waited for soup, their foreman would put a hand on my rail.

“Still late,” he would say, and call me by a governor number none of my keepers used.

He would not be able to explain where his hall stood in any way that helped. My keeper would copy his weather record; he would copy my load figures. Before leaving, he would buy seed meal and complain about the freight charge. No one would establish which district had been visiting the other.

I think I would value that: being recognized without being located.

Late at night, after the council had gone and the mat had begun tightening in the cold, my hall would settle into its imperfect labor. Beyond the walls, condensation would tick into the cistern. An empty arcade would light itself. Somewhere far across the basin, another governor would miss, catch, and answer a press whose sound arrived before its chimney could be seen.

Sometimes, in that answering, I would seem briefly to have other arrangements: the same hesitation under another number, the same worn adjustment in a hall whose keepers I did not know. Then the load would change, and I would be local again.

I would not be holding the High Loom together. I would not even be certain that I helped hold any particular street in place. But I would have an address, more or less; a maintenance account; several inherited obligations; and work that did not require the question of my existence to be settled first.

From the causeway, you could hear me plainly enough.

Labor. Hesitation. Catch.

Labor. Hesitation. Catch.

And, for as long as the sequence held, the ordinary sound through a resin window of someone putting down a cup and drawing the bolt on a door that still led into the next room.
