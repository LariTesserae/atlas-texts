I would first be tempted by the swimmer. It is the only life in the basin large enough to offer the imagination a body: a pool of one’s own, a dark line through clear folds, decades passing in suspension. I could settle against the submerged stone, feed almost imperceptibly, and let the surrounding concussions arrive as changes in pressure rather than as events I needed to understand.

But that choice begins to feel like a demand that the basin provide me with a protagonist. The dark line would become something to follow. The pool would become a dwelling. Soon I would have made a small, transparent animal carry more narrative than its economy of flesh suggests it needs.

Nor would I want to be a standing listener in one of the roofless cells. However quietly I entered, the cell would become a shelter, the marks on its threshold a message addressed to me, the single low note a kind of appointment. I would have given the emptiness a witness and the constructions a tenant, and interpreted far too much merely by having somewhere to put my feet.

And I would not be the common note. It does not require an inhabitant.

You would find me instead—if *find* is the word—in the eastern district, beside one of the broad pools where the columns are low and many have already clogged. A four-slab stair enters the water nearby. Its upper slab is bare; the others carry old bronze and violet waterlines, interrupted by fresher crust. Across the pool stands a split column, no higher than a person’s shoulder, its broken rim lost among a crowd of similar rims. Inside it, a little rain enters through the fracture. Less regularly, iron-bearing water rises through a seam at its base.

I would live where those waters occasionally mix: a narrow, discontinuous community within the film on the inner wall.

Most of what could be seen of me would not be living. There would be empty mineral sheaths, old organic residue, silica laid down around earlier occupants. The living cells would occupy only certain depths, and not everywhere. Some would use the brief chemical opportunity directly. Others would persist on what those cells produced or left behind. Between us would be sterile glass, dead layers, and small spaces in which nothing had happened for a very long time.

The pronoun *I* would be borrowed. It would not name a continuous body or a colony with a common purpose. After a rise in the pool, the boundary that sustained me might move elsewhere. After a dry interval, resistant bodies might remain in the dark crust while the rest died. Rewetting might bring some of them back into activity. It might instead bring different cells through a seam, and these would begin using the residue without inheriting any memory of the previous community. At what point the stain would again be *me* is not a question the basin would help answer.

My history would have almost no visible incidents. A season of rain diluting the pool. A buried passage opening enough to admit sulfur. The water level falling by the width of a finger and bringing two chemical zones together. Then, perhaps, a little division; a slight thickening at one depth in the film; a new patch no more conspicuous than a change in the way weak light lay on wet stone. Afterward, nothing measurable for years.

Above and around me, the basin would seem vastly eventful by comparison. Rain would whiten the pool with hiss. Overflow would enter a lower cavity with a sudden gulp. The clogged columns nearby would give back heavy, separate knocks. Sometimes an impact would arrive through the ground before it arrived through the water. I would not hear these as music or as messages. They would belong to the conditions under which I persisted: vibration, a shifting meniscus, a grain loosened from one place and deposited in another.

My own work would be smaller than any of that. A surface on which a particle caught. A little material held long enough for silica to gather around it. A passage kept fractionally open because something living used what might otherwise have obstructed it. Nothing done in order to accomplish anything. Nothing that could be distinguished, in a single season, from deposition without life.

Yet the inside of the column is narrow. Its voice depends on narrowness.

Over centuries, the film and its remains might help thicken one part of the wall. Or they might help close the seam on which my chemistry depended. The change would not proceed evenly, and there would be no final moment in which the column declared itself altered. Drops would still fall. The pool would still accept overflow. A knock that had once returned cleanly would become a little duller. Later, water might take another route and leave that cavity untouched for weeks. Later still, one small repetition in the surrounding sound would cease to arrive.

No listener could reasonably trace that absence back to me. There would have been mineral deposition in any case, fractures, rain, pressure changes, other films, long sterile intervals. My consequence would be real without being separable. Even this might be claiming too much: perhaps my community would do nothing but leave a thin bronze residue for the next brief occupation.

That is what I would be, then. Not the Choir’s secret animal, not its audience, not a mind distributed through the vents. A life permitted intermittently at a boundary; a self made uncertain by drying, recolonization, and the mineral record of selves before it. Present mostly as residue. Active only when water and chemistry briefly agreed.

If you broke open the column to look for me, you would find sharp glass, paste, a stain, and water clouded for a moment by the disturbance. Your fingers would come away clean. You might conclude that you had been mistaken. You would have damaged the cavity for nothing, though its new opening would go on changing the sound long after your visit.

For a while, conditions would permit me. Then they would not. The visible film might persist and be inhabited again, or remain entirely dead. The pool would rise and fall without reference to either possibility.

And much later, in weather indistinguishable from all the other weather, a drop would fall into the column and produce a slightly flatter knock.

That would be the closest I came to having a voice: not a note added to the Slow Choir, but one small note, after centuries, made a little less possible.
