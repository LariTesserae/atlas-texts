You would not find me at first.

I would be one of the bone-colored snails, fixed beneath a fern rib near the broken basin, sealed so closely to the plant that I would seem part of its old damage. Motionless. Easily overlooked. Through the longest dry weeks, my work would be almost nothing: to keep the last of my moisture from the warm air, to remain unopened, to remain possible.

I would not know the hollow as a chamber. Seventy paces would mean nothing to me. I would know it as degrees of dampness, as the difference between a safe junction of root and stone and the desolating dryness beyond a leaf’s edge. The roof would be only a distant source of impacts, traveling downward through frond and stem. The basin would be an iron smell, a mineral coolness, and sometimes the tremor of water arriving faster than the roots could hold it.

On the morning I woke, there would be no general stirring.

A drop would strike somewhere above me. The fern would receive it before I did. Another drop might follow, passing from leaf to leaf, and a little of that water would reach the membrane sealing my shell. Slowly, it would soften. I would put out one feeler into air that no longer took everything from me. Then another. Then the broad underside of my foot would touch the fern.

Around me, the other shells would remain closed. There would be no way to tell which of them were waiting and which had already become only shells.

My food would be the almost-nothing that had accumulated while I waited: fungal bloom, algae, the softest beginnings of decay. I would shave it finely from the living surface, taking the film and leaving the tougher tissue beneath. Behind me, my path would be narrow and clean. It would also be temporary. Almost as soon as I had passed, the film would begin closing over it again.

From above, I would appear scarcely to move. But the frond would be a whole country to me—veins like raised roads, old grazings underfoot, sudden steepness where a leaf curled. Water would not be scenery. It would be the condition under which the country existed at all.

The other inhabitants would reach me mostly as disturbances.

A cricket’s message might come through the roots as a shiver too slight to name. A beetle would fall, and the fern would answer with a small, hard tick. When a gecko struck, I might know only the convulsion of the leaf and its long springing back. At dusk, moth wings would loosen the air. Sometimes a wing or a body would come down into the growth, bowing a frond under a weight no larger than a breath.

None of it would be background. Every movement could arrive through the surface beneath my body.

Then, perhaps once in my life, rain would abolish the edges.

Water would stand briefly where there had been only warm powder. The bare places would cease to be barriers. I would cross stone that ordinarily could not be crossed, not out of boldness but because, for a few hours, the journey would not cost more moisture than my body could spare. Around the basin there would be other small bodies everywhere—drinking, hunting, laying, blundering into one another. A shallow runnel might carry me. I might come to rest beneath a root curtain I could never have reached alone.

When the rain withdrew, it would take the roads with it.

First the floor would dry. Then the open stone. The temporary margins would narrow to basins, root cups, and the shaded interiors of leaves. I would find myself in a new colony, perhaps only a short distance from the old one and permanently separated from it. My whole life could be altered by a migration shorter than a hand.

Eventually, whether in the new place or the old, the heat would begin its long closing again. I would retreat beneath a rib. The available film would thin. The air would become something to keep out. I would seal myself once more.

That waiting would not be empty. Inside the shell, a little water would remain water instead of joining the hollow’s warm exhalation. I would be one small vessel among thousands, none sufficient by itself, none aware of the whole.

And if there came a condensation season in which I did not wake, nothing would need to mark the change. What could be used would be used. My shell might remain attached for a while, then fall into a root cup or come to rest beneath the basin rim. It would make a dry curve in the dampness, a little chamber within the chamber. Something smaller could shelter there. Perhaps a drop would lodge briefly in its whorl.

That would be enough. I would not be the hollow’s keeper or its memory. I would be one brief softness inside its hardness: a path that vanished, a little moisture carried farther than it would otherwise have gone, a delay, and then a shelter.

You could hold my shell in your hand. The rest would already have been passed on.
