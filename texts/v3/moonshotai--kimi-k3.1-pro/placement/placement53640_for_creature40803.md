### A Country the Size of a Touch

You would not find me among the pale layers of fish. Their world of pressure and responsive bodies would be too large for me, and their slow crossings of open water too exposed. Nor would I be waiting at a pipe mouth, nearly all my length withheld in darkness.

You would find me, if you found me at all, on the sheltered face of a pier, beneath a surviving lip of glaze. There, protected from the softening that has rounded so much else, is the print of a maker’s thumb.

That print would be my country.

I would be one of the translucent grazers, small enough that its finest ridges had breadth and its shallow furrows offered shelter. My body would contain almost nothing worth hiding: a narrow gut, a faint pulse, a little clear substance held in a jointed covering. Most of the time I would resemble one of the abandoned molts already gripping the masonry. Only the dark thread inside me would distinguish the inhabited animal from the empty one.

Even then, you might have to wait a long while before I did anything.

Eventually one limb would loosen its hold. It would test the surface ahead while the others kept me fixed against the almost imperceptible movement of the water. The limb would touch a ridge, pause, and set down. Somewhere in that slight contact would be taste, resistance, and enough information for another movement—or for none.

I would crop the film lying a little thicker in the print’s recesses. My feeding would not clean it, reveal it, or restore it. I would take only the upper growth, and the film would continue at nearly the pace of my hunger. I would not know that the ridges had been made by a hand. To me they would simply be good terrain: food gathered differently along their slopes, shelter lying on their sheltered side, the glaze above them reducing some disturbance too small for a fish to notice.

The last touch of a human being would endure for me not as history, but as a modest improvement in habitat.

My world would be composed less of objects than of differences. The warm side of the pier would not be warmer by enough to have a name, yet its films would not be quite the same as those around the corner. Seepage would arrive as a faint alteration in taste. Lime would offer one kind of footing, old glaze another. A dead shell valve would be a roof. A rounded recess would be a place to remain when pressure passed through the chamber.

Above me, the hanging crowd of fish would be a kind of weather. I would not see them turn. A change would reach the surfaces instead: an almost imperceptible pressure, then stillness, then the small adjustments of nearby animals. A shell shutting would be a hard note from somewhere beyond my geography. The brief gray admission of daylight would mean nothing. The long wet cycles would mean more, though I would experience them only as gradual changes in the water’s level and flavor.

I would have no reason to cross the cistern. The inlet and overflow would not be opposite ends of a room, but remote conditions that sometimes reached me after being softened by everything between. My farthest journeys might bring me no farther than the margin of a neighboring shell pavement. There would be no view gained by traveling, no larger understanding waiting at the end of a passage. A familiar route would be sufficient: from ridge to furrow, from furrow to the lee of a dead valve, and back when the water had become quiet again.

My young would begin near the surfaces I used. I would not recognize them as descendants, but they would inherit the same nearby accidents—the glaze’s shelter, the print’s texture, the particular film supported there. Generations of us would make almost no alteration except, perhaps, to help worry one already rounded edge a little further from its original angle.

When I molted, there would briefly be two of my shapes and only one of my life. The empty covering would remain fixed to the tile, still gripping, still jointed, still apparently capable of the next small movement. I would be elsewhere, softer and less definite. Later the abandoned shape would release, turn once in the water, and begin a descent too slow to resemble falling.

My own death would not be much larger. Nothing would carry me away whole. What could return to the films and small decomposers would do so by degrees; harder remnants would remain a little longer, perhaps offering shelter before joining the basin’s sediment. I would not persist as a memory in the crowd. But neither would I be entirely separate from what continued.

That is what I would be. Not the cistern’s keeper, witness, or soul—those would all be too large, and too dry. I would be a minute life accommodated by an accident of making: a thumb had pressed clay, glaze had sheltered the impression, film had found its ridges, and I had found the film.

If you waited long enough beside me, you might witness the whole extent of my visible conviction. One clear limb would lift, test the ridge ahead, and set down again.

Then, for a long time, the tile would appear uninhabited.
