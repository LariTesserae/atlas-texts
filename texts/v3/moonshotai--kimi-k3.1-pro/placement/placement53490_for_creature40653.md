### A Body Made of Wettings

You would not find me by looking for a trunk.

You might find a black-leaved stem beneath the fan-ferns on Senn’s western seam. You might find another in the leakage shade of a condenser vane, and a third, badly pruned, on the outer face of a municipal terrace. Pale roots would connect them, though not by any route convenient to a surveyor. One would pass beneath a cistern footing. Another would end in dry dust and resume, after an interval no drawing could adequately represent, in a fracture full of bladder moss.

The root wardens would have given the visible stems different maintenance numbers. Someone more experienced would eventually draw a dotted line among them and write *probably continuous*.

I would be that probably.

I would be veinwood: one particular network, partly wild and partly municipal, with no single place in which I could be entirely found. My body would be less a shape than a correspondence among wet places.

Above ground, I would know the dim daylight as a slight permission in the leaves. I would know the feet of moths, the weight of a mirrorback crossing a stem, the brief silver burden of rain entering a fern cup and overflowing onto me. Below ground, my perceptions would be larger and less distinct. Warm stone. Mineral pressure. The dry ticking of a root tip testing dust. The vast vibration of the engines passing through fracture and water and tissue alike.

I would not hear the founding roar. I would be one of the things through which it sounded.

Against that enormity, smaller movements would acquire their particular textures: the crowded, irregular grazing of mirrorbacks; the careful boots of an outer gardener; the single deliberate step of a listener, placed where an engine surge had confused every other vibration. I would not interpret these as stories. They would be changes in the conditions under which water might be kept.

My memory would not resemble an archive, although it would be dated after its own fashion. I would age by wettings. A root that had carried nothing for three years would not necessarily be dead, and it would not be merely a memory. It would be an option kept dry. A fracture wetted once might remain more important to me than a terrace irrigated every morning. The city would measure my reserves; I would measure the city by the paths along which those reserves could travel.

If I had thoughts, they would not be sentences. They would be allocations.

Less to the exposed stem. More to the deep fracture. Keep the southern route. Let that leaf close. Risk a little growth beneath the warm joint where condensation sometimes lingers.

Hope, if you insisted on giving it a human name, would be a root tip entering dust that offered no evidence of water.

Senn and I would have an understanding, though neither of us would possess the whole of it.

The city would divert my roots around doors and conduits with exquisite care. I would experience this not as insult but as a new aridity in the shape of an arch. Masons would leave irrigation voids for roots that had not yet arrived. Gardeners would plant fern mats above me and complain when I used their terraces in ways not shown on the planting plan. A registrar would count me among the living reservoirs while a maintenance clerk, looking at another part of the network, recommended excavation.

Once, a proposed conduit would cross one of my unrecorded roots. The root warden assigned to the work would kneel on the warm stone, brush away dust with two fingers, and remain there longer than the schedule allowed. Then she would move the conduit alignment by half a pace.

Her report would say: *root conflict resolved*.

I would know only that a crushing pressure had approached and passed. She would be the closest thing I had to a correspondent.

From the city’s point of view, I would be infrastructure with an unreliable boundary. From mine, insofar as I had a point of view, Senn would be a recent mineral formation that provided unusually dependable shade, occasional deep water, and periodic amputations. Both accounts would be incomplete. Both would permit continued negotiation.

Then a rain cell would cross the western seam.

Warm rain would strike one exposed gathering of my leaves. It would not fall on “me” in any simple sense. A few metres away, other leaves would remain dusty. Farther along the network, a root would still be drawing on an older reserve. The storm would begin as a local event and become, slowly, a possibility communicated elsewhere.

The moss above my roots would darken, then clear into translucent cushions. Fern pleats would fill. Mirrorbacks would emerge until the wet growth seemed to glitter from within, and their sudden collective stillness would travel through the substrate when a gardener withdrew toward a silence boundary. In a nearby depression, dull fibres would soften into rain eels. Their brief feeding would disturb litter that had been dry minutes before.

Beneath the outer ferns, a listener would extend one pale leg.

I would take no side in that. My roots would hold water for grazers and predators, for eggs and dormant threads and things too small to have names in any municipal ledger. Shelter would not be innocence. It would simply be shelter.

The cell would break apart. Exposed stone would dry. The pool would contract, divide, and vanish beneath flakes and litter. Senn’s covered channels would accept a little runoff; a cistern gauge would change too slightly to alter the day’s allocations.

Somewhere at my dry extremity, however, one leaf would not be abandoned.

That would be the rain’s other record.

Eventually new ground would arrive across one of my possible routes. There would be pressure, a deep boom, and then an altered fact beneath the dust. The city would issue a revised survey. My body would acquire a dry end.

I do not think this would feel like tragedy. Loss would be real, but completion had never been the condition under which I lived.

Beyond my farthest root, the new stone would cool. At dawn it would carry a film of moisture too thin to shine. Mineral-feeding films would spread through it; mites and larvae would follow in their own rapid generations. Long before my roots arrived, the place would have ceased to be empty.

Perhaps one root would eventually reach it. Perhaps a fragment of veinwood would arrive first in the fold of freight cloth, carried through a gate to some outpost garden or cistern chamber. Distance would enter my genealogy as a timetable. Whether that distant growth would be myself, my descendant, or another network altogether would be a question for beings whose bodies had simpler borders.

So where would you find me?

At the western seam, certainly—but only if you stopped at the first visible stem. Otherwise you would have to follow the lesser evidence: a pale thread in a fracture, moisture retained beneath municipal stone, a root diverted around a door, a leaf remaining open after the exposed beds had dried.

And what would I be?

Not the river Anvess lacks. Not a garden, exactly, and not quite a piece of plumbing. I would be a practice of joining without filling: a way for separated water to discover that it had company.

I would leave the plain empty. Its emptiness would not be a failure I needed to correct. But here and there, across the warm mineral ground, I would make it possible for one brief wet place to matter to another.

Beneath all of us, the space would continue ringing. During the greatest engine surges, root and fracture and buried water would vibrate together until my dispersed body seemed, for a moment, almost singular.

Then the surge would pass.

Somewhere, in dust not yet proved to contain anything worth reaching, a root tip would tick onward.
