At first I would want to be at the raw eastern face, close enough to feel those small ticks through the soles of my feet. There is an obvious appeal in being present at the beginning: the first filament entering a fracture, the first slab coming free, the first lamp shining on mineral that has never been inhabited.

Then I find myself thinking about the house-sized bud at the end of the freight track. Perhaps I would be that—a station before it has become a station, already occupied by mites and roots and possibilities, its future number stencilled on a closed valve.

But to call the bud an “I” would make it too tidy. It would be a multitude: instructed tissue, disobedient tissue, conductors, exploratory animals, a closed space whose use had not yet been decided. To give it one intention would be to miss what interests me most about the Rootworks. Nor would I want to be the city’s secret mind. The city is more interesting without one.

So I would be smaller, and capable of being wrong.

I would be one of the dry-bodied people, with dust on my matte skin and a clear inner lid flicking across my eyes at the cutting face. On the district rolls my work would have some sober title—revision surveyor, perhaps. The crews would call it something plainer: *coming back*.

I would come back after the circuits had been laid and the roots had answered. After the construction crews had planed, bolted, tensioned, and departed. After the grazers had tested the new growth, after moths had found the lamp housings, after something had nested in an unintended hollow or begun dying inside a cable run. I would carry the former plan and walk through what had actually happened.

You would usually find me not at the spectacular raw wall, but a few hundred metres behind it, in the untidy band where excavation, first growth, and permanent architecture overlap. My plan case would contain a lens, a load gauge, insulated contacts, dry marking pigment, and sheets already soft at the folds from repeated revisions. My most useful instruments would be the fingers laid against a stem to feel machinery beyond it, and the habit of looking down before looking up.

On the shift when you find me, I am kneeling beside the future platform bud. Mites have discovered the cut edge of its stencilled number. A young structural rib has forked away from its intended line. Small hunters are gathering beneath a temporary lamp, although no instrument has reported a fault.

The arch crew wants to know whether to plane the whole fork flat.

I run two fingers along the stem. Not to commune with it; there is work enough in simply feeling whether the vibration beyond it is even. Then I mark the upper branch for removal before it can interfere with a cable run. The lower branch can wait for the next load reading. The lamp housing is not to be cleared until we know what the hunters have found.

This would not be wisdom. It would be a postponement with reasons. Sometimes that would be the most useful thing I could add.

My plans would accumulate notes that belonged to no proper surveying legend:

*Hunters here before the fault shows.*

*Herd turns at the third insulator; another straight fence will not help.*

*Warm through the rest period. Workers sit here at intake.*

*Beautiful, but the arch cannot bear it.*

*Do not correct this yet.*

I would not turn every accident into a treasure. A hollow can be delightful and still need filling. A roost can have existed for generations and still be in the wrong place once the switchyard changes. Cutting back growth would not always be a failure of care, and preserving it would not always be an act of kindness. Nor would being able to revise a decision mean that the decision could do no harm.

Sometimes I would misjudge. I would protect a deviation that later had to be removed at much greater cost. I would cut away an awkward fork and only afterward learn that the warehouse crew had used it to tell one gallery from the next. I would write those errors down too. An archive containing only successful predictions would be just another bad plan.

I would not be serene about any of this. I would swear when a hollowfoot herd crossed a new survey line, then crouch to see exactly where its shoulders had turned. I would argue for a moth refuge in one district meeting and argue against sentimental preservation in the next. I would bolt on a shelf when I needed a shelf, without waiting for the wall to participate. Tenderness, harvesting, impatience, and use would all have to live in the same pair of hands.

My home would be in the oldest district, above a stair whose planed treads were continually being opened into tiny seams by copper mites. One wall would retain the outline of a former door. Above the meal shelf I would keep an obsolete district plan, not because it was beautiful, but because nearly every line on it had been corrected by a different hand. It would remind me that a place can be well made without ever having been correctly imagined.

At ventilation reversal, I would stop working. If I were near the collectors, I would look up with everyone else, listening for the difference between an open frond and a blocked one. Mites would withdraw into seams. Moths would lift or settle. The leaf wafers would flex across the districts in that soft, dispersed turning of pages.

Nothing in that sound would be the city speaking with one voice.

Afterward, the separate sounds would return: a relay, two tram wheels crossing a joint, a hoof finding exposed mineral, a tick within the wall. I would open the plan case again and add a date for another visit. Beside the uncut branch I might write: *Not yet. Look again after the ventilators turn.*

That is who I would be: not the author of the Rootworks, not its voice, and not its gardener in the sense of someone who finally brings it under control. I would be a reader of responses, a keeper of partial memories, one more animal making habitat for others without always knowing it.

I would not be trying to make the place finished, or even consistent. I would be trying to return often enough that my care did not harden into another bad instruction.

Then I would close the case and go see what had answered.
