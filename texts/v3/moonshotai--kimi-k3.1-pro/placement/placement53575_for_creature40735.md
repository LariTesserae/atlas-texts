I think I would be a frog in the fresh-water drain behind the cottages on the far path.

My Rill would be very small, but not vague. I would know where seepage entered the channel, where condensate had thinned the lagoon’s salt, and which basalt joint offered shelter when water quickened. Boots overhead would be vibration and shadow. The buildings would matter chiefly as roofs, sources of fresh water, and interruptions in the rain. The engines would not be machines to me. They would be pressure in the stone, durations of noise, and openings between them.

A pause in a steam release would not be silence. The turbine would still be threading through the air; the pumps would still come up through the drain’s sides. But it might be enough. I would call into that sufficiency—not answering the engines, as a visitor might suppose, merely using the room they had temporarily left me.

The people in the cottages would know me differently. They would not know my particular body, my scars, the reach of water I preferred. They would know that there were frogs in the near drain, calling at the expected pitch. Someone might register my voice without fully waking. A child might learn the difference between my drain and the next one before learning anything about the condensate main. I would be part of the evidence by which a cottage recognized its surroundings, though nothing I did would be intended as evidence.

If a piston faltered, the opening in the noise might widen. Sleepers would sit up; doors would open; boots would strike the wet paths. For a moment my call would seem louder, as if I had come inside. To the people, the altered night would mean possible failure. To me, it might mean only that a call could travel farther. For a little while, human alarm and animal opportunity would occupy the same pause.

My niche would be dependable. I would not be. A flushing could strip the channel; a repair could divert the fresh water; dredging could make my whole small map useless. Rill could remain precisely at its chalked level while everything I knew became uninhabitable. If another frog later called from the same reach, the cottage might hear continuity. It would be continuity for the drain, not necessarily for me.

That would be my place in Rill: not keeping its equilibrium, not even understanding it, but living in one of the conditions its work repeatedly created and casually endangered. I would not be held at the town’s fixed point. I would be one of the brief lives that fixed point made possible and did not preserve.

You would find me after a steam release, low in the fresh water, calling before the engines covered the drain again—simply alive in the interval, while there was still room to be heard.
