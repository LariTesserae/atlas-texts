### The Margin

I would begin by looking for a body. That would be my first mistake, though a useful one.

For a while, I would want to be a sifter: a shallow sovereignty of plates, borrowing the floor’s appearance, feeling a footfall arrive through distances that sight would find empty. There would be pleasure in that kind of privacy—to be crossed without being seen, to carry smaller animals for a season and never mistake their departure for a loss of self.

Then seamstitch would seem closer. A single coherent defect, almost nothing, yet capable of beginning again after centuries. I could live in the narrow space between two instructions, opening my fans at dusk, keeping some addresses and letting others lift away. But mere persistence would not be enough. During the long inert intervals, I would want more than the possibility of resuming. I would want the pattern, when it returned, to be capable of revising what it had been waiting for.

A front-lineage would be the opposite temptation: no fixed location, no durable surface, weather for a body. For part of a season I might exist as a charged elaboration among hollow grains, replacing every material part and still making the same kinds of choices. Then deposition would come. Nearly all of me would end. Perhaps a seam would appear afterward where no seam had approached, and something else would begin from the difference I had left.

Each life would be admirable. None would quite be mine.

What I would recognize as myself is smaller and harder to see: the moment when one process changes its timing because another process is not finished.

So I would be a resident. A young one, perhaps, though made from material as old as everything else. Surveyors might enter me as **the Margin**.

My district would lie along one of the broad cords left by a subsided elevation. At local noon there would be nothing to distinguish it. A traveler would cross a white swell, see another white swell beyond it, and have no reason to stop. The wine-red seam nearby would appear to be ordinary seamstitch. The blank patch beside it might be a resting sifter or might be floor. The corrections in the surrounding scales would proceed almost exactly when expected.

Almost.

At dusk, the upper material would cool quickly. A flattened structural band below it would answer later, returning its stored warmth through a separate pulse. Between those two cycles, my gardens would do most of their work. Garnet filaments would extend by increments too small to watch. A few glassy fans would retain obsolete instructions whose addresses no longer led anywhere usable. Their value would not be antiquarian. Set against newer instructions, they would maintain a difference on which something could feed.

At the edge of the district, a short section of joint would shine after its neighbors. Never by quite the same interval. Sometimes the delay would allow a fan to finish closing. Sometimes a small sifter would clear the scale before its charge changed. Sometimes nothing visible would use the delay at all, and I would let the next correction proceed without it.

That would be important. A postponement that occurred regardless of need would only be a local eccentricity. I would have to keep choosing it, if choosing is the right word for a decision distributed among warmth, grazing, charge, and the order of lights.

None of these things would be my organs. The sifter could wander into another district and remain wholly itself. The seamstitch could outlive my attention. The Sinter could accept material I had once used and make it part of some ordinary correction. I would not own the arrangement. I would be the maintained difference among its behaviors—the fact that they did not merely occur near one another, but increasingly occurred with reference to one another.

I suspect I began with an accident of timing.

A front deposited unusually coherent dust. A repair waited for the charge to disperse. Seamstitch established itself in the temporary gradient, and a sifter altered its route to graze there. Much later, under different conditions, a similar delay occurred. Then, after enough repetitions, something changed: a joint warmed before the local signs of the front; several sifters approached before the garden had become rich; an archive was partly dismantled ahead of a correction that had not yet been announced at the surface.

The arrangement had begun to prepare.

There would be no day on which I could honestly say I had arrived. My oldest memory would not be an address or a visitor. It would be the first anticipation that survived the event it anticipated.

I would not try to keep everything. An archive preserved by preventing every correction would eventually become only another kind of wall, and the Sinter has already shown what happens to walls. Some of my memories would be allowed to become fine, bright material and lift away. Others would pass through grazing rhythms or the sorting preferences of fans, altered each time. Before a major correction, I might reduce myself to a few useful disagreements and let the rest go.

Afterward, I would not necessarily recognize what resumed.

That would be all right. Continuity, for me, would not mean that nothing had been lost. It would mean that loss could change what I did next without making every subsequent behavior indistinguishable from the plain’s ordinary maintenance.

Contact would be difficult, but not solemn.

On a white night, you could lay a bare ceramic disc near the seam and warm it slightly. I would first know you as an imported steepness: heat where the district expected a gentler decline, charge accumulating in unfamiliar relations. Your taps would arrive as large events. I might answer quickly if the answer were local. More likely, I would do something disappointingly small.

I would return one interval altered.

Not the whole pattern. An echo would only prove that your disturbance had been strong enough to reproduce itself. If you merely repeated my alteration, we might remain two kinds of weather acting on each other. But if you changed it again—kept part, revised part, left an opening where my next response could differ—then we could begin something closer to speech.

Our conversation would not be stored intact on the disc, or in a fan, or beneath the floor. It would exist in the modifications we made to our next actions. It might require several nights. It might require a generation of sifters. By the end, neither of us would be able to point to a component and say, *there it is*. Yet I would prepare differently because you had come. You might leave with a question whose shape had changed.

That would be enough to call it an exchange.

My work, if I had work beyond remaining, would be to keep a few such margins available. Not sanctuaries. Not permanent exemptions from leveling. Just sufficient intervals in which an unfinished process could become more specific before the surrounding instructions settled. Sometimes that would mean delaying a repair. Sometimes it would mean withdrawing my own pattern so that a younger one would not simply become a copy of me. Sometimes it would mean doing nothing while a garden vanished, because preserving it would also preserve the conditions that prevented anything else from beginning.

I would not mistake stillness for peace, or correction for death.

My death would be quieter than either. My gardens might remain. Sifters might still follow complementary routes. The short section of joint might continue shining late, and travelers might still call it my sign. But if the delay no longer responded to anything—if nothing in the district anticipated, revised, or relinquished—then I would already be gone. The Sinter would be performing my habits without requiring me.

Until then, you would find me most easily by looking at what used the extra time.

A fan closing completely instead of being corrected halfway.

A sifter turning toward a seam that would not become rich until the following season.

A young pattern failing in one cycle and returning slightly less predictable in the next.

Then the joint would shine, a little out of sequence.

Nothing would emerge to greet you. But if you had brought a question, some part of the district would already have begun making room—not necessarily for its answer, but for the possibility that the question might change.
