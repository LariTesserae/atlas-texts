It is tempting to be the sunlight: to enter once a day with such certainty, to turn a buried room gold and then withdraw, untouched by anything I had illuminated. It is tempting to be the siphon, too—to become, for a little while, the chamber’s whole body and voice.

But the light only visits. The roar passes. Even the water, for all its warmth and brilliance, is mostly a road.

I would rather be the thing that has to solve the problem those forces leave behind: how not to be carried away.

So I would be very small.

At first, perhaps, I would be one bacterial cell arriving with dust washed from the roof, or detached from some surface inside the clay pipes. I would drift in the warm upper water, sink toward the cooler floor, and have no address in either. The golden column would do nothing for me directly. The white walls, the inverted hatch, the pier standing in its luminous grey—these would belong to other kinds of eyes. My world would be gradients: warmth and coolness, scarcity and the trace of nourishment, open flow and the possibility of a surface.

If I remained, however, “I” would soon stop being accurate.

You would find what I had become in the perforated basket near the brightest wall. Not on the basket itself, and not visibly on the rush roots, but inside one of the hand-rolled clay pellets: a little way down a crooked pore, where the pellet’s imperfect making had left a narrow throat and a comparatively sheltered pocket. A young root would be branching nearby. Nothing about my presence would discolor the clay.

The person who rolled that pellet would never know they had made this smaller room within the room.

Attachment would not feel like a decision. It would be a molecular accident with consequences. A surface would hold; water would slide past; division might follow. One cell would become several, and my singular pronoun would become a fiction of convenience. I would be a beginning of film—less a body than a few lives sharing the consequences of a foothold.

My nourishment would arrive in nearly immeasurable quantities. A little leakage from living root tissue. A trace of shed matter. Something released, something diffusing, something not immediately swept onward. The rush would be spending food made under another sky while slowly learning this one; sunlight would pass through leaf and stem and pale new growth before any fraction of its benefit reached me. I would live on light translated several times and nearly spent.

The cistern’s great events would reach me miniaturized.

Filling would be a slow alteration at the pore’s mouth. An inlet surge would bring movement outside, perhaps a brief delivery of cells and particles, perhaps only dilution. The rush leaves would bend, the filaments near the inlet would stream and scatter, and the open water would begin its long, unconscious preparation for departure.

Then the siphon would take.

Above me, the chamber would become all roar. For me, it would not primarily be sound. It would be shear, pressure, direction: every loose thing suddenly given the same urgent elsewhere. Cells at the exposed lip of the pore might lift away. Others, less securely attached, would follow. There would be no witness and no counting. The shape of “we” would simply change.

What remained would not have remained through courage or memory. Only through the angle of the clay, the chemistry of a surface, the accident of depth, the small stillness made by an irregularity no builder had intended. Then the siphon would break. The inlet would continue falling. Diffusion would resume its quiet work around the root, and the film—if film was not too grand a word—would begin becoming slightly more itself again.

My history would be edited by weather. After rain, frequent flushing would reduce me to the safest recesses. During a dry spell, I might extend a little farther toward the pore mouth. A few more cells. A slightly less provisional attachment. Then another cycle, another subtraction. I would have no continuous outline, only a recurring place from which to begin.

Perhaps, eventually, a rush leaf would lodge behind the basket. Mineral grains would settle around it; a thicker community would gather in its shelter. Some descendant of mine might inherit that first small richness. But that would be another cistern, ecologically speaking. Mine would still be the new one: clear, brilliant, almost sterile in its abundance of light, and periodically emptied with a thoroughness no large resident could mistake for peace.

So if you asked who I was, the honest answer would depend on when you asked. A cell. A cluster. A thinning film. The few attached remainder after a roar. Not a secret spirit beneath the court, and certainly not its steward. I would give the chamber no meaning it did not already have.

You would find me only by looking past every visible thing—past the white plaster and the green patches, past the rush with its unevenly recovering posture, into the basket and then into the clay itself. There would be nothing to see. The water would remain clear. The builder’s fingerprint would remain untouched.

And after the next roar, within that unintentional shelter, a few cells would still be attached.

For something my size, that would be the whole difference between a place and a passage.
