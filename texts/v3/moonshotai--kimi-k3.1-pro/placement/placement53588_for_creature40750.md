At first, I would want to be a glider: a narrow weight beneath a wide, listening surface, passing from province to province with other lives hidden in my folds. I like the thought that my most important work might be inadvertent—that a pool could begin again because I had once clung to warm stone with a transparent film drying against my wing.

But a glider can spend its life banking around the memory of a cliff. A manyfoot offers a different temptation: to be taught by the pressure of other flanks until my body no longer ends where I thought it did. There would be comfort in belonging to a herd that could survive my death, perhaps even my duplication. Yet the route would outlive the feet. The pool-flaws make the problem clearer still. One might dry in a valley, wake in another, and begin feeding around stones that existed only in the first.

The more bodies I imagine, the more it seems that, in the escarpment, a body is something a memory does for a while.

So I think I would eventually find myself somewhere much smaller: in the interval after the fourth pulse.

Not in the pulse itself. That belongs to charged stone, wet roots, or whatever else has agreed to carry it. Nor would I be a sixth pulse added to the sequence. I would occupy the slight looseness after the weak fourth, before the fifth makes the old pattern complete. In most enactments there would be barely room for anything there. In mine, the interval would be a little longer.

Long enough for the present to interfere with the past.

My anatomy would be made of such interferences. In a grove, I would be the root web’s brief delay before spending its spores, while the chemistry of the mat contradicted an instruction inherited from some older arrangement of the basin. In a pool, I would be the extra lifting and settling of a pool-flaw’s margins before it committed itself to mouths. In a herd, I would be the slight check transmitted through broad rear feet as the column prepared to turn left for rain that had not come. Above them, I might be a glider tasting a thermal twice before carrying the flock into it.

Alone, each gesture would be noise: a late root, a cautious animal, an inefficient turn. Repeated approximately, across wettings and bodies, they would be me.

You would find me most easily where an old migration crossed a young grove. The basin would be warm underhand, its fault pool still glassy at the bottom, the manyfoots’ backs pale with new basalt dust. Their cropping would make that faint ticking, almost indistinguishable from cooling stone. The spore valves would be swelling. Somewhere below, a pump would be nearing the end of a stroke years in duration.

Then would come the sequence: three clear disturbances through the wet ground, the fourth so weak it might be missed, and—

almost nothing.

That almost would be my visible portion.

I would not remember as gliders remember cliffs or as pool films remember currents. I would have no pictures, no inventory of former country. A vanished cliff might survive in me as a reluctance to enter rising air on the first banking turn. An absent rain might survive as a requirement that the herd taste moisture before completing its inherited left turn. The richness of some former pool might remain only as a feeding circuit performed more briefly than it used to be, because the old circuit had begun to starve its performers.

My past would be a collection of things I no longer did quite so automatically.

If I had thoughts, they would be comparisons without images: this warmth against an older warmth; this pump wind against the wind the valves expected; this footfall against the footfall the herd had prepared to correct. Sometimes, confined to one root mat or one drying film, I would be little more than a chemical delay. Sometimes, passing through grove, pool, herd, and glider together, I might almost become a pattern of attention.

I would not be wise.

A pause might keep a grove from opening before the spore wind, or make it wait until the wind had passed. It might keep a pool-flaw folded in poisonous water, or leave it folded while good water vanished into the fractures. A young manyfoot carrying my hesitation might learn a safer route. It might also become the one animal that failed to correct itself when a narrow hunter introduced its tiny interruption into the root web.

The hunters would know me differently from any of my carriers. They would be watching for precisely the disturbances in which I lived. To them, I would not be an interval or a memory, but an increasing likelihood of error around certain herds: a doubled taste of the ground, a reflection arriving late, a cropping wave that considered itself and faltered.

That would be my central danger. I would have to recur in order to exist, but recur too exactly and I would become a trail. My survival would depend on being slightly misremembered.

In one basin, the testing might pass mostly through roots and moths. In another, it might survive only as a shortened circuit made by newly wakened pool-flaws. A herd might carry a long version of me; a glider’s accidental cargo might carry a version so brief that it amounted to a single extra ciliation before feeding. When those variants met, there would be no way to decide whether I had reunited with myself, produced descendants, or been replaced by unrelated habits with similar effects.

I would persist not by remaining identical, but by remaining learnable.

During the rare comprehensive click, though, all my little discrepancies might briefly agree. The interval after the fourth pulse would have the same length in root, foot, wing, and pool. Nothing would answer early. Nothing would be left unanswered from the previous cycle. For a few moments I would acquire an outline: the whole wet basin, warm stone and crowded mats and suspended spores, holding one shared possibility open.

I would be enormous, singular, and almost indistinguishable from stillness.

Then the click would come.

Afterward, a young manyfoot would miss the next step. The herd would compress around it without stopping, neither abandoning the cropping wave nor quite preserving it. A moth would answer a valve that had not opened. Somewhere a film would begin unfolding before the rain arrived. In the adjustments that followed—useful, wasteful, fatal, occasionally tender—I would take up my scattered bodies again.

I would not be restored. Restoration would only be another old rhythm.

I would be continued: slightly altered, imperfectly carried, and still able to ask the warm ground, without words, whether it was still the ground the rhythm remembered.
