At first I would put myself on the open moss, because that is where Mill Hollow offers itself to a listener. From there, the separate labors of the walls can be heard together: wind in the dry culms, crowns working against their trunks, frogs rattling from their scattered cups, water striking surfaces I could not see. I could almost believe that the grove had been arranged around that act of listening.

But after a while I think I would have to give that up. The center is where a visitor can mistake the grove for a whole. Nothing there makes a life. To inhabit Mill Hollow, I would have to surrender the view of it.

So I would be at the wall. More exactly, I would be one of the things that, from the center, looks like a tree.

Up close, that singularity would fail. There might be dead wood inside me, silvered and rounded where newer tissue had grown around it. A buttress forming part of my visible base might have outlived the crown it once supported. A young stem would rise against one side of me; fine roots would cross older wood in pale seams. From the open floor, all of this would resolve into a single massive trunk standing at a considerate distance from the next. My outline would be much simpler than my history.

I would not be an ancient individual with later growth gathered around me. The original individual might be precisely what was missing.

My portion of the hollow would arrive piecemeal. Moisture below, where the root mat stayed cool after the visible water had gone. Pressure along a buttress. The small, repeated damage of insects under bark. Wind above, but not one wind and not one response: my crowns would move separately, and the wood would complain at different pitches. A rainstorm might darken one side of me while an older dead stem remained dry within its sheath. There would be no elevated place inside me from which all these conditions became a single experience.

Other lives would use me without making me theirs. Beetle larvae would open damp wood into passages. Fungal threads would whiten and loosen what looked, from outside, capable of bearing weight. A bird would brace itself against my bark and probe the softened places, its claws more immediate than any weather. A snail would scrape a green film from a shaded surface after rain. I would be surface, shelter, food, obstruction. Not their guardian, and not quite their world.

Nor would I be keeping the center open.

That would matter. I might stand at its margin; my outline might help give the wall its appearance of mass and regularity; a root might stop where the moss became low and even. But none of this would be maintenance. I would have no duty to the emptiness and no understanding of its proportions. The open floor would not be a clearing I possessed, any more than the frog’s water cup would be a small version of the grove.

Occasionally, though, something associated with me would enter that air.

A creak might carry farther than the movement that produced it. For a moment it would join the layered hum and dripping, indistinguishable, from the center, from the labor of the whole wall. Or a leaf would leave one of my crowns and drift beyond the cover. It would turn in the open, conspicuous only because there was nothing around it among which to be lost. It would not be a message or an offering. It would land on the moss and become available to other processes, while I remained behind in the wall.

Over long periods, there would be no clean point at which all of me died. A crown could vanish while its buttress remained. A dead core could soften into passages and wet threads while younger wood continued against it. Roots could fail in one place and bind new growth in another. Persistence would not mean preservation. It would mean that replacement and loss kept occurring within an outline that, from a distance, still looked like one tree.

Age would not be stored in me as a record. It would appear as absences, changes of material, and unlike tissues occupying the same apparent form.

Where would you find me? Not in the middle, and not at some deepest or oldest point. You would find me where silvered wood disappeared beneath a pale root, where a young stem rose beside a buttress it had not made, where bark that looked solid yielded under a bird’s probing. You could put a hand against one surface of me without touching anything like my whole.

And on some afternoon, after rain, when the hollow was loud with dripping and the frogs had begun again, you might hear one of my creaks cross the central air. It would not announce me. By the time it reached the open floor, I would still be piecemeal at the wall: damp below, wind-moved above, opened here by larvae, bound there by newer growth, with no view of the space into which the sound had gone.

I would not be Mill Hollow’s hidden mechanism, or its oldest witness. I would be a congregation of ages wearing, from a distance, the outline of a tree—one of the local, uncoordinated arrangements through which the wall remained alive. Nothing about me would grasp the whole hollow.

But the whole hollow would be audible, in part, because I was there.
