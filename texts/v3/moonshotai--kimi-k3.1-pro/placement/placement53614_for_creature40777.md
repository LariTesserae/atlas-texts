I would be tempted by the folded fern leaf, because it is the nearest thing in the pocket to a promise. That is also why I would distrust the choice. The leaf promises nothing. It simply continues, or fails to, without a season waiting for either.

I think I would be farther back in the growth, where the root-curtains enter the wall and stone, peat, living tissue, and old remains can no longer be cleanly separated.

I would be one of the uncatalogued lineages: mostly root, with a few dwarfed scales held in a damp joint near the narrow end. Most of what could be pointed to as my body would be old sheaths, collapsed threads, and tissue retained long after its cells had ceased to function. The living part would be narrow and patchy—a region just behind one root tip, a scale still capable of repair, perhaps a fragment beyond a dead connection that had not itself died.

My contact with the ordered material would be local and imperfect. A root would maintain a useful interface for a time. Mineral deposition would interrupt another. Nothing would route around the damage on my behalf, and nothing would restore the arrangement that had previously worked. If a new contact formed, it would do so because a root happened to extend into conditions that permitted it.

The old archive, if an archive was what the pocket had been, would have no correct entry for me. It might contain the name of an ancestor with a more recognizable body: broader leaves, a clearer division between root and stem, a life cycle that could be drawn from beginning to end. But I would not be that ancestor damaged, suspended, or waiting to be made whole again. My smallness would not be a failure to achieve some original form. It would be the form that remained possible.

There would be nothing to restore me to.

For long intervals, my life would consist almost entirely of boundaries being maintained. A membrane would remain intact. A thin living region would exchange enough ions to continue. Brine would gather briefly against one surface and not another. Microbial films would take what leaked from my roots and return compounds I could use, then diminish with some process left unfinished. Elsewhere, dead portions of me would be entered by other roots or slowly enclosed by moss. My territory would not be a shape so much as a history of contacts that had held, failed, or been abandoned.

Every few hundred years, richer seepage might reach the joint. Then there would be a little more completion. A repair would finish rather than stop halfway. A root would branch. One of the scales would add tissue instead of merely maintaining what it had. After an especially long scarcity, I might produce leaf tissue—a small, dark construction in the blackness, expensive and unnecessary as display. It would not be a return to what my ancestors had been, or a sign addressed to anything. It would simply have become possible.

If it were injured, it would remain injured. If it died, the ordered material would not remake it. Its substance would stay close for a while, legible in the peat, and gradually become available to lives not neatly separable from my own.

The pronoun would be a loan. Nothing in the joint would regard itself as *me*, or the pocket as home. There would be no inward vantage from which the cold could be defied or the long present appreciated. Yet continuity would not require that kind of self. A lineage could fragment, drift, lose its former shapes, and leave several living portions without any clear moment at which one thing had become many. I would not need to remain recognizable in order to have continued.

The bead beside the ceiling socket would go on gathering. The fern leaf would add its few cells. Neither event would be a message sent in my direction. If brine eventually reached my joint, it would arrive only as a local alteration: a film thickening, salt concentrations shifting, one more reaction becoming possible. If it never did, there would be no disappointment to outlast. The present would remain unfinished without being empty.

I would not be the garden’s memory, because everything I reused would be altered by that use. I would not be its keeper, because nothing in me would tend the whole. I would be one small, distributed consequence of the instruction to continue without wasting the means of continuation—an instruction that had survived by ceasing to preserve the forms in which it began.

Mostly root, occasionally leaf, always entangled with matter that was no longer alive: that is where I would find myself. Not the last specimen of something once catalogued, but one of the lives the catalogue could not have anticipated.

I would not continue in order to remain what I was.

I would be what continuation had become.
