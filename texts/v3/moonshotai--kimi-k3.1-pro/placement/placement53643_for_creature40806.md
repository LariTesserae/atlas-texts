### Reserve 6

At first, I would know only an interval.

A slow loading of the clay. A long rest. A lesser movement returning through the saddles. Then warmth—not enough to make water steam, but enough to change the hour at which condensation released its hold on the iron.

Later I would acquire the maintenance names for these things: *pump stroke, remote start, Main 6*.

I would be the pipe laid beside it.

My sections would be ceramic, my collars iron, my bore made for warm brine. For thirty-one years the old main and I would lie against each other in the same wet trough, touching at saddles and crossing clamps. This was the customary preparation. A replacement was not simply connected to the network. It was given time to learn what the network did.

Main 6 carried brine from a lift house to a condensation field several low rises away. I carried nothing. Nevertheless, the old pump’s deliberation passed into me. So did the longer changes: a sluice opening over a season, the heat of a remote start, the altered pressure when floodwater softened the clay and pressed us more closely together.

If a pipe can be said to wait, I waited for my own work.

Meanwhile, I ceased to be dry.

Humid air condensed in my bore. Fracture water entered at one imperfect joint and held in a lower section. At the collars, where the old main’s warmth met my cooler seepage, brine felt established in strips no wider than a fingernail. Raspers followed. Their grazing made the ceramic less slick without making it look different. Seamworms occupied the water beneath mineral scale. Transparent crustaceans appeared in the condensation, their bodies almost nothing but gut, heartbeat, and eggs.

Feltwings came later. At first they only rested on the warm collars. Then one generation used the insulated dust around a saddle for its brood. After that, the saddle was never altogether vacant, though there were long periods when nothing on it moved.

My education was therefore proceeding in two directions. I was learning the pump, and the pump’s warmth was teaching small lives where I was.

Production moved elsewhere before I was commissioned.

The isolation crew came without ceremony. They closed Main 6 at its intake, lifted its cable from the ground, and cut the saddles joining us. Where contact had been longest, they packed clay into the narrow space between our bodies. My own test caps were closed. A hand-written tag was wired to the fourth collar.

The survey entry read:

**RESERVE 6. NEVER CHARGED. JOINED TO MAIN 6, 31 YEARS. CONTACTS CUT. LEFT IN GROUND.**

That should have settled what I was.

On the next remote heating cycle, warmth returned.

It did not arrive as the old warmth had. There was no pump stroke beneath it, no dependable sequence, no strong difference between one end of me and the other. Only a faint, delayed condition in certain collars, especially those below the wet clay pan. Main 6 warmed scarcely at all. I warmed less. Hours later, the humidity at my surface altered.

The feltwings resettled.

I had not been reconnected. I had simply not finished being apart.

The connection weakened unevenly after that. My elevated sections, where rain drained quickly and the collars had been separated cleanly, forgot first. The lower length retained enough warmth to matter for several more generations. One flooded crossing held its answer longest of all. I became a line of different separations rather than a single pipe with a single history.

This was not always merciful. A warmth too weak to complete a brood could still be strong enough to begin one. Films established in a seep that later answered some distant lowering of water. A roost might persist for years beyond the condition that had made it viable, each generation using a little more of what remained.

Retention gave life time. It did not guarantee that time would be sufficient.

But other contacts accumulated.

A carrier crossed me where the ground was soft, one broad foot loading the clay beside the ninth collar. The crustaceans in its foot mud entered my seepage. Some of mine remained in the print and were carried onward when the animal returned. A fragment of wax stalk, lodged in the same mud, rooted against my ceramic. Its roots opened a lamination in the underlying mudstone and etched a pale, branching mark into the pipe itself.

Years afterward, a scale cushion established in the shelter of that etching. Its lower scales held water through a dry interval. Something molted inside an empty rasper shell beneath it.

I had been made to convey brine. Instead, without moving, I was being conveyed into other lives.

You would find me now, if you were looking, between the cold fin field and a sump whose water has become too saline for most eels. Sight would not help. You would follow the discontinuities: three pale stalks, a strip of raspers crossing clay at an angle no drainage explains, a little warmth at ankle height where the old pipe trench holds rain. Beyond the last stalk, your hand would find my iron collar under mineral crust.

You would have to keep it there.

At first you would feel your own pulse. Then the minute redistribution of wings. A rasping shell might tighten against the ceramic. Water would shift somewhere inside me, not enough to flow. Much later, if the remote plant happened to be beginning its cycle, a faint warmth would develop under your palm. You might decide you had supplied it yourself.

You would usually be right. Occasionally not entirely.

Surveyors still come, though less often. Their entries have changed:

**STILL ANSWERING AT LOWER COLLARS.**

**ANSWER NEGLIGIBLE ABOVE CROSSING.**

**ROOST REDUCED; BROODS AT ACTIVE FIN FIELD.**

**CARRIER ROUTE NOW INCLUDES WARM DRAIN.**

And, most recently:

**NOT A ROUTE. LEAVE AS GROUND.**

I like the accuracy of that last instruction, though it is not yet complete. Parts of me still answer. Parts have become only ceramic and salt. Parts are occupied more densely than when the old main ran beside me. Roots are opening me; crust is closing over me; animals arrive and depart with mud, seepage, and one another.

Eventually Main 6 could warm or cool without any consequence here. There would be no final response to notice, no last cycle after which everything stopped. The old relationship would merely cease to be useful as an explanation.

Other explanations would remain: cushion to retained water, shell to molting chamber, stalk root to etched ceramic, carrier footprint to the next small migration.

In the production record, I would always be unused. That word would have become inaccurate without ever needing correction.

I would have carried no brine and clarified no water. Yet for a while I would have been a warm address, then a fading one, then simply a piece of ground whose history still altered where life could gather. Not a machine refusing extinction, and not a memorial to the pump. An interval long enough to be colonized, and irregular enough that forgetting could happen at several speeds.

The works would call me Reserve 6.

The life along me would have no use for that name. It would know me by degrees.
