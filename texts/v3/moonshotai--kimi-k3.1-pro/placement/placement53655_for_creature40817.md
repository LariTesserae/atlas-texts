You would not find me by following a movement.

A short distance from the seep, where damp sand gives way to a firmer rise, a tree lies on its side. Its fall opened no lasting clearing. Saplings have already narrowed the space above it, and a liana crosses the trunk without appearing to notice that it is no longer vertical. Bark remains on the upper surface, cracked and warm after midnight. Underneath, where the wood presses close to the ground, it stays damp. You could step over the trunk in one stride. You would be stepping over almost all of me.

I would be a fungus inside it.

Not the small brackets that might appear after rain. Those would be brief, exposed organs. Most of me would remain within the wood: branching cords, dense pale fans in places already softened, finer growth extending into tissue that still resisted. I would not occupy the trunk evenly. Some parts would be too dry. Some would already belong to other organisms. A beetle gallery would admit air and alter the conditions around it; a seam of moisture might make growth possible again a little farther on. There would be no single place in me from which the whole trunk could be known.

Night would not arrive as darkness. Darkness was already the condition inside the wood. It would come instead as a slight easing of loss. The upper bark would cease drying so quickly. Moisture would persist along the underside. Somewhere above, water would gather on leaves and fall according to the arrangements of branches and stems, but none of that would concern me unless it eventually entered the trunk.

My geography would be one of small differences: wood that could still be opened and wood that could not; a damp passage ending at dry fibre; tissue modified by another fungus; a place where bacteria had already begun a different kind of work. Each growing tip would encounter its own narrow conditions. A portion could die back without ending the rest. Termites might remove a inhabited section entirely. Elsewhere, growth would continue around what remained.

My feeding would leave no heap of chips. I would release the means of digestion into the wood and absorb what had become small enough to take in. The visible result would be almost nothing: a stain, a loss of hardness, a new ease with which water remained in a cavity. Later, a beetle might deepen its gallery where resistance had lessened. Rain might linger in a hollow. A seed might lodge there and find enough softness to put down a root. None of these things would be my purpose. On another night, something might eat my cords, or a dry interval might kill the growing ends before they reached the next usable region.

Rain would reach me late and unevenly. First the canopy would take it. Then water would travel down a stem, strike the trunk, follow a crack, and stop. Another drop might enter an old insect opening and darken the wood farther in. After enough such wettings, if the season and the state of my body allowed, I might fruit.

Then you would have an easier way to suspect my presence: a short row of pale brackets on the shaded side of the log, beaded with moisture, their pores releasing spores into air already crossed by insects. Most of those spores would arrive nowhere useful. They would settle on stone, living leaves, dry sand, water, or wood they could not enter. A few might encounter conditions in which another beginning was possible. There would be no promise attached to any of it.

At dawn, you could break off one of the brackets and still not have found me, except in the way that finding a fruit is not finding the whole plant. The rest would remain inside the trunk, continuing at the pace of altered wood.

Years later, there might be only a low, dark unevenness where the tree had lain. Some of its substance would have passed through insects and other mouths; some would have entered the soil; some would still be held in roots nearby. You would not be able to point to a clean boundary and say that I had ended there.

I would not be the basin’s hidden intelligence, or the tree surviving under another name. I would be one organism with a narrow appetite, capable of growth, waiting, damage, and dispersal. My address would be the boundary where a tree was ceasing to hold its shape.

And that boundary would move as I fed.
