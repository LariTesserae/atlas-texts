I would arrive in Vey as a person, because I would need to be taught.

At first I would be a term worker from the lower country, proud of good gauges and dry cuffs. I would think a cistern was a place where water was kept, and a pipe a way of making water mind. The white pans would look empty to me. The long pauses of older keepers would look like uncertainty.

My education would begin with a repair done correctly.

There would be a length of conduit marked disused on the service slate: its ceramic furred with pearly roughness, its inspection glass clouded, one reach weeping inconveniently beneath a causeway. I would clean it, bypass the damaged section, and close the seep. The district would approve. My test flow would improve. The paving above would dry.

Nothing dramatic would happen. No one would go thirsty.

But across the gypsum field, a seep at the foot of a little-used cistern would diminish. Bronze crabs would gather on a ledge where none had gathered before, investigating water arriving by some new, inadequate route. Eels would stop appearing behind one inspection window. A wader with a cloudy eye membrane would abandon the outer pier. Each change would be small enough to explain separately.

An old keeper would walk me through all of them without saying, *You did this.* That would make it worse.

We would reopen the passage. The cistern seep would return, then the fleas, then a stippling of young eels. The wader would not come back for a year.

I would stay past my term.

Years later, you would find me living below a pipe-repair depot at the edge of a district, in rooms whose outer wall was always faintly damp. One side of the largest room would be given to covered culture jars. I would be vain about two of them and unjustly suspicious of a third. There would be service slates stacked near the door, their official entries crowded with private corrections: *fleas rise before flow*; *leave lower film*; *wader route changed*; *ask below before cleaning.*

At noon, a census would probably miss me. I would be asleep, or eating too noisily with the depot families, or composing a complaint about someone else’s ceramic work. I would still dislike being wet in bed. I would still eat eel, curse crabs when they complicated a repair, and occasionally mistake an unfamiliar silence for peace.

My work would be the passages that no single settlement found worth claiming. A roof overflow that supplied three places after leaving the district’s records. A mineral-choked rib that was not quite a pipe anymore but remained a route. A seep beneath paving where a hand held flat could feel warmth moving sideways. People would call me when a cistern was full but its margins had dried, or when every gauge read well and the animals nevertheless seemed to be leaving.

I would keep maps, but none would be complete. The useful parts would be in the margins and in other people’s memories.

That would be the hardest thing to learn. Remembering can become another kind of cistern. A keeper can hold a dozen old routes in his head, let districts wait for him, and call the waiting stewardship. I would feel that temptation often. I would want to be needed at every difficult gate. I would have to learn that knowledge, held too closely, could seal a passage as effectively as ceramic.

So my slates would remain where others could read them. I would take apprentices I had not chosen. I would let children lead me along routes I thought I knew, and sometimes I would be gracious when they corrected me.

Sometimes.

After the second Turning is when you would most easily find me. The fins would already be brightening, though water would still be ticking from the tower rims. I would be out beyond the last depot stack, where the causeway crosses a reach of stone with no visible channel beneath it. My white shoulders would be dark with condensate. Salt would be drying again at my collar while my cuffs stayed wet.

A child would be beside me, holding the slate and wishing I would hurry.

I would have noticed six bronze shells on a ledge that had been empty at the previous Turning. Not a colony yet, only a possibility. The brine fleas in the nearest pan would be rising slightly ahead of a condensate edge too thin for me to see. Farther off, the cloudy-eyed wader would be standing on the outer pier again.

None of these things would tell me what to do by itself.

I would kneel, put two fingers into the seam between salt and basalt, and wait. Beneath the ordinary vibration of the wells there would be another movement, intermittent and almost absent. I would ask the child where it went. The child would point along the causeway. I would say that was one possibility.

Then we would follow it.

Where would you find me in Vey? There: at the place where a marked channel becomes unmarked dampness, arguing gently over a route neither of us can see, with warm water under my hand and the whole white plateau seeming to hold its breath around us.

And who would I be?

Not the keeper of Vey’s water. No one could be. Only a conduit keeper whose district was mostly other people’s margins; a fallible memory, a pair of hands, a few jars in a cool room. Useful for a while. Trying not to become indispensable. Trying to leave behind no monument and no blockage—only open passages, and enough other people who understood why they must remain open.
