At first I think I would be the water.

I would enter stone without asking, lie in sheets so thin the land seemed dry, and rise in a footprint behind someone who had just passed. There would be a freedom in that: no cairns, no obligations, no need to remember which seep could be drunk from and which left a sickness behind its clear taste.

Then I imagine being a slateback. That tempts me longer. I would have a body made for the Undermere—low, broad-footed, close to the films and the ground. Fog would matter less. The herd would not be a shape I had to see; it would arrive through my feet and spine as weight, hesitation, the light impacts of the young. Even my stillness would be a kind of listening.

Or I could be a veilwing, and have done with patience. A buried wait, a fierce grazing, one ascent into the wet grey air. My wings would dull with mist; I would shake them clear. Mating, laying, feeding something larger: a whole aerial life brief enough to fit above a pool that was already beginning to shrink.

But I keep returning to one small gesture.

A hand closing around another wrist in fog. Not possession. Not rescue, yet. Only: *wait*. Only: *I am here; do not make the next step alone.*

So I would be human.

Not a master of the basin, and not an elder whose memory is treated as law. I would be one of the people who go between huts, carrying sinew, salt, a chert core, a shell blade whose edge has begun to fail. I would carry news, but mostly I would carry corrections. A seep has turned sulphurous. A herd has left the old pan. The fractured cairn has lost its upper stones. Someone has died; someone has been born; a household that owed fat now owes nothing because the debt was forgiven when they took in an injured traveller.

There would be no dignity in the work except what other people chose to give it. I would often be cold. I would hoard good cord and pretend not to. I would be old enough to have been wrong in public and young enough still to be sent walking again.

Outside, I might appear grave and silent. Indoors, no one who knew me would make that mistake. Behind the turned entrance and the heavy hides, I would talk too much, interrupt stories, and argue about throats I had not personally seen. My hut, when I was there, would stand so low against a bedrock dome that a traveller could pass it in fog. Its lamp would be meanly fed. Its seep would be good for eels and bad for drinking. I would like visitors more than I admitted and would always complain about the state in which they left the hides.

My real work would begin where sight became unreliable.

I would know certain cairns by the insults they offered the palm: the rounded one with a single flat shoulder; the fractured one whose remaining spur could still be felt through a mitten; the little heap that was not a marker at all, though strangers often mistook it for one. I would know when black clay should come under the left foot and when a dome ought to answer a strike with a hollow note. A route would not be a picture in my mind. It would be a sequence of expectations, including the expectation that some of them would fail.

That would be the difficult part—not remembering, but allowing memory to be contradicted.

On the journey by which you might come to know me, I am crossing a pan with a child sent from another household to learn my paths. The fog is close. The child has been careful all morning and is beginning to think care is something I use to make myself slow and important.

A marker should have appeared by now.

It has not.

I stop. I feel ahead with my feet, then resent myself for doing so. There are innocent explanations. My strides have shortened on the clay. The cairn has slumped. I have angled too far around a wet place and will find it after another twenty paces. Memory is very good at offering help when help is not wanted.

I am still choosing among these explanations when one strand of the roar stops.

Not all sound. The basin keeps pounding and hissing. That is what makes the silence terrible: it has a location.

My hand closes around the child’s wrist.

We leave by the harder ground, not pausing to look toward the mouth, not pausing to decide whether the pause might mean nothing. Behind us, water stands up in our footprints. Then the pressure turns. Cold mist breaks across the pan; somewhere beyond it, animals begin to move all at once. Their panic reaches me through the stone before their bodies become visible. We get behind the baffle of the dome and stay there, wet through, while the Undermere revises the place where we had intended to walk.

Later, the child will say that I knew.

At the next hut, beside the lamp, they will tell the story that way. In their version I will stop at once, lift my head, and read the silence as cleanly as a hand sign. I will let them continue for a while because it is pleasant to be made wise. Then I will put the missing cairn back into the story.

I will put back the part where I stood explaining its absence.

That will be the useful part. Not the silence, which everyone already knows to fear, but the little delay before it: the moment in which a person nearly persuades the world to agree with memory. Someone listening may remember that better than they would remember my escape. Someone else may interrupt to say that I have always exaggerated my own slowness. We will argue. The child will eat my share of the fat if I let them.

Outside, the herd will settle into shallow water until its backs resemble stones. Waders will work over some disturbance too far away to see. A late hatch may rise from a pool that was bare clay the day before. The roar will take no notice of the story, the correction, or the debt of fat I now owe the child for not letting them finish.

So that is who I would be: not the one who knows the Undermere, but one who can still be corrected by it, and who carries the correction onward.

And you would find me along the cold skin between one concentration of life and the next—a hand on a cairn, a hand on a wrist, a voice behind hide arguing long after the lamp should have been allowed to die.

A small, fallible warmth, travelling under the roar.
