You would find me beside water, but not in it.

Outside the old cord-maker’s house on the middle terrace, a bark trough carries roof-drip toward a covered jar. One seam leaks. Beneath it she has set a slab for shade and a shallow tray where the drop falls. The tray is empty. Just beyond its edge, in a narrow finger of moss, something wine-dark is holding the stone.

At first gray light you might take me for growth fallen from the roof and rooted there. At mist-rise, if you watched long enough, you might see a small green back lift from the moss and test the wet rock with a limb you could reasonably call a foot. Return after rain, and you might reasonably call it a root.

The census would count the cord-maker and her many jars. It would not count me.

She did not plant me. Years ago she prepared the tray, arranged the slab, and replaced the leaking trough only when the crack threatened to waste too much water. Then I settled just beyond the place she had prepared. She moved the slab a hand’s width, laid a cord toward my fracture, and rebuilt the offer around me.

For eleven years I was mostly *held*.

My back opened into dark leaves. The legs that had brought me there diminished into the moss. One frond kept a crooked scar where a stone splinter had sheared it; by that scar the cord-maker knew me from the other growth along the wall. While I remained rooted, she occasionally cut a few outer stems and dried them. When I began to loosen, I became bitter, and she left me alone.

She owned the slab, the tray, the cord, the trough, and the work she had spent on them. She did not own me. My persistence was not loyalty, and I was not tame. Her intentions reached me only as conditions: shade at one hour, cold stone at another, the small repeated concussion of a drop, a narrow road of fiber into dampness.

Eventually one end of me began lifting while the other put down new roots.

There would be no single morning on which I changed from one kind of thing into another. A few frond-undersides would pale. A seam of backs would separate from the mat. Somewhere else, still attached to the original scarred growth, a root would thicken. When part of me finally took wing, *I* would become an inconvenient pronoun—not because there were many minds conferring in the moss, but because the word implies a boundary the cleft would not supply.

At mist-rise the cord-maker would see the walkers gathering. She would cover her cup, move a basket from the lane, and stop work. No hammer-blow. No attempt to hold me down.

Some of me would pass into the court with the other soft-backed bodies, a wine-dark patch here, a strip of old bark caught among the roots there. She might follow at a distance. She would be losing years of care and a useful harvest; there would be no reason for her to enjoy it. Perhaps she would swear softly when the last of the walkers passed the slab she had prepared. But she would not drive me home.

The winged portion would fare differently. Some of it would drift over the covered jars and catch the cold above the cistern. Some would thicken beside a folded brown leaf. The leaf would open, and afterward there would simply be fewer wings.

After rain, the place beside the trough would stand bare. The cord-maker would inspect that absence before she looked for arrivals. Beneath the lowest stair, however, where no gardener had prepared anything, there might be a little pale growth furring a fracture.

She would not pretend to know whether it was me, something released from me, or another life accepting similar terms.

Something might persist: a tolerance for that mineral seam, a tendency to root where water had once been guided, a scar visible for a while on one held frond. You could call it memory. She would more likely call it growth, and then check whether the new colony was safe to approach.

By the next gray morning she would be lashing a new tray beside the trough—not because she expected me back, but because a little shade and a dependable drop were still worth offering.

And below the lowest stair, something small would be pressing into new moss with a root that had recently been a foot.

You could call it me. For that dim morning, I would be *held*. The word would not mean still. It would mean that, for a little longer, the stone and I were suitable to one another.
