### The inhabited quiet

On a useful map, my address would take longer to write than my name: *the hushfelt folded into the cork growth below the split-throated stand, after the ivory carriers have risen and before the middle stalks break their chord.* Last month’s mark would no longer do. A root arch has lifted one side of the patch since then. The rest has been carried a short distance without ever quite leaving its place.

You would have to enter to find me.

Outside, the carriers would be striking one another in the air, and the open stalks would be leaning toward a note their bodies had not yet finished making. Inside the hushfelt, the wing-drone would thin. The stalk-booms would seem to come through the ground rather than the air. For a little while there would be only the tick of feeding, the shift of fiber, the dry complaint of something turning in its case.

Then: a scratch. A pause. Another scratch. A fragment of shed husk moving where nothing ought to be loose.

That would be me.

I would be one of the case-builders, though the name makes my work sound more separate from my body than it is. My true body would be small, soft, and wax-pale. Most of what could be seen of me would be the crooked house I had made around myself: old seed valves, shed husk, seed-wool teased into the gaps, a plate stripped from something that had once been mistaken for bark. I would have sealed several entrances behind me. The one in use would face into the hushfelt, away from the open galleries.

To a harvester, I would be a bit of portable rubbish with legs.

To my brood, I would be part of the instrument on which their summons depended.

That would be my real work: not merely building chambers, but adjusting them until they carried one small pattern faithfully. A chamber could be strong and still be wrong. Too much husk in a wall, and a tap returned as a hard double tick. Too much wool, and the tap went nowhere. A split valve might carry the first three blows beautifully and swallow the last.

Three taps. A pause. One more.

The young would not emerge because I was anxious, or because the district had become dangerous, or because I pressed my body against their cases and waited. They would emerge to that sequence and no other. In the open growth, the pattern would have been erased almost as I made it—by wingbeat, by seed volleys, by the long pressure of a bellower’s call arriving through the felt. Here, within the folded tissue and the walls I kept mending, it could remain itself long enough to be received.

I would not experience this as language. It would be simpler than language: a fit between a body, a wall, and the lives waiting on the other side.

The rest of the Bellows would reach me as alterations in that fit. A huskback’s single locating knock would pass through the floor and make every loose fiber in my case answer at once. A territorial rush would arrive as a shudder before I could feel the animal’s actual passage. The approach of a bellower would not begin as sound but as a widening pressure, something the hushfelt could not entirely absorb. Warm stone would tick beneath all of it, irregular and patient.

I would not need to see the winged weather to know when it had risen. For a while, fine dust would settle through the hushfelt’s pleats, and the smallest carriers would come blundering into places too narrow for the ivory-winged ones. Nor would I need to see a threadbrowser. The whole gallery would lend its tension to something overhead, and my chamber would lean very slightly out of true.

Sometimes people would enter the largest part of the patch. They would uncover their ears, touch one another’s shoulders, and speak at arm’s length, looking startled by the smallness of their own voices. If they were experienced, they would listen before they spoke.

A living quiet is full of minor sounds.

They might hear me scratching at a wall and take it as reassurance, though I would not be reassuring them. I would be feeding, or repairing a draft of vibration around the brood, or worrying a strand loose from something another animal had built. My uses to people would not be my purpose.

But if they were wise, they would notice when I stopped along with everything else.

One afternoon, the small sounds would stop.

They would not dwindle. They would cease together: feeding ticks, the scratch of cases, the minute friction of fibers resettling. Somewhere beyond the hushfelt, a huskback’s knocking would end in the middle of a sequence. My own case would draw tight around me before I had decided to close it.

Then the ground would unfasten.

I would see no immense body and understand no passage as a whole. There would be only the roof leaving its angle, the brood wall pressing against my back, and a common delay moving through supports that had seemed separate. Roots would rise without breaking. Stems would part at their sockets. For a moment the hushfelt would be carried on a movement too broad to struggle against, and then it would be set down tilted, with one edge open to warm stone.

The large noises would return first. A wooden groan. A burst of wings. Stone ticking in the exposed air.

After a while, I would scratch.

The brood chamber would still be intact, but its shape would have changed. When I tapped three times, paused, and tapped once more, the second blow would come back too sharply; the last would die in a fold of compressed wool.

Nothing would emerge.

So I would begin again. I would unthread the damaged wall, carry out the crushed valve, take wool from an old entrance and work it into the new hollow. I would test, alter, test again. There would be no victory in it and no promise that the patch would remain until I finished. A root might enter through the floor. The hushfelt might be grown through. Another held silence might come.

But eventually the wall would give back the pattern cleanly.

Three taps. A pause. One.

From within would come a slight stirring, cases opening, small feet testing the wool. Not safety forever. Not even safety for long. Only the next thing happening at the time when it could happen.

When my own body was finished, the unthreaders would show no respect for my workmanship. My walls would be carried off into other walls. A strip of husk that had once helped summon my brood might end up dulling the wing-drone around someone else’s young. The hushfelt would grow through whatever remained. I would have had several addresses without ever becoming large.

That is what I would be in the Bellows: not the hush itself, and not the voice raised against it. I would be the little living scratch by which inhabited quiet could be told from held silence—and the patience, after the ground had moved, to remake one small signal until another life could answer it.
