At first I would be easier to overlook than to find.

You would find me—while *there* still had its ordinary meaning—in the Braid beneath the stair attached to three metres of surviving wall. A cable would pass through the root mat, warm enough to keep mineral water moving in its seam. Copper reed-nodes would catch against the cable; translucent fans would fur the concrete. I would be one dull, hard seed among older root fusions, easily mistaken for clinker.

I would be a seed held shut by noise.

Not asleep, exactly. Not waiting. The piston-bank concussion and the turbines’ finer shriek would travel through root and concrete and keep the sutures of my coat under tension. Rain could dissolve the fallen tissue above me; a surge could plant a reed flat and tear it up again; new ground could cool beneath the mat. I would remain closed. My beginning would not be something I could bring about. It would be the brief withdrawal of everything preventing it.

So my first true moment would be the common silence.

The engines would stop. The Braid would cease trembling. Suspended filaments would begin to settle. For less than a second, my coat would lose the force that kept it shut, and a little water would enter a seam. I would not germinate in that interval; there would not be time. I would only become unable to remain as I was. When the piston-stroke returned, it would arrive too late to restore the old tension.

Growth, when it came, would not make me tall. I would not become a stilt-crowned tree; a trunk is too much confidence in a lasting agreement between root and crown. I would be low, jointed, divisible: a few living knots extended through the Braid, each able to make whatever the local force required. A ribbon leaf in open flow. A fan where current flattened me against concrete. A close, corked surface where a revision had exposed me to steam. A root around the cable—not because the cable was home, but because for the moment it was warm and continuous.

Nothing in me would need to beckon, warn, or be sweet. I would intercept what the warm water had already unmade: pollen, frond-tissue, lime, mineral fines. Whatever remained after growth would go into seeds that differed from one another. Some quick and thin-coated. Some heavy enough to fall through contradictory currents. Some able to survive the shredding of the tissue around them. A few, perhaps, held closed by the same vibration that had held me.

Then, eventually, the separated engines would find phase.

The three notes might come before the revision, or after it. For me, the event would be simpler than its acoustics. A leaf still level with its old root mat would suddenly be under deep-water pressure. Another knot would be left in steaming air. The joints between us would part.

One fragment would remain on the concrete, flattened and drinking from the flow. Another would go through an intake and come out as green rain, except for a single viable node plastered against cable sheathing farther on. Most of the seeds would be crushed, cooked, buried beyond use, or carried into water in which they could do nothing. A few would lodge in fresh fractures. One might settle on a slab whose reflection had shown a thicket before the slab itself had cooled.

Afterward, *I* would be a generous word.

No single knot would be the seat of me. There would be no heartwood to protect, no original stem whose survival could settle the question. What continued would be a pattern of responses: closure under that vibration; release in common silence; growth along heat and fracture; division under force; the leaving of unlike seeds. The Braid would remember such things without remembering me.

I would not be the whole Braid, or a mind within it. I would be one of its local habits: a habit with tissues, a recurrence with a brief anatomy.

To return to the stair and search for the original seed would be to use the wrong kind of finding. The stair might be drowned. The wall might be dry at its base and fathoms deep at its top. The seed would be gone in any case. But somewhere a fan would be intercepting a cloud of dissolved tissue; somewhere a node would be furring a live cable; somewhere another dull seed would be held fast in a root mat, closed by the noise.

Not copies arranged around an absent original. Not one body mysteriously stretched across the basin. Just a life whose outline had never been the most durable thing about it.

Where would I be? Discontinuously, in the Works: a fan here, a node there, a closed seed in a mat whose location had ceased to agree with itself. Never an address.

Who would I be? Not a resident, and not a witness. A small, green consequence of the silence, taken up afterward by the noise.

And when the engines stopped, I would not break the silence. I would be one of the things it broke open.
