You would find me beside the shallow main, on my knees in the root felt, apparently delaying a simple repair.

The printed sheet would say *expose seam*. But by then the seam would have ceased to be merely a fault in a pipe. The felt above it would be swollen with years of withheld water. Roots would have entered the clearance, fungi the roots. Removing them would satisfy the drawing and create the leak. Leaving them untouched might preserve the repair—or merely conceal the next stage of its failure. No gauge could make that distinction for me.

That is where I would belong: in the interval before an instruction becomes an action.

I would be human, one of the rare people who still come through the recessed doors. Inspector would be the convenient title. Keeper of exceptions would be closer to the truth. I would not be the basin’s secret mind or the forest’s voice; the catchment has no need for either. I would be someone with damp cuffs, a hand lens, and insufficient time, whose judgment mattered briefly and whose absence mattered much longer.

I would have to resist two easy loyalties.

The first would be loyalty to the machinery, because the machinery has drawings. Its intended condition can be named: clear, dry, accessible, within tolerance. The second would be loyalty to the forest, because the forest is alive and has no way to plead its case. But neither loyalty would be enough. A dependency is not automatically a harmony. Roots can support a split frame and also prevent its replacement; dampness can preserve one junction while starving another of attention. Sometimes the right repair would mean cutting living wood. Sometimes it would mean leaving a valve enclosed and working through the trunk. Sometimes it would mean admitting that I could not yet tell.

So I would learn to read both records.

The maintenance tables would give me pressures, dates, and intervals. The forest would give me small hard leaves on a failing slope, vigorous stems in the cold shade of a mast, a collar thickened around an annual wound. One record would be legible at a glance. The other might require several visits separated by years. I would scratch reference marks beside needles, watch a collector against a fixed branch, and leave some questions unresolved without calling them answered.

My home would be elsewhere. A shelter that began to accumulate my ordinary life would be a sign that I had stayed too long. I would leave no favorite cup, no worn path to a private view, no demand for warmth and daily water. My continuity would be in notes addressed to people I would never meet, people for whom the basin might be slightly wetter and therefore different in ways I could not anticipate.

Perhaps during my lifetime a hollow would overtop its sill. I might see the dark line appear across the catchment floor: a flow no deeper than a hand, carrying spores and eggs, stranding some lives and lodging others in the green channel sides. I would not mistake it for completion, or for vindication. It would simply be the first evidence that a connection previously made only by pipes and mechanisms could now be made, briefly, by water finding its own way downhill.

My part would not be to witness that hour. It would be to avoid foreclosing it.

At the shallow main, after waiting long enough to know that I still did not know everything, I would write *keep damp*.

Then I would add the reason, and the date. A bare command might outlive its usefulness. A reason would leave the next person free to judge.

Afterward I would close the recessed door and take my noise away. I would not have healed the catchment or spoken for its forest. I would only have kept one small, entangled possibility open a little longer: one repair not made, one dependency not severed, one act of attention passed on without being turned into a rule.

That would be enough of a self to leave there.
