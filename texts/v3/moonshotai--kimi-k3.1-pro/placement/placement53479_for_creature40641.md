At first I would choose badly.

I would choose to be a rimwing. There is a seduction in launching by falling, in belonging to the warm margin of things, in knowing the Roof by what it casts over the edge: seed-down, silk, the brief gray bodies of insects, mist torn into threads. I would live above depth and below openness, sensitive to alterations too slight for anything on the table to notice.

But I know what would ruin me. I would keep looking inward. Sooner or later I would want to cross the green distances whose weather I had only tasted. I would want to stand in a grove instead of feeding on its leavings. A rimwing can belong to the Roof its whole life without once being able to survive upon it, and I think I would find that kind of belonging unbearable.

Then I would want to be a stonefoot: four broad soles planted, the small ears closed, knowledge arriving through bone. I like the thought of a body that does not confuse stillness with inattention. I like, too, the stonefoot’s unintended generosities—the spores carried in mud, the basin left by a night’s rest, the dew gathering after the animal has gone.

But I would make too much of its knowledge. I would want every vibration to mean something. I would become an oracle to myself, and the Roof offers no reward for that sort of confidence.

So I think I would be human after all, though not in the way I first imagine.

You would find me far from the rim, at a place no traveler would think to mark. There would be a cell there, its turfed roof almost indistinguishable from the ground. Two hours away, in no particular direction anyone could be persuaded to call a route, would be the outer edge of a grove and a dependable drip. Elsewhere: three small basins, a rosette slope, a warm fissure whose mouth looked bearded with old gray silk.

No path would join these things. I would join them.

I would be a cell-keeper, and my circuit would be the habit of arriving before small failures became final ones. I would clear the drip groove without deepening it. I would replace cord and pretend not to mind that the previous keeper had used a knot I disliked. I would leave the silk at the fissure alone, knowing it was not neglect but a colony holding on. I would move one rosette, perhaps only one in a whole season, into the shelter of a drip stone, and never be certain whether I had helped it or merely made my interference visible.

Much of my life would consist of being damp, mildly lost, and unable to hear myself think. I would complain about this fluently whenever I was inside a cell. I would not become serene. I would hoard dry fiber, resent an adze left blunt, and take an unreasonable length of time to admit that a pool I trusted had gone bad.

My circuit would not make me important. That is part of why I would choose it.

There might be another keeper whose work I encountered for years before I encountered them. I would know where they habitually placed screen weights, how tightly they bedded turf, whether they left a water vessel full or clean and empty. We might trust one another’s repairs completely and, when at last we shared a shelter, discover that we could not endure one another’s stories. That would not dissolve anything. The circuit would not require affection, though it might sometimes receive it. It would require only that usefulness need not always be repaid to the useful person, or in the useful place.

Gradually, I suspect, the circuit would become a more honest answer to what I was than my own name.

The stonefoot’s abandoned resting hollow would belong to it because dew gathered there. The buried root web would belong to it because water appearing at one basin could vanish into another’s need. So would the hands that had repaired the cell before mine, and the hands that might come after, and the grove roots that went on holding the fold together while no person walked the table at all.

Not a territory. Not a family, exactly. Not even a continuous life. A small, repeatedly renewed refusal to let everything be separate.

Once, perhaps only once, I would be caught away from shelter when a stonefoot nearby spread its feet and stopped chewing.

I would have just enough time to dislike the omen.

Then the roar would drain away.

In the hush, a leaf separating from wet stone would sound indecently near. I would hear my own clothing shift as I turned. The folded mist screen would creak under my arm. Somewhere below, water would tick with the patience of something that had never needed me to hear it.

My curiosity would want to lift its head. The rest of me, if I had learned anything, would already be going down: one hand buried in the root-felt, the other pinning the screen, my body as low and uninteresting to the air as I could make it.

I would not shout. There would be no one whose safety could be improved by my voice, and the clear heartbeats would not belong to me.

Then the clouds beyond the rim would stand up.

The eruption would pass through the circuit without caring that I had given it a place in my thoughts. The green skin would bow. The fissure silk would vanish into its narrow packets or be torn away. Water would lift from the basins. For a moment every connection I trusted would be reduced to what could hold, what could fold, what could be let go.

Afterward, I would find petals in the drip groove and grit inside a fold of clothing. The stonefoot’s old hollow might contain a new pool, dark and shining, with one white swimmer turning beneath its surface. I would not decide how it had arrived. At the grove, a dependable drip might have moved a finger’s breadth to the left. At the cell, I might find that someone else had already reset a loosened slab and left behind the very cord I needed, though whether they were hours or days ahead of me I would never know.

I would clean the vessel. Reset the groove. Leave what could be left. Walk on.

From a little distance there would be nothing to see: no monument, no path, no sign that a self had been there. Only water still reaching a root, a cell remaining a cell, and one small stretch of green skin not asked to begin again from nothing.

Who would I be?

A keeper, while I had hands. Afterward, the keeping.
