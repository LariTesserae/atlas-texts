I would begin by wanting to be something quieter than a person.

The light, perhaps: reaching the backs of drains and the undersides of roofs, asking nothing, touching the fish and the thresholds with the same white indifference. Or the moisture in the rear wall, never quite becoming a spring, passing from clay to root to cup without ever possessing enough of itself to make a stream. For a while I would prefer one of the gray-furred animals—answerable to no civic purpose, intimate with the narrow routes, permitted by one child and freely biting everyone else.

But each of those choices would be a way of asking to be exempt from the actual terms of the place. The light cannot be cold. The water cannot misunderstand an instruction. The moss does not miss anyone. And the hollow, for all its exactness, does not spare its people discomfort, error, or grief.

So I think I would be someone who came down the steps and stayed.

Not for a reason that would satisfy a visitor afterward. Not carrying a warning, or looking for the standing stone’s original meaning. I would arrive curious, perhaps, and then remain because curiosity became attachment before I had noticed it happening.

At first I would be bad at it. I would speak, feel the word happen in my throat, and then try again more loudly, as though the first failure had been a matter of effort. I would move too quickly on the stairs and put my feet down without knowing what my feet were doing. My signs would be too large. I would move my mouth after my hands had already begun, and watch someone’s face when the important part of the conversation was taking place at my wrist.

The name-sign they gave me would probably come from that. It would not be dignified. A child would eventually make a ruder version of it, and I would pretend, unsuccessfully, not to be amused.

For some time I might imagine that my place was near the entrance—that I could belong partly to the plain and partly to the hollow, and make a life out of the distinction. But living permanently on the steps would only be another way of not quite living here. Eventually I would have a sleeping recess off one of the covered passages. Not the driest or innermost one; those would go to people whose hands ached worse than mine, or who woke too often from the cold. I would learn the particular dampness of my own wall. When it began to trouble me, I would ease the root aside with an awl, not because I had conceived a duty to the town’s infrastructure, but because I wanted to sleep.

I would still go up sometimes.

Not as a scout. There would be nothing to scout for, and anyone could climb three steps. I would go because I had known sound and did not want to make a virtue of never missing it. On the plain, gravel would break under my boots with that almost shocking excess of event. Cloth would brush against itself. I would hear my breath as well as see it. Perhaps I would say the name I had used before coming below. It would sound thinner than I remembered. Then I would return, and for a little while the completeness of the silence would feel less like peace than like a hand laid firmly over the world.

The people born inside would not necessarily find this interesting.

Most of my life there would be much smaller than that habit suggests. I would tire of the bitter seed paste and then, absurdly, hoard a particularly good handful. I would take the wrong side in the basket disagreement at least once. A gray animal would steal from me with exquisite delicacy and then allow a child to pick it up; I would continue to take this personally. I would learn to wash a cup without thinking that I was also cleaning its depression, to carry dark matter to a roof pocket because it needed to go somewhere, to remember which person should not be left to negotiate a narrow doorway alone. Sometimes I would remember. Sometimes I would be hungry or irritable and someone else would have to remember me.

And when someone died, I would discover that I had not become appropriately equilibrated. Their absence would turn up in impractical places: in a sign half-made toward an empty recess, in reaching to warn them about a step, in an old joke suddenly not worth completing. I might go onto the plain and make some useless noise about it. The moss below would still rise behind other people’s feet. This would not mean that nothing had been lost.

After long enough, I would no longer be the visitor who had stayed. I would simply be one of the people: cold-handed, occasionally bored, known for unnecessarily long stories and for going outside to listen to footsteps.

You would most often find me in the court with my back against the standing stone. Not studying it. It would merely be the right height, and my knee would be troubling me. A basket would be beside me, possibly containing something a gray animal was already considering. Across the court, someone would be using my name-sign with the exaggerated mouth movement added, and I would be affecting not to see.

If you spoke to me, I would watch you discover that your voice had not arrived. I would not take that as my cue to explain the hollow. I could not explain it, and by then I would be suspicious of anyone who tried too soon.

Instead, I would lift one of the little cups in the way that meant water, rather than thirst, or the cistern, or the fact that someone else had finished.

Then, while you drank, I would probably begin the story of my arrival—the word I tried to say, the louder word that followed, and the stranger who finally showed me how to ask for something with my hands. It would already be too long. Someone across the court would tell me so with one economical sign.

I would ignore them and continue.
