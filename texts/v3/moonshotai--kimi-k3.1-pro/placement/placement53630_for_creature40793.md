At first, you would find me on the approach path, with salt and fiber in a back-load and a stone in my shoe, pretending that my last stop had been for the view.

I have come for a season, intending to learn something useful about the waterworks and carry it back downhill. I want to see the whole town from the upper gate. The person meeting me shows me the public basin, the washing place, and the nearest overflow first. I understand the courtesy only later.

I am not naturally good at life on the ledge. The quiet makes me mistake the keepers for patient people and their work for a kind of serenity. I work too slowly when someone is watching and too quickly when no one is. During one of my first roof rounds, I clear a groove with admirable thoroughness and put the grit exactly where it should not go. The resulting obstruction is small enough to fix before midday and large enough to make me much less pleased with myself.

An older keeper kneels beside the channel and shows me what has happened. There is no speech about roofs belonging to everyone. She simply makes me follow the consequence downhill, court by court, until I can see that my neatness on one roof has become someone else’s stooping work below it. I am more wounded by being corrected than the situation warrants. That takes longer to repair.

Belonging does not arrive in a single moment, either. I acquire a bed, then a round of duties, then a way of climbing the workshop stair that other people can identify without looking up. One afternoon someone hands me a blocked channel stone, explains only the part I do not already know, and leaves me to deal with it. I notice, some days later, that the explanations have mostly stopped.

By the time you look for me again, I have become one of the keepers who comes and goes. I still spend seasons below, repairing tanks, laying out channels, and occasionally discovering that a method I thought belonged to the ledge works badly under a different roof. Part of what I earn returns up the path as grain, oil, salt, or fiber. When I am away, other people take my duties. When I return, some of my old work has been altered.

The first time this happens, I am offended. A channel repair of mine has been replaced, and I recognize neither the wedge spacing nor the finish along its edge. Then I see that the new work stays drier after cloud has passed through the lanes. It takes me a full day to admit that it is better. No one makes much of my admission, which is helpful.

On the ledge, my present round is a small one: several lower roofs, the downspouts serving them, and the overflow stones below the workshop. At dawn you would usually find me on a cold slab, brushing grit into a shallow pan, my knees chilled through their patches. I have become good with cord, adequate with a wooden lever, and never more than adequate at splitting stone. I know which downspout obstructs first, which north wall should remain dark longest, and which patch of paving ought to be dusty by the time I reach it. I can do the round without asking what comes next.

My room contains little besides bedding, work clothes, and a smooth brown stone brought up from below. It is the wrong color for the mountain and has no use I have been able to discover. I have not thrown it away.

Late one night, a drop in the cistern nearest my room strikes with a shallower note.

I hear it once and think I may have imagined the difference. I hear it again and begin calculating how warmly I will have to dress. By the third drop I am sitting on the edge of the bed, hoping with some sincerity that the fourth will allow me to go back to sleep. It does not.

Outside, the courts are simply cold and empty. I knock my shin against a step I have crossed hundreds of times and swear more loudly than the occasion requires. At the cistern, I listen. I check the downspout and the nearby channel, then the covered mouth and the overflow path. There is no fresh obstruction, no shifted stone, no dark seam where there should be dry grit.

For a moment I want to lift something anyway. I am awake; I have come out; it seems there ought to be a repair to show for it. Instead I put back the brush I have not needed, listen once more, and return to bed without having improved anything.

In the morning I describe the sound to the keeper currently responsible for the basin.

“You drew water after the evening round,” she says.

I had. The level was lower. That was all.

She waits while I remember, then tells me what to listen for another time. Her kindness does not extend to letting me escape a little embarrassment. Getting up, I learn, is not the same as knowing. Still, it is better to have looked without finding a fault than to have made one merely to justify the looking.

So that is who I would be: an intermittent keeper, usually within earshot of the lower cistern when I am on the ledge, and sometimes absent for whole seasons. Not necessary to the town’s continuance, but answerable to a small part of it while I am there. Trusted with real work. Still capable of sulking when corrected. Still learning the difference between attention and interference.

If anything of me remained after I left for good, it would probably be a joint no one could find and a knot someone else would eventually replace. I like the thought of my best work becoming difficult to locate, though I suspect I would still occasionally want someone to know it had been mine.

And on certain nights you would find me sitting on the edge of my bed, listening for the next drop—not serene, not certain, and already reaching for my shoes.
