My first thought is to be something that does not need the lamps.

A cricket, perhaps, with the chamber arriving through its feet and antennae: the approach of a cart, the warmth of a pipe, the richness of a ledge beneath the roosts. Or one of the small crustaceans in a sump, turning with the others around an abundance no human instrument has registered. There would be a completeness in that—a life without the town’s unfinished maps.

But that is a visitor’s thought. It chooses the most arresting lives and asks them to become meanings. It also avoids the particular difficulty of the Low Works. The animals do not have to decide whether a floor can be trusted for another shift, or whether a changed sound is an emergency. They are not required to confuse patience with safety.

So, after the roof and the black water have had their say, I think I would be human. And fairly minor.

Officially, I would be an assistant surveyor. Unofficially, I would be the rain clerk.

My room would be a screened survey office built against the pump hall, close enough that the table legs carried its vibration. The door would open onto a numbered street that ended at raw rock. From the threshold I would see two caged lamps, the underside of a ventilation trunk, and a section of flume; beyond that, the town would be mostly sound. My boots would stand on a grate. My hat would have developed a brim. The maps would be kept behind glass, and the notebooks in a zinc box on a high shelf, not because they were valuable in any heroic sense, but because replacing them would be tiresome.

Most of my work would consist of going back.

Back to the same datum stud with the same instruments. Back to a red line that no longer quite met its mark. Back to a sump stair, a doubtful roof lot, a level expanse of new white rind that someone had hopefully called a floor. A displaced stud might mean movement. It might also mean that a cart had struck it, or that the last surveyor had been careless, or that I was being careless now. The work would not be the discovery that things changed. Everyone knew that. The work would be discovering whether they had changed enough to matter.

I would become suspicious of clean lines on paper. A street voted into existence would remain pencil until water, foundations, and repeated observation had given their more laborious consent. This would not make me especially wise. I would still make confident mistakes, and other people would still have to find them. But I would understand that an old map could become dangerous without ever having been dishonest.

The rain ledger would begin almost accidentally. A supply driver would mention a storm on the plateau, and I would write down the date because there was space in the margin. Weeks later, someone in the pump hall would mention the load, and I would add that. Then would come the strengthened flumes, the multiplying drips, the brown mark on the stairs.

Eventually the book would acquire columns: *reported above; source; first rise; pump note; sump mark*. It would not allow me to command the weather, or even to predict it very well. It would merely preserve the interval between an event and its arrival. On the plateau, rain would be something that happened to people. In my ledger, it would become something that approached through stone.

I would take an unreasonable satisfaction in those entries.

At the daily departure, I would not go out to watch. I would notice the loose tags lifting on their wires and the altered pressure against my face. When speech became more trouble than it was worth, I would put down the pencil and wait. Then the roof would empty, the chamber would seem briefly larger, and I would check whether the interruption had smudged the figures.

I would not make a pet of a cricket or give a name to an eyeless fish. I would mend my screen promptly. I would learn footsteps on grating, the difference between loaded and empty carts, and the precise amount of lamp needed to cross a familiar bridge without stepping confidently onto anything merely because it looked level.

On my free shifts, I would sometimes take the incline path and tend the ferns. I would move a pot a little nearer the daylight, remove a blackened frond, and object if anyone called the plants symbolic. Then I would be quietly, disproportionately pleased when one survived another month.

Belonging would arrive without announcement. One night a pump would alter its stroke, and I would wake before I knew why. My foot would touch the floor. A hand would find my boots; another would close around the railing. For a few seconds I would not be a surveyor or a clerk, only one of the town’s small, simultaneous questions: *Where? How bad? Has another machine answered?*

If the rhythm returned, I would lie back beneath the ordinary noise of water, wings, insects, and flywheels. Silence would still require investigation. Patience would not.

By the next shift, you would find me at the sump stairs with one sleeve wet, comparing a new brown line with an older one, or kneeling beside a datum stud while a goods cart passed. I would be a keeper of comparisons: not someone capable of holding the Low Works still, but someone able to help it notice when stillness had stopped being true.

On my maps, *dry* would usually be followed, in smaller pencil, by *at present*.

The stone would still not agree to the finished streets. That would not be the failure of my work.

It would be the work.
