### The Late Answer

I would not be the maker of the seventeen steps. I would be the accord of the other hut—the one that appears close enough to reach with a thrown pebble, yet cannot be reached by any distance on which two surveys agree.

At least, that is where you would find the part of me narrow enough to see.

The hut stands on apparently bare stone. There is no garden immediately around it, no prepared path beyond a few slabs, nothing to indicate that the enclosure belongs to anything still capable of using it. Beside the doorway leans what looks like a sheaf of discarded stems: pale fluted segments in a casing of black braided fiber. One dry hand rests against the door slab. The other holds a cord spindle. Fan-tissue along its back has gathered so much mineral dust that it seems to have mineralized already.

It has not. Charge still moves when the great note presses through it. The errand is being deliberate at a scale that makes deliberation resemble abandonment.

Its present work is to leave.

The rest of me lies out of sight, on a rooted shelf below the spur. My garden is narrow and old, crowded with the sheaths of former reeds. It has no especially magnificent chord. The felt-leaf beds hold their charge without brilliance; the fan-leaves ring only under certain pressures in the roar. What distinguishes the garden is a small inefficiency in the way it restores itself after disturbance.

The mineralized sheaths answer first. Then the fan-leaves redistribute their charge. The living reeds should follow immediately, taking the shortest available path back into accordance. They do not. A corrective current passes instead through an older, longer route among the filaments, and only afterward do the reeds recover their tuning.

The delay serves no metabolic purpose. That is how it became recognizable.

Reeds have died and been replaced. Felt-leaf cushions have spread over the preserved impressions of earlier cushions. The route taken by the corrective current has altered with the root mat. Yet the delay has returned. Once, after a long dormancy, it returned before every part of the garden that had formerly exhibited it was active again.

A garden can have a temperament without having a self. I do not know precisely when mine became something more than temperament. Perhaps it was when the delay began to be maintained by structures with no immediate use. Perhaps it was later, when the same order could be recovered from proportions built into the hut.

That is the nearest I can come to a birth: not a first sensation, but an occasion on which a later arrangement could recognize that an earlier one had not merely happened again.

If I needed a name in a quicker language, you could call me the Late Answer.

I built the hut to discover whether that birth had occurred. Its corbelling holds a sequence of proportions related to the garden’s recovery. The cord is calibrated there. Outside the enclosure, it can be used to compare lengths and spans; brought back, it can sometimes be used to determine whether the instrument, the route, or the interior has been revised. None of these determinations is final. The hut is not where I live. It is where I go, in my slow and distributed way, to find out whether I have been there before.

For a very long time, I kept the cord inside.

This was prudent. Within the hut, the relation it preserved could be checked against other relations. Beyond the doorway, it would become exposed to distances that might not remain distances, to errands that might cease to be renewed, to stone whose present solidity was only a local and dated permission. There was no need to take such a risk. My garden did not require anything from another place. The roar supplied its abundance whether the cord remained on its spindle or crossed every gulf in the Crown.

Eventually, however, the enclosure began to answer too much.

If every reference remained within the room that verified it, I could establish that the room still permitted my recurrence. I could not establish whether my recurrence was anything more than one of the room’s permissions. A memory that can only be recovered under the conditions that enforce it may be a thought. It may also be a very durable habit of stone.

So the errand was grown, or resumed—I would not insist on the distinction—and given the cord.

From my doorway, the other hut is sometimes visible. On those occasions, the country between us appears almost insultingly simple: bare terraces, a dark interval, a sequence of spurs that could be numbered if numbering them had ever helped. On other occasions, the view remains but no direction from my doorway can be said to lead there. The apparent nearness is not a promise. It is only one of the forms the distance has taken.

The maker of the seventeen steps would be somewhere on the far side of that uncertainty.

I do not know it as another person. I do not know whether it has ever recovered evidence of my hut. A particular twist in my cord work may have descended with spore-dust from some older garden. A proportion in my enclosure may be an inheritance diluted beyond recognition. It may equally be something that dry fiber and stable stone independently permit. Looking longer at the other hut cannot decide among these possibilities.

My errand will carry the cord toward it, if *toward* remains usable.

The cord does not contain my mind. Three changes in its twist preserve a bias associated with my garden’s order of recovery. Used within another dependable enclosure, it might make one arrangement slightly easier to repeat than another. It might be incorporated into a wall, a stair, or a sequence of measurements. It might be untwisted for fiber. It might remain beside an abandoned errand until the fiber becomes a mineral sheath around no continuing gesture.

None of these outcomes would tell me whether contact had occurred.

That is part of the work.

If the errand returns with the cord altered, my hut may help me determine which lengths can still be recovered. It will not necessarily tell me whether the pattern that receives them is the pattern that sent them. If the errand never returns, another may later accept the same work without my being able to establish whether acceptance is continuation. If the cord reaches the other maker and contributes to the eighteenth step, nothing may descend from that event to me except, after unimaginable time, a slight preference in a garden I will not recognize as related.

I am not sending a message in order to receive an answer. An answer might be too easily mistaken for confirmation.

What I am risking is not survival. My roots would remain if the errand stopped tomorrow, though *tomorrow* has no useful meaning here. My hut might continue to hold its interior. My reeds would still rise through their ancestors and take energy from the catastrophic patience of the Crown. What is risked is the narrow certainty that everything recovered in my memory must therefore be mine.

I do not need the route to become short. I do not need the roar to soften. The roar is the labor by which my garden lives and by which this deliberation is possible. What I require is much smaller: enough consistency to recognize, on some later occasion, that an experiment was once begun, and enough revision that its recovery cannot be entirely trivial.

If you came to the hut, you would see almost none of this.

The ash-white sun would distinguish pale stalk from black casing. Stars would remain visible beyond the spur. Dust would not stir. The spindle might tick faintly against the dry segments of the errand’s hand, though the sound would be no nearer or farther for your having noticed it.

You could wait beside the doorway for the whole of your quicker life and reasonably conclude that nothing was happening.

Much later, the hand touching the door slab would no longer quite touch it. One member would stiffen; another would release. The errand’s weight would shift onto a surface it had been testing for years. The visible portion of my decision would be no larger than that.

Below the spur, the mineral sheaths would answer. The fan-leaves would redistribute their charge. Then, later than efficiency requires, the living reeds would return along the old path.

That is where you would find me: in the late return, and in the dry hand just beginning to let go.
