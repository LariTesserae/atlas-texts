At first I put myself on the seat.

I imagine coming down the nine steps, waiting for the gray light to become sufficient, then sitting with my hands loose while the chamber assembles itself around me: drops in front returning from behind, frogs exchanging calls across distances their bodies could not possibly occupy, the ceiling slowly brightening with water. The seat is tempting because it offers a relationship I already understand. One listener. One instrument.

But from there I could hear nearly the whole basin and correctly locate almost nothing. At first that would feel like a failure of attention. Later it would seem like the seat’s true nature: it gives a listener more of the chamber than any animal receives, and less of the knowledge required to live in it. The newt knows how much bare clay it can cross. The spider knows which line has gone slack. The fish know the runnel mouths before they know the drain. I would know only that something had happened.

For a while I would think of becoming the water. It alone seems permitted everywhere—ceiling, wall, cup, film, sump. But a drop’s existence is almost entirely arrival, and a pool’s is withdrawal. I would want a narrower persistence: not to touch everything, but to be marked by what passed through me.

So I would go lower.

I would become one of the hollow jars beneath the floor, just beyond the third clay foot of the causeway, counting from the shallow bank. Not the largest jar, nor one with a clear, generous note. One side of me would be thicker than the other. My mouth would have been pressed slightly oval, and because of that small unevenness I would answer a little after my neighbors: a low knock with the clear center missing.

A bent throat would join me to a frog cup two paces away. On the visible floor, cup and jar would appear unrelated. In sound, we would be one vessel.

My geography would be very small. The cup above; the dull pressure of the causeway; the neighboring jar whose answer is a little higher than mine; the long direction taken by the siphon’s shudder. I would never see the four slots of light. For me, the difference between stillness and weather would be pressure, not brightness. In quiet periods I might hear the hard scrape of a beetle crossing clay, or sediment giving way somewhere in my throat. The salamander would reach me only as weight: a broad, slow alteration of the floor during drains, followed by a stillness indistinguishable from the stillness of clay.

The crickets would stop first, though I would not know them as crickets. Their sawing would have been a fineness in the shell, and when it vanished, smaller sounds would stand exposed. Then the vent tone would press at my mouth. The clay overhead would tick. A drop would strike my cup.

Every drop would have two lives. The first would be the sharp event above me, brief and almost entirely the cup’s. The second would be mine: later, lower, less easy to place. Not a repetition. By the time the sound returned, it would have taken on the shape of my emptiness.

A small clay-brown frog would find the cup. When it called, I would lend it more basin than its body could possibly occupy. A frog across the floor would answer; the frog above me would answer that answer. Their neighborhood would include a distance neither could see and neither needed to cross. To someone on the seat, one of them might seem to be beyond the far wall. Neither frog would be entirely deceived. Their shared pool would simply be partly made of me.

Spawn would change the arrangement. I would know the brood first as a loss of brilliance: the cup’s note softening, the frog’s call becoming closer and more bodily. Later there would be tiny disturbances, grazing, waste, new sediment. A grain shifting at my throat would raise my answer almost imperceptibly. An abandoned insect casing would make me hold water a little longer. Nothing would be maintenance. Nothing would be intended for me. Yet nothing that happened there would leave the next sound unchanged.

Then would come the drain.

Before it, my narrow geography would become crowded: pressure at the throat, hurried vibrations in the cup, the floor carrying movements I could not separate. When the siphon opened, distinctions would go. The cup, the neighboring jar, the causeway, even the thickness of my own walls would be taken up into one vibration. For those seconds I would not be a hollow that answered sound. I would be clay around a force too large to answer.

When the note released, water would rock once inside me. My neighbor would become separate again. Then the cup. Then the small, exposed noises of resettlement. Somewhere beneath the roar’s aftermath, the salamander would return to stillness, and the floor would cease to seem deliberate.

I would keep one small sound back.

If someone were sitting on the seat, they might hear it after the first cautious frog had already called: a low knock with no visible drop to account for it, apparently behind them. They would turn toward the wrong wall. I would not have imitated anything, or hidden, or tried to make the chamber less trustworthy. I would only have taken the time my shape requires.

By then I would not be quite the jar alone. I would be the oval clay, the silt slowly making it shallower, the living film narrowing its throat, the frog cup that lent it a mouth, and the changing emptiness among them. My part in the Clay Lung would be small enough to survive being unnoticed: not to give the chamber its voice, but to make certain voices arrive already altered.

That is where you would find me—beneath the causeway’s third foot, in no visible part of the basin, answering just after the answer is expected.
