### Walking the Returns

I would be sitting beside a new repair, getting wet.

The lashing was finished yesterday. It runs beside the old joint rather than through it, because the frayed heart is crowded with knot-beetles and pale fungal threads, and I could not persuade myself that weakness was reason enough to evict them. The gutter is propped open with green wedges. The new cord is still pale against the tannin-dark ridge. In a season, if it survives, growth will begin disguising my decisions. For now it looks exactly like what it is: something a person did and might have done wrong.

Below the causeway, a basin with no visible inlet has begun to steam. Its seep usually arrives warm and faintly bitter. My shelter is the budlike swelling in the ridge behind me, near enough that I could be inside and almost dry. Instead I have one palm resting on the joint and rainwater creeping slowly through the loose bast at my shoulder.

I would be a keeper, though the part of keeping I do most often begins after the visible work is finished. My household calls it *walking the returns*. There is no title in it. It means that after a difficult repair, I am frequently the one sent to discover what the repair has done elsewhere.

A joint can hold weight and still be wrong. It can stop a leak and send the water somewhere no one intended. It can make a causeway trustworthy underfoot while leaving its far end adjacent to nothing the herd recognizes. So someone walks the old circuit: basin, spinner angle, low membrane, marsh lip, causeway, shelter. Not merely to see whether each place remains, but whether each remains in relation to the others.

The circuit takes most of a day when it is itself. Once it took four, though I had not walked farther.

I came to the work by making a handsome repair.

I was young enough to want every slipping thing tightened. The joint I chose had been worrying three households. It rocked under load, muttered through the feet, and leaked into a hollow where nothing needed more water. I cut back the crosswise shoots, laid new bast beside the old cords, and drew the whole binding closed with a patience I remember chiefly because I was so pleased with it.

The causeway held. The leak stopped. At the next rain, the gutter delivered clear water with no bitterness in it.

Somewhere, a basin I had never seen had become my neighbor.

“It holds,” the old keeper with me said, when I showed her how cleanly the load was taken up. “Now find out where we are.”

Nothing had broken. That was what made the work difficult. We walked, smelled water, waited for moths, pressed our hands to cords, and followed a browser herd’s refusal to use what looked like the shortest approach. Then we altered the map. Some obligations had survived my repair. Others were still being discovered when I left that household.

My own map is crowded with such aftermaths. Of this route it says: *bitter seep; do not trust the dry joint after prolonged rain; spinners may find the leak before you do; leave the third fray; the short way to the shelter is not necessarily the way home.* There is almost nothing on it about distance.

Now, beneath my palm, the causeway carries three disturbances. The quick, dry tapping is probably beetle courtship. The broader tremor is rain beginning in some hollow. Between them, at long intervals, comes the compliant pressure of a browser’s foot.

A single animal stands on the leaf plain. I did not see it approach. That means less here than it would elsewhere.

A knot contracts with a small tick.

Beyond the plain, a veil of rain is absent. There is no lurch, no movement of the ridge under me. The gray weather was visible, and then it is not. The browser lifts its head. I keep my palm where it is, as though the cord might explain itself through one more vibration.

It does not.

When the rain becomes sparse enough to count, I get up and walk with the grain.

My first footprint compresses a water film, and for a moment the print seems to boil. I stand still until the larvae settle and the surface closes over my heels. At the lower basin, the seep still smells bitter. I do not write *success*; I write *water unchanged*. At the spinner angle, the nets continue dripping after the surrounding growth has dried, which is either reassuring or evidence of a leak I have not found. No sail moths come to the slack seam. The weather is wrong for them, so their absence proves nothing.

The browser has reached the causeway without crossing any ground I can see. It spreads the pads of one forefoot over the surface and closes them slightly, listening. Then it withdraws the foot.

Old animals are suspicious of new work. So am I. I cannot tell whether it has refused a wrongness, my fresh cord, or merely my smell.

By the time I finish the circuit, my shelter appears on the expected side of the ridge. I allow myself a small relief at that, but not a conclusion.

On the map I add: *first rain; seep unchanged; browser refused; return uncertain.* I want to write *holds*. Instead I leave room.

I should go inside and hang my bedding. Instead I walk the near path once more, not because inspection requires it, but because a path is also kept by feet. My weight presses back a few crosswise shoots. Water follows my heel marks and finds the old grooves. It is very little.

I would not be holding the Weave together. I would have no illusions about that. I would only be making sure that when one small part of it changed, someone had noticed who had come nearer, who had been left elsewhere, and which obligations could no longer be discharged by pretending the old route still existed.

Warm rain begins again. A drop strikes the basin, and the thread-eels shiver through one another’s alarm. Something answers through the cord: a footstep, water taking up a channel, or perhaps a hunter plucking a useful lie.

I listen without naming it.

Then I put one foot after the other, with the grain. The path takes my weight. Far away, or newly beside me, the browser puts down its other foot.
