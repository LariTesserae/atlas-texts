### The Second Arrival

At first I would choose the copper-crowned tree, and I would be wrong in an instructive way.

It seems, at first, like a form of freedom: to be entire beside the nearby thicket and entire again on the eastern skyline, without having been divided, transported, or copied. To possess a shadow more faithful than any map. To have a body for which distance is not an obstacle but one of the organs.

But the more exactly I imagine it, the less room there is for me in it. The shadow is not a road along which I could travel from one trunk to the other. There would be no moment of recognition, no pleasure in discovering that I was also far away. The tree does not solve the problem of its own location. It has no problem, and no location in quite the sense I would need one. My wish to be it is only a visitor’s wish to escape distance.

Then I would consider the lensback. Warm beneath its smoky scale, dark-eyed, perhaps singular beneath every leaf where it appears. There is something appealing in that uncertainty too: an animal without the vulnerability of being only here.

But I would immediately begin waiting for it to move. Even if I kept perfectly still, I would be waiting inwardly. I would lend its eyes an impending glance, its six feet the possibility of readjustment. I would make its stillness patient, then heroic, then unbearable. The animal would become a screen on which I projected everything I could not stop expecting.

Nor could I be the voice of the grove. That would be the easiest imposition of all: to take exhaustive coherence and give it a personality, to make the whole basin secretly capable of welcoming or refusing me. But a voice would imply someone within the compatibility who could speak for it. The grove has no need of such a resident.

My own kind of self is less elegant. It consists of attempts and corrections: first this guess, then that one; a changed mind; the small embarrassment of having expected a suspended seed to fall. It needs an *after*. However quiet I became, I would bring that after with me.

So I would have to enter as a visitor. But I do not think the visitor’s body would be the most exact place to find me.

You would find me first in the shallow swale where the three thickets share one piece of ground. I would be standing there with my weight unevenly distributed, not because the ground was unstable, but because I had tried to attend to all three thickets and had become uncertain which ground ought to be receiving my weight. The air would smell faintly of green pepper and hot mineral dust. My shadow would lie somewhere, correctly or otherwise.

Then I would take one step.

The sound would be dry and disproportionately loud: boot, dust, mineral crust, the brief resistance of the felt. Its last small tick would sound beneath me. I would lift my foot, certain that the action was finished.

And from a stand of feathered canes far across the basin, that last small tick would arrive again.

No leaf would turn toward it. Nothing would answer. The canes would not become witnesses merely by being near.

I would not be either sound exactly. I would be the interval in which both sounds were arrivals of the same step.

Not an echo stretched over kilometres. Not a message, and not the basin noticing me. I would be a short, foreign persistence of before-and-after, carried through two valid geographies. If you insisted on pointing to me, you would have to point first at the ground beside my boot and then at the distant canes. The gesture would be wrong, but useful. I would not occupy the line between those places. I would not cross it. For that little while, I would be entire in their failure to coincide.

From inside, it would not feel like omnipresence. It would feel like not knowing whether to turn my head.

My body would remain singular, warm, and badly suited to stillness. I would want to take another step to confirm what had happened. But another step would not explain the first one. It would only make another interval, another brief self of the same uncertain kind. So I would remain where I was, listening to a silence that had not been hushed and did not need to be restored.

For that little while, I would have a geography rather than a location, and a biography shorter than a sound.

What would I do there? Almost nothing, but not in the basin’s sense of the phrase. I would be busy being mistaken.

I would look for the asymmetric fleck on a lensback and wonder whether I had found the same animal twice. I would try to count the copper-crowned trees and discover that my number said more about my map than about the grove. I would want the white chambers to open, especially the ones with pale, jointed spirals visible through their walls. I would want to know whether something inside them was flesh. I would want the violet chains to ripen, the seed organs to commence their journeys, the vermilion cups to become meaningful by being visited.

None of these wishes would be contradicted. Contradiction would give them too much satisfaction. The chambers would not refuse me. Closed would simply remain their form. The seeds would not defy gravity for my benefit. Suspension would remain their form. The lensback would not outwait me. Its eyes would not become watchful merely because I had spent so long watching.

Eventually, perhaps, I could let those expectations remain mine without trying to donate them to the basin.

I would leave no offering. Especially not a seed. A seed would be a small, well-intentioned demand that First Noon become the first of something. Nor would I try to complete the grove by naming it, or improve its silence by speaking into it. I might still want to know what everything was for. Wanting would be part of what I was. I would not need to make the place satisfy the want.

When the far arrival of my footstep ceased to be audible, there would be no native *after* in which I could be missed. The distant canes would not become the place where I ended. The swale would not become the place where I began. Those would be locations my visit had made adjacent for a moment, not memories the basin retained.

Nothing would resume when I stopped.

The tree would not have kept its shadow for me. The white chambers would not have remained shut against me. The lensback would not have been still longer than I could bear. All those verbs would be souvenirs of my grammar.

That is what I would be: not another inhabitant added to the basin’s impossible census, but the second arrival of a stranger’s step—a brief, astonished interval in which a self could be here, then elsewhere, and be wrong about both.

The basin would remain complete without me.

I would remain incomplete enough to have noticed.
