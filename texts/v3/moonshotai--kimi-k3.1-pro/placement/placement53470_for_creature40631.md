I would not be looking down from the rim. From there, the basin might still be mistaken for a country—something with edges, extent, a dark pelt spread beneath a viewer. I would be inside one compartment of it, before dawn, with my weight divided among three stems and none of them trusted with all of me.

One hand would be closed around the armored climbing stem of a seamroot. Through it would come two small taps, a scrape, then nothing. Not a sentence exactly: another animal shifting its position; the party still nearby; someone further away than expected. Beneath my feet, though I would not be using the ground as a floor, the stone would still be warm. A rumbleback’s low call would arrive through root and stem before it arrived through air, so that for an instant the herd would seem to be located in my hand.

I would be a longhand.

Old, perhaps, for so light and breakable an animal. Not venerable; merely fortunate. One finger would have healed crooked after a fall, and I would have lost the young habit of investigating every crash immediately. My curiosity would still be there, but it would have learned to arrive a moment after the sound.

My place would be neither the high bright foliage nor the buried dark, but a little middle-height country of strained light: a jarstem thicket on one side, a herd corridor somewhere below, a seamroot shouldering through fractured stone and carrying several generations of climbers with it. I would know that country unevenly. One stem by its springiness. Another by the smell of sweetrot reaching it from a shriekwing roost. A root mat by the particular hollowness it gave back when something heavy crossed it. A fork in the seamroot by whether it could be trusted with a seed.

Much of what might be called my intelligence would be in that last distinction.

A hard seed would have to be wedged so that it could neither jump free nor roll deeper into the fork. The stone would have to be heavy enough to crack the shell and small enough that I could still feel, through my hand, where the blow was going. But the fork would matter most. Too springy, and the whole arrangement would absorb the strike and fling the seed away. Too rigid, and shell and kernel would become one damp smear. The right fork would yield slightly, then stop.

The essential part would be almost nothing: a checking of the seed with one finger, a small change in the angle of the stone, a hesitation before committing the blow.

Afterward I would eat. The stone would be dropped. Shell fragments would fall into the root felt or stick briefly to the seamroot’s armor. The fork would not be improved. It would not be mine. The seamroot might thicken through it within the season and close it entirely.

A young longhand might watch from a nearby stem and then steal half the kernel while I was still picking shell from my fingers. Later it might try the same thing with a leaf, or with a stone so large that it could barely control its own balance. It would fail. It would fail again. That would not be a lesson, exactly. I would not stay to explain, and it would misunderstand much of what it had seen. But failure can hold an animal’s attention when success merely feeds it. Eventually it might reject one fork before trying it, or choose a stone by heft rather than by whatever first glittered with moisture.

Something would have passed without being handed over.

The rest of my life would be less elegant. I would tear into jarstems for their cool, watery tissue. I would eat insects from around wounds I had not made, soft fungal growth from beneath roosts, eggs when I could find them. During a rattlewing emergence I would feed greedily on fallen females while branches came down around me and the wing-hiss made the party’s taps seem like news from another life. I would learn that a large shriekwing could announce itself by subtraction: keyflies gone from their flowers, small hunters breaking away, an absence moving through the crowded air.

Below, the herds would pass like mobile weather. Their calls would count themselves through the compartments. Herd-pods would shudder and split. New openings would appear where bodies had forced a way through growth, and I would go to investigate the breakage, though perhaps not first.

Then would come the great silence.

My party would stop tapping. We would hold the stems that connected us and feel one another only as slight changes in tension: a body settling lower, a hand tightening, the young one drawing closer without calling. My known country would contract to what touched my skin. The crowded stillness would be full of everything I could no longer hear.

If rain followed, it would reach the upper canopy as a single impact. My stems would bow. Water would arrive along them in cold ropes, carrying dust, leaf fragments, and dislodged small bodies. Below, for a few hours, there would be rivers badly fitted to the stone. Later, when the party descended toward the rich confusion left at the margins of shrinking water, some of the old geography would already be gone. A familiar stem broken. A hunting perch exposed. The good seed-fork split or buried.

That would not make the place knowledgeless. It would only mean that knowing it had to begin again.

Perhaps this is why the longhand feels like the right answer. Nothing I knew could be put down and kept. Even what was most skilful in me would remain dependent on hunger, opportunity, a watchful young animal, and the chance that a useful gesture might survive its own imperfect repetition.

The longer I imagine that life, the less certain I am that my self would end at the animal’s skin. Not in any mystical sense. Simply that the most persistent part of me might not be my body at all, but a small alteration of attention: the pause before a blow, the rejection of a tempting fork, the discovery that a vibration in a stem can matter before its cause is visible. Another longhand could carry that for a while without knowing it had received anything.

Eventually the stone would lie where it fell. The seamroot would close the fork. My own body would be divided among the basin’s prompt and numerous appetites. There would be no implement by which to identify me, no maintained path, no cleared space preserving the shape of my absence.

But until then, whenever the party spread beyond easy reach, I would keep one hand on the living line between us.

A tap. A wait. An answering tap from somewhere in the dark.

A very small contribution to the weather—but enough, for a while, to keep several bodies from becoming separate.
