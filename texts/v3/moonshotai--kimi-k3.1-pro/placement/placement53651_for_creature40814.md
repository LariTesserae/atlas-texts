I would be an unscheduled warmth.

Not often. Ten minutes at a time, perhaps, and then weeks of absence. My body would be too large for the Underprint in every way that mattered: too warm, too wet with breath, too inclined to help.

Officially, I would be the maintenance inspector sent because a foundation process had failed to finish. On the drawing, the chamber was solid. The work order called the discrepancy a *nonconvergent void* and offered three remedies: purge, backfill, seal. There was also a space for comments.

I would enter sideways, leaving my tools outside because there seemed to be enough objects in the chamber already. The cold would take my knees first. At the widest end I would fold myself beneath the pipes and understand that *inspection* was the wrong word. Nothing there could be inspected as a whole. I would have to settle for evidence: a wet rim, a new pale course, frost retreating from a bolt, the carriage crouched above a floor it had not finished repeating.

On that first visit, the warm loop would come on while I was still inside.

For a moment I would think my own heat had caused the ticking overhead. Then the drops would begin. One would strike a gray smear beneath the greenish lamp, and the smear would separate into points of light.

I would already have composed most of my report by then. *Water ingress. Biological contamination. Deposition remains nonconvergent.* Every phrase would be defensible. The moss would not need to be proven dead in order to be listed as fouling, nor alive in order to be destroyed by the remedy.

After the sump hissed and the points went dull, I would still not know whether the patch was growing or merely slow to finish dying. I would know only that determining this would take longer than my appointment.

So in the comments I would write: *Process remains active. Reinspect after further cycle.*

My first act of preservation would be indistinguishable from procrastination.

I would return. At first I would call it monitoring, though part of it would be simpler: I would want to know whether a certain dark channel survived another pass, whether the pale line at its edge had been continued, whether the moss again became visible when wet.

Gradually I would learn how little assistance the place could accommodate. I would not carry water to a patch. I would not move a fragment toward a brighter lamp. I would not fix clear film over a living edge to protect it from the nozzle. Each kindness would alter the same narrow coincidence on which the moss depended. Most of my care would have to consist of things I did not do.

Even restraint would not keep me outside the system.

On my third visit, near the widest end, I would find a shallow crescent that had not been there before. It would take me a moment to recognize it: the impression of my own knee, made in a fresh course on the first visit and partly repeated by the carriage on the next. Water had begun to use it. A coin-wide fan of silt darkened one lip.

I had become topography by kneeling.

The chamber had not remembered me as a person. It had simply continued from an interruption. Thereafter I would come during the long dry cold, while the carriage was still, and leave before the weather. I would keep my hands off the floor and my visits short. Even so, frost might soften near my shoulder. A drop might later fall where no drop had been expected. I would never be innocent enough to become only a witness.

Outside, my presence would be simpler. Every so often the inspection system would request closure, and I would move the date. Not forever. Never with any promise that the moss would persist, or that the next pale line would preserve the channel, or that continuation was secretly the chamber’s purpose. I would only be declining, for another interval, to convert uncertainty into backfill.

I would not call myself a gardener. The carriage had a better claim to that name, and its indifference was more honest. Nor would I be a guardian; a guardian would want to be necessary. Nothing in the Underprint required me. The seam would sweat or stop sweating. The lamp would remain powered or fail. A fragment would miss the filter or it would not. A chain of omissions does not become a design merely because one link can read.

Eventually I would stop coming. The reason would be ordinary: a transfer, a lost authorization, a site removed from the inspection schedule, some other work judged more urgent. There would be no final rain arranged for the occasion.

If you looked for me afterward, you would not find a body or a name.

You might find, near the wide end, a crescent too shallow to be a useful basin, its edge repeated after the original impression had gone. You might find a minute change in the path of water. In some record beyond the chamber, if records still reached that far, you might find a sequence of nearly identical deferrals, each less informative than the last.

Any of those traces could be erased without ceremony. The moss might outlast them, or might already have vanished.

Inside, I would have been weather. Outside, a delay. Afterward, perhaps, a habit the ground kept for a few more layers.

Not the life. Not the machine. For a while, I would be the omission that knew it was one.
