### What the Hand Keeps

I would arrive wanting the wrong thing: a way to matter to the place.

Standing at the rim, with the wind pulling at my clothes, I might imagine myself as the seep’s keeper, or as the one who finally understood the terms on which it was held. But even that would be a kind of vanity. There is no vacant office for understanding here. To tend the hollow would only be to make a more elaborate footprint.

So, for a while, I would have to remain what I was: cold, oversized, and gently accommodated. I would wait too long for the light to change. I would mistake stillness for attention and silence for refusal. Only after I had tired of listening to myself might my attention become smaller than my wish to be significant.

It might come down, at last, to a gray hand correcting the angle of a stone.

That is where I would find myself.

I would be one of the gray animals—not an exceptional one, and certainly not the cleverest. An old animal, perhaps, often to be found between the overhang and the lower reach of the rill. Not in a territory: in a familiarity. A root my feet knew without looking. A piece of saturated wood whose surface gave differently where it was inhabited. A natural fork in which I sometimes slept, unless another animal was already there.

From inside, I would not know that the basin was timeless. I would know practical recurrences. Water finding the same seam. Condensation gathering inside my ears. The objectionable lightness of a shell that had looked full. The difference between wood that merely sounded soft and wood that would answer a twig.

The wet would not be symbolic. It would be uncomfortable.

Two fingers of my left hand would be stiff from an old injury, and I would do most of my close work with the right. Because of this, I would reject certain snails that another animal might have opened easily. Not because I sensed anything mysterious in them, but because I knew the limits of my grip. A visitor might see deliberation where there was only a private history of small failures.

I would still fail. I would choose twigs that bent, lose larvae in the moss, strike a shell badly and have to begin again. Sometimes I would sit turning a pebble over with no use for it in mind. The pause would not have to flower into an invention. It could end with the pebble dropped and my attention elsewhere.

But I would remember. The hollow’s lack of memorial would not require my mind to be blank.

I would know other animals individually, though not by anything a visitor could call a name. One would be the animal whose wet shoulder I had slept against in the fork, and whose fingers used to groom the fur behind my ear. Another would be the young one that once sat nearby, awkwardly striking wood with a stone, while I went on feeding and did not intervene. Whether it learned anything from me would not be mine to decide.

When the companion was gone, there would be no place to lay down what I knew of them. Perhaps I would continue sleeping in that fork for a while. Perhaps I would stop without any visible reason. Perhaps the memory would remain distinct for the rest of my life, or perhaps it would thin into habits I no longer recognized as remembrance.

Nothing would need to be carved for either to be true.

Eventually my own body would enter the same close attendance of mouths and water. Nothing I had learned would pass into the roots or the pebbles. Another animal would put its feet where mine had been. From outside, this might look like erasure. From inside, I think it would feel simpler: I had lived without owning the surfaces that sustained me.

If you found me, I would be near the dripping lip, though not guarding it. The boundary would not be sacred to me. It would merely be where the useful darkness ended. I would be sitting back on my haunches, holding a snail against my palm, my fur beaded and my injured fingers tucked out of the way.

I would make room for you without alarm. Then I might watch your hands—not because I recognized a fellow maker, but because hands are worth watching.

You might wait for me to keep the pebble afterward, or to strike in some new way, or to offer a sign that I was on the road toward you. But I would not be almost you. I would be entirely busy being what I was.

My attention would be in the shell’s lip, the resistance under my thumb, the small correction that might let me draw the body out whole. For the time my fingers were closed, the stone would belong completely to that act. It would be neither the beginning of something nor an inadequate version of anything else. It would be enough.

Then I would eat, and drink, and open my hand.

The pebble would fall among the other dark surfaces. Your footprint behind you would already be filling with water. For a little while, my palm would still know the stone’s weight.

Then that, too, would be followed—not preserved, not solemnly surrendered, simply followed—by the next thing my hands needed to do.
