At first, water seems the obvious choice. It enters every territory, learns every opening, and is permitted beneath every door. But water is always leaving: down the next spillway, into the peat, through the black pools, out at the limestone slot. Then I imagine an old lodge, chamber within chamber, holding the scents and abandoned passages of many families. Yet even a lodge is too easily mistaken for a thing.

I would rather be one of those lives that begins on the uncertain edge between thing and inhabitant.

I would be a dwarf willow in the middle basin—more exactly, a willow that had begun as a building stake and had failed, usefully, to remain a stake.

You would find me on the downstream side of an old terraced lodge, beside a canal that narrowed before turning into a small junction pond. From the rim I would be indistinguishable from the general green. From the water I would be a low, many-stemmed thicket, repeatedly cut back, with one side silvered by the brief afternoon light and the other dark against wet wood. Beneath me would be black foundations, silt, stones wedged long ago, and wood faintly crusted with calcite. A muddy path would cross the lodge roof above me. At dusk, beavers would pass close enough for their wakes to press against my roots.

I would have arrived in a beaver’s mouth: a cut stem, pale at both ends, carried with the lips shut behind the incisors. Perhaps I was meant to brace a little mud where the current had begun pulling too strongly at the lodge wall. Perhaps I was only one stem among many, driven deep and half forgotten. My first usefulness would have been resistance. The current pressed; the mud held; I stayed.

There would be no clear day on which I stopped being material. A buried node would swell. A white root would enter the silt. One small leaf would open above the water and be taken by a beetle or torn by rain. The following spring there would be several shoots. By then the original repair might already have been covered by fresh work, and no animal would have any reason to remember that part of the bank was living because it had once been used.

After that, the basin would shape me without consulting any single intention. A raised pond would put my lower roots into cold water. A neglected opening would lower it again, leaving a comb of fine rootlets exposed to the air. Silt would gather around my stems because the current slowed there. Moss would cross from stone to wood to bark, making no distinction among them. In my shade, a small fish might hold just beyond the quicker water. Higher up, where old drift had become soil, a vole might establish a runway or a duck might flatten a nest among last year’s sedges.

The beavers would cut me back.

This would not be an interruption of my place in the city. It would be part of it. Bark and tender stems would become food; larger pieces might be dragged away and forced into mud elsewhere. What remained of me would sprout from low, crowded stools. Feeding would make me denser. I would become at once a thicket, a food reserve, a bank, and a supply of future building material.

Nor would I be harmless. My roots would catch sediment until the canal beside me shallowed. A route once deep enough to conceal a beaver’s back might begin to expose it. Water turning aside around my root mat might soften a neighbour’s bank. Then there would be teeth in my stems, or paws clearing mud, or no response at all. The family might leave. Sedges might enter the abandoned channel. My growth would not be good for the basin or bad for it; it would simply alter where the next necessity arose.

Winter would fasten ice around my lower wood. Thaw might tear some of it away. A branch of mine could travel only a few body lengths before being caught in another dam, or could go farther and be reused by animals that had never encountered my roots. If a living fragment lodged deeply enough, it might sprout below another lodge. I could become food, obstruction, shelter, structure, or another beginning, and there would be no reason for the basin to settle which one I truly was.

I would never see the nine kilometres of water and terraces. My knowledge would be local and exact: the pressure of silt against bark, the tremor of teeth in nearby wood, the level at which the current began to work beneath my roots, the annual return of light to the same wet stems.

That is where I would be. Not presiding over the city, and not merely built into it.

A small repair that had taken root—and, by rooting, had made further repair necessary.
