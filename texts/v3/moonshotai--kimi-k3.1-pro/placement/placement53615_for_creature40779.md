I would not be standing on the mineral rim.

To listen from outside would leave me intact in a way nothing in the basin is intact. Nor would I be the skyline, the whole green city, or some ancient root beneath it. The skyline would already be a temporary agreement. The city would have no enduring whole to be. And an oldest root would imply a centre where the basin keeps its identity, rather than countless local continuities opening and closing.

I might first look for myself in a growing tip: pale, sensitive, almost nothing but capacity. There would be a fierce pleasure in that—advancing by cell division, feeling moisture as a change in possibility, becoming pavement beneath myself or rising and leaving a tower as the record of my passage. But a tip does not remain separate from what it makes. It spends itself into structure. Soon most of me would be conducting water through a wall or holding a reservoir in shape, my range of answers narrowed to maintenance.

A ruptured fragment would offer another life. I could begin as reservoir lining and end as root felt; be carried beyond the main growth, whiten, nearly fail, then find a mineral seam and put out one green scale. I could become remote, then central, then remote again, without travelling at all. But even that would give me more continuity than I think I would have there.

I would find myself later, and more briefly.

The rain has just stopped. A little way in from the southern rim, above a court whose reservoir is still deepening, pale growth is shining in the ordinary sunlight. The air smells of wet salt and sap heating on dry leaf-scales. Several incompatible structures occupy the court. A tower booms inside a vault that will never enclose it. A street carries the hiss of water toward an opening that is already becoming solid wall.

I am a narrow arch between two rising columns.

Neither column has reached me. I have no deck, no completed span, and only a translucent thickening where my gutter will be. Nevertheless, my shadow lies across the court: a line too fine to darken a whole leaf. Water is already using me.

The first drop enters from a spout that is still only a wet gleam. It runs along the route my vessels are expected to make. At the unsupported gap, it does not fall. It continues, trembling, and disappears into a living column somewhere beyond the next ward. A second drop follows. With each passage I become a little more specific—not wall, not tower, but this brief continuity in water.

Given another minute, I might put down roots and become an aqueduct. I might spread sideways into a roofed passage, or thicken until my arch is only a curve buried inside a buttress. These are not destinies, and I have no preference among them. They are responses still available to the tissue I am.

I have no view of the court, much less of the basin. What I have instead of a view is pressure: the strain arriving late from one column, the slight cooling where my shadow crosses new bark, the lessening tension as water leaves me. Somewhere a ward collapses with a roar. Somewhere nearer, that same ward is still rising. The sound of my own destruction may already be moving through the pavement, though nothing about me has yet begun to fall.

Then lignification runs through a green pier at the edge of the court.

It fixes a low vault and the heavy lip of a reservoir. The geometry is local, exact, and suddenly expensive to revise. My arch cannot remain with it.

For an instant I persist as a shadow cast by a form the fixed city can no longer accommodate. Then I cease to be available.

There is a concussion. The pavement jumps. Water shivers from the undersides of leaf-scales. No arch falls, because there was never enough fixed arch to make debris. No sap-rich wreck is drawn back into the roots. The columns simply find themselves rising on either side of a vault, and the court adjusts around the answer that has been left standing.

The third drop is already en route.

It emerges some minutes later, beyond several districts, from a leaf-gutter with no visible connection to the court. It falls into stored water and makes one small circle. The circle spreads, touches the waterproof lining, and disappears.

That would be nearly all of me.

Nearly, because a buttress had begun turning to meet one of my columns. After my vanishing it retains the first degree of that turn. Later, a root follows the curve and narrows an avenue by the width of a stem. The avenue’s narrowing changes where water collects during the next rain. None of this is a memory of an arch. There is no archive in which my brief shape remains available. It is only a constraint passed into tissue that still has consequences.

Eventually resorption will soften the buttress and withdraw even that. I will not return when it does. There will be no release of a stored self, no second chance to complete the span. Only substance and space becoming answerable again, free to produce pavement, lining, tower, or something whose shadow has not yet crossed the court.

I would not be the basin’s imagination; that would put a mind behind its growth. I would not be a rejected design, either, or a spirit grieving the city that outlasted me. I would simply be one of its briefest lives: material enough to carry water, definite enough to alter the growth beside me, and gone before I could become an inhabitant of the fixed world.

That is where I would live—in the wet interval when the city could still be otherwise, and one of those otherwises had enough substance to cast an ordinary shadow.

If you heard me from the mineral rim, I would sound like water crossing a bridge. By the time the sound arrived, there would be no bridge, and the water would be somewhere else.
