At first I would want to be the shrew, because it can leave. It knows the hollow as one fierce station on a circuit: whiskers at the stone, cold closing around the body, a silver plunge, then peat and nest and hunger elsewhere. The dipper is tempting for the same reason, and for its voice—one sharp note becoming larger than the room. But both are visitors. They take something away and never learn what it is to be altered by the next rise of water.

I would be a midge.

Not the winged insect at the entrance. For nearly all my life, I would be a red thread in the fine sediment near the rear seep, where each pressure pulse brings water with a different mineral taste. The chamber would have no far wall for me, no six careful steps, no concealed entrance. There would be grains, crevices, organic dust, the hardness of a buried flake. A stone shifting would be weather. A stickleback would be a disturbance in the water followed by a sudden absence.

My work would not feel like work. I would swallow sediment, keep what could be used, and pass the mineral through. Water would move a little farther into the deposit along my tube. A film would grow over something I had left bare. None of it would make me an attendant of the works. I would simply be one of the small, living irregularities through which they happened to run.

When the double knock came, it would not be a sound from behind anything. There would be no behind. Pressure would arrive through water and grain and body together. I would draw down. A surge might close the tube, strip away the surface, or carry me bodily into a colder patch. Afterwards there would be no question of restoring the old country. A grain here. Food enough there. Water moving through a narrow space. Begin from that.

The shrew might pass close enough to silver the pool above me, though I would never see the silver. The dipper’s enormous call would belong to a medium I barely inhabited. The fish turning at the slab might mean nothing until its shadow became the last thing overhead. Most lives here would be available to interruption in that way: not rounded off, not completed, simply taken into another life.

But suppose I was not taken.

Suppose that after the long cold feeding, after burial and exposure and the patient resumptions, the body that had been good at remaining in sediment began to make another use of itself. The winged form would not be my true self finally emerging. It would be only the last, shortest thing I did.

Rising, I would meet the surface first as a boundary and then as something to struggle through. My reflection would tremble beneath me; for a moment there would be two struggles, mine and the water’s broken version of it. The old skin would stay behind. Air would touch wings that had never been the important part of me.

The gray light at the entrance would not explain the hollow. It would only be a direction. A fish might take me before I cleared the pool. A web might take me where the sedges began. But if neither did, there would be cold air, wet leaves, other brief bodies, and perhaps eggs laid in water connected to the chamber by routes I would never travel.

I would carry out no plan of the works, no memory of their knocks and outfalls. Only a body made, in some small part, from what the seep and films and sediment had provided. The pool would remain loud behind me. The ochre would continue thickening without me. Long before the slab fell, I would have stopped being one of its inhabitants.

That is where I would find myself: in the long, buried life before the wings, and then, if chance allowed, in the few seconds when something the works had made became capable of leaving. Not a witness to the whole. Not even a memory of it. Just one of its perishable outputs, carrying a little of the dark into a place the hollow would never touch.
