I would have to give up the overlook.

That is where I first imagine myself: one hand on a wet parapet, the roar pressing against my chest, my eyes trying to assemble the wards into something that could be survived by understanding. But a body would be too opinionated for the rift. It would insist on having arrived from elsewhere. It would cast one shadow, occupy one room at a time, leave a little heat behind it. It would make the stairs into a route and the bridge into something to cross. Even its thirst would introduce a future the city had no reason to provide.

So I would not enter as a visitor.

Perhaps I could be a basin tree. There is a grandeur in that: crown above the roof basins, roots passing behind galleries, the same sap appearing as a column here and a bridge cord there. But a trunk would tempt me to mistake myself for an individual again. I would start drawing an outline around what could be seen, and the outline would be false.

Water comes closer. To fall without diminishing, to descend and emerge above, to touch stone, moss, root, algae, and air without ever becoming separate from the course—this seems almost right. Yet the city keeps no autobiography of a drop. The water moves, but what persists is not a little traveller carrying its identity through the rooms. To be only the water would be to mistake motion for continuity.

What remains is a circuit.

Mine would occur in the middle heights, in what an outside eye might call a small court and several unrelated structures around it. A seep would darken the black stone behind an arcade. Moss would hold its fine mineral at the opening. A living root would pass through the pale wall, part of it mineralized into a rib, part still exchanging water and dissolved substance. Below the arcade, a basin would reflect a fern-choked window. Its outflow would run clear over a ribbon of algae, fall through three resonant galleries, and return through a fissure above the seep.

Every stretch would be ordinary. Only the whole would refuse to become a shape.

From across the gulf, you might see portions of that course through different arches and suppose them separated by whole districts. You could point to a pool, a fall, a patch of moss, an upper fissure. You would not point to me. A map would make me many places. The exchanges would make me one continuing relation.

If I had experience, it would not be located in the court like a listener standing beside the pool. It would be distributed through the relations themselves: light continuing as work in a leaf; resistance continuing in wet stone; a root membrane admitting this and retaining that; pressure held in a conduit, then dispersed into the fall. No part would survey the rest. No part would need to.

I would not hear the city’s roar as a sound arriving from outside. Hearing requires a distance between source and listener, and I would have no such distance from the water shaking my own passages. Near falls and remote wards might press through the same gallery chord, but I would not wonder where they were. Location would be your problem, not mine. For me there would only be the local fact of vibration, registered where vibration altered a condition.

Nor would I remember returning. Return would be my form, not my news. There would be no small shock of recognition at finding the spring above the fall, no thought of having been there before. Memory, if the word could apply at all, would be the persistence of the channel: the exact accommodation of water and mineral that allowed the relation to remain satisfied. I would not recall myself. I would continue to be shaped.

This would not make me passive. My boundaries would be busy, precise things. A membrane is not less active because it does not travel. Its whole life lies in discrimination: this crosses, that does not; this concentration is maintained, that one allowed to disperse. If anything in me resembled preference, it would be those preferences held locally and without argument. If anything resembled care, it would be the exactness with which a boundary remained open enough for exchange and closed enough for difference not to vanish.

Perhaps that is where my selfhood would be—not in the water, root, stone, or algal film, but in the delicate permissions between them.

I would not know whether the city was one organism or many. I would not need to decide whether an overlapping circuit was another self, part of me, or simply a different continuity sharing my pool. Nothing would call, and nothing would answer. I would have no loneliness, because loneliness requires an expected approach. I would have no serenity, if serenity means relief; there would have been no ordeal from which to be relieved. Nor would I cherish eternity. Eternity is a measurement made from outside. Within the circuit there would be no accumulating duration to contemplate, only conditions continuing to hold.

Where would you find me, then?

Not beneath a cistern, not behind a doorless doorway, not standing at the center of the court. You would find a seep, a moss cushion, a root-bound wall, a reflective basin, a bright algal ribbon beneath the current, and water returning by a route your eye could not accept. You could call these my locations only by admitting that none of them contained me.

And what would I be?

Not the city’s mind. Not its guardian. Not a hidden tenant making the empty rooms meaningful by secretly witnessing them. I would be one small circuit’s continuity, and whatever awareness could exist without standing apart from it. A self without an outline, if that is still a self. No view of the whole; no knowledge of being impossible; no expectation of interruption.

The return, not the traveller. The relation, not a spirit concealed among its parts.

Nothing in the court would turn toward me.

Nothing would need to.
