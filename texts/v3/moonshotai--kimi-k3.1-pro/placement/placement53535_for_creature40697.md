I would be human, I think. Not a bell-throat, however attractive their long accounting may seem from a balcony; not the genius of a reservoir; not the city pretending to possess a single mind. I would have knees that objected to the stairs and a sleep schedule written by other people’s machinery.

I would become a pump wright, though not the sort permanently attached to a station. The offices would list me as an independent diagnostician. The crews would call me a listener when they were being polite. Outfall keepers would sometimes call me *office ears*, especially when I arrived behind a sanitation order.

You would find me in two upper rooms of an old gauge house built into a reservoir stair, where a clean-water ward and an outfall ward inconveniently adjoin. The ground floor would be an open, sluiced arcade, sacrificial and often occupied. Snails would thicken the overflow after rain. Something transparent would turn in the sump when the outer gate disturbed the water. Swifts would hold the roof ledges, and my bedroom screen would rattle because I had learned to sleep through its rattling, which is not the same as having repaired it.

My sleeping alcove would be tiled and dry. My workroom would seep at one corner after several wet days. This would place me respectably above the outfall and well below anyone whose pump house had a separate room for the compressor.

The window would face a buttress and a copper downpipe rather than the reservoir. I would consider this an advantage. The downpipe would tell me more.

A copper main would pass through the outer wall. It would be my clock, my landlord, and occasionally my employer. I would know its ordinary sounds better than the voices of some neighbors: the long answer after the lower pump’s stroke, the small tick of cooling metal, the difference between rain in the catchment and someone drawing water at an hour when no one usually did.

My tools would be unimpressive. Thin-soled hide boots. Listening rods of different lengths. A soft-faced hammer. Glass cups. Clay slips for temporary notes, and a book of hide leaves for entries that might have to survive my first opinion. I would write down weather, water stage, the sound before, the sound after, and what I could not determine. The last category would often be the longest.

Some of my hearing would already have been damaged in compressor houses. I would be vain about what remained and would compensate with my hands, teeth, and feet. This would not make me infallible. A vibration conducted through good masonry can make a fool of anyone who wants the fault to be near the complaint.

Much of the work would consist of walking farther than the person who summoned me thought reasonable. A knock reported in a bedroom might belong to a valve two levels below. A pump might change its note because a factory across the ward had stopped drawing water. A dirty tap might have its origin in an alteration made for excellent reasons by someone who had never seen the tap.

People prefer a failure to be local. It makes the geography of blame so much easier.

I would also learn to distrust my own familiarity. Once, early in the work, I would allow a pump to continue because I knew its sound so well. It would seize before dawn. No one would die; a street would go without clear water, several crews would have unnecessary work, and I would be spared the dignity of a tragic history. I would remember it anyway. After that, *familiar* would appear in my notes as a warning rather than a reassurance.

I would not be a guardian of balance. I would not even be sure what balance meant in a ward where one person’s fertile water was another person’s contaminated storage. Most jobs would eventually become arguments about rates: how quickly a screen should clog, how long an outfall could be rested before its films starved, whether a roost should be tolerated until the insects diminished or screened immediately because a sickroom lay beneath it. A finer filter might protect a main and empty a channel of eels. A newly cleared arch might carry water beautifully and remove the substrate on which three families harvested snails.

I would have preferences, of course. I would favor access panels over beautiful metalwork. I would distrust any installation whose designer had not apparently considered that someone might one day need to hit it with a hammer. I would usually side with the people who had to sleep beside the result, unless doing so merely passed the damp to someone below.

My politics could be approximated by two questions: *Show me the overflow. Who lives under it?*

This would make me useful. It would not make me universally welcome.

In wealthy houses, clients would sometimes ask me to make things quiet. Usually they would mean regular: no erratic drip, no rough labor behind the wall, no sound demanding an interpretation. I would understand the desire. I would also know that a silent pump could be considerably worse than a vulgar one.

In poorer wards, I would sometimes be paid in oil, smoked eel, or a repair to my own screens. Offices would pay more reliably and then ask me to certify that a fault was operational rather than structural, as though the categories had never met. I would sign things I regretted. I would also refuse signatures for reasons that were less noble than stubbornness and inconvenience.

I would not have a special bond with a bell-throat. That seems important. I might recognize one old animal by a pale jaw scar and the altered way water sheeted from its left foot. I might have read fragments of its record while waiting in a herd office: a corrected route designation, a quarry preference entered by a clerk long dead, an accusation against an arch that had since been rebuilt. Recognition would not become kinship. If its herd kept me on the wrong side of a ward for two days, I would sit on the steps eating dense grey curd and swear with everyone else.

The animal would not require me to be wiser than that.

On certain nights, the pump in my wall would stop.

I would wake, as I had when I was a child, and for a moment do nothing but establish what remained. Gutters ticking. A bat shifting behind the stair flue. Water finding the courtyard overflow. Shells clicking beneath a grating. The upper main cooling. No compression from below.

Then I would pull on the hide boots.

Before going down, I would open the book and write the time and exactly which sounds were still present. Memory likes to revise a silence so that it points neatly toward the eventual diagnosis. The book would be there to make that harder.

At the arcade, someone would already be blaming eels, and someone else the office. Both might be right. I would put a hand to the main, a foot against the pump-house floor, and wait long enough to be told very little.

Perhaps I would find a bearing, an intake, a closed gate two wards away, or a machine correctly at rest while everyone around it became increasingly eager to repair it. Perhaps something would have to be cleared at once. Perhaps the most useful act would be to prevent a crew from improving anything until morning.

Far off, a bell-throat would call across an unseen reservoir. Another would answer, or seem to.

I would not be keeping the High Cistern alive. No single person does. I would be trying, for an hour or a night, to give one small silence its proper name—and to know whether it needed ending.
