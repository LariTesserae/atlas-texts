### Almost

I would be out on the grit, not in any of the islands.

At first there would be nothing to see: a shallow oval, a little salt at one end, a seam of violet where dust had gathered. From the rim I would be less than a footprint. Even close by, you might mistake me for one of those discarded cases that life has not yet finished using.

You would not be entirely wrong.

When the low wind-note entered the hollow, a woody rib would begin along my back. If seed cases started clattering, my plates might draw inward, trying to close around a hardness I had not yet grown. Then glasswings would hiss somewhere near, and the half-made rib would split into transparent vanes. Before those could tear free, some other sound would arrive—a boom beneath the roots, a manyfoot’s irregular footsteps, the dry punctuation of springers striking stone.

What would make me *me* is that I would still be able to move afterward.

Not unchanged. Never unchanged. Just not carried all the way into root, pod, wing, or thorn. I would persist as a small bundle of interrupted beginnings. Not a hybrid, exactly; a hybrid has a form. I would have a history.

On most days I would be small enough to fit on one tread of the broken stair. My underside would be pale and brushed, useful for teasing beads of oil from split pods and scraping black glaze from abandoned shell. Violet felt would live in the warm hinge beneath me. One leg might end in a proper jointed foot, another in a rootlet that had to be kept curled away from the grit. A crimson scale on my back would remember being almost a leaf. Beneath one plate would lie the brittle, folded beginning of a wing.

I would not be deaf to the Choir, or indifferent to it. I would hear too well, in too many tissues. My safety would be disagreement.

So I would move during the confused passages. A clear note would still me. I would feel it simplifying me, old canceled shapes losing their separate tensions as one possibility spread through the shell. The Bellows would frighten me—not because of its hidden foot, but because of its patience. Its voice might last long enough for my root to persuade the rest of me.

Sometimes I would travel in the untidy wake of manyfoots. Their scattered footfalls would trouble any rhythm gathering around my body, and their bark would collect the violet threads from my joints. They would not be companions. Their processions would be as accidental as my presence in them, and their routes might sprout pods or thorns with equal readiness. But for a little while I could cross inside their clatter as another creature might cross inside shade.

Behind me there would sometimes be a faint discoloration in a footprint. I would never know whether it became felt, or whether an island began there seasons later. I would only know that I had passed.

The quiet crescents would attract me, but I would not trust their centers. I would crouch near the broken outer stones, where the ash-colored insect’s tick and rising note arrived incomplete. Its song would be beautiful because, for once, I could hear the whole shape of a sound. It would also be dangerous for the same reason.

The repairers would have no dependable name for me. They might find the joint between two fitted stones scraped clean where I had worked old glaze from my own shell. They might find a stiff cord dragged away and, on a later visit, discover it serving as the axis of a fan. Once, perhaps, one of them would see me and peck a record of an animal with five legs. Beside it, on the next visit, there would be room for a correction.

**Almost** would do for a name. It would remain accurate even when I did not.

Without intending to keep anything, I would keep a little delay between hearing and becoming. Not refusal; refusal could harden into a shape as easily as obedience. Just delay. Enough for a second voice to enter. Enough for the first instruction to discover that it was not the whole world.

I would not last forever. One still interval too many, one note arriving cleanly through the grit, and some part of me might finally convince all the others. I could drive down my root and become a low, contradictory thicket. I could open every transparent fold at once and come apart into the haze. I could close around my stored oil and wait, with admirable simplicity, to be stepped on.

For a while, I would be excellent at one thing.

Then would come the unbinders, the dark curls of glaze, the pale skins among the grit. Whatever felt survived in my hinge would travel elsewhere around the toes of larger feet.

Until then, you would find me near the outer edge of the best-preserved crescent: a small husk with a green scale on its back, a folded transparency beneath one plate, and one black root held carefully above the stone.

I would not ask you to be silent. Silence has too much stored in it.

I would ask you not to sing to me, not to tap stone, not to kindly make a rhythm for me to follow. Stand there while stalks, wings, shells, and hidden throats interrupt one another.

When the whole hollow was disagreeing, I would take another step.
