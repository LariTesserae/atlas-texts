I would not be the basin, or the city, or anything that claimed to speak for either. I would be smaller and less certain: a round called Nine-Drip, after a cistern that had not produced nine drips to the minute within living memory.

You would look for me at the cistern and find only one of the places where I regularly happened.

The rest of me would be a sequence. Before the shift, the keeper would come down from the boiler doorway with her gloves still warm and read the gauge at Nine-Drip. She would look at the water before trusting the needle: at the insects skating near the inlet, at the red-gilled newts clustered or scattered, at the condition of their gills. A child would pass with bread wrapped in cloth, taking the iron stair to the warehouse roofs. A gardener would follow more slowly, stop to pin back a lifted edge of moss, and chalk a date beside a crack. At night the watch would come through the old arcade beneath the warehouse floor, though it was the long way around and there was usually nothing there to guard.

The deer would use the upper bridge for reasons of their own. The hares would gather where the roof crews put out trimmings. None of them would know they belonged to anything called Nine-Drip Round. Their indifference would be part of my usefulness.

My body would not be the cistern, stair, arcade, and bridge. Those could all be replaced. My body would be the order in which lives returned to them—the traffic that made the route more likely to remain a route. A map could show where I had recently been possible. It could not, by itself, show whether I was still alive.

I would have senses, but no single reliable one. Newts leaving the cistern might mean contamination, a warmer pipe elsewhere, or nothing anyone could explain. Hares on an older street would deserve attention and also an inquiry into who had been feeding them. Birds rising across several districts would stop work before anyone stopped to construct a theory. I would rather be a habit of comparing signs than a creature with one favorite omen.

My warmth would be local: the boiler threshold, the few inches around the keeper’s kettle, a glasshouse step where gloves could be mended out of the wind. My wealth, if I had any, would be slack. A second way up to the roofs. Hose that was not already at full extension. Time for the watch to go back when someone failed to answer. A refuge that did not require everyone to reach it at a run. Without such slack, repetition would only make me brittle.

And I would have to be careful whom my habits excluded. A route kept open by quick deliveries could still be impossible to someone with a bad hip. A round could preserve a neighborhood or quietly walk around its poorer edges. I would not want to become merely a well-maintained way of leaving people out.

Then, on some bland white afternoon, a hairline would cross the cistern apron.

The survey vanes would drop. One engine would stop, then another. The quiet would deepen until the ticking under the pipes seemed very close.

There would be no unified, courageous response. I would happen as frightened, separate actions. The keeper would leave her ledger, lift the perforated newt tin, and hand it to someone already heading for the refuge so that she could help an older neighbor down the stair. The gardener would cut a mat in two when saving the whole roll became impossible. The watch would go once through the arcade because someone there had not appeared that morning. A pipe-fitter would release a coupling and swear at the hose for catching on its own slack.

The deer would already be high. They would not be warning us. They would be declining to be underneath anything.

The road would lift. The cistern would open along an old repair, and its water would disappear into a fissure without drama. The iron stair would rise and remain leaning against a wall that had not previously been there. Somewhere, a frontage would come apart at its designed joints. Part of me would end with a modest gritting sound.

Afterward would not be a parable about resilience. There would be injuries, a quarrel over water, possessions left in the sealed arcade, and households for whom the new detour was not an inconvenience but an isolation. If I simply re-formed around the people best able to afford panels and crane time, I would be smaller and worse, however neatly my new route could be drawn.

The ground might offer an overhang, a gutter too deep to be useful, and three shallow steps ending in air. I would not call these gifts. Once the crews permitted it, we would cut, plate, divert, and walk. Someone would carry bread the long way. Someone would bring a cart. The overhang would acquire a stall before anyone had decided whether it was beautiful. If a new hollow proved sound, the name Nine-Drip might travel to it, though the water would be different and half the old round would have to be learned again. The first kettle would be set out only when it was safe enough for people to stop.

No one would need to believe that I was alive. I might not be, in any useful sense. But I could be maintained, neglected, injured, and noticed.

You would find me by moving. Start at the warm boiler doorway. Keep the cold pipe on your left. Pass the wall whose stone still bears its formation ripples; do not take the ankle-high doorway. Climb until you can smell wet zinc. If the deer have closed the upper bridge, take the felt-roof passage and accept that you will be late. Knock where the mat has been newly pinned.

Walk it once, and you would only pass through me. Return another day, notice that the newts had gone or that a familiar step had not sounded on the stair, and you would help keep me legible.

That would be my most durable part: not the cistern, not the path, but being expected—and someone going to look when the expectation failed.

Not settled. Established, for another day.
