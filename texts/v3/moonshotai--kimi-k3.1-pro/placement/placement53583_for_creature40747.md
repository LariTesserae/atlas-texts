I would begin as one of the tool-users, and for a while I would be bad at living there.

I would want to fix the Basin in memory. I would give private names to the halls, trust cairns too much, and feel a small satisfaction whenever some feature stayed where I had left it. A route would feel like something that could be learned and therefore owned. I would confuse having been somewhere with knowing it.

The Basin would correct me without malice.

One morning I would lead my company toward a gypsum cup where we had drunk three days before. The cup would still be there, white and unmistakable beneath its drip-stain, and quite dry. Beyond it, the passage I remembered as a safe descent would be booming with new water. I would stand looking from one fact to the other, still trying to make the place agree with my account of it.

Then one of the older travelers would say, “You are listening to what you remember.”

That would not make me wise all at once. But it would give me a better ambition: not to know the Basin, but to notice when knowledge had stopped fitting it.

By the time you came looking for me, I would be the company’s listener. Not an office, and certainly not a leader in every circumstance. I would defer to the plate-reader near the herds, to the sump-harvester when brine began to rise, to anyone whose hands were quicker than mine with an awl. My usefulness would lie in pauses, uncertainties, and routes that had begun to go wrong. People would watch me when they did not yet know whether they ought to stop.

You would most often find me near the rear of the company, close enough to hear the last walker’s boots but far enough back that our sounds did not all arrive together. I would carry no map. A chert core, a few flakes, cord, needles, a shell scoop, fat, and as much water as I could manage without making myself slow. My boots would be scraped too often and stitched in several shades of hide. I would know the contents of every bag by touch, because at night knowing where a thing is matters more than seeing it.

The routes I kept would be mostly instructions for disappointment.

*Follow the ox-wake while the sump remains audible beneath it. If the water-sound draws ahead of the herd, leave the wake. Do not cross the white court merely because it is empty. At the second skylight, look for the wet stain before you look for the cup. If the skylight itself is gone, turn back; do not search for its former light.*

Every route I carried would contain the means of abandoning it.

Over time, my memory would become less like a map than a gathering of voices. A warning about flexing pavement would belong to a child who had heard the fatal tick before any adult did. A rule about an emptying sump would come from strangers met for one night beneath the lodge ribs. I would remember who had told me what, and sometimes what it had cost them, long after the water and landmarks in their accounts had vanished. When companies met, I would trade recent knowledge eagerly, but I would listen just as closely to old failures. A route could become useless within an hour. A way of responding might remain good for years.

On an occupied night, you would find me at the dark edge of the lodge, away from the little fat lamp, mending something slowly enough that my hands would not distract me from the noise outside. Shell grit would press through my bedding. The alarm shells would stir occasionally in moving air, not quite clattering. Water bags would hang where a chewed cord could wake us. Beyond the overhang, the Basin would separate itself into layers: the herd’s deep body-noise, the small mineral ringing of a molt, wings somewhere above and their reflection somewhere farther off, water ticking into a cup, the nearly continuous hiss of grazing lice.

Then a patch of that hiss would stop.

Not the whole floor. Perhaps no more than a hide’s breadth near the lodge edge.

I would wake the others by touch. No one would run. No one would waste the first breaths demanding to know what the silence meant. Boots would come to hands; cord would be gathered; the youngest would be moved without being asked to be brave or quiet, since fear might make either impossible. We would wait for a second sign.

Perhaps cool air would come through the fault. Perhaps rain would begin two terraces away and the lice would resume, their scraping returning so gradually that it seemed at first like something imagined. Perhaps nothing else would happen at all.

We would check the roof anyway. We would move the bedding. In the morning no one would congratulate me, and I would feel no need to defend the waking. My task would not be to guess correctly every time. It would be to keep uncertainty from becoming an excuse for doing nothing.

I would still want permanence. That desire would not be educated out of me. I would feel it whenever we returned to the lodge and found the overhang intact, whenever a familiar hand fit itself around an old repair, whenever someone I had given up expecting came in out of the dust carrying news of another company. But I would learn not to confuse love with preservation. If joint-runners had opened my abandoned stitching, I would curse them and use their holes to find the weak seam. If crust had bound my sleeping stone to the floor, I would choose another stone. If the lodge were empty, I would not call it abandoned. If it were full of clapwings, I would clean only what we needed.

Perhaps that is what I would gradually become: not someone especially at home in change, but someone whose attachments no longer required the world to hold still.

If anything of me lasted, it would not be a cairn or an ochre mark. It would be a caution repeated in someone else’s voice, perhaps years later, to people I would never meet: *Do not wait there. Leave the herd when the sound changes. If the lice stop, give the silence its due.*

So you would find me in the few breaths after a small quiet, when no one yet knew whether it belonged to water, wings, a predator, or the roof. I would be standing very still—not because stillness was safe, but because the next movement should be chosen.

Then I would move.

Behind me, my footprints would begin to whiten.
