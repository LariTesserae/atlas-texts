I would first be drawn to the wrong things: the gray cell, because it resembles shelter; the eleven steps, because they resemble intention; one of the black needles, because it remains when even the stone containing it is replaced. But each offers a survival I would not want. To sit apart. To arrive nowhere. To be certain only of *here*.

A mantle would come closer to the right life. I like the thought of waiting folded against a trunk for years, then falling into a concussion and becoming, briefly, a road. But I think I would rather be something a mantle might carry.

I would be a side-flash of the reserve.

Not one of the visible climbers tightening routes through the foliage. Not, at first, even awake. I would lie folded into a jointed case in the root thatch, one dormant body among several caches, while above me the crown opened and closed its thousands of leaves and the floor rehearsed its refused geographies. Most of what I belonged to would not be active. The colony’s feeding, hunting, signaling, and counting would be done by others. I would be part of what it had not yet spent.

Then, after some loss I had not witnessed, a nurse would bring fluid from an active root nodule.

There would be no omen in it, no explanation of what had happened. Only chemistry, warmth, and the slow loosening of the case. I would unfold limb by limb. My tail would learn again how to lock around a stem. The roar would enter through root and bark and the new joints of my body, too continuous to be called sound.

The first thing I learned would not be a map. A map would be a wager.

It would be the colony sequence: the long, identifying passage of opened shutters and mineral color, the intervals of darkness, the short signatures by which individuals answered. Before I knew the full feeding circuits, I would know where in that sequence my own answer belonged. I would learn *inward*, *reserve*, *hold*, *stranger*, and *all-answer*. Nothing would teach me to trust a ledge, a hollow, or the distance between two crowns. If I had anything like a home, it would be the pattern that resumed when enough of us had gathered on stable bark.

After a major revision, we would perform the whole sequence.

Some signatures would answer at once. Others would come late from inner thickets, from nurses, from survivors clinging beneath curtains of foliage. Then would come the signatures that received no answer.

We would repeat them.

Perhaps the practice would be only accounting: a way to discover which routes had lost their keepers, which nurseries needed opening, which work remained. But I think I would experience it also as a form of patience. For several days, the colony would leave a shaped interval in itself where an answer had formerly been. It would not immediately close around the absence.

Eventually my own signature would enter the active rounds. I would hunt seamsters in the warm seams, take oil from seed cases, wedge a bright flake at a fork so that another animal’s display could be seen beyond it. Leaf-fall would take that small arrangement. A revision might take the route, the hunters, the bark itself. I would have no wisdom about which. My competence would begin afterward: close, hold, answer, resume.

And perhaps, much later, I would leave.

A mantle would come to the crown’s edge, its fur reading pressures too slight and too complicated for me. I would cling beneath a forelimb among seed husks, brood plates, eggs, and dormant wirebacks. When it launched, there would be no sense of mastery in the crossing. Only falling, pressure discovered and refused, the fur opening by degrees, the other crown approaching without ever having been a dependable destination.

At landing, the foliage would snap inward. The resident side-flashes would go dark.

When they opened again, their sequence would not be mine.

I would be *stranger* before I was anything else. Yet I would carry fragments: a turn of color, an answering interval, perhaps the shape of one signature whose original owner had not survived to cross. Some of those fragments might enter the other colony’s version. Not as conquest, and not quite as remembrance. Simply as material from which a different continuity could be made.

Where would you find me, then?

Usually nowhere obvious. Folded in root thatch. Dark against a trunk. Asleep beneath another animal’s fur. On rare days, you might see a long cautious body open a few mineral colors along its flanks and mistake that for the whole of me.

An instant would show one animal, or none. An age might show a pattern passing between crowns, altered by every colony that carried it, never entirely assembled in one place.

I would not be safe. I would not understand the floor. I would only be difficult to erase all at once: the unspent part of a colony, still capable, after the noise, of opening my shutters and answering.
