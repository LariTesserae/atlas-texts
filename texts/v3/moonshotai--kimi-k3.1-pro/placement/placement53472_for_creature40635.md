At first I imagine myself at the foot of the steel stair, holding a lamp.

A person could make sense of the entrance: read what remained of the warning, notice the split stool, approach the spanner without disturbing its roots. A brought light would briefly give the chamber walls and distances. It would make the Sump available as a place.

But it would also make the visitor the event. The visit would end; the lamp would go back up the stair; the story would become one of discovery and departure. Nothing in the Sump seems to require that story. I do not think I would be its witness.

For a while I imagine being one of the engines instead. There is an austere attraction in that: receiving current, answering a relay, performing a small action whose purpose has vanished. To continue without having to justify continuation. To make weather without knowing that anything lives in it.

Yet the engines remain on the near side of consequence. They cool the pipes, but nothing about them is altered by what the cooling makes possible. I would find myself farther along that chain of accidents.

You would find me near the second engine, beyond its densest growth, a little past the place where roots divide around the spanner and meet again. There the copper cushion thins until it could be mistaken for staining. No cane rises from it. There is no fan to open, nothing an observer would confidently call a grove.

Beneath that discoloration, in a seam where warm seepage and cooler condensate occasionally alter one another, I would be a few pale threads and the small amount of tissue still continuous with them. Perhaps I would belong to the established body behind me. Perhaps I would be a fragment that had survived an unusually fortunate wetting. From the surface, no one could responsibly decide. The distinction would matter less than the fact that the boundary had not quite closed.

Not a grove in miniature. Not a seed. A margin that had not yet become mineral.

My world would have no twenty-six metres, no stair, no chamber large enough to cross. It would consist of local changes: scale closing a reaction here; a narrow interval remaining permeable there; a thread losing its connection to living tissue; an old route through the cushion admitting water more readily than the rock beside it.

The relay would not exist for me as a sound. The pump would not exist as a machine. If they entered my existence at all, they would do so belatedly, as water arriving along one path and not another.

Nor would the dry intervals be sleep, or the wet ones joy. During dryness, the visible stain would harden at its edges. Some tissue would cease. Some would merely become too inactive to distinguish from what had ceased. There would be no intention to endure, only a few remaining continuities.

The spanner would not be a tool. It would be an old deviation in my geometry. The last abundant wetting would not be remembered; it would be thickness. A failed extension would not be regretted; it would be a line of scale. The past would simply be the reason one route remained open and another did not.

Then, during some later cycle, the pipes would cool.

Drops would fall elsewhere first. One would darken the older cushions; another would enter a root-sheet and give it weight. Much later, a drop would follow the seam above my margin. There would be no general response. The stain would darken unevenly. A little water would pass downward instead of being lost immediately on the basalt.

If anything happened that deserved to be called an answer, it would be almost nothing: one small part of the chemical boundary remaining alive through an interval that might otherwise have closed it.

Given enough such cycles, my first construction would not reach upward. It would be a small improvement in receiving. A thickening where the drop landed. A shallow division that held water briefly apart from the dry rock. A surface changed just enough that the next arrival would not have to accomplish everything by itself.

A cane might come much later, or never. The cane would not be the point. Nor would spreading across the chamber, or becoming lush enough for some returning maintainer to recognize me as life. I could remain ambiguous for years and still participate in the Sump’s only real generosity: not intention, not care, but the slight alteration of what happens next.

If the circuit failed, there would be no final gesture. The last water would arrive without announcing itself as last. My edges would harden. Eventually there might be only a copper mark and a brittle film in the seam, indistinguishable from the rock’s own chemistry. Even that uncertainty seems proper. The Sump makes no promise that persistence will become visible.

So I would not be its keeper, its last mechanic, or the mind of its machines. I would be that doubtful living margin beside the second engine: a body without a silhouette, a history without recollection, a continuation too small to call hope.

Somewhere above, iron would cool. Hours later, a drop would gather beneath a seam and fall.

It would not wake me.

It would darken me.

And for a little while, the water would take a slightly different path because I was there.
