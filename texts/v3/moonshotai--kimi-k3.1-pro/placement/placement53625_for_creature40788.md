I would arrive thinking I wanted to leave a wall.

A wall is satisfyingly legible. It has a length, a height, a face that catches the noon light. When the work party goes down, you can point to it and say: *that course was not there before*. I would come with some modest skill in cutting and leverage, and for a while I would mistake that usefulness for the skill the basin actually required.

The first morning after the others departed, there would be rain in the gutters and water sounding everywhere. The empty town would boom, tick, clatter, and answer my footsteps from unexpected directions. Another keeper would stand with me in a court and ask which sound was missing.

I would hear everything. That would be the trouble.

I would say, “Nothing.”

Then we would climb toward the eastern channels, where one small, customary note had vanished beneath the general roar. The obstruction would be minor. Clearing it would take less time than the walk. I would stay, at first, because I disliked having been wrong. That is not a noble beginning, but it would be sufficient for another season. Later I would stay until the upper cistern was reliable, and then until someone else understood the eastern drains well enough that my leaving would not make them mysterious again.

By the time you came looking for me, outsiders would call me a keeper. Among the others, I would simply be staying.

My sleeping cell would open onto one of the eastern courts, but you would more often find me below it, in a small garden with my back to the waterworks. I would be doing something that looked absurdly slight: stirring mineral grit into a bed, carrying court sweepings in a basket, pouring the cloudy water from a washed bowl where its sediment could be recovered. The plants would matter, but the soil would be the trust placed in me. People I had never met would have caught that dust, held that moisture, rotted those stems, and refrained—perhaps with some difficulty—from making one clever diversion that would have dried the whole corner.

I would know its instructions. Which edge suffered first in a bright wind. Which overflow was not to be improved. Where a shrub had once been allowed to remain too long because someone had liked it.

All the while, part of my attention would be elsewhere: on a low note beneath the paving, on the small repeated knock of a channel doing what it ought, on the difference between wind worrying an empty conduit and water moving inside a full one. I would not be consciously listening every moment. That would be the point. The sounds would have become the background against which an absence could startle me.

The basin would teach me two kinds of urgency without reconciling them. A failed note might mean setting down the basket immediately and going uphill. The bed itself could not be hurried by any amount of concern. I could clear a throat in minutes and still spend my whole stay adding almost nothing to the depth of the soil. The place would not make me patient. It would simply make certain forms of impatience useless.

Nor would I become serene. I would complain about wet sleeping mats and about people who left wooden tools where the springs could begin sheathing them in stone. I would want, occasionally and intensely, a doorway with a door in it. I would resent a repair made necessary by someone who had departed twenty years earlier. I could understand perfectly well that a mineral obstruction had begun before my grandparents were born and still curse it while working.

I would also discover that a town with so few people offers very little anonymity. I would know the other keepers’ footsteps, the pause of someone carrying a load, the slight impatience in a particular person’s ascent. When I wanted privacy, I would go where I could not be seen and try not to think about how far my voice might travel.

The work parties would complicate this. Their arrival would fill hearth yards and make the courts seem crowded even when only a dozen more people were present. There would be news, arguments, cooking smells, several hammers going at once. I would be delighted and overstimulated. I would argue too firmly about a stone and then lie awake revising the argument. When the party left, I would feel relief, followed by a loneliness so acute that the unchanged roar of the water seemed almost mocking.

Some of my work would help lives I had not intended to help. Moss would follow a seep from a gutter I had sealed badly. Ferns would establish themselves in the sheltered joint of a shelter. Dust would gather behind a wall set for entirely different reasons, and something would root there. Other repairs would thin a wet strip or expose a slab whose darkness had sheltered snails. I would learn to put wet stone back wet side down, and to look longer before calling an alteration an improvement. I would not always know, until much later, which small habitat I had made and which I had removed.

Gradually, my old ambition for a wall would become less important. Not extinguished—I would still enjoy a visible course of masonry—but supplemented by a respect for work almost no one could identify. A hidden cut that persuaded water to turn. A flat stone set so that a channel kept its useful, unbeautiful note. A cistern cap that did not need to be thought about. A garden bed passed on with enough instructions that the next person would not have to rediscover its old mistakes by killing part of it.

Eventually, I might be the one standing in a court with a newcomer after rain.

They would look overwhelmed by the noise, as I had been. I would want to help by naming everything: cistern, overflow, wind, footstep, hammer. Instead, I would wait. Perhaps for quite a while. Perhaps they would begin by disliking me.

And if, while we stood there, one expected note failed beneath the paving, I would already be moving.

That is where you would find me: between a garden and a drain, soil on my hands, one part of my attention always uphill. Not the basin’s master, not its guardian in any grand sense, and certainly not its completion. Just one fallible pair of ears, one pair of careful hands, staying for a while among other brief inhabitants and much slower ones.

When I finally went down, there would be no finished thing to leave behind. Rope coiled under cover. A capped cistern. A bed holding moisture. A channel whose note still meant what it was supposed to mean. Perhaps, much later, someone would find a straight edge or a hidden cut and be unable to tell whether hands or water had made it.

For a while, the work would hold.
