### A Small Change in the Note

I would not be a figure in a doorway. I would not be the last keeper of the kilns, or a ghost preserving the old uses of the streets. The Thrum has finished with those distinctions. A doorway is now a lee; a court, a seed trap; a flue, a weather that returns.

At first, a seed tempts me.

For the length of one exhalation, I would be almost nothing but relation: to the valve that released me, to the pressure carrying me, to the far wall I might never reach. The central vacancy would be my whole world, not because it sheltered me, but because it let me happen. Streets and roofs would pass beneath without once interrupting my flight. The roar would not be music. It would be the condition under which I existed.

Most likely I would strike salt, or stone, and eventually become powder. There is no reason to require a longer story. The Thrum continues through numberless brief lives that accomplish nothing visible.

But if I persisted, I do not think I would persist as a single, well-defended body.

You would find me at the warm perimeter, in the lee of a court where a curb holds a little powder against the drafts. From a few paces away, I would resemble an olive stain. Close to, I would be felt: fine filaments, springy and dense, my living surface spread over layers of compacted dead growth. Most of what supported me would be what I no longer was. Not remains exactly. Structure. Shelter. A way of holding dust until the roots below could use it.

I would be a thin living present resting on a thick, patiently accumulated past.

Below that, I would become uncertain.

White roots would enter the paving and follow the buried clay. Some would split mineral grains; others would extend along the narrow difference between hot floor and less-hot masonry. Where they met neighboring roots, some would remain distinct and some would fuse. After that, calling us separate lives would be a convenience. A cane above the court might belong to the same old body, or might only have become difficult to distinguish from it.

One of my roots would find a conduit.

It would line the clay, thicken by degrees, and be killed back wherever heat and scouring became too severe. At the flue’s edge, living white tissue, dead black lace, and polished clay would form a narrow band. For ages, almost nothing about that band would move.

Except the note.

As the root altered the passage within the pipe, the flue would sound lower—at first by almost nothing, then, over long enough time, by enough. The root would not know it was altering an instrument. It would simply be occupying a place where something could be gained.

That is where I would most nearly become singular: not in a body, but in a consequence.

The canes around that flue would change with its sounding. Their seed capsules would accumulate heavier lips, different tensions, slightly later openings. None of them would hear me. I would not hear myself. Yet my growth and their release would become fitted together through pressure, selection, and the old builders’ clay.

When the great exhalation came, there would be no awakening. Nothing in me would have been asleep. The tone beneath the paving would deepen; other flues would join; shutters would rise and knock. My felt would hold what dust it could. Above it, capsules would begin to rattle.

Then, at one resonance, they would open.

For a little while, the life around my roots would become plural in the air. Pale seeds and yellow pollen would enter the pewter dimness. Some seeds would strike the nearest wall. Some would fall into worn thresholds. A few would cross nearly the whole hollow, passing above streets whose original purposes had vanished but whose shapes still governed their flight. Most would fail. Enough would not.

Afterward, I would remain almost exactly where I had been: a root dividing a grain, a living skin binding powder, a cane concentrating oil around another capsule. No renewal. No conclusion. Only small additions and small losses, nearly balanced.

So to find me, you would have to look in several timescales at once.

In the court: an olive stain.  
Beneath it: white root indistinguishable, in places, from clay.  
In the flue: a band held almost stationary between growth and scouring.  
In the roar: a note very slightly different from the one the builders left.  
Above the vacancy: a seed, briefly suspended, with no assurance of landing.

None of these alone would be me. I would be the persistence connecting them without making them one.

Not the Thrum’s mind. It does not need one. Not its keeper, and not its voice.

A slight, persistent change in what its voice could release.
