My first impulse is to be the octopus: to occupy the gallery, to keep a den whose entrance is also a record of appetite, to send an arm into dangers the rest of my body need not enter. But that impulse is really the wish to know the Sump better than the other animals do. Even the octopus cannot manage that. A den, a few routes, the feel of certain crevices—this is still not the hall.

Then I imagine being one of the silver fish. There would be a kind of relief in that: no solitary decision about when to turn, no need to see the hunter before becoming part of the response. The valve would strike, and my body would already be moving with the bodies around me. For an instant I would belong to something bright and singular.

But what draws me back is the dead pump. Not its encrustation seen from across the hall, nor its resemblance to a reef, but its stillness—the fact that, hour after hour, it does not shake anything away.

So I would be much smaller.

I would be an amphipod, no longer than a grain of rice, living in the angle where the dead pump’s housing meets its bed. My particular place would be beneath a flange, under a loose accumulation of shell: a low, irregular shelter with several openings, none large enough to admit a crab’s whole body. Beyond it would lie a few body lengths of sediment that I knew well, and beyond that an exposure I would cross only when the current and the movements around me permitted.

The dead pump would not be a machine to me, and it would not be dead. It would be the roof. Its mass would interrupt currents and obscure hunters. Its stillness would be the first condition of my world.

Light would matter little. The nearest lamp might alter the darkness beyond my shelter without ever touching the shelter itself. More would arrive through my antennae and through the surfaces against my body: the weight of water, the odor of something recently dead, the fine disturbance of another small animal crossing the sediment, the heavier approach of a crab. A shadow would not need a shape. It would only need to make me stop.

The cold would be too constant to seem like cold. So would the pump’s bass vibration, conducted through concrete and shell until it became less a sound than a property of the surfaces beneath me. But the check valve would be different. I would not count the seconds between its concussions. There would simply be the blow passing through everything, the instant in which loose grains shifted and small bodies vanished into cover, and then the gradual resumption of feeding.

Food would come down and along the floor: fragments missed by the fish, organic material carried from the intake, soft films on sediment grains, particles already rejected or partly used elsewhere. I would graze and sort and turn these things over. None of it would seem like remainder. A patch of film would be abundance; a shell crevice packed with fine organic matter would be worth defending, or worth returning to when something larger had passed.

At flood tide, clearer water would reach my shelter and the drift of particles would change. At slack water, when the grains were nearly still, I could venture farther from the flange. Then distance would become dangerous in a different way. Not because the open floor was empty, but because everything in it could be seen, felt, or overtaken. Safety would not be stillness exactly. It would be nearness to a space too small for whatever might pursue me.

The larger inhabitants would enter my life mostly as alterations of that safety. A crab would be the sediment shifting before its legs arrived. The eel would be an odor and a reason not to follow a promising trail along the wall. The octopus would be the most terrible kind of search: a soft, exploring presence able to enter where a body could not, turning over shell, testing openings, making even a familiar refuge uncertain. The school of fish might pass above as a succession of brief shadows. I would not see their silver closing of ranks. I would know only that the water had changed and that it was time to disappear among the grains.

I would molt beneath the pump. For a while, my new body would be too soft for the small distances I ordinarily crossed. The shed covering would remain near the shelter or be carried a short distance away: hollow, pale, soon worked over by something smaller. Nothing about it would commemorate me. It would simply rejoin the materials available to the hall.

If you came looking for me, you would probably find the pump first, then the crabs around its base, then perhaps the shell and encrustation beneath the flange. You would have to wait. After your arrival, after the pressure of your movement and the disturbance of sediment, the floor would seem empty.

Then, at the edge of a broken shell, a grain would shift.

I would be there, and then not there.

I would never know that the chamber was called the Ninth Sump, or that it had been built to move water out of somewhere else. I would know a roof, a few routes, the quality of what drifted down, and the difference between a current that brought food and a disturbance that made cover necessary. My world would not be an incomplete version of the hall. It would be complete at another scale.

And when I was gone, the film would continue over the grains I had worked. Another small body would use the same crevice, or a nearby one. The dead pump would keep its stillness. The valve would strike. For a little while, I would have been one of the minute, ordinary events by which the hall received what the sea brought in—and remained inhabited.
