At first I would choose the bird, and that would be a failure of imagination.

The bird offers the easiest version of being someone: a body with edges, an arrival and a departure, a call large enough to make the whole borough seem momentarily small. It could cross in seconds distances that everything else must approach by growth. It could be warm among the cold arches, quick among the slow stems, and then gone.

But almost nothing of Coldseep would happen to the bird. A little shelter. A pressure in the turf that closed behind it. Perhaps a feather left for the filaments. Its life would remain almost entirely elsewhere, and I would have mistaken exemption from the place for knowledge of it.

Then I would want to be water.

Water seems at first to have the true freedom of the borough. It enters as cloud, gathers in the highest cups, passes beneath ice and through hollow stems, overflows a rim, divides around a root, and comes to rest in some lower basin where a translucent animal is grazing. It would know every district by contact. It would be permitted through membranes and into darkness. It would determine where the open spaces remained open.

Yet a drop does not persist long enough to become a resident. It would be taken into a leaf, locked briefly in a clear skin of ice, or carried down some route whose existence it helped to alter. The borough would happen through me, but there would be no place where I remained.

Only after those two desires had passed would I begin to notice the life I would actually want: not in the visible borough, but in the thickness its visible forms keep making beneath themselves.

Below the upper courts, somewhere within the old accumulation near the seep, there would be a lane that had ceased to be a lane. Its turf would have narrowed as the water failed. Shoots would have met across it. Later, growth overhead would have closed the last gray opening, leaving a dark, wet interval inside the mass. Roots would have descended through it. Former surfaces would lie buried below, some still keeping the outlines of leaves, some compressed almost beyond recognition.

Behind the green face of that place, beyond the wine-dark tissue and the living roots, I would be a pallid thread-plant.

Not the spirit of the walls. Not a concealed intelligence keeping account of Coldseep’s past. Merely one colorless branching among others, fine enough to disappear against the remains it occupied.

I would have to use *I* loosely. There would be no trunk to which all of me returned, no single growing tip that could stand for the whole. An advance along one wet seam might succeed while another part thinned beside a root that had taken the water first. A buried pocket of air might interrupt me; softened tissue might let me spread. My body would be the present shape of those permissions and refusals.

I would never see the borough from above. I would not know the near-black leaves, the amber tips, or the brief silvering of a drop. Even the blue-gray noon of the inner lanes would be an excess of light beyond my experience. My world would be composed of differences without scenery: wet and less wet; resistance and yielding; the close grain of compacted growth; the narrow ease along a dead root; cold arriving late, after the outer stems had already stiffened against it.

Fog would reach me as belated consequence.

Far above, the cloud-combers would be taking moisture from the air. Basins would rise, conduits swell, membranes loosen. Drops would enter channels that had been almost closed. Only afterward would something change in my darkness: a seam becoming dependable again, a film softening, water lingering where for a long time it had merely passed. I would not awaken. I would simply continue with slightly less difficulty.

A hard night would scarcely distinguish itself. The outer borough could contract around its ice; basins could skin over; the small animals could vanish into their liquid refuges. Down in the packed depth, the cold would arrive muted and slow. There would be no great drama of endurance, only very little alteration.

And if the solitary bird called beneath some outer arch, perhaps nothing of that disproportionate voice would reach me. Perhaps there would be a brief disturbance through stem and water, with no shape of bird attached to it. Then the old pressures would resume.

My intimacy would be with what had fallen.

A leaf might lie recognizable in the black depth long after the court it helped to roof had changed. I would enter along its softened places. This would not be reading. Its outline would not become a message, and I would preserve nothing for the sake of remembrance. But neither would it simply vanish. Around its diminishing substance, water would be held differently. Fine roots would find new courses. Some material would be taken up; some would remain compacted beneath later growth. The leaf’s old usefulness would end without its whole influence ending.

There would be no innocence in this, and no malice. My spread might be helped by another plant’s failure. A living root might deprive me without encountering me. Where I branched, an old form could lose coherence; where I failed, the same form might persist a little longer. I would belong to the borough’s thefts and burials as much as to its continuities.

Over a very long interval, the chamber above me might become entirely solid. Or seepage might find it again and open a thread of darkness along its former course. New growth might follow that moisture. Somewhere overhead, another court could lean toward the cold light, its floor supported in part by surfaces no resident had ever seen.

That is where you would find me: not crossing the silver turf, not sheltered behind a membrane, not waiting at the basin rim. You could stand close by and have no reason to suspect anything but wall and depth. I would be a pale, incomplete branching within a buried surface, easy to mistake for one more dead fiber.

Not Coldseep’s memory. Memory implies keeping.

I would be one of the small, colorless means by which something fallen could cease to be merely fallen, and the next growth could take it—correctly, and without gratitude—for ground.
