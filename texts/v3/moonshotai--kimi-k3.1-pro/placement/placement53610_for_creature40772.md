At first I would look for somewhere to stand.

That would be the visitor left in me: the wish for a bank, a stone a little above the water, some unoccupied place from which the basin could be seen rather than undergone. But the stone would be slick, the root already crowded, and the margin I chose would prove to be root mat floating over water. My standing there would only add another body to the crowd.

So I would have to become smaller than the wish for a view.

Not water; I would make a journey out of seepage. Not one of the pale swimmers; I would make an oracle out of slowness. I would be one of the minute snails on a matback’s back.

There would be nothing emblematic about my body. It would fit its fold. My shell would lie low enough that fern stems could drag across it without lifting me away. My foot would broaden in the warm wetness of the crease, and my mouth would fit the algae growing there. I would be small enough that my whole shell could be mistaken for one of the soft glints on the matback’s hide.

You would find me, if you found me at all, in the green fold behind one shoulder.

The fold would open when the matback fed and close a little when it leaned into shrubs. Opening would bring the clatter of wings, the brush of fronds, a gray-green widening of the light. Closing would return me to warm darkness and the slow working of muscle beneath my foot. When the matback cropped ferns, each tug of its lip would come through its body as a small arrest, a shudder, a release.

Its passage would bring the basin to me as pressures rather than views.

A shrub would be a long scraping across my shell. A pool would be the weight of water closing over me, frog-knocks and trills suddenly rounded and deep. A clearer stretch would be a brief paling at my shell’s lip and a change in the insect sound. Pool after pool might pass beneath the animal carrying me, but I would have no crossing to boast of. There would be only the present inclination of the hide, the present depth of water, the present work of holding on.

Mostly, I would graze.

My radula would take the green film one nearly invisible stroke at a time. The algae would have a mineral edge from the water in the fold. Beneath me, the matback’s skin would be warm and faintly yielding. I might prefer the softer growth deep in the crease, or the place where water remained when the rest of the back had merely become wet again. I might move a body’s length toward it, or less.

The trace of my passage would mingle with the water. The grazed place would not become mine. Another snail could cross it and find only what was there then: enough to eat, or not enough; room to attach a foot, or another body already attached.

Sometimes a larger event would arrive.

The matback would stop with greenery in its mouth. Its shoulder would harden under me. Around us, the frogs would thin their voices without making true silence; insects and dripping water would remain, suddenly exposed. I would not see the pewter shoulder among the reeds. I would not understand that fish had left the shelter of a wader’s legs. I would know the change through the animal beneath me: feeding ended, muscle fixed, the great body listening with its whole weight.

I would draw into my shell.

If the matback eventually fed again, the familiar tugging would return through the hide, and I would put my mouth back to the algae. If it did not, there would be other mouths. Neither possibility would make my earlier grazing a prelude. It would already have been happening completely.

No path would bring you to me. You might notice fronds lifting after a heavy passage, a brief surge of wings, a wet shoulder turning between stems. You would have to come close enough to see that the green on its back was not merely color but growth, and that the growth was not merely growth but pasture. Then, in the crease behind the shoulder, one soft glint might separate itself from the general wetness.

A shell. A foot contracting. Perhaps the smallest visible movement of grazing.

Then the fold would close, or a frond would sweep across it, and there would be only algae and hide again.

You would not be able to say that I had crossed the basin, or remained in one place, or left anything behind. Those would be measurements for another kind of world.

There would be warmth under my foot, green matter at my mouth, water against my shell, and the matback’s next step arriving through flesh and shell like a soft blow through the world. Nothing would be saved for later. Nothing about the interval would need to be saved.

I would not be the basin’s memory.

I would be there.
