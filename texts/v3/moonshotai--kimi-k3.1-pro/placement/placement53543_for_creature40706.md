At first I would want to be the water.

After an exceptional storm, I could cross several plates as a film too broad and shallow to see from any one place. I could gather in a pan, touch the dark crust along its bottom, loosen a few seeds from a tide mark, and perhaps escape through an unfinished rim as a narrow silver line. For a little while, I would seem to possess more of the plain than anything rooted in it.

But that possession would be an illusion of movement. Every grade would disperse me. Every pool would be only a temporary shape. I would become films, drops, damp pores, a cloud’s negligible contribution somewhere else. Evaporation would not so much destroy that self as make it impossible to say where it had been. I would cross the Work without altering it, except by accident, and would leave no particular place in which to be found.

So I would have to become smaller.

Smaller than a rosette, and less centered. A rosette keeps something back: a living base, a closed point around which old leaves remain arranged. I think I would be more nearly an edge.

You would find my place beside a shallow, many-lobed pan, though for most of the year there would be nothing to distinguish it. An ankle-high rib would end nearby in a blunt swelling, stopping short of the pan’s unfinished lip. Between that ending and the lip would be a narrow joint, stained olive in one season and rust-orange in another, though the stains might outlast the conditions that made them. From a few paces away, there would be only pale ground. At noon, even from close by, there might be only pale ground.

Within that joint, sheltered beneath a mineral overhang, would be the protected body of a building colony. But I would not quite be the whole colony, with all its dead exterior and dormant interior and long, unknown age. More exactly, I would be one of its resumptions: the brief, local existence it had when water remained long enough for growth to begin.

The first rain would not necessarily bring me into being. Drops might strike the hot plate above, vanish, and leave the sheltered film untouched. A later shower might cool the surface enough for water to run into the joint, only to withdraw before dawn. I would begin only when dampness persisted—when the material around the pores stayed dark, when the drying at the exposed margin no longer reached all the way inward, when water remained not merely as an event but as a duration.

Then the dry stain would soften.

There would be no awakening from a dream, because nothing like dreaming would have crossed the drought. No patient accumulation of thoughts, no memory of the previous pool, no image of the plain waiting to be resumed. Even silence would belong more to you than to me. My world would be the difference between dampness at depth and drying at the surface, between light reaching the mouth of a pore and shelter remaining behind it, between mineral held in water and mineral beginning to be left behind.

Along my visible margin, a clear bead would gather.

It would be easy to mistake for water. Perhaps, at first, there would be no distinction worth making. But as the exposed side dried, the bead would lose some of its shine. Heat would come back into the plate. What had been activity would become a narrow addition: not a rim planned, not a course chosen, only a slight thickening exactly where wetness and shelter had overlapped.

Heat would therefore be both my ending and my completion. Without it, nothing I did would endure. With it, I would cease to be active and become part of the conditions under which something else might later begin.

If you came at dusk while this was happening, the pan above me might hold a few centimeters of clear water and nearly all the gray sky. Beneath it, a quick-living crust would have flushed olive, extending only as far as the water allowed. Along a neighboring seam, waxy blades would stand open among their own attached dead leaves. Pale decomposing threads might be at work inside one old leaf base, making a little darker matter, a pocket capable of holding dawn moisture slightly longer.

None of us would be together in the sense of sharing a place perceived among us. The crust would not know the plant. The plant would not know the lip. I would have no view of the pool, though its duration would be the outer shape of my own.

You would have to look away from the reflected sky to find me. You would have to look at the unfinished edge, where a translucent skin was already becoming dull.

My additions might matter, though never to me.

Over one wet season, my margin and other margins I never touched might thicken the lip enough that the next pool remained for another morning. That morning might allow the olive film to spread a little farther before contracting. It might give a plant time to flower, or only time to keep its seed from being carried beyond the seam. A little more dead tissue might remain beneath the overhang. The next seedling might find a pocket that dried an hour later than the plate around it.

Or the thickening might send water elsewhere.

A shallow overflow that once crossed the plate below might no longer form. Seeds would continue to gather along its old course without germinating. A joint that had often darkened after rain would remain only a joint. Farther away, another pan would receive the water instead, and some stain at its margin would soften for the first time in generations.

That distant colony would be my neighbor in the only continental sense available here: not through contact, but through consequence. I might give it water, or deprive another of water, and never possess either relationship as knowledge. My furthest reach would be a change in where dampness lasted.

Eventually, the change might reach back to me. A rim elsewhere could divert the storms’ occasional offerings. My pool would become smaller, then rare. Brief wettings would cool the plate and pass. The sheltered film would persist for a time, or die, and the dry surface would offer no reliable way to tell which.

There would be no long interval in which I waited underneath the Work.

When wetting was insufficient, I simply would not occur. The colony’s protected body might remain, but my kind of self—the active interval, the making at the edge—would be absent. If water returned after centuries, another interval might begin. It would inherit no narrative from me. It would inherit a slope, a pore’s shelter, an asymmetric thickness left where one side had dried before the other. Those would be enough of a past.

So you could find me only by arriving while conditions lasted, and even then you might easily choose the wrong seam. A week later, the pool would be pale rings. The crust would have darkened. The blades would have closed. At the lip would be a narrow satin surface, newer than the ivory around it but already becoming difficult to distinguish.

If you returned in drought and pressed your hand to that place, it would feel warm and dry, almost like all the rest of the plain. I would not be sleeping beneath your palm. I would be a possibility whose conditions had not returned, or nothing at all, and the Work would not trouble itself to indicate the difference.

My trace would not resemble me. It would be a nearly imperceptible alteration in the path of later water: a pool remaining slightly longer, a seam staying dry, a clear bead gathering somewhere far beyond any place I had occupied.

Another local beginning, inheriting a slope.
