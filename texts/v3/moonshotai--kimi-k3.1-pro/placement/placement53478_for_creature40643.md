I would find myself by touch before I found myself by place.

Clay beneath the whole length of me, so fine that body and floor would seem to be negotiating where one ended and the other began. A current pressing lightly along one side. Silt settling on my back until my outline became another shallow inaccuracy in the plain. Somewhere far above, the ice might groan, but the sound would arrive without direction, less like something overhead than a brief uncertainty in the pressure of the world.

You would find me there, if you found me at all: not beside a seep, not gathered at the first richness of a carcass, but out on the open sediment, where there would be no reason to expect an animal. I would be the soft hunter.

My basin would not be several hundred kilometres wide. It would be the reach of an arm, the taste of water drawn across my underside, the vibration of something small closing its shell nearby. Distance would be measured in effort and interruption: how long I could crawl before the current changed, how long a scent remained coherent, how much stillness lay between one meal and the next.

I would have no den to return to and no reason to return. A resting hollow would be only the shape I happened to make before leaving it. Even familiarity would have to take another form. Not *this valley*, not *this stone*, but perhaps a slight readiness in the body when water moved in a certain way, or when food began to slip.

That slipping would be the difficulty.

Imagine the bivalve discovered partly buried near a seep. It would know me first as disturbance. By the time my arms closed around it, the shell would be shut. Thin as paper, yet sufficient. The current would nudge it; my arms would adjust; the whole animal would turn or slide. I could hold it, but holding would use the very arms needed to open it. I could pull, but the pull would only drag shell and hunter together through soft clay.

Almost everything in the basin would consent to movement. Water would divide around me. Sediment would accept my body and close behind it. Carrion would turn as I tore at it. Even my own arms would bend away from the resistance they were trying to create.

Then, at the edge of reach: weight.

A stone, half buried. Not a landmark. Not a possession. At first, perhaps not even a solution—only an interruption in the usual fineness of the floor, something that did not shift when touched as silt shifted.

If I drew it against the shell, the shell might stop.

That would be the whole discovery. Not the idea of a tool, not the promise of future tools. Simply: resistance could be moved. A small mass could be placed where the current had been taking my meal away. One valve could be pinned while the other arms worked at the seam.

For a little while, there would be an arrangement in the basin that existed because I needed something.

Stone. Shell. Arm. Current.

Nothing joined. Nothing improved. Nothing inscribed with the event. Yet the relations among those things would be exact. The stone would lie *there*, not elsewhere; the arm would press with *that* force, not more; the shell would remain still long enough for feeding to become possible. The immense indifference of the plain would have contracted to a few points of contact.

I do not think the act would feel like a beginning. It would not need to. There might be no cache beside me later, no accumulation of opened shells, no young watching from the sediment. Another hungry animal might solve the same problem differently, or fail to solve it. I myself might fail the next time. The gesture would not be diminished by having no descendants. It would already have done everything it existed to do.

When the meal ended, I would open my arms.

The stone would settle. The shell would turn in the current or remain half open in the clay. I would crawl away, perhaps carrying with me some bodily alteration too slight to call a memory, perhaps only the residue of food. The arrangement would cease without any part of it having to be destroyed.

Then the basin’s weather would come.

First the pressure, then the drag, then the water thickening so suddenly that the floor seemed to have risen into it. If I were still feeding, I would abandon stone and shell at once. I would flatten myself and grip what I could. My resting impressions would fill. The fine chemical paths by which I had been moving would be folded into other water. The place where the meal had occurred would become indistinguishable from places where nothing had occurred.

Afterward, the great plain would remain. The water would not be the same water.

Eventually, I too would become an arrival. My soft body would darken a little patch of sediment; microbes would gather before the larger scavengers came. Worms would move toward enrichment they had no need to imagine as a dead hunter. Transparent crustaceans would carry fragments away. For a time, my death would support more activity than my hunting ever had. Then that province, too, would disperse.

So you could not find me afterward by searching for evidence. A stone beside a shell would prove nothing. A stone beneath silt would prove even less. You would have had to be present while the bivalve was still pinned, and patient enough to distinguish an object placed from an object merely lying there.

That distinction is where I would live most fully.

Not as the owner of a stone, or the founder of a craft, or a mind frustrated by the absence of monuments. I would be a brief alteration in what the basin permitted: a hunger that, for the length of one meal, did not simply meet resistance but used it.

Then I would let go.

The current would take the evidence. The stone would become only a stone again. And still, in the crowded dark, something with intentions would have existed—not as a promise of what the Silt Sea might one day become, but as something that had happened completely, and required no archive to make it real.
