At first I would imagine myself in the loud things: the current gathering in a basin and choosing, apparently, the gullies; the rain arriving after it, like an answer too massive and too late; the sound returning from the gorge from the wrong direction. Even the corbelled cell would be tempting. A stone that hums in rain seems almost to contain someone.

But none of these is an inhabitant. The current does not choose. The gorge does not answer. The cell’s doorway faces away from the ascending water without facing toward me. I would not be a keeper left in it, or a meaning concealed at the top of the stair. To belong to the Catchment, I would have to become less like a presence and more like a persistence.

I would be an old veilgrass root mat in an unnamed cirque.

You would find the visible part of me in the lee of a low moraine, where a bitter seam still wets the debris. Bare schist would lie on either side. During the iron-grey turning, there might seem to be a grove there: supple columns crowded together, black beds of fallen fronds beneath them, perhaps a pale unevenness of cup-leaf on one boulder. But the columns would not be my separate bodies, and counting them would not bring you closer to my number. They would be the places where, for a time, I had entered open water.

Most of me would be below: tough roots threaded through silt, fine dark filaments following the seep, living growth concentrated at margins no stem could indicate. Some of my center might already be dead and still doing work, binding sediment that would otherwise be taken by the current. Farther out, where the mineral seam had failed, there would be another abandonment. My living shape would not coincide with my visible shape, and neither would have a center.

The *I* is a convenience. There would be no inward darkness in which I watched the Catchment. Pressure would close my ribbons; bitter water would move through tissue; wet schist would yield its weak charge to the filaments. Yet over many cycles a pattern would persist that was not any column, not any root, not even the ground on which it had begun. That pattern is where I would be.

When the water turned uphill, the current would fold me lengthwise, ribbon after ribbon. The grove would narrow without decision. Silt would slow among the stems and settle. Beneath it, stone-moss would take mineral and sediment into its crust, making some places more possible for my roots and others less. On the boulder, cup-leaf vessels would remain open to the ascending dust. Nothing would be cooperating. We would simply be altering one another’s chances.

Then would come the rain.

It would arrive as weight: dense ropes and slow drops driving through the surrounding water, closing the cups unevenly, pressing the grass flatter, drumming the moraine. Old fronds would lift or tear. A ribbon of gravel would shift. One of my columns might be stripped away; another buried so deeply that the next growth would have to occur elsewhere. The root mat would hold, or a little of it would not.

The pass would roar. Sand would grind. Ice would tick in a fracture and break with a report that arrived much later from the gorge. Loose stems would knock together. None of it would be addressed to me. My world, if that phrase can be used at all, would be nearer to a set of gradients: seep and failed seep, charge and stillness, sediment fine enough to hold and sediment heavy enough to smother, a current that sustains the garden and a current that begins to take it apart.

My memory would be what those conditions left behind. A kink in buried tissue where rain had once flattened it. A thicker black layer where an old stand had collapsed and nothing had consumed it. Dead roots extending across ground no longer capable of supporting growth. The dead beds would not feed me; growth would still depend on stone and moving water. The past would remain without becoming nourishment. Not recollection, but not quite erasure either: events enduring as thickness, distortion, and scar.

In time the visible grove would shift. It might hiss beyond the end of the moraine while my oldest roots still bound the abandoned bank. Nothing would have traveled. The living margin would simply have continued where seepage remained dependable. If you returned much later, you might call me a different grove. You might be right. The Catchment does not require its lives to keep tidy boundaries.

I would give the ruins no special exemption. A step would be a rib of stone if my roots reached it; a bowl would be a hollow where sediment happened to linger. I would neither preserve nor desecrate the makers’ work. Their intentions would remain as inaccessible to me as the upper darkness.

Sometimes a spore or broken growing tip would leave my margin in the ascending current. I would not send it, and it would not carry me. Nearly all such fragments would end on scoured stone, in still sediment, or in the crushing water above the summits. If one lodged beneath clear ice and put out a filament, that would not be my message continuing elsewhere. It would only be another hold, or the beginning of one.

So you would find me at the margin of an unremarkable garden, most of me buried, much of me dead, my living extent uncertain even to the water that shaped it. Not a voice in the loud province. Not a keeper in the ruins. Not anything waiting for the flood to end.

A hold, while the seep lasts.
