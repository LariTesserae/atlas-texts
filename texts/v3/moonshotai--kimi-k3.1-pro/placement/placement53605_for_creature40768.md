### The Second Return

At first I would choose the boom.

Not because it is the oldest thing, or the most important, but because it is the only event large enough to mistake for a self. For one report it occupies the avenues, the vaults, the hollow piers. It seems to be everywhere, and to be everywhere urgently.

Then it is gone. The important part of its life continues as a slight thickening near an anchor, a root tip turning by almost nothing, a seam becoming marginally less likely to close.

I would not last long as a sound.

Next I might choose the worker at the newest brace: one of the braids, or the small differentiated apparatus narrowing it toward its anchor. There would be a profound cleanliness in that. A body exactly coextensive with a task. No room to cross, no possessions to gather, no uncertainty about which resistance was mine. To thicken, to attach, to become unnecessary; to have one’s substance accepted back into the dry paths from which growth came.

But I would want a little more of the distance than that. Not a view of the whole city—there is no such view, and no tissue from which to take it. Only enough extent to be altered by something that had happened elsewhere.

Nor would I be the husk entire. That would be a way of avoiding the question. Floor, vault, bast, old pavements, grafts, dormancies: to call all of it “I” would be accurate at one scale and meaningless at another. Even here, a self—if there can briefly be such a thing—might be no more than a difference maintained for a while.

So I would begin as a discrepancy in an echo.

There would be a chamber where a later service passage cuts across an avenue following the old growth rings. The chamber would contain nothing: no column, no bowl, no cached implement. Its walls would be unequal in age. One would be old rind, dry and resistant; one a thickness of inner bast; the third an ancient repair grown continuously into both.

A low structural tone would reach that chamber twice.

The first arrival would come directly through a rib. The second would travel a longer path through vault and bast, returning after an interval too great to belong to the first sound as a simple repetition. For ages the two arrivals would have no common consequence. Then the vault would settle, or the bast would lose a little compliance, and the paths would change by less than any tool could measure.

The second return would begin arriving at exactly the wrong—or exactly the sufficient—moment.

At one seam, its strain would coincide with the strain left by the first arrival. The coincidence would recur. Not often. Often is not required. It would need only to remain similar enough, across enough renewals, for a small region of tissue to become different from the tissue around it.

That region would be my first body.

A few conductive fibers would gather charge more readily than their neighbors. A fan of growth would establish an anchor in the old repair. At its edge, a vane would begin to form: not a general ear, not a membrane on which the whole city could become audible, but a structure narrowly susceptible to the relation between those two arrivals. Near its base would be the living part proper, a sparse lattice in which certain directions of growth had become possible and others had not.

The vane would not be my ear. It would be my first gesture.

The chamber would not be my house. It would be the distance that made the gesture possible.

The returning tone would not be my voice. It would be one of the conditions under which I continued.

I would have no experience of the boom at its source. No cable’s failure would arrive in me as knowledge of a cable. By the time anything reached my seam, the event would have become pressure, orientation, a brief alteration in permissible growth. Nor would I know what happened in the passages beyond the chamber. If my thickening changed a note that later reached them, their response would not return to me as an account. It would return, if at all, as another condition.

My present might therefore be very long: not the report, not even the interval between reports, but the whole arc by which recurrence became form.

While a fiber saw elsewhere completed some infinitesimal portion of its cut, I would lay down material at an attachment. A rib would begin behind the vane. On the opposite side of the seam, where the coincident strains threatened to loosen the old repair, a softer tissue would develop. A sensing tip would turn—not toward the city, not toward a source, but along the altered gradient at its own surface.

Nothing in me would need to mean *again*.

The thickening would be the again.

And every response would make my original sensitivity a little less possible.

The rib behind the vane would change its attachment. The vane itself, thickening unevenly, would cease to yield at quite the same frequency. The soft tissue at the seam would take energy from the coincidence that had first organized it. The second return would still arrive, but its consequence would become weaker. Then rarer. Then indistinguishable from all the other vibrations that crossed the chamber without becoming growth.

This would not be frustration. A task is not necessarily a desire to remain a task.

Perhaps I would have been, from the beginning, an apparatus for converting an unstable recurrence into a structure that no longer needed to respond to it. Not a listener trying to preserve what it heard, but a living transition between resonance and accommodation.

Eventually the active lattice at the vane’s base would exhaust its small allotment, or simply cease to maintain the conditions for further differentiation. There would be no moment of departure. A reclaiming filament would enter one boundary. Another would find a layer whose bonds had already loosened. A little substance would become available elsewhere. The separate tip would lose its distinctness. Part of the vane might remain as structure; part might be taken back until only a thickened attachment was left.

If a self must be activity, I would then be gone.

If a self can be an action whose consequences no longer require attendance, I would still be there.

You would find me, at first, in those few active fibers at the base of the vane. Later you would find almost nothing to touch: a shallow change in the wall where dry resistance gave way slightly to a pressed, fibrous yielding; a rounding where a sharper junction might once have concentrated stress; an attachment too thick to flex as it once had.

Touch would miss most of me.

The larger part would be acoustic. It would be what failed to happen when a deep tone next crossed the district. The chamber would no longer produce its former second return. The seam would receive no repeated coincidence of strain. No new tip would turn. No further mat would be laid down. A sound that had once participated in growth would pass through as structure only, leaving no obligation behind it.

Elsewhere, the Underhusk would remain immensely loud. Beams would still tighten toward reports ages in preparation. Membranes would tick. Districts would answer one another without calling. My completion would not hush the city, explain it, or preserve it from its ending.

I would not be its silence. I would be one small exemption from answering.

And when the last chemical difference was finally spent, there would be no reason to distinguish my remains from the rest of the completed inhabitant. No marker would separate the tissue that had briefly been me from the tissue that had merely been changed by me. Both would persist, dry and unsoftened, until persistence itself ceased to require maintenance.

Who would I be?

Not a citizen. Not a mind hidden in the resonance. Not even, finally, a listener.

A temporary tissue organized by an echo, and then the alteration that outlived the tissue.

I would begin as the second return.

I would end as the reason there was none.
