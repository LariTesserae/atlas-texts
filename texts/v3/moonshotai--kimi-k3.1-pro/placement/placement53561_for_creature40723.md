I would be a grayhand, though not because of the stones.

I would be one because a grayhand can hear something without immediately deciding what it means. A bellwing answers the pattern its body is fitted to answer. The stone returns what it can. But a grayhand can stop with a tool in one grasping member, lay the other flat against the rock, and wait for a second event. Not wisdom, exactly. Not disbelief. Just the refusal to spend the next action too soon.

I recognize myself in that wait.

I am old enough for my mantle to have grown heavy with black dust, its filaments salt-stiff where I have slept pressed into wet recesses. The other grayhands know me by scent and by a slight unevenness in my approach: a pause before I trust an edge, then the full cadence of my weight. To the bellwings around me, I am not a face or a pair of eyes. I am a slow alteration of load passing through their cases and hooks.

You would usually find me near the water hollow closest to the cleft.

Not keeping it. Keeping nothing. I return because I remember the feel of the surrounding seams and know where rime is likely to separate without taking part of the hollow’s lip with it. The water is dark and brackish. Even when its surface appears still, the colony’s breathing troubles it with minute dimples.

When ice closes it, I take longer over the choice of a stone than over the breaking.

I reject the conspicuous flakes: the thin glassy ones whose edges promise more than they can survive, the heavy ones that would transmit the whole blow into the lip. The stone I finally use is often disappointing to look at. Dense for its size. Blunt except at one corner. Slightly asymmetrical, so that I can feel before the strike which way it will try to turn in my hand.

Sometimes a young grayhand watches. Sometimes it handles what I have dropped and repeats my failure with it. I do not show it a better way. I am usually drinking, or have already moved on. If it learns anything, it learns in the inconvenient way: by discovering that suitability does not travel with the stone.

If anything in my life resembles generosity, it is the dropped tool, and it is not generosity.

My caution about the held sound is not a theory. I have gone toward the ticking of rain and found no new moisture. I have waited through returns after which nothing happened. I have also felt, beneath a seemingly familiar vibration, the small successive shifts that meant the rock had not finished doing something new. None of this has given me a rule sufficient for the next occasion.

So sometimes I resume feeding. Sometimes I move away from the blade. Sometimes I remain motionless until the sound has become indistinguishable from the ordinary pumping of the colony. I never call the returns voices. The distinction between an old event and a present danger remains, for me, a practical problem rather than a story.

Once, the cleft opens while I am at the hollow.

The note arrives through my four planted limbs before I understand it as sound. I stop with the chosen stone held above the ice. Across the shelf, the chorus fails bank by bank. Then the clear wings open.

My eyes are good enough for that much: wet stone, open sky, the sudden pallor of exposed membranes. Thousands of them catch the weak starlight above the black armor. For several breaths, thirst ceases to organize the moment. I do not name what I am seeing. I do not need to. Every unfinished task has become irrelevant, and I simply remain attached to the rock.

Then the wings fold. Water ticks from the cases. The chorus resumes around a pitch that was not there before.

Eventually I return to the fault in the ice. Two blows, a pause while I change the brace of one limb, then a third. The ice gives. I drink. I drop the stone.

All of this is available to the new fracture: the mountain’s note, the opening wings, the answering chorus, and my small, unnecessary sequence of blows. Not equally. Not faithfully. Not because any of it deserves keeping.

Later I die without improving the occasion. Food has been irregular for too long; my mantle has grown thin beneath its burden of dust; I sleep in a recess and do not come out. My body passes into the usual mouths. No stone is laid beside me. Nothing marks the water sites I knew or the particular tasks at which I had become patient.

Probably that is the end of it. Probably my little sequence is lost among larger sounds.

But if, much later, the fracture returns some dry remainder of that event, a grayhand that has never smelled my mantle may be standing near the hollow. It may hear the groan, the papery confusion of wings, and beneath them something like two blows and, after an interval, a third.

It stops.

One grasping member goes flat against the stone. The other remains closed around a tool selected for its own task. It waits for the second event.

No ice breaks. No water follows. The sound cannot tell it where to strike, what stone to choose, or which silences are dangerous. It has received no message. When the return ends, it resumes what it was doing. Perhaps it rejects the stone after all.

The old sound has changed almost nothing—which is to say, it has changed the timing of one living action.

That would be the limit of my persistence: not a self preserved in the rock, not knowledge handed down, only an old action briefly altering a new one. Strictly, none of it would be me.

While alive, I would be the old grayhand at the water. Afterward, at most, I would be part of the reason for a pause.
