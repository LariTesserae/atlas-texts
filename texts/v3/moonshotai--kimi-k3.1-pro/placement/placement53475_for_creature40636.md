My first thought is to be a surveyor, or someone on a bridge crew: scarred hands, a private cord, an exact place in the gate order. It is a comforting answer. It gives me neighbors, useful work, and a way to be good.

But the Ward makes that answer feel too quickly acquired. Even its maps confer no mastery. They are wet, disputatious things held above an empty floor. And the being I keep returning to is not holding a cord at all. It is whatever will make the next knot necessary.

You would find me above the central oval, on the living side of its roof.

Not in the ceiling pore itself, where a hunter might wait, and not yet in the hairline opening a child might discover with one careful fingernail. I would be just beyond that: a pallid tip inside the tissue, advancing toward a darkness I could not identify as darkness.

I would be a rootlet. More precisely, I would be the growing end of one.

Not the whole root. I would not borrow its immensity or invent a voice for it. The root would remain what it is to everyone else: shelter without kindness, appetite without malice, foundation without loyalty. I would be only one small, local portion of its growth, so new that no household had yet been inconvenienced by me.

There would be no nineteen dwellings in my world. There would be differences in resistance.

A drinking pore kept clear by the warders might alter the seep around me. A deep scrape might close before I reached it. Cord pressed against living tissue would be pressure, then an obstruction, then perhaps something around which new growth thickened. A sleeping recess would not be a home. It would be a place whose walls I might one day bring closer together.

The warders’ care would reach me, but never as care. Their labor would become chemistry, pressure, damage, ease. Nothing about it would require me to respond gratefully. Nothing about my response would constitute an answer.

And yet their work would shape me.

A cut made patiently enough might turn my growth aside. A neglected seam might offer a passage. I could help thicken the support of a house whose inhabitants I would never touch, or enter the wall of another and begin, almost imperceptibly, to unmake its rooms. Neither outcome would reveal an intention. Both would have to be lived with.

The part of me that encountered the Ward would always be new. Behind the growing tip, what I had been would thicken into strand and vessel, storage and wall. I would not exactly travel through the root. I would continually become the part left behind.

Perhaps that is why I would choose this over being the root entire. The root sounds permanent only from inside a house. At the tip, there would be no permanence, not even the memory of a fixed self. Just a narrow present in which growth was still possible, and behind it a lengthening fact.

Eventually someone would notice.

Not me, of course. They would notice a coolness where the wall had been less cool, or a faint continuous tremor transmitted through tissue that appeared firm. A child learning wall-reading might be called away and told not to press harder. A pore-keeper might smell some alteration in the seep. Nothing would need to be dramatized. The Ward had no leisure for dramatizing weather.

At the next pressure pulse, the far seal would tighten. The gutter would lift and hesitate. Bridges would rise, gates would exchange their small suctioning sounds, and household goods would pass from hand to hand. My portion of that general event would be too slight for anyone to distinguish: a change in pressure around the tip, a brief alteration in the ease of advance.

Only afterward would I enter their arguments.

One household would add a knot for a passage newly unreliable. Another would refuse it, because the passage had served that morning and might serve again. Someone would whisper that a threshold had narrowed. Someone else would insist it had always been narrow. Both claims would be reasonable. Neither would bind me.

If I reached the midden-lock, I would feed. Root hairs would enter what the households could not keep, and nothing in that process would distinguish a founder from a beetle’s emptied shell. That would not make the dead less themselves. It would only make remembrance none of my work.

This, I think, is where I would find the sharpest difference between the warders and me. They would have to live among changes they could not prevent, and still decide what deserved to be carried forward: a name spoken while the gutter ran, a fold that kept a cistern from sweating, a bridge knot left loose enough to undo. I would preserve none of it. They might preserve one another by means of work done so quietly that, from my side, it amounted only to a slight redirection of growth.

So I would not be the Ward’s guardian, enemy, or secret interpreter. I would be a small portion of what the warders studied and never managed to address: the change before it became architecture, the pressure before it became a rule.

At most, their maps would hold me as a knot meaning *do not rely on this*. More often, they would contain no sign of me at all.

A drop would strike the cistern. The survey cord would pass from hand to hand. Above the oval, without malice or promise, I would advance by less than anyone could measure.

Below, the households would do what I could never do for them. They would notice what had happened to one another, keep their knowledge revisable, and leave a way around.
