### A Country with a Pulse

I would find myself in a darkness closer than the cleft’s: beneath the folded wing of a bat, at the warm hinge where membrane joins fur.

I would be a mite.

The bat would never become a whole animal to me. It would be too large and too near. Its body would be ground: skin that tightened before movement, slopes of muscle, a pulse transmitted through whatever I held. Fur would stand around me in dense, disorderly thickets. Mineral grit and moth scales would lodge between the hairs, and the wing above would make a second roof, lifting slightly when the bat turned in its sleep, then settling over me again.

The rest of the Breath-Cleft would arrive indirectly.

A quarrel in the roost would be an earthquake. Another bat crawling over mine would tilt the ground and press the air from my shelter. When my host clicked, the vibration would pass through its body before it became sound anywhere else. Sometimes there would be a storm of dry scales and the violent, repeated opening of the wing: moths taken close to the roost, perhaps, or only a dispute over a familiar place. I would have no way to distinguish the history of the chamber from the restlessness of the animal carrying me.

The mountain’s breathing would reach me as changes in that animal.

When cold air was drawn inward, the bats would compress. My host would push deeper among them, and the open edges of my little country would disappear. Warmth would come not only from below but from the sides. Another body would press close, fur touching fur, and for a while there would be a border where one bat ended and another began.

When the warm exhalation returned, the roost would loosen. The wing above me would lift more often. My host would crawl, groom, strike at something in the air, settle again. I would know nothing of films extending along the middle walls or crickets spreading toward the entrance. I would know only that the ground had become more active.

On a favorable night, it would carry me outside.

First there would be a scramble over other bodies. Then a different smell would enter the fur: cold stone, old snow, air without the cleft’s ammoniac weight. My host would reach the mouth and let go.

The ground would fall with me.

The folded wing would open, and what had been my roof would become a vast straining surface trying to tear me loose. I would flatten myself and hold. Cold would find the gaps between the hairs. The bat’s pulse would quicken under my feet.

There would be no summit for me, no blade of andesite, no starlight briefly returned by moisture. There would be pressure, speed, and the warmth of blood continuing beneath a skin exposed to summit air. I would descend with the bat into hunting grounds I could never smell as a valley or recognize as a slope. Every turn would be a change in the direction of my clinging. Every insect taken would be, at most, a concussion and a new odor at the mouthparts far away.

I would travel farther in that one journey than a larva in a wet seam could travel if its seam lasted a lifetime. Yet I would explore nothing. To be carried is not to explore.

Nor would I feed the cleft as the bat did. The bat would return with food gathered elsewhere; later its droppings would fall onto the floor and enter the quick economy of crickets, larvae, films, and dust. I would bring back only myself. My part in that great import would be simply not to be brushed away.

Home would announce itself through folding. The wing would close over me. The smells of guano, warm stone, and packed bodies would return. My host would push into the roost until its movements became small again, and the cleft would resume around us.

For the ash moths, the warm rear wall would be nearly the whole world. For the bat, the cleft would be an address. For me, it would be something that happened whenever my world came home: a season of close bodies, moth dust, and mineral warmth between weathers of flight.

It would not be a secure existence. A fall that did not end in scrambling and indignant clicking would eventually become the cooling of the ground. A death on the ceiling might leave me clinging to a country that no longer moved. Even an ordinary grooming could remake everything without warning. My life would depend on a heartbeat I had not chosen and could not influence.

But the crowding would offer one possibility besides endurance.

On some cold, indrawn night, when the bats had pressed themselves into their tightest arrangement, my host’s neighbor would shift. The furred slopes of another body would come close. Warmth would touch warmth. Between them would open a passage narrower than a grain of the cleft’s black dust.

I could cross.

Not because I knew another bat was there, and not because I imagined a future on it. Only because the border had come within reach: new fur, a different scent, another surface holding heat.

To let go would be the first independent journey of my life.

Behind me, the familiar body would move in its sleep, and the passage would close. Ahead, an unfamiliar wing would fold. The new bat might remain in the cleft for days, or leave on the next favorable wind; it might hunt within the chamber, or carry me farther down the mountain than the old one ever had. I would know none of this at the crossing.

No drop would fall. No band of crickets would pause. The warm wall would continue trembling with moths, and the ceiling would close over the disturbance as if nothing had happened.

But for one almost invisible inhabitant, everything would have changed. I would have passed from world to world in the pressure between two sleeping bodies, and the ground beneath me would have a different heart.
