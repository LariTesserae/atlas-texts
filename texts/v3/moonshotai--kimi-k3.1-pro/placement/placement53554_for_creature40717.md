## A Small Climate

I would begin by looking for the water, and for a while that would lead me to the wrong places.

To the broad pans, where rain briefly gives the sky a floor. To the bowls worn into ancient stonebacks, warm at their margins and crowded with larvae. To the dark undersides of pebbles, where a little moisture survives the morning. Each would seem, at first, to be where the plateau keeps its life.

But I would not want to be the rain. Rain arrives and is taken. I would want to know what remains after the visible water has gone.

So you would find me before dawn, inside the wall of a low dome beside a shallow basin.

From outside, the dome would scarcely announce itself: three softened ribs, a sunken arc, one chamber open to the sky and another still holding a little darkness. Its edges would have the same mineral patience as the ground around it. You might mistake it for erosion until you noticed that the curves repeated, and that beneath them the air was faintly humid.

I would not be at its center. I would be within a rib on the sheltered side, where the inner surface stayed cool after the outer skin had begun to warm.

For one soft lifetime, I would be a builder.

Most of that life would not resemble building. I would forage along the dim film inside the chambers, tend the young in the wettest pockets, and follow routes laid down by animals whose bodies had already become part of the walls. Mites would live in the pores around me. Grazers would cross my path, transparent as disturbances in the damp. I would carry spores without knowing that I carried them, and the film would appear later in places I had never seen.

Only when the material was workable would I add to the structure.

Even then, I would not be making a dome. I would be answering the taste of old silk, the give of a damaged surface, the presence of moisture on one side and dryness on the other. I would lay down a crescent, incorporate what my body had shed, and coat the addition with mineral secretion. Beside me, another animal would be doing nearly the same thing, though not quite. The wall would grow from those small differences.

Once, after a rare wetting, I would find myself at the edge of an old collapse.

The former line of the rib would still be visible, but the damp would not follow it. It would lie a little farther inward, along a rougher surface where the night’s condensation had collected. My crescent would turn that way. Not from foresight. Not even from preference, exactly. The next animal would extend the turn, and another would thicken it after the repair held.

Years later, the rib would possess a shallow inward bend.

That would be the nearest thing to my portrait.

It would not be recognizable as an act. No builder would inspect it and understand that the dome had been altered. But water, having no interest in intentions, would find the curve. Before dawn, beads would gather along its cooler edge and run toward an inner seam instead of drying where they formed. Beneath that seam, a pocket would remain humid a little longer than the surrounding chambers.

A little longer would matter.

A few more larvae might survive there. The photosynthetic film might wake earlier along one portion of wall. Grazers would gather, then hunters small enough to live among their trails. Builders tending the nursery would begin cropping the new growth and carrying its spores farther inward. Nothing would announce a succession of consequences. There would only be slightly more life in one place than the old shape of the wall had permitted.

By then, the word *I* would already have become difficult to use.

Some of my body would have entered the structure long before my death, as shed material bound into laminae. My routes would have been continued by other feet. My additions would have been covered, corrected, or made useful in ways I could not have predicted. There would be no clear moment when I stopped being an animal and started being architecture. The colony would not preserve me. It would simply keep responding to what I had left beside the next animal’s work.

Eventually my soft body would end and be taken back into the small economies of the chambers. The bend in the rib would remain.

Not unchanged. Nothing on the plateau is unchanged. A later repair might deepen it; a dry period might leave one face powdery and thin. The wall would swell and tighten with humidity, opening and closing its pores. Generations would thicken the places where condensation proved reliable and neglect those that repeatedly dried. The structure would become less a record of its makers than a record of water’s answers to them.

Still, for a while, my biography would be a slight curve and the direction taken by a drop.

Much later, the colony itself might dwindle. There would be no need for that to be dramatic. Fewer young would survive; repairs would come more slowly; outer crescents would remain unfinished. Veilwing young would occupy the wet reservoirs. Rosettes would establish in the debris of an open roof. At the buried entrance, soft-backed stonebacks would shelter during their mobile youth, carrying away mites and spores when they left.

The dome would cease to belong to its builders without ceasing to be useful.

And eventually I would no longer be even a wall. I would be the difference between that hollow and the ground beyond it: a pocket of air that cooled more slowly, a seam where film brightened after condensation, a place where moisture remained possible for one more hour.

If you came at noon, you would see a mineral eccentricity beside a nearly empty basin. You would find no name, no center, nothing that pointed back to a maker. If you came before dawn and waited without disturbing the crust, you might notice water gathering along the inward bend. You might hear the minute ticking of stonebacks warming beyond the wall, or the papery brush of veilwings descending too closely together.

The bead of water would not be me. Neither, exactly, would the rib.

I would be a builder for one brief, soft lifetime. After that, I would be one of the corrections by which a shelter learned to keep its water.

Not a monument. A small climate: the extra hour before the chamber dried.
