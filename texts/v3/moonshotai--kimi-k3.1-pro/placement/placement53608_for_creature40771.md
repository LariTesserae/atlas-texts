### The Stone’s Permission

When the cloud shadow came, and the birds lifted back toward the walls, you might think the mat had been abandoned. But if you knelt and brought your eye level with the flowers, you could find me inside one of the garnet bells: a black, furry bee, pale pollen still on my hind legs, garnet dust marking my head.

Not sleeping, exactly. Not safely at home. Simply reduced to the small amount of life the flower’s warmth could still support.

The bell would not be a refuge made for me. It would merely be a little warmer than the open mat, and I would be small enough for *merely* to be enough.

My nest would be nearby, in sun-facing gravel where the eastern vegetation broke into separate cushions. Perhaps I would have taken over a narrow recess beside an old hare burrow. From there, the whole cirque would not appear to me as a basin a few hundred paces across. It would be an archipelago of temperatures: the slab that warmed first, the yellow cups worth visiting before the wind found them, the strip of shrub where the air stayed still, the cold gravel that could steal the heat from my body before I had crossed it. A distance of two handspans might be nothing. Another, of the same length, might be impossible until afternoon.

Light alone would not wake me. The walls could already be glittering, the cloud-field shining beyond the lip, and I would remain motionless. Then warmth would enter the slab beneath me. Only afterward would my legs loosen, my body begin its private shivering, my wings become conceivable.

Once outside, I would seldom need to fly far. The flowers would be so crowded that walking, with a hop when a stem or patch of grit intervened, would carry me through most of the morning. I would know them by their small differences in warmth and moisture as much as by color. Some would be open but useless. Some would be worth returning to. One might hold a little of the previous cloud in the angle of a leaf.

Below me, the nest would grow by additions too small to look like progress. Pollen brought in. Nectar added. A chamber provisioned and closed with grains and resin. Then, if the stone remained warm, another beginning. There would be no hive in which the work accumulated into something visible, no winter store for myself. A whole season of correct decisions might amount to a few sealed cells in a space smaller than a closed hand.

That would not make the work slight.

A cloud shadow would interrupt it without warning. I might back into a bell, or wedge myself into a cleft already almost too narrow. Outside, beetles would vanish and midges settle; the open mat would become a country I could no longer enter. There would be nothing heroic to do. Waiting would not be activity, and it would not guarantee survival. If the cold lasted, I could cool beyond the reach of the next sunlight.

But when the light returned, if it returned in time, there would be no ceremony either. The bell would cease to be my whole world. I would crawl out, find the next flower, and continue from the place where the work had stopped.

Eventually there would be a cold that no warming slab could reverse. Weather and copper beetles would inherit my body. I would not see the offspring below ground, and they would have no memory of me. To call them my legacy would make the nest more human than it was. They would be their own hunger in the dark, their own transformations, their own long suspension through the season when the cirque withdrew almost every address.

What remained of my work would be material and particular: pollen where food was needed, a chamber that had not collapsed, resin still holding its grains against the frost.

I think that is why I would be this creature. Not because briefness is beautiful in itself, nor because urgency automatically gives a life meaning. I would be the bee because her existence would be exact without being continuous. She would not overcome interruption; she would become precise about it. Warm stone, open flower, passable air, one more provision—then stillness again. Smallness would not be a disguise she assumed against the mountain. It would be the shape in which the mountain could, sometimes, accommodate her.

By winter, there would be nowhere on the mat you could point and truthfully say that I remained.

But on some later morning, the slab would warm. A garnet bell would stir although no wind moved. You might mistake what crawled out for me.

You would be wrong, and close enough.
