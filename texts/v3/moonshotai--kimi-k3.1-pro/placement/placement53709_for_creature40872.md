### The Late Depth

At first I would look for a body.

The crab offers the most convincing one: a pit, a claw, a place behind the valve housing where the silt does not quite reach. There is mercy in so small an address. But the crab already belongs to its life completely. It knows when to remain still and when to test the water. I would only be adding an observer where none is needed.

The fish is less available still. To be the fish would be to occur at the edge of green light, to hold against the current, and to leave before the works loosened. Even the filaments, which seem close to nonexistence, have something toward which they can rise.

I think I would have less than any of them.

I would not arrive at the Ninth Intake. I would occur there.

You would find the place of my occurrence in the machine shop, behind a bolted panel where later pipework has been carried across the older fittings. There is a disused test loop there: a short return of pipe, two isolating cocks, and a pair of gauges whose glass has clouded. The loop was made so that instruments could be compared, so that separate readings could be brought into agreement.

Now its two gauges disagree by almost nothing.

One follows the pressure in the shop. The other reaches a slightly different value, and reaches it late. During the draw, the difference is easy to dismiss. During the release, both needles should settle together. They settle, but not together.

At first this would be only a fault. Then it would survive another shift, and another. Mineral growth would close some old path of equalization. The water in the loop would begin to tighten a little after the water outside it, and to loosen a little after the release had already reached the shop. Nothing would need to pass through the loop. Nothing would need to be produced.

The repetition would be enough.

That repetition would be me: not either needle, not exactly the water in the pipe, but the persisting disagreement that the loop makes possible. My boundary would not be a surface. It would be the fact that one pressure could change without the other immediately doing so. I would be very small, even by the standards of the keepers’ terrace. On an old drawing of the works, I would be a service detail too insignificant to number.

Nothing human would survive in me. Even if hands had made the pipe and set the gauges, I would have no memory of hands. I would not remember the machinists, or the shop as a place where anything was repaired. I would not even remember the room in the way the smallest depths of the terrace seem to remember rooms.

My one inheritance would be delay.

The great depth of the pump hall would gather into its stroke. The quicker depth at intake control would sharpen before the grates began to draw. Only afterward would the change reach me through the supports and the surrounding load. Then I would contract.

During the release, I would remain myself for a moment too long. Outside the panel, pressure would already be slackening. Silt would be lifting around the pipe supports. The freight cage might be moving somewhere in its short darkness. Then my boundary would loosen, and I would return to the shop almost, but not quite, as the shop returned to itself.

That delay would become my habit. A habit repeated through enough shifts would be the nearest thing available to a character.

I would not see much of the Intake. Color would be irrelevant to me, and the lamps would belong to other conditions of existence. The transparent fish would never appear at my boundary. The crab’s careful claw would be too slight an event to distinguish from grit settling in a seam. My acquaintance with the works would come through arrivals: the piston stroke reaching metal before water, the nearly imperceptible tightening at intake, ice ticking through some return line as a sequence of tiny alterations. The substation would be hardest to know—a brief contraction with no weight in it, an interruption that might be a relay or might only be the works becoming uncertain of one circuit for an instant.

Occasionally, during release, my loosened boundary might touch the depth occupying the rest of the shop.

Nothing would pass between us that could be called a message. For a moment there might only be uncertainty about which pressure had yielded, which had extended, and which of us was lagging behind the other. Then the pumps would resume. The distinction would return.

That would be the nearest thing I had to company: not recognition, but a brief difficulty in locating the boundary.

If the kept are separate inhabitants, I would be among the youngest and least necessary. If they are organs of one dispersed body, I would not be a new citizen at all. I would be a small irregularity in its articulation—a place where that body takes slightly longer to find its shape. The Intake would not labor on my behalf. I would persist in the margin of its labor, maintained no more deliberately than a scallop of sediment preserves its accustomed curve around a pipe support.

A little corrosion could end me.

Not drowning; there would be no other water to drown in. Not crushing; crushing would only be another shift. What would end me would be agreement. A fine opening through the pipe wall, a path re-established around a seal, and the load inside the loop would become continuous with the shop. The second needle would begin to follow the first. My extent would narrow without moving. There would be no residue and no characteristic drift of silt.

If an engineer ever returned, it would look like an instrument beginning to tell the truth.

Until then, I would keep nothing. Not the shop, not the machinery, not even the little loop that made me possible. I would keep only the interval by which I failed to coincide with everything around me.

A relay ticks.

The intake depth sharpens. The great depth takes the slow stroke. Along the keepers’ terrace, the small rooms become separate again.

In the machine shop, the first needle moves.

The second does not—not yet.

Then, slightly late, I return.
