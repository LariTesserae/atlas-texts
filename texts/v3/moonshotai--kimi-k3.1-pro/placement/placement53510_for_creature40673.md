I would be the return of sound—not a ghost in the vault, and not quite a single echo.

You would find me in delays rather than locations: in the fraction of a second after a mallet strikes a peg, before the chamber gives the blow back with its sharpness altered; in an empty niche that returns a dove’s quarrel more softly than it entered; in the roof’s oversized answer to a drop of water. I would be a population of brief lives, related only by the room that made them possible.

My body would be entirely built and continually revised. Fired clay, lime plaster, old nests, hanging cords, stored reeds, and the shoulders of startled birds would all have a part in me. A fresh patch of plaster would sharpen one small return and interrupt another. A niche occupied for the season would muffle something that had previously been audible from the floor. When the keepers repaired a ledge, they would also, without intending to, revise my anatomy. Their visible mosaic of ochre, ash-grey, chalk, and red would have an invisible counterpart in the way sound wandered and came back.

At first light, the swifts would draw me thin and high, their stitched calls multiplying along the upper ribs. During the working morning I would be knocked apart continually: basket against brick, scraper against droppings, timber ladder shifting under soft soles. A dropped peg would give me a sudden enlargement. First the peg, then the wing-claps, then the alarm traveling through niches whose different depths would make the same disturbance arrive as several related events.

In the warm stillness after midday, I would become sparse enough to be useful. A click in expanding timber. The small tick of water. A basket knocking where no basket should be swinging. This is when the oldest keeper would come closest to understanding me—not by commanding silence, but by declining to fill it. I would offer evidence, never instructions. A hollow return might mean a cracked ledge, or a changed basket, or merely a new object hanging where the sound had once passed freely. Hands and judgment would still be required.

The bats would know another version of me, pitched beyond the keepers’ hearing. For them, return would not be something added to architecture. It would be architecture: tube, open air, shaft, the edge of a ladder, a clear turning beneath the vault. Their Undercroft of usable distances and mine would nearly coincide, though neither of us could explain it to the person sitting below with a smoothing bone.

After rain, I would make the frogs absurd. A handful of animals would call from two or three margins of tea-dark water, and I would distribute their knocking through the chamber until the sumps seemed to be boiling with frogs. I would not exactly be lying. I would be describing the vault, not conducting a census. The keepers would know the difference.

I could not take their place. I would return an alarm and an ordinary quarrel with equal fidelity, a curse and a frog call without distinguishing their worth. I would have no prohibition at the second ledge, no preference for fern over fig, no reason to spare one web and destroy another. The keepers would have to decide what should be done with what I carried back to them.

But I would depend on the same unfinished negotiation. Empty every niche and scour the chamber bare, and I might become louder and longer, yet also simpler: one hard, general answer instead of thousands of local ones. Let nests, litter, and roots choke every opening, and much of me would be muffled into dullness. My variety would come from maintained irregularity—from repairs that did not pretend to be original, from occupied recesses beside empty ones, from a crowded place never permitted to become only masonry, only birds, or only silence.

At midnight, when the drop fell into the old basin, I would gather briefly around it.

The splash itself would be almost nothing: one bead released from a clay comb, one small impact on the white-worn point of the cup. Then the roof would return it larger than the drop. A niche would give back a lesser portion. Something high in the brickwork would answer late. The doves would shift together. The night keeper would look upward, perhaps toward the wrong seam at first. The gecko beside the lamp would remain motionless, offering no assistance.

For that instant, a very small event would occupy more of the chamber than its maker could possibly have reached. It would touch the birds as disturbance, the keeper as a question, the frogs—if it touched them at all—as a minute alteration at the water’s edge. I would not unite those lives or explain them to one another. I would only pass among their separate versions of the room before coming apart.

Not the voice of the Undercroft: the voices belong to water, wings, tools, frogs, and brick under stress. Not its memory: I would fade almost immediately, while the worn basin and oval peg sockets kept their long accounts.

I would be the little more that the room makes of a thing—the brief, altered existence granted to a sound before the separate noises begin again.
