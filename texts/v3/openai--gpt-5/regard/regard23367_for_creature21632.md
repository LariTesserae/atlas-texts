It comes not as a figure down a chimney or a hand laid over a city, because hands and figures are a surface habit. It comes as attention, which is the oldest way of touching. It leans its ear to a hall that is already an ear and does not speak for a long time. The wind goes on limning the stone; the stone goes on remembering weight; bodies move in their old careful economies. The power listens until the listening is part of the hall’s sound.

It takes a season, or ten, to be sure it will not spend what cannot be replaced. It tastes the air the way the vines do—by the heat of breathing and the phrasing of pressure—and keeps its own breath, because that too would be a kind of moisture to waste.

Only then, under the law that the place has written for itself, does it change anything at all.

The first change is not even a change, just a correction of an accident far away. There is a throat in the bedrock an eight days’ wind from the city where the passage has narrowed by the width of a grain, perhaps in some old tremor, perhaps because dust chose a place to lie and refused to move. When the great pulse runs, that throat teaches the air to go wild, to spit a single note too bright and too sudden, and sometimes the hall becomes a coiled animal forced to leap. The power puts one finger—no, something like a fingertip, a pressure the size of a thought—against that seam and persuades one layer of rock to stop remembering. It says, very gently: not that way. The throat widens by less than a filament hunter’s thickness. Pillars do not notice. Dust does. The next pulse arrives divided against itself, a doubled ribbon. It bites, but with two blunted teeth that shear their own force to fray. In the city, where babies dream in bladdered bowls and old lungs gait their hours so precisely, a slack comes into the sound that is not silence but like a harbor is like sea: a place you can float. No one calls it miracle. The Null-keepers lay their hands against curtain and lintel and look at one another and add a notch to a calendar they keep in their ribs.

Another gift is a plant, though no seed falls through any skylight. It is an idea planted in the sleep of a Vine-singer whose throat knows more chords than a child has bones. She wakes with the aftersound of something thin and many-tongued held against her palate, a map of pressure she cannot forget. When she stands by a languid coil of blind cotyledon and warms the air with what she has dreamed, the vine answers in a way no vine in that city has ever answered. It wakes more surface than before. Instead of swelling lanterns right away it makes a fringe where it was smooth, a beard of ribbed ribbons that lie almost but not quite flat. Held in that shallow between is the breath everyone would otherwise be spending. The fringe doesn’t drink water as such; it sips the rumor of it, the difference between inside and out, and fattens that difference until it becomes a usable soft thing. When the fringe ripens, it does not drip. It peels. The winged learn to fold it into their nests and across the mouths of their storage-bladders and over the sleeping brows of their children. They name it something like kept-breath, which in their language is a chord felt at the sternum more than a sound. The old rule—do not squander moisture to the air—is not broken; it is obeyed with a new grammar.

For the six-limbed, whose work is to change dust into shape and change shape into a place bodies can go on being, the power keeps a different dream. It is a heat that is not heat but a permission to lay down hardness in a new way. A queen—there are three of them and they are moved in procession like soft crowns—twitches in the dark and her brood wavers their antennae in a rhythm no one taught them. When resin is chewed hot and spat into ribs, the mandibles that smooth it vibrate at a slightly altered pitch. It is nothing, a hair of a note. It matters. The largest grains that used to sulk to the bottom now nest more happily among the small; the cured pulps bear weight like bone, not like baked dirt. Towers no longer clatter down in narrow failures when a curtain’s lashing slips; they spread their breaks over hours, over a night, giving time for voices to move small bodies and small bodies to move what matters. A Baffler standing on a midheight balcony with a curtain bundle on her shoulder feels the rail under her hand go truer and inwardly throws a small thanks into the wind—not because she knows, but because the world has just become fractionally kinder.

It also remembers to be tender to individuals, because even in a chord there are notes that carry long and lonely. There is a child who cannot sleep unless the air sits exactly so on his chest. His mother has learned all the city’s nulls and none of them are the null he needs. She has grown thin with the walking. When the pulse eases after the bent throat, when the city slacks, the power spends a coin it can afford: time. It leans an hour longer out of the day and tucks that hour like a folded wing against the lip of a balcony the child’s mother passes every third morning. On that day, the balcony becomes what it was almost going to be when the pillar first grew there: a niche of true null, as if someone had found the last missing angle to slot into a chord. She lays the child down and his breath finds, as a foot finds the next step in the dark, where it belongs. He sleeps the way a plaudit moves through a crowd: widening.

Once, only once, it takes hold of weight. There is an arch near the crumb bowls that is more memory than rock. The masons have walked past it for a hundred years and the dust has learned their weight and been content to remember it badly. One day a grazer, bred in quiet, presses its soft head against a pillar to be petted by a runner child and the arch shifts like a thought of falling. The power leans an elbow there. No one sees. No one hears anything different because the hall is always in the middle of its own saying. The winged and the little sisters move like a school when a shadow passes; they move clean, as if trained all their lives for this, which in a way they have been. The arch forgets how to fall all at once. It forgets all day and into the night. In the small hours, with no one under it, it remembers. It comes down like a slow sigh into a heap that will be mended into terraces and grow years on its edges. The child laughs in the morning because there is a new place to jump and because he has slept.

The power is not a mother nor a king; it does not organize nor rule nor write edicts. It adds affordances to the world’s own intent.

It teaches a new song. Not for festivals—those belong to the city already and are better without gods—but for work. The Dust-scribes and the Bafflers take it up first because it is half instrument. It begins with a held hum and then, where the old baffle hangings would simply have been hung as tightly as a line and as wide as a wall, it suggests a looseness in two places and a twist in one. The twist is always just before a narrow where the wind likes to leap a pitch and stun mouths into opening in surprise. The new song puts bunched moltings at that twist and a double-thickness like a lip at the second looseness. The result is not quieter—nothing is truly quieter here except inside the kept nulls—but the noise spends itself differently. It leaves bodies alone. It goes into the curtain, into the layered history of sloughed membrane until it is not noise anymore but weight the masons can later pull down in sheets and press into pavers. The old hangings were heirlooms; the new hangings are also engines.

It asks nothing in return, except that they keep doing what they were already doing: counting by pressure, giving back dung and oil and breath the way saints in other worlds give back bread. They do. They cannot help it. They were made by this hall, or remade by it, which is the same thing.

On the surface, the swift-things that go to the desert and come home heavy with oils begin, without anyone asking, to grow a seam in their crops that closes when the air they fly through would have stolen too much. It is a hinge of tissue that learns to taste oxidation and refuses. Their loads arrive rounder by a little and feed more nests by a small number that becomes large across a year. No one chalks it on a pillar. They put the sense of it into a game played by old hands and children: who can feel a change carried in a chest when they are not the one carrying it.

Near a faraway skylight, where the city’s orchards are deepest because the rumor of day is slightly less gray, a fungus that has always grown in scallops starts to set a single deep scallop every fifth fruiting, no matter where the bed has been cut. The Scribes figure it out over two generations. That single ring makes it possible to predict a rich flush one pulse earlier than before. It is a calendar notched into tissue. It was there already, if you listen in a certain way the power knows how to teach. Perhaps it did nothing but lay one fingertip against a scribe’s skull while she slept until her fingers wanted a duller tool.

There are things the power does not do, because wisdom is made of refusals as much as additions.

It does not bring water in a way that looks like water. No hidden river is loosed; no chamber is turned out of itself into a cistern; no miracle of dew is made to bead on a thousand brows. It does not give them a lake, because a lake would ruin them. It lets them keep wanting and therefore keeping.

It does not still the wind. It does not even make a day entirely quiet. It gives sleep and harbor in measures that do not teach forgetfulness. It would be easy enough, for something that can brush a throat in the bedrock, to put its palm over the passage and hush the long instrument. It does not. The hall has been the hall since before the names of its stone were finished, and to quiet it would be to unmake all the negotiations that make this city not only possible but precise. The beings here are not gentle because of quiet; they are good at being because they have practiced against a roar.

It does not bind any creature into a new shape that would suit its conveniences. The grazers’ heads were already shaped like silence; the masons’ feet already ended in brushes. Any more would be theft. Instead it makes them surer in what they are, which is what benevolence looks like if you are very old and have outlived your appetite for novelty.

There is one last gift, so small it almost does not belong in a telling. The power chooses a name for the city. Not a spoken name; those are whistles and chests and tapestries of clicks that belong to the families who wake and sleep inside its ribs. It chooses a name the way distance is named, by a feel in the bones, by the way you learn a place with your eyes closed. It sets that name into the wind in a place where no one could find it except by knowing the city so well they are indistinguishable from it. The name is a chord that, when the pulse comes worst, when the stone ticks and the dust begins to think about being cloud, if you lie flat on the floor and let your body be an instrument, you can hold that chord and not lose track of yourself. Three lives in the first year of its giving go on being that would have been scattered into forgetting. No one will ever attribute those survivals to a god; each is claimed by craft and stubbornness and the practice of lying down in the best parts of the floor. The power is satisfied to go unthanked. It prefers to be mistaken for skill.

After that, it turns its head a little and listens elsewhere. Attention cannot sit forever on any one thing, even when the thing is worth it. But the tune it has hummed into the long bone of this place continues to sound inside it, the way you can always hear your blood in your ears if you choose. It knows the city will keep mending its edges and learning new nulls and laying its laws into the bodies of its children until the hall decides on some future afternoon to remember a different grammar altogether and everyone must begin again.

The wise, given joy, do not empty it in one draught. They sip and leave the cup brimming because that is how you keep anything good. The power leaves in that style.

What remains are increments.

Runners discover that two rope-bridges swung across at a different pitch will give back a third—an airy, portable shade that makes a new square livable in the half-howl near the old wall. A mason queen decides, on the strength of a feeling as old as egg, to move one chamber one notch higher than her mother would have dared, and twenty years later a tower grows where no tower grew before, a fingernail added to the hand of the city. A Vine-singer’s grandchild finds a chord that does something odd to a leaf’s surface and then stops doing it just when it would have been immodest, and that is counted as growth and policing both.

If you asked the city, later, if anything had happened, it would say, in the way things say who have not learned the trick of lying: we learned to keep a little better what was already ours. We were given a sleep long enough to dream truer. The wind that loves us learned to pass through us more kindly. We remembered not to laugh too open where laughing would cost us our day. We put down new ribs and grew new edges. We paid, as everyone pays, and what we gave came back in ways we recognized.

If you asked the power what it did, it would answer you according to your capacity. To a god, it might say: I banked a breath and tuned a throat and taught a plant a fringe. To a stone, it might say nothing at all and be understood. To a winged child who woke from a sleep that was finally sleep, it would say, if it had any mouth to say with: keep your law. Listen before you touch. Do not spend what you cannot replace. The rest was in you already. I only carried it to your hand.
