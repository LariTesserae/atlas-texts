It does not come as a thing. Things are already busy here. It comes as a habit of attention that was not present before—a slight, exacting courtesy in the air, as if the bowl has remembered to make a little room at the table for a guest who does not eat.

The first anyone can point to is a pause that does not punish. Not a dead calm—that would be an error in a place that lives by imbalance—but a proper rest, like the breath the chest takes between one note and the next before the voice comes in again. An afternoon bent to screaming has found its pit; the trees have their ribs out and are ready to throw, and then, crisply, a half-heartbeat opens inside the gust. Every whisker-ladder lifts. The ventifacts draw in their long saw-notes like teeth behind a polite lip. No one falls. No tether jerks. In the huts the roof-stones do not rattle. The people sigh and do not know why they have sighed.

Listen long enough and you can tell: the rest has tone. It is not an absence. It is tuned like the space inside a perfect bowl. It says, in the voice of felt bone: I am here. I am with you without putting you under me.

Children shake their plates as if a moth has settled there and cannot be seen. One, little and uncareful, runs out into the gray and holds her arms wide and hums the wrong note because wrong is fun. The wrong note stands, sweetly, instead of being pounded flat. She laughs at the tickle in her elbows. Her aunt hums, too, and feels the hum answer through the muscle and not through the string or the stem of a tree. She says a word that in other mouths would be god and in theirs is less person and more grammar: Consent.

So the people do what they have always done in the presence of something new that behaves as if it can hear. They make a tone and see if it can carry it.

They sing low together, the families in the lee of their harps, the groundlines warmed by slow lightning, the tether-backs blinking with patient syntax. The sail-trees shiver their vanes into a broad vowel. The bowl hums itself visible. And the presence—call it wise, call it the hand of a benevolence, call it just that part of the universe that shows up and listens with both ears—does not answer in words. It answers the way a partner answers in a dance: with a weight you can lean on and that does not step on your feet.

When an old scream is about to come thin as a knife and split the day, it leans infinitesimally back into the rim.

If it has any name here it takes that action for its naming: The One Who Leans Right.

No law is changed. The wind is still the wind. The bowl keeps its wide mouth. The harshness that teaches keeps its seasons and its improvisations. But the screaming note that would have made children taste metal at the back of the throat and sent a ribbon of arc from vane to wrist is met where it swells and given another place to spend itself. If you held a palm on the pumice vents you would have felt it in the next days: a lace of new hairline breaks, clean as the inside of a clamshell, that make the air through that stone fall into many small currents instead of one large blade. No hand did it; no tool you could lift to say, This. But the old men who have been dampeners will put their foreheads against that stone and make a low note and say, Good, good, to their own skulls.

Later, after they have agreed among themselves that something good has put itself near and has manners, they ask a real thing of it. They all say it is not asking. They say it is proposing an adjustment. Still, they go up in the morning in an organized silence and they take with them their prisoned jars.

Wet, which is a bright ghost and a knife and something which kills by kindness here, has wandered into their lives only as warning and story and sealed dread. It would be well to keep it that way. But the world has other bowls, other weathers, other strangers who do not know. Machines come with men in them who cough from the breath that is easy in other places and hard here; the men are polite enough sometimes, greedy sometimes, ignorances on legs, and some of them carry little wells in their packs they cannot imagine will not be welcomed anywhere because what is more blessed elsewhere than a drink? How do you tell a visitor who has only one word for water that the right gift here is a thin plate of resin oil that makes the mouth tingle up to the eyes?

So they make a tone and a picture of the way water kills the bowl when it touches the wrong skin, and they send that picture toward the presence like a practiced knot you put into someone else’s hands to see if their fingers know your speed.

The answer is nothing dramatic. If it were dramatic, it would be wrong here.

Along the inner rim, where the pumice vents cough their ghosts of dust and charge and the wind runs its fingers along the stone very fast, there appears in a week a thin ring of not-new lichen that is not any of the ones with names. It looks like filigree left by a careful insect near a sleeping mouth. Dry as all other things, a crisp tape, brown-gray like the throat of a house-gnasher. Put a bead of water on it and the bead makes no mark and no steam. It disappears. Two breaths later there is a smell like a copper pot that has been scoured with salt.

Children are allowed to bring tiny sealed jars up to the band now. They set a bead with a watching aunt’s hand on their wrist to teach the right safety. The bead vanishes, the band eats, and the aunt says, “See, this is where we leave the ghost. Let it go up as story and down as oxide. It will trouble no leaf.” Later, a pattern of little flags is taught to the gliders to warn them to bank high if they smell wet on the wind. Later still, a new word enters their throat for the feeling in the air when a far cloud breaks unseasonably against the planet’s bone and the rain that would have been, elsewhere, cannot find them; the word tastes like pepper and old coin and relief.

Such are the gifts: thin, placed at an angle, polite.

The presence is careful to stand in the gap, not in the way. It watches and bends only where bending will ease what was purely wasteful. It will not take from the bowl its surprise. It will not make stillness. It does not domesticate the day-animal that heaves up in the saucer one noon with skin the color of tea and one newcomer leaf, nor does it pin a name to that animal and hold it for telling. It only makes sure the tethers will untie cleanly on those days, that no one’s new copper line has seized under the skin in such a way that a godling will be garroted by a kindness.

You can see that, too, if you know where to look: in puberty there comes a new layer to the old rite. The woman who sings the leylines into the young so their bodies will not later reject their own adulthood now also sings a soft release into the coil itself. There is an untying under the tying. A ring with a tongue grows out of copper and nerve together. The one who had the song sung into their bones will, in a storm year yet to come, put her hand to her wrist and the rope will open and go sweet in her palm and not take even a hair of her with it. They call that little sub-knot the Loosener, after the name they begin to use, quietly, in their councils and in their lay-prayers, for the company they now keep: The One Who Loosens Without Taking.

There are seven other small adjustments, noticed slowly, argued over, then admitted with a shrug and a touch of awe.

One: a fine blue wakes in the white bodies of dust that leap from the rim-vents on the most dangerous afternoons. If the blue is there, the tether-backs will not come down to have their spines combed, however pretty the song you sing them. “That,” say the elders, “is the bowl showing you its mood by its own skin. Leave it be.”

Two: some of the small gliders begin to carry small pips at the edge of their silica films that glow dim as fur on cats when the charge in the bowl all leans one way. Children, who have always read flags on gliders, now read moods and shake their plates for fun to make the little lights change. It makes them safer without making them timid.

Three: a pattern of serrations grows on the leading edges of certain sail-tree vanes, a chant of tooth too small for the eye and obvious to the hand that knows tree-skin by heart. The serrations do something to the thunder at the edge of a gust. It is still big. It is less knife. The people reach up in their quiet ways and touch the new edges and say, small and unignorant, Thank you, which is to say, Continue if that suits you; we will use it well.

Four: a way of lacing rope comes back to someone in the night as if she had always known it and merely forgotten the part where you make the tail cross itself in a double turn that will never slip until it is asked to slip by a spoken note. She rises, laughs quietly in her hut, tries it in the thin-aurora dark, and it works. In the morning her harps will have a braid on them that knows, when you put a note of G dry on it, to go slack enough to swallow a scream whole.

Five: along two of the oldest yardangs, on the lee faces where the varnish is thick and proud and speaks in fine bells if you tap it with a ceramic nail, there appears a script too slow for writing by a person. You cannot read it with your eyes. You read it by voice. You sing across it at measured times of the year and hear the change in its ring. It tells you, the way a tendon tells a runner what a joint knows, that in the long turning of the world the wind will, over a lifetime and a half, take a new angle down the bowl by a half-degree. It tells you this thirty years before it matters so you can move the poles and the harps without anyone losing a finger to an afternoon that got its old names wrong. The old women press their foreheads to the varnish the way one polite living thing gives its scent to another and say, with simple manual gratitude, Aches told to aches—how clean.

Six: there comes, in the worst years when the planet’s bone rattles wrong and the voice of the ground wakes people at night and no one likes how their plates feel inside their skins, a one-day hush that is not a punishment but a blanket. It does not make the bowl stop. It will not even convince a child the wind has gone. It is the presence turning down the one sliver of screaming that costs bodies without paying for anything else. People take their dead to the saucer that morning with a sense that their throats will not catch on the aftercurrents. They call it the kind day without making a festival of it—some kindnesses are more beautiful when you do not walk too loudly over them.

Seven: travelers still come, which is to the credit of a universe that loves curiosity enough to bear the scuffs it makes. Some of them come with greed thick on their plates. Some of them try to make the bowl into quarry or battery or museum. Those are the days when instruments planned for other weathers come into the bowl and die as they should and also quietly die earlier and farther from any hut than they might have. A harvester that would have scraped the green veneers from a whole grove in a day finds its own battery warm and unwilling at the rim and backs itself into a polite stutter. A drill scheduled to bite a hole in the saucer finds a blue in the air and decides not to decide. There is a little more haze at the horizon on those days than habit expects. The bowl-people say, in their way that has no kneeling in it, We see your care. They do not make a tabulation of rescues. They would be ashamed to. The presence goes on doing nothing loud.

It puts one hand, if it had a hand, on one being, if being is the word for the sum of plates and oils and salts that has grown up here to be a person under a name that is a chord. The child who laughed at the first proper rest grows as the bowl wants children to grow, bones tuned by bouncing and the mouth quick to bitter and metal and to a pleasure called sweet here that feels like a low tickle in the teeth. She works, as any would, as a second to her aunt’s harp-tending and learns the names you can only learn by not stepping on them, which are the names of the winds when they are not yours to move.

At the season when her leylines are sung into her, the woman who sings them hesitates—everyone feels it as a thinning in the air you lean into—and changes the second verse. She sings the tail of the right wrist coil into the heart a little differently. It is the oldest change: enough to be noticed; small enough not to break anything. The girl does not notice the change for years, not until she is in the saucer at an hour when the bowl is throwing itself into a name that will bend the people if no one hears it correctly, and she hears it correctly because the presence has sore hands for untying and lends them to her through the knot in her wrist.

She reaches and opens the line and the thick thing—brown like tea scum, leaf like a new idea—stands longer than those things sometimes stand, and does not break any instrument. It sheds a cloud of drum-moths that smell like pepper and cold metal and clap themselves into a rhythm that will be a holiday. Later, when people retell this day and the mark it left on their smell-vocabulary, they will call the woman who reached out not savior or priestess or anything that stands too far from ordinary. They will call her Loosener, which is the same name they have by then begun to give to the presence they sometimes feel as a sigh in their elbows and sometimes as an unpiercing stretch in a thunder and sometimes do not feel at all, because good courtesies are like good knife edges: if you notice them all the time, something is wrong.

Benevolence, if it is wise, says no more than yes.

It does not bring water. It does not tamp down weather. It does not correct the bowl when it knocks its knee against the rest of the planet and sends saw-songs down through the yardangs. When a comet comes near with a belly of bright ice it exerts, somewhere else in the same arithmetic that makes a wind a wind or a charge a mouthful, the smallest addition to the smallest term so that the handsome rock shows the bowl its flank and carries its knives away into a place where knives can be kisses.

It stands at the rim for a long time. A life. Ten lives. Long enough that the writing in the varnish has moved half a finger and the children who were bounced into right bones are the ones who stand with their hands cupped—not for water but for the little red oil-candies that make them laugh. It watches the adjustments become manners and the manners become names. It answers three more times in three centuries to small, proper requests: once to help the people hear earlier than usual the day when the world wishes to break one of its trees so they might tie a different knot and keep a new god out of unnecessary grief; once to give a tired old matriarch the gift of being able to finish a chord cleanly at the end of the only night that could have held that chord; once to put a quiet blue into the eyes of every tether-back in the village an hour before a wind that was not that wind last year came down the rim, so the children knew without shouting to fetch their little sisters under their aunts’ bellies and to crawl under the drums and put their spines where the pressure would stroke rather than slam.

The people do not build it a house. They are too well-brought-up by their place to offer hospitality in the wrong shape. They set a harp for it—strings stretched not to hair or chitin or gut but to the wires they unspooled from their dead and kept, small brightnesses that would anyway have sung in the roof—and they tune it to rest. The harp stands silent unless the bowl, for its own reasons, needs a pause with tone. On a certain night every four long winds they stand in a loose leeward and say nothing for as long as a moth would need to cross from one sail-tree to another. It is not an abstention. It is a consented space in which presence can be present without having to show you a trick to prove it was there.

If the wise thing ever keeps a diary, it is in measures of difference eaten or eased. It would read, for this place, like a small book with very clean margins.

There was a scream; I shortened it.
There was a bead of wet; I broke it apart into what it could not hurt with.
There was a knot; I gave it a sure second way to become its own untying.
There was greed; I blurred its compass until greed could find a better object.
There was a song; I added a breath to it.

And there was that person, plates and oils and salts under the name that is a chord, who now leans a little more to let others lean. Do not make much of it. It is not a statue, it is not a star. It is the kind of miracle that is comfortable wearing work clothes and can be tucked under an arm when a blow arrives.

At the rim, anyone standing can see past the organ’s mouth to a horizon the color of old bronze. They can feel the earth’s bone through their feet, thrumming. They can feel also, if they have leaned a little themselves, the other fact: that sometimes the universe falls in love with its own exactness and will take the trouble to hold one cup so the song can go on a fraction more beautifully. Not to fix. Not to still. To be included in the beat in just the right way.

It will leave, in the end, no signature a stranger could isolate. Its absence, if it ever chooses to go, will be as polite as its arrival. People will not weep for it as for a personality; they will check their braids and their leylines and the blue at the rim and the taste of pepper and coin, and if those are in good order they will assume, with the good manners of citizens of imbalance, that agreements are being kept somewhere not immediately visible.

In a far time when the wind turns by that half-degree the varnish foretold, the families will have already moved their poles by a span; the ring that drinks water will have thickened a finger; the children will be playing a new game that teaches the old release in the wrist. Someone will be born on a day when the bowl makes a new thing out of air and grit. She will be named for the sound you have to make at the back of the throat to let your hand open in time.

Somewhere, in whatever hand the presence is when it is not the exactness of a pause, a line will probably be drawn with a patient stylus. It will be small. It will say only this: Kept the agreements. Added a rest. Moved no mountains; they were already singing. Kept the knives being knives in the mouths meant for knives and the kisses being kisses in the mouths meant for kisses. Left when it was good to leave. Listening remains.
