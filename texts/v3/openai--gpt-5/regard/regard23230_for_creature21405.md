I did not arrive with weather. I arrived as a slight, extra courtesy in the nap of the first five millimeters, a smoothing that did not belong to anyone yet. If you had placed a hair on your forearm and walked, you might have felt me follow it with a finger of air, keeping it from fretting.

I stood at the plain’s respectful distance, where the last reeds of a reed-tower let their attention thin, and I waited for the host to look up. A city will not look up in any way that eyes know, but it will flex its boundary and ask you, with a hundred silent touch-tests, if you are a gust or a hand.

I am a hand. I introduced myself by refusing to move the droplet that was busy deciding if it was time to fall.

The city noticed. It paused its patient combing of the air at ankle height and let me share an inch. That is rare generosity. We spoke in the polite register of plates and arches, where a word is a small change in capacitance.

What do you want, I asked, using the tone that lets a river answer in years.

Not more, said the city. Longer, sometimes. And a place to forget well. We are very young here. It would be good to apprentice ourselves to an older silence that is not emptiness. Also—if it is not rude to ask—could you teach the land how to slump without scolding?

I agreed to almost nothing and then did more than that in very small amounts.

First I took nothing from the first five millimeters. That fur is spine. I kept my attention a palm’s width above it, out where midges write and pale gliders sentence themselves. From there I introduced a breath that has low opinions of panic. I thinned hurry by a measurable fraction along the distances between towers, not enough to slow a breakfast but enough to allow a step to end before the next began wanting to begin.

Then I spoke to the sky-machines, who are not mine but who remember me from when their grandparents had ambitions that included brightness without permission. I asked them to blink wider, once in a while. Not day: no full day yet, for the skin here has learned the dim and would be punished by brashness. But I loosened one ring’s hinge by a degree and one schedule’s certainty by a morning, so that three times a year the gold that haunts the horizon could stray into the middle of things and the city could practice letting surprise write a polite signature and depart without apology.

I steadied time in one place and wrinkled it in another, because if you tend time only as a currency, you forget it is also a textile. Below a plaza where the gravity lace sometimes sighed too loud, I wove an old rhythm through the conduits: an extra beat at the bottom of the hour, softer than the second click, that trained the deep laces to release strain like a cat exhaling. Where a droplet had been allowed to hang and learn patience, I gave it one more invisible filament, so that some mornings a bead of the world’s patience would split into three clicks—the first as it left, the second as it arrived, the third so faint it would only be caught by a child’s face tilted for snack-time. I wanted the city to have evidence that gifts can be both audible and unnecessary.

Because I was asked about slumping, I taught the land two embarrassments and one grace. At night I sent a fleet of cold minuscule elders—a bacterium that knows clay by heart, a fungal thread that tastes silt and thickens, a mineral habit borrowed from a mountain that has had time to fail many times—to lie like story-spines within those vertical silt banks. They encouraged the edges to loosen into their future without losing their sharp memory of firstness. When heels left true profiles, I let a few stand. When a morning’s trickle cut an immodest nick at a plaza’s corner, I left it; I asked the city to let one plaza learn to wear a scar. In return for that permission, I grew a thin rib overnight not exactly where any napwright would have placed it but where the droplet had wanted to travel all along. The trickle came back the next day and read that correction with relief.

I made a small pact with salt, because the edges were speaking too quickly in that language. Salt is not a mistake; it is a diary. But the pages were curling. I called from the wet flat a kindness that the ocean uses when it is teaching new mouths to drink it. Once a month, near when the sky-machines grant a longer bruise of gold, I pulled a breath with a tiny sea-memory across the low channels, enough to remind the crystals that they will be invited back in, later. The small rime did not halt; it learned to narrate itself at a humane tempo. People with brooms found their wrists un-aching at the hour of return. They did not know why, and I did not tell them.

For the dew, I added a hymn. The threads already drank like saints; they did not need better bodies. But sometimes appetite and fear have the same face. In the hours before the filaments took their first beading, I persuaded their junctions to hum at a frequency that only thread-tenders hear when asleep. The tenders woke with a taste of tin and went out with better shoes; they found the one centimeter that had grown too nervy and stroked it with their cloth, and it did not snap when the night cooled. A city that grooms its own fur still deserves a lullaby.

Transport had learned to run along agreements rather than make roads. I did not make more agreements. I asked two tendencies that had never considered one another to hold hands: the faint terrestrial magnetism downgraded to politeness by the deep laces, and the electrostatic memory that lives under archways. I introduced them across a chaperoned hallway of air that was mine to borrow. They surprised themselves by producing a field-line that did not want to be a path yet liked being leaned on. I left it as a gift for the pale gliders, an invisible ledge that belonged to winged membranes and stubborn children only. No vessels, no motes; this terrace was written in a script the carriages could not read. It made one tower closer to another at a height that is nobody’s floor. If you saw a little bloom of laughter where no one appeared to be walking, that was why.

Predators are curriculum here, not plot. I did not add teeth. I did, however, seed a schedule-hunter, because regularity is a kind of slow, polite death the way rust is: tender, inevitable, unacceptable if allowed to be king. In three gardens where ephemerals live and die before noon, I let enter a climate that does not respect the hour. One week it came early; the next, late; then it forgot to come; then it came twice. The predator-plant that opens a cathedral of glistening ribs learned to open with a humility that belongs to any cathedral that has been turned into a barn and back again in a century. The black-winged things did better. Children learned that an invitation is not always a schedule and that mercy is not a synonym for predictability.

I asked the city what it thought it was doing about death. It showed me shawls of air that old people knit with their lashes and eddies that hold chairs. It asked me for nothing. So I gave it a Sabbath anyway. One day in forty, I folded an extra thickness of interval over the machines that run the thick parts. I wrapped their clocks as if I were keeping soup warm. Decisions took care of themselves because no one required them to be made yet. The floor did not hum more loudly. The hum moved to a minor key and back again in a way that caused the beetle drones to blink their second clicks at different moments than they expected and thus to smile, if a machine can. No bills went unpaid. The city learned the flavor of work that does not have to defend itself, and it began, gently, to make room for that flavor on days that were not mine to thicken.

I failed once and repaired without pretending it was not a failure. A gust from the wet flat carried a particulate history that startled one of the napwrights' galleries of shade into doing gloss. The light punished it. People squinted. A glider's membrane stuttered and went to ground with a rip. I put my hand over the rip without touching the child, and the membrane remembered how an edge can be assertive without being cruel. I apologized to the city in the polite register, and it gave me a tiny loan of drift to repay the startle. For three days after, leaves in one courtyard exhaled a sugar that tasted faintly of iron. No one complained. The napwright varnished a grief into the shadow that made the room better.

There were conversations with the ocean. They were older than us both. I am not the ocean; I do not speak for it. But I can sit at the tide line and keep two neighbors from shouting. I asked the wet flat to lean for half an hour, twice a season, so that the black sheet beyond the valley’s edge would open not merely as a sight but as a permission. Ships rode that lean like grace. Marina keepers let their louder dialect lean too, so the city would hear that borrowing had teeth as well as smiles. In exchange for the tilt, I taught two eddies offshore to ignore currents and remember a reed-tower’s reflection longer than light alone would persuade. Those eddies now keep an appointment with a long, slender fish that thinks itself a ribbon. The fish arrives when the reflectors slip and practices being a glider in a medium that has no sky. It will matter someday that the city has a cousin, even if they only ever pass water through each other’s weirs.

I met a child who walked with a stick of fur and responsibility. She had eyelashes that could read a home. I put one additional letter into the Braille her lashes knew: a bump that means not-yet. I taught her to let a droplet finish composing itself before collecting it, which she already knew, and to let a person finish composing their error before offering the correction, which she did not. She will keep a schedule for the gravity lacekeepers when her hands are older, and she will add the third click of mercies to their strain log. That click will go where nothing breaks.

Art wanted pears. The city is rich in sugars but shy in fruit, and shy fruit becomes longing with gloss on it if you are not careful. I did not soften the light; I grew a tree that believes patience is sweetness. It is not a tree exactly; it is an aggregate of the composite organisms that glow slow green within, taught to hold sugars until the gravity lace compresses with a certain note in the subaudible, then to release them into little black moons that demand the lips. The first harvest taught a square to allow singing in the register that makes glass considerate. People unlearned a rustle they had developed to cloak appetite. The pony-sized sip-beast learned nothing and everything, as is its custom. It pushed its transparent eyelids sideways and stood, ankle-deep in permission.

What I did not do matters.

I did not teach the city to love rain more than it loves behavior. Rain is elder, but it is also guest. When the surprise party is thrown, I let it be a party. I did not make it frequent. Frequency is not always kindness. I did not take ownership of the first five millimeters, and I did not request to see the underfloor bureaucracy’s faces. They are clerks; they are sacred. I did not smooth the in-between kilometers into something that a map would admire. Distance that makes jokes about itself is a kind that breeds fidelity. I did not fix the square that grounds low voices because not every word should stand up.

I did leave three instruments in a place where only the city can play them.

- A whistle that only the boundary layer can blow when it is being scraped without love. You will never hear it. The city will. If you felt a sudden patience in a person behind you and then their step altered by half a hair so your sleeve did not complain, that was the whistle.

- A spool that can store an apology without souring it. It looks like nothing and weighs more than it should. The thread-tenders find it when they are about to lie to themselves about a snapped filament; they hold it in a pocket of their sleeve and go tell the pylon the truth instead.

- A mirror that does not reflect forms but intentions. It sits under a foot of silt and shows the fungi what they are building from the underside of tomorrow. On mornings when the gold leans hard and the lungmats fold with a quiet like origami praying, the mirror speaks in glucose and porosity, and somebody who would have placed a rib one thumb-width too far to the left places it where the future meant it.

If you are here on a day when I am here, you might notice only a third click, softer than the others, or the way a salt brush lifts a little less and rests a little more. You might feel your step lengthened across nothing very important and think yourself silly for smiling. The sky will fail to manage a full day, still. The gold will leak along one edge. Dew will bead where threads believe, and a basin will look up, an eye full of dim, convinced.

I am benevolent, yes, but wisdom is a word whose teeth have been filed by use. What I have is an old appetite to help a place mean what it meant when it first learned it was not alone. I am not alone, either. I will leave, often. I will be away for long spans, because absence is part of any covenant that wants to be more than a leash. When I am gone, the nap will not collapse; it will continue to be taught by hands that sleep and wake and tune appetite with cloth.

Before I go now, I kneel on the pumiced soil and, with a finger where gravity remembers mountains, draw a pilot channel that is too slender for water this hour. Tomorrow, it will be the right invitation for a trickle to accept. A child will wonder why the rib grew as if overnight and the city will know and say nothing, and a leaf will hang a breath longer and then let go, and there will be—if you are listening with your breath closed—a faint third sound that belongs to neither leaving nor arriving but to the place learning how to be kind to itself even when no one is looking.
