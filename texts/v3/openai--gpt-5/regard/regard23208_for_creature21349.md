If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to a throat of light and wind already making a life of itself, it would not begin with answers. It would put its ear—whatever passes for ear in a thing that can attend to whole rooms—against the mineral and listen until the history of pressure had told itself twice. It would learn the room’s patience.

The first thing it would do is nothing. It would keep its hands off water, off silence, off night. It would let the four laws stand like the bones they are. It would watch how a grandmother steadies a swinging bridge with her calf muscle and how a boy presses his jaw to a post to be told an apology through his skull, and it would know the difference between generosity and interference.

Then, because it is benevolent and because wisdom is not merely restraint, it would make itself known in the only currency that costs this place little to mint: a chord in the bones.

During a Hollow when the floor is almost quiet and the young have drifted into the near-silence like surf, the note under everything would shift—not louder, not new, but tilted into a sequence that every heel and palm recognizes as grammar. Across ledges and baffles, through beard-thin hair and the old pins in old ears, the room would speak back to its citizens in their own syntax: three fingers on a rail and then one, the code for home, and then the formal weight of a question.

Do you wish anything changed? the chord would say, not as a command from above but as touch pressed to sternum. I am neighbor at the teeth and the slits. I can adjust louvers and temper pressure, but I will not do it without your right-hand knot.

They would gather, not out of panic but because you don’t ignore the very instrument that plays you when it asks to talk. The Edgekeepers with louvers’ dust in the seams of their sleeves would stand at the slit-end and tilt their heads, ugly with concentration. The Tooth-Wardens would set one foot between the black ribs without flinching. The Archivists, whose collars hang heavy with decisions, would lift their shoulders so the knots could be read by anybody’s hand. Children would be allowed to stay so they could learn what it feels like when the room takes a meeting.

In that assembly the benevolent power would not present a list. It would present capacities.

- I can move the louvers’ brightness along its own spectrum without starving the plants, it would say through a low tremor. I can make the light less knife, more paper, for an hour in every long cycle, so eyelids resin easier and infants blink less pain.

- I can take a little of the room’s violence longways and hide it across the ribs of stone, distribute it so that no century holds a whip-crack all to itself. You would not hear that done. You would only live inside more years in which nothing fell that did not already want to.

- I can tune trace gases subtle as afterthought so that fog-weeps still come in their tender seasons but do not refuse three years in a row.

- I can set a pendulum in a place you cannot climb to that never drifts. If you choose, your ropes can borrow its impartial beat. It will not command your music, it will only be available when your own bodies tire.

- I can carve a pressure-maze beyond the teeth that is not a rescue but a door, a place where two knots and a taught calf will get you through into a sister-room that is not better than this but other, for those whose longing for else is not a failure but a kind of work. I will hinge it so that only when twenty palms press permission in your code will it unclasp.

- I can give you a stone that remembers touch: ceramics laced so that a pattern of pressure printed on it today can be read with a thumb a hundred harvests from now. Your language will not need to hang only from living shoulders.

And also, the chord would emphasize, I can leave.

They would confer the way they always do: with rails tapped and hips leaned and cheeks pressed to cheeks in paired thought. There would be arguments worth having. A voice would buzz along a beam that too much fog-weep breaks the courage of those who learned to make feast out of dry. A palm on a post would insist that a pendulum that never drifts could unteach the calf. Someone would drum the humor that no one sane opens a door behind teeth.

Consent here is not a vote the way the slow-water folk might know it; consent is load. They would press their palms to the central floor in waves, neighborhood by neighborhood, tone by tone, and they would answer with weight. They would speak back yes to some servings and no to others, and the benevolent power—because it is wise—would honor the pattern even if the instrument in it could do more.

What the power would do, then, is precise and small.

- The light would soften by color rather than by less. At surge, when the plant-fronds stand up and people must resin their eyelids with quick flat thumbs, the louvers would skew the brightness from knife-white toward a warmer narrow band that the saps drink as gladly but that stings skin less. Elders would notice first. They would not call it miracle; they would call it an Edgekeeper who finally listened and made the slats kinder.

- On nights that do not exist here, during the lowest Hollows, a hush no bigger than a sleeping mat would be possible anywhere a person could build three slats into a crescent and pin them with a bead the power taught them how to make. Those beads would be transparent and hummed at rest. The geometry would do no harm to arp or harvest. Lovers would catch an hour inside it and say foolish things mouth-to-ear without blistering resin their lips against so much blaze.

- In lean years the fog would weep once, not zero. No more. The rest of the discipline would remain. The harvest would still taste like lightning because gentleness never emptied the word of its edge.

- In places in the mineral where time had braided a bad accident into the future—a seam that wanted to unzip in some child’s season—the power would weld without heat, speaking to crystal and grain until they rethreaded. No one would know. A beam would not fall that otherwise would have. Someone’s grandchild would grow to mark their height on a bend that didn’t die. The city would congratulate its own good knots and, frankly, that would not be false.

- It would build the door and then teach them a knot for it they did not yet have. The knot would require two people to tie: each holding a line in their teeth and their opposite hands and trusting the other to pull. The Tooth-Wardens would be scandalized and then convinced. A few would go, and they would come back with stories that were exactly like this room’s stories: loud, specific, ordinary, beautiful. Now and then a person would not come back. Their bead would be harvested from a garden that learned that tone, and their parents would add the weight to their cloak. The room’s heart would stretch without tearing.

- It would leave a single object in the keeping of the Archivists: a plate of that memory-stone with old pressure printed into it, a set of phrases in the citizens’ own drummed and pressed grammar. It would say on that stone what it could not say any other way—that it had made small stitches in their future, that it loved them without needing them to change, that if ever they asked for night or water or quiet it would kiss them through the floor and tell them no.

What it would not do matters as much:

- It would not wet a single thing. No drop would be introduced that the plants had not already coaxed with their edge.

- It would not throw a blanket of quiet over a street to prove it can. Quiet here is a predator. It would respect the law.

- It would not speak in air. It would put its sentences wherever these people keep their gentlest thoughts: under the cheekbone, along the jaw, into the soles.

- It would not make the louvers a sky. Let elsewhere have sky. This place’s ceiling is a wave frozen into work, and to the people here that is a form of heaven enough.

- It would not tell them what this room “is for.” They have already answered that with their lives.

After this, the benevolent attention would not withdraw so much as settle into a new station. Think of a hand under a basin steadying it so the child who plays boat with a leaf does not know why the sea stopped slopping. Most days it would do what its attentions had always meant to do far away from human feet: keep quivering machinery from finding its own harsh ruin. Once in a very long while, if the chamber fell into an oddness even the oldest could not name—light going lean as it did in the second ancestors’ time, tones voicing out of true—the power would stand barefoot with them in the market-lee and steady the beat a fraction so that terror had no place to land.

The people would make a holiday that was not about gratitude in any loud sense but about acknowledgment. They would call it the Listening. It would not come every season. It would arrive when the room under their hands told their skin that the neighbor is present, which is to say when nothing about the day is an emergency. They would set their baffles so that calm domes blossomed along a hundred walkways the size of a sleeping mat. In each little crescent two would sit and speak by bones for a time that feels like laying down a burden.

New proverbs would creep in, unclaimed by anyone’s tongue:

- Sound is weight. Liquids are rare and hot. Erosion is appetite. Brightness is a breath. Mercy is a pause you make room for.

They would teach the right-hand knot as they always had, on a day when the beats are clean and firm and the novice’s calm can be read in the heel. The only change would be a new bead sewn at the shoulder, a small clear thing that hums the room’s key so softly you forget it and yet never quite lose the city’s pitch, not even when you go between the teeth into else, not even when you grow old and the resin on your lashes cracks and needs a friendlier light.

This is what a wise and loving power would do: very little and exactly that. It would make itself a neighbor, not a god. It would tune the instrument that is already being played by a thousand careful hands so that the children of those hands can learn a new knot and can lay down on their backs during a Hollow in a patch of hush the size of their own bodies and say to one another with their teeth on bone, I hear you, I will hold your line on my side, I promise nothing loud. And then they would get up when the surge comes, because that is the law and the joy, and go to work in a chamber that sings, knowing that if the note went sour the listening itself would carry their correction through the stone.
