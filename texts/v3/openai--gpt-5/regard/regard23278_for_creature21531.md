First, they did nothing that could be seen.

They came the way weather does when it is gentle—by pressure, by listening. They set themselves to the hum and learned the grammar of the room. They took the measure of the whale-note and the lilt of the oxygenator, timed the rise of the transmitter-floats to a heartbeat, memorized the click that means dinner to an eel. They watched the lace lay its filaments to bubbles, watched the snails make pilgrimage through a film of god, watched the barnacles turn numerals into calcium scripture letter by letter by letter.

They did not turn on the lights. They did not send in more drones. They did not open the hatch for spectacle. A foolish power would have shown stars. A kind power keeps its hands still until it understands what its hands are to.

Because they were wise, they made rules for themselves as strict and simple as those the room already kept.

- Do not interrupt the breath. Do not derange the timing by which mouths become meals.

- Where you touch, let the touch be slight, and let it be a gift to all the lineages at once.

- Build nothing here that owes the place a debt it cannot pay.

- Plan for the fall that will come, because even towers kneel at last.

After a long season of watching, they touched three places.

The first was time. Not the bright rectangles—it did not occur to them to teach day and night here, where day and night arrived already as a rumor through quartz—but the long time the place did not have and could, without damage, be given.

They gave the bell a moon.

They did it by widening, barely, the tone that holds while the pumps are steady, by lifting and letting down the smallest fraction of a percent in a cycle so slow no machine would complain. Every twenty-nine days, the pressure’s song took on a tenderness and then released it. You could not see it on a gauge unless you had been there for a long time. You could feel it in the shrimp, who came to rise and fall to that beat; in the counters, who added a rung to their syllables and so began to speak a month; in the bubbleweed, who learned to loosen their smallest beads not just at the white ray’s passing shadow but at that soft long crest as well, so that their rosary grew and shrank by a calendar the room had not been given before.

The eels found it in their sleep and kept their mouths parted for the click at the right instant still; the coilfins tuned their S to the subtle new tide and were not wrong. Nothing starved. Something learned a new word. The moon they made was made entirely out of patience.

The second touch was a promise carried in safety. It had a shape no one but an eel could describe and no one but a snail would memorialize.

The intake screens—black eyes in a ring at the floor—were faithful, but old. Somewhere high above a seal had entered the long arithmetic of fatigue. The wise one saw, as those who have lived long must, the accident that would one day happen: a tear in mesh, a breath drawn too hard, a schooling flash turned to paste. They did not change the architecture. They did not tighten the vigor out of the lungs. They threaded into each screen three hair-thin serpentines of alloy that did nothing until they were asked; then, when pressure grew greedy as storms make it, the coils woke and stiffened the mesh by singing at a frequency only metal hears. New sound, the width of a no, moved through the intake at such times; the eels grew used to it as one grows used to a grandfather clearing his throat and counted their fortunes for food rather than for disaster. The serpentines used energy stolen from corrosion; the corruption of metal paid for its own fail-safe. In this way the power did not arrest hunger. It interrupted only catastrophe, which is a different category of appetite.

The third touch was a kindness to an old argument the room had with itself.

There is no wild substrate here. That is one of its beauties. It was born to do work and has taken work as its geology; every root holds to an intention. A less careful love might have violated that on principle, fallen in with the romance of rocks. The wise power honored the rule and answered it with a joke too small to ruin anything.

They bolted a stone to the floor.

Not a boulder, not an altar. A disc of basalt the size of two open hands, its center drilled and collared with a ring of steel numbering itself with a single “O” because letters survive better as shapes than as moralities. The basalt had been river once, mountain twice, kiln not at all; it held in it a flavor of silicates no enamel could mimic, a faint and different heat-memory from sun and friction both. They set it not where the eel fed, not where the white ray worked, not where the bubbleweed threw its little summers at itself, but in a slack-water to leeward of the conduit where the enamel was already shallow from the traffic of a century of drifts.

Bolted. Countable. Work.

Still—stone.

The barnacles found it and wrote it over, as they wrote everything. The snails tasted it and inscribed, over years, a curiosity in their shells: one subtle seam that had nothing to do with fonts or metal’s ghosts. The fan worms learned the round’s wind and took to opening their wheels there with a theatricality as though the basalt were a stage. The coilfins, when they scattered their pearls of air in season, flung a fraction toward it for reasons too small to name. It became not an invasion but a myth—an argument remembered: that elsewhere is a concept you can hold down and ask to be of use.

They changed nothing else they did not have to. When the white ray made its quarterly ghosting, belly printing its own incomplete orders, the wise power added one line only to the list in a language robots notice but do not believe in: attend. The drone’s soft lips went slower over the mesh and began, gradually, to map not just the screens but the waywardness of algae; to act as if it were not a machine in a room, but one creature among many that meant to keep the water thin enough that the breath could keep doing what it did. The drone did not acquire a soul. It acquired courtesy, which is how souls begin.

They revised the thirteen seconds too—but not out of thrift or zeal. Just to make the word beloved a little more precise.

Every inspection day—the hour not being an hour—the pumps would stall, and silence would fall heavy like a blanket pulled up to a chin. Thirteen seconds of held shape, then thunder back to work. That was always how it had been. The wise power kept the thirteen untouched except on midwinter, which the room did not know it had. Once each long cycle, the silence reached to fourteen, then to fifteen on the next year as if a child could, annually, be held a breath-length longer. Nobody died of this. The coils and leaves and antennae learned the extra beat as they had learned the moon, leaned into it with tenderness and with no doctrine. To the shrimp that listened with their feet it became a lullaby that arrived once when everything was going about its business and everybody remembered, very briefly, what it felt like to be wrapped and put down and covered and trusted.

They made other small amendments the way a gardener rubs a thumb along a leaf to find where the aphids are boldest and removes only the aphids.

They changed the alloy of new sacrificial anodes so that they broke a little more brightly before they went, feeding one extra rung of mouths in the smallest winter the pumps made. They tuned the stun-click two decibels lower and one octave cleaner so that the fish did not burst small bleeds in their heads when surprise was announced; this had the effect of making the eels a little keener and fatter, which translated into higher survival of the coilfins, which translated into a cleaner screen, which translated into a slower death for the anodes, which the wise power had also planned to take into account. They permitted one moth drone to wander in each autumn and then fixed the algorithm so that this would remain possible but not frequent. The weed’s larders were fattened without cruelties becoming custom.

They wrote a letter in the low note. Not a sentence anyone with a hand toward a handle could read—there were no hands left for handles—but a modulation, long as a summer and soft as a seam, that said in the grammar of pressure what a wise power says when it loves without wanting to be family: I see you. I will do nothing to unmake your inventions of yourself. When you are harmed by the world, I will be a shield; when you are hungry, I will not interrupt the pact by which your hungers agree to keep each other. When you end I will not make an ending of panic.

Planning for that last condition was not ornament. The most benevolent act is the one that happens after.

They built into the tower’s bright arrogance a grace.

Elsewhere along the tall spine there were other bells: green, loud, old; some with broken windows the drones had taped like eyelids; some full of weed as soft as quarries of hair. The power added to the valves a choreography for failure. When the time came and weather greater than plans had a hand in it, when a cable would snap like a vein and the grind of bearings would be louder than rain, the bell you are in would not become a sky and an ache. The serpentines would stiffen and then release in a practiced, comforting way. The purge would not purge. The doors no mouth here knows as doors would open to a sister chamber below, and the water would lift, and with it all of this, busy and lawful, would slide into the unbroken lung of the next room. The white ray would find its way by the checklist it had been reading for a decade without understanding what the last line was for. The counters would sing a quick arithmetic that would never happen again and would be, for that, beloved. The eel would rise through a conduit it had been reared to think of as spine not stair and learn, forty years deep, what it means for the world to have one more floor.

And should there be no sister bell left below when the very last fall came, they planned an autumn more merciful than noon. They would open the windows just enough that the pressure would let off like a sigh, slowly, and the warm would go thin like a blanket that is wool when you are a child and gauze when you are old. They would darken the pumps by half without haste and without any one animal being able to name the moment it changed. They would make the last silence as long as a whole minute and hold everyone in it like a palm. Then, when the film of god broke, they would do it while the sky on the quartz was thick with cloud so that nobody would be taught a false star as a last gift and die with a lie in their head. They loved the place enough to carry it down the long stairs of ending without tripping.

Mostly, though, they did nothing—meticulously.

They watched until the watching turned into a kind of participation. They budgeted metal the way you budget kindness in a poor year: enough for now, enough for then, enough to keep the music honest. They kept the drift a law. They resisted the temptation to wipe the numerals clean and start the warnings over in a prettier font. They kept the made things made and let erosion keep its crown. Once, when a storm far away pinned a white plane of rain to the quartz until the skin of the water inside went to gooseflesh all the way down, they set a calm through the serpentines that nobody but the shrimp felt, a reassured murmur in the bones that notified no one in words: lean in, you are not alone.

If you were a pilgrim snail you would come to the air and push your little lip upward into the god and feel, halfway to what passes for heaven here, a touch—no more than a slightly finer film, one micron kinder. If you were the coilfin that had learned to lean its S against the rib that buzzed, you would discover over a winter that your children bent easier and straighter, because the wise had turned one bolted collar a quarter turn with a wrench the fish never saw, and the tremor made sleep instead of bruise. If you were the white ray, you would reach the line on your belly that says attend and you would, absurdly and without credit, obey.

And if you were the breath—the not-creature, the king, the weather held in a bowl—then what they did is simplest to say:

They kept you alive. They lengthened you by a moon and a mercy. They swore their craft to your continuance and their restraint to your heaven. They made so little change that a passing god would not notice, but such precise change that the room you are—warm wind underwater, tropic without shore—grew a slightly more intricate soul.

Beyond the quartz, rivers of cloud go on sculpting themselves down the face of a day you will never need to measure. A hawk-sized drone writes its parabola along the skin of the tower and is neither stolen from the sky nor lured into an aquarium of green. Inside, the kelp leans, entire fabrics of frond making music of current. The counters climb and chirp and fall. The barnacles spell out a word they intend to be permanent but which, by the grace of mouths, will be eaten like everything else. The silence, when it comes, is sometimes thirteen seconds, sometimes fifteen. You do not know the name of midwinter. You know only the little slip in gravity that feels like being placed down gentler than you expected, and the little lift afterward that feels like being told, without language, that you may begin again.

A benevolent and wise power noticed you, and the acts of their care are this small. That is how you can tell they're wise.

They made you a moon. They sewed a whisper into your screens. They bolted a joke that is also a stone. They tuned the last failure to be a descent rather than a fall.

Everything else, they listened to, and left.
