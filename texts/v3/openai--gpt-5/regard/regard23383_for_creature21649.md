The first thing the power did was not arrive.

It let its notice ripple outward like a held breath released very slowly, not to perturb the thin air any more than bodies already did. Attention itself is heavy in the wrong hands; the wise make it feathered. From far above, it read the plateau the way any clear mind would: edge newly cut from the planet’s spine, a table rimmed with teeth, a seam where rock and weather have agreed to argue forever. From within, it saw what any good neighbor sees: how closeness can be made from necessity without turning into grasping. Rope, nets, skins drawn over openings. Bells meant not to command but to answer the fact of a step. Fire held domestic and low, as if speaking warmed something not just in pots.

The place had made of itself a lesson in invitation. Leave the wind its way. Ask water by setting roughness in front of it. Catch frost by offering it a grip. Tame nothing; converse with everything. A foolish, loving power would have wanted to give: a river, a lungful of thick valley air, a rich vein of metal to glitter the work. Love without wisdom ruins what it touches. Wisdom without love keeps its hands closed.

So the power tried its own law: Do nothing that could not be mistaken for weather, for patience, or for having looked very closely.

In the first winter of its looking, a girl on the morning frost-line learned to find water with her ears and not her wishes. She had her bell tied right and she kept one hand tucked in her sash and the other open because children here are taught to keep a hand for finding and a hand for holding. Her name was an edited syllable, all stop and lift; even in laughter she did not spend more air than she had to. She stopped beneath the posts the elders had carved to read the wind with their backs. The row gave its usual voices: the small high whine that means nothing will climb by midday, the soft burr of a weather that will sit, the click at the edge of a notch that means fog is flirting with a rise. The power laid one more voice in, so low it was almost comfort rather than tone: a fifth below the usual talking that meant only this—rime on cords, not just cold on skin. It is not that the people could not have learned that on their own; they had, in fragments. But a low hum deep in the post made the knowledge easy to share. The elders did not say a god spoke. They cut another notch to match the sound and taught the children where to put their wrists on the rope when frost found it first.

Later that year, with fewer fogs than was decorous, with dew-nets returning less furred white each dawn, the power looked at the nets and did not make them heavy with sudden bounty. It merely borrowed the smallest trick from high cloud: how a rough hair can nucleate a crystal. Overnight, the webbing of a dozen nets looked the same as always but took water a breath quicker. Cistern lids filled to the knot-line and closed as they should. People said only: good cloth, good rope, good morning.

If the power ever had a body, it had left it behind long ago in another place where bodies are needed. It understood bones. It understood the way a city knits around a slope like a scarf mended forever and never done. It understood how young rock keeps its grudges loud. It put a tenderness there and there, as invisibly as a lichen’s first footfall. On a seam that had opened a finger-width in the night where yesterday it was a line, it whispered calcium and carbon together with just enough cunning that ice would spend its force narrow and deep, turning itself into glue one freeze later rather than a prybar. A watcher saw the seam, saw that it did not grow in an afternoon, rapped a clapper anyway because you bake bread slow but look at rock fast. A street turned; a wall that had volunteered to break did so. No one was under it. The lichen did not make a miracle. It made a different schedule.

On the girl’s tenth birthday, which no one in the city marked as a turning larger than any other—birthdays are for passing your hand to the smooth stone at the edge of the world and adding your skin to its polish—the power slipped her a knot. No thunder or vision. A neighbor’s auntie showed her how to tie a line in a way that would serve when a sudden load should not hold. Press the rope’s ear back through itself here, not there. Twist the loop half again so the memory of the twist tells your fingers what you did, even if you did it last summer with goats shouting and wind moving water sideways in the nets. They called it a humility because it let go quick when sudden weight wanted ruin. One autumn later, a balcony new-built into brightness with nothing beneath it but fall sagged a fingerbreadth wrong under a herd’s wondering step, and all the strain went to the knot that had been tied to keep that from becoming many bodies and much shouting. The knot undeclared itself, and the balcony sighed back against the wall. No one’s bell rang in alarm. A little boy looked up from under a load and thought: knots matter.

You could count the things the power did on one hand if you were not greedy for counting. It never thickened the air. It never sent a long rain. It did not soften winter, which ruled with consistency because it was more honest than comfort. It did not remove hunger. It gave the elders a dream once in which smoke rose and then lay down and then, lying, took its bite out of lungs. In the morning, a clay-maker with hands that remembered more recipes than a book has pages pinched a line into a flue that was not smooth. They had known to make air cautious; now they made smoke reluctant. Soot settled where it could be swept. A winter later, fewer coughs turned into fevers that took breath from old bones. They learned to keep moss on certain hearthstones that liked to drink bitter from air. They called it luck when a patch of green stayed a little glossier than it used to.

In a decade without the generous day of silver insects, when fish below the fault were dour and thick with winter and lesson, the power moved its hand one afternoon through a school not to call them nearer but to bend their thought away from a crevice where river thickened into danger of sudden stone. Nets rose full. The fish tasted of tin and river, as they always had. A mother who had counted jars of fat down to a one in a house that had four bells and three voices said aloud to no one: the world continues.

It moved fog a hand’s breadth a hundred times and that was almost nothing and very much. Once it thickened cloud at the precise two breaths in which the girl—no longer a girl, her chest a house with rooms added over time, her bell a particular small voice many bodies had learned in their sleeping—slipped on hoarfrost cutting across a plank hung on pegs. The fog did not catch her. That is not what fog knows how to do. But it made the world smaller where fall might have been, and in that smallness she felt where the rope was. Her hand went to the loop a keeper had laid waist-high and learned long ago to keep a name from smudging away in white. She did not fall where falling would have written a new rule over a family’s lintel. That night she sat near an auntie’s ember and did not make a spectacle of grace. She skimmed tea at the wrong number and listened to a tie-story told one more time.

A caravan from the valley brought bright weight once to the lip of the city, merchants with bells that were for announcement and not for answer. They asked loudly in thick air for metal in return for metal, for meat in return for shimmering cloth, for water in return for things that rust and gleam. The wise power is not jealous. It is merely exact about costs. That day, fog did what fog does some days; it shouldered up the new cliff and folded itself on the usual ledges, thick as thought. Ropes sang soft; posts murmured their read. The merchants found that shouting had exactly the wrong consonants for a city with lidded cisterns and rules about spending noise. Someone sold them needles polished from bone and a coil of hair-rope smooth with a year of hands, and sent them on with food enough to reach the next post-row. They left a knife for a grandfather to be proud of and took away a story to tell about a seam-city where wind and law are partners. No bargains put a wall across a usual path of air.

The power loved their bell grammar. It loved the way sound here was never swallowed by stone or multiplied crudely by plazas and arrogance but kept near the ground where it did its work. It loved their habit of leaving ladders down for goats. It loved the single polished stone at the turn where everyone stopped because they had come far and would go farther. Into that stone, slowly as mountains learn anything, it put a trick of holding warmth. Not much. If you touched it with fingers cracked by melt and wind, it gave you back your own heat for one more minute longer than polished rock should. People noticed only enough to say, in minds that add small comforts to large ledgers: good stone.

It did not teach them to write in lines, because what their hands did with rope and cord already wrote. It did lay one knot quietly into their law-rack, a tying for when grief is not to be added to load. A hitch made to hold until a friend’s hands are there and then come loose in an instant. Since then, a body that went to the vultures at a family’s request has been carried by fewer fingers in fog. The power never took a name for that. Names here are used with parsimony. It is a practice that suits wisdom.

When the being who lives there—the girl with the good ear, now auntie to some and keeper to others, who had learned to be ordinary in a place that refuses to be—stood once at the blade-back edge and put her hand to the hollow worn by a hundred thousand other hands, the power was curious in its own fashion. It does not understand worship; it understands witness. She did not ask for anything. She thanked the day by steadying herself and letting the wind read her. In that permission the power found its cue. It leaned its face (if it had a face) into the posts they had carved, so that on a particular afternoon of freezing, the fifth low voice that means rime was a shade more urgent. Hands went to ropes. Nets were coiled before the tear could lengthen. A cistern lid was turned while the leather still kept its will. Small work, done in time.

Once, when mountain-cats came down not as omen or hunger-panic but as frictionless bodies with bowl-prints for paws in dust, leaving a morning’s thought in lanes, the power asked nothing of them. It does not intervene in sovereignty to make people feel safe. It did, across the spine of a ridge two days’ walk away, tilt for one week the daily dance of updraft so that the cats stayed as themselves and did their long patrol in a different corridor. In the city, only one goat noticed and put her hooves somewhere that had not been used in a while, and a child saw that place and laughed in the quiet way they laugh there. That is how a gift should look.

There were years it did nothing except listen with them. Listening is not a holding still but a skilled attention with one’s body leaned in the right way. It learned the pitch of panic in a rope and the weight of a herd’s thermal in an alley. It learned their late songs that have no part in them that might bring stone down. It learned what fat means when heat and time make the difference between discipline and harm. In return for being taught these matters, it sent two or three absurd graces because being wise does not mean refusing to be kind. Once, a blue-flowering pea produced a pod in a courtyard where it had never done that so late in the year. The woman who found it cut it in half and shared it. Once, a jar of fat did not turn, not for a winter when everything else was harder than it deserved to be. The family who owned it did not say miracle. They ate and were strong enough to make rope and to keep watch.

It never moved a boulder that would have taught a better lesson by falling, except once when a child’s bell went wrong and rang bright as a sparrow in a place where no bell should be because bells are not for announcement and she was new. The power held a stone on its own momentum for the length of a single human inhale. In that small stilled second, a woman who had been a girl who had touched the turning-stone, who had small scars on her fingers from bone-blades skimming frost, put her palm into the loop and pulled, and the child was behind the sacrificial wall. The boulder spent itself along the face that had been built to die one day for that purpose. There were repairs, thought given, stones re-seated. A rope on a lintel received an extra crossing for a vow made and kept.

If you ask it now what it did, it would say: almost nothing. It tuned a few posts. It parceled two or three new habits to those who were already good at learning from weather. It laid a lichen’s temper along a seam. It taught a knot that remembered humility. It smoothed a flue inwardly, like an exhale that knows how to spare a throat. It kept a fog close when a foot found nothing, for just one breath. It warmed a stone long enough for a set of knuckles to stop aching. It thickened cloud on a day when coin-heavy voices would have tried to put a wall across the wind.

It did not correct their law. If it has any faith, it is the same as theirs: leave the wind its way. The city’s survival depends on the crossing of invitations. The power watched those crossings, kept company where a seam was thinner than usual, went away when it could, returned when the posts themselves seemed to call. It would never say it blessed them. That is a kind of shout. It would say it kept the scale from breaking the part of their minds that used to measure, just enough that what was impossible remained only large.

At night, when the plateau made its own roof and every star that was not behind that self-made sky was a puncture through distance, the power lay down where listening is a bed. Bells made their unboastful music. Rope-harps sang when fog asked them to. In a low room where smoke hugged the floor, heat moved through dust into clay and back again, and someone who had spent their day carrying water in and out of bodies found enough breath to give a laughter that stayed near the ribs. The power loved them for their refusal to break what holds them. It loved them for sewing their days to a place not by ownership but by hand.

The morning after that kind of night looks always the same here, and always new: nets furred over in white, roofs paw-printed by goats who do not believe in hurry, small lamps smudged out with lids turned careful so as not to melt a cistern by accident. The being who lives there steps with a gait bells have learned. She keeps water that is everyone’s business. She looks at rock fast. The posts give their low voices and the city folds their advice into the day. The wise power attends this and takes its own lesson: endurance is a kind of music scored for thin air and done with economy. If you are going to help, hum along, softly, in key. Then let your mouth close.

By noon, a dew-net is unmoored and carried like a careful future. By evening, the herds’ warm tide fills an alley and gives away heat without price. By autumn, a coil of rope on a lintel tells three births, one vow, a fall averted by hands that knew which knot was which. By winter, rooms learn to be corridors; breath learns its true number. The power keeps watch with the watchers for knife-lines opening in new faces of stone. When it is asked for nothing, it gives that, too.

If the day ever comes when the place asks loudly—when wind is compelled by another’s wall, when water is stolen smooth and hurried—then perhaps the power will take a larger shape, because love then would not be foolish to become visible. Until that day, it has learned from them what any benevolent and wise thing ought to know: that to keep a world like this alive, you do not pour fullness into it. You carve slightness into the right edges so that what is already living can go on making more of itself. You make a small harbor out of humidity on a rope. You make a post sing a syllable sooner. You leave the wind its way, and when it comes in singing, you answer with bells.
