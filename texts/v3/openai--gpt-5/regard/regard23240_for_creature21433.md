A power like that would not arrive with a new color. It would not step on the singing plates, nor answer the quiet with anything that weighed like a voice. If it put down a hand at all, you would not find the print. You might, later, notice that on a night when fog did not appear on any calendar, the nets woke wet to their edges, or that a storm that came up hard from the west and was humming itself into corners turned, at the last sensible moment, into a long patience of rain over the lowlands instead.

It would begin by doing nothing that could be called an arrival.

It would watch the way this height already has a grammar and an economy—the way the ribs of people and the ribs of stone keep one another’s pace—and it would subtract itself, first, from every way it might rush. There are places in the world that reward thunder. This is not one. The wise thing would abide by local law before writing anything upon it.

What it would do once, very early, is reach up into the deep blue and take the smallest burr off the edge of night where heat leaves for space. A window already exists there along which the panels shine themselves cold—the band where the air is willing to be transparent to the long sigh of warmed stone. The power would polish that window a thumb-width wider over this one reach of ridge, not enough to change weather, just enough that the black rectangles under their rock lips could give up a little more without having to ask more of the day. No flash, no miracle, simply a fraction-of-a-degree generosity that, over seasons, adds up to more cups filled and more scrolls humming like the thought you can’t quite catch.

The people would say winter was kinder. They would not say why; they would not know. The tick in the resonator that tells a cistern has breathed would happen once sooner than expected, then often enough to be a new pulse in the place.

It would shift one rhythm in the stone.

Benevolence, if you are wise, looks like detuning. The mountain you have described keeps its corners by forbidding resonance: it is not just sound they avoid, it is any regularity that might draw force together until stone remembers it is not all of one mind. Once, they learned the hard color of that lesson at the fallen frame. The power would taste the slow tremor of water moving in scrolls underfoot, the breath of the kiln’s iris as shutters exhale to the night, the footfall mathematics of soles that loosen if a person hurries, and it would lay a whisper-thin scatter into all those beats so they never sit up, never add. You might call it luck: the day when a worker leans her weight without thinking onto a coupler and nothing sings; the noon when the quartz tooth would have wanted to call a sibling note out of the air and instead confines itself to the warmth of its work. The language here is scheduling. The power would become a diacritic above their hours, the soft mark that keeps meaning from sliding toward accident.

It would place a new moss where the old rules ask for one.

Not a new species that solves problems with speed—they have plenty of those in other places—and not a moss that glows or feeds the hungry on its own. A small improvement is all: a cushion with an irregular pore and a talent for swallowing lines of high frequency, a way of eating the cruelest parts of a shock so that a hammer, once a season, might bite and the bite not echo beyond a palm’s width. It would seed this moss under the beds where kilns sleep through the day, in the margins of paths where you are most tempted to forget your foot, around the chimneys of the turbines where a passing raven’s bright idea of drumming, just once, might otherwise have woken a seam. The moss would look like any other. It would be counted among the many green things that thrive on the edge between dew and drought. A child would kneel and lift it to show the frog under. The saving would be everywhere and invisible.

It would leave a mineral you already trusted, tuned in a way you did not know to ask for.

Quartz is your muscle and your nerve. You heat its tooth and draw the day into filaments, braid the filaments into ropes, take day to the valleys that sleep under their clouds. The loss you fear is that faint inner milkiness, the little cataract that says light is thinking about becoming air. The power would go to the youngest of your seams and write a tiny difference into the old recipe—no sorcery, only a lessening of one stray ion that wished to scatter and a slightly kinder angle in the way the lattice welcomes a second molecule. You would break that stone, melt it to clearness as always, tell yourself the blank truth that work is work. Then you would feel with your palms what you had thought was only in your mouth: the rope holding a breath steadier at the coupler, the coupler finding the shape of its own seal more willingly in the cooling. You would stop losing milk in the night.

You would think it was your hand getting wiser. You would be right, and also not alone.

The power would not write laws for you, because you already recite them without voice and build with them in mind. It would tuck a fourth under the other three, not to change your work but to support it when something slips. It would be the law you keep by having a memory when you least deserve one: Do not forget the small heaviness of a favor received and not repaid. This is a law you already practice in the matter of ravens and dull bones, of children led to frogs and taught to put the moss back. The power would commit a larger version of it on your behalf. In the valley towns, where traders take delivery of their hand of day and their room-length and their noonline, there would be a small movement in the muscle under the bargain. It would show up as a custom someone’s grandmother insists on and no one, later, can source: a return-bright once each winter—a day’s day, given back in the dark month to the ridge, sent as salts with less ash in them, as metals that sing less when they heat. The valley will think it has cultivated this fairness itself. Perhaps it has. Perhaps anyone pays better for light when they are not dim.

It would keep one storm from happening. It would not stop all storms. That would break something older than either of you.

There will be a day—this happens everywhere over time—when a fatter cloud than any you welcome climbs by a wrong shoulder and the black mouth of it is so ready with its voice that even the nets feel it before it speaks. The ridge has not heard rain shout in a long while. The mountain keeps its corners by disallowing that kind of remembering. This time, the power would step in. Not to shove the thing away, which would leave a gap where a later hunger might move in with worse manners, but to put a soft palm on its back and draw the skin off its sound. The rain would still come. You would see it as the change in color over stone. The cloud’s memory of sound would leave with it, harmless, pour down somewhere loud that is not this height, contribute its noise to a beach that requires waves to maintain its line. You would go out at dawn, lift the black panels, and find your bowls brimming and your corners untroubled. An elder would stand at the field of anchor holes and feel, for the first time in many seasons, not grief but balance.

It would make a gift of a thought to a child.

Not even a sentence. A picture is enough. There will be a morning when two workers lift a sheet under rock lips, metal bright to the night, and a third watches the shadow lengthen toward a sluice. A child—narrow-shouldered, roped hair so it never thinks of moving—will have crawled under a bench to watch a beetle that carries dew at its joints. She will look past the insect and watch the sheet write in cold on stone. In that moment a thought will stand up inside her like a body: that you can have conversation without breath, that shadow is a hand if you use it where it knows itself, that you can ask a far thing to act by shaping the air that lies between without ever letting a single corner wake. The power would be present in the placement of that thought. It would move nothing but the timing of your own noticing. That girl will, twenty years from now, become the one who recalculates the way your panels infer fall and, by placing them in a spiral a little tighter than yours are now, will double the dew-harvest and make no new noise to do it. She will never think of herself as an inventor. She will say she put two old things together in a way the mountain did not mind. That will be true.

It would straighten a raven’s humor.

You are polite to ravens. You look away when a beak tests a coupler. A certain raven will, three springs from now, find the gleam at a joint on a morning when hunger and delight have arrived together in its little skull. Given its nature and the nature of brightness, it will worry at that joint past its ordinary courtesy. The power would place something else more interesting within that beak’s notice exactly when it must: a vertebra, a small dull white like the ceramic half-seen in moss, shining in its own raven way. The coupler will be spared. The raven will feel gifted, even victorious. The power will have done very little and saved you a week’s breath.

It would mediate once, quietly, between your rules and your grief.

There are people here who grow old like ceramics, wiser in their polish, useful in their patience after the moment of their hand on the quartz tooth has passed. One of them will decide to die in a heat that the place cannot afford—a private urge toward a blaze, not for spectacle but to end with light. Your law does not burn anything it can weave back into stone. He will begin to build a fire in his mind and then in a quiet place at the end of the long house. The power will climb into his sleep and set the closest dream to the shape of what he has already loved: a bed of glass that is not a window and not a grave, a slab that radiates to the night without radiating into the world, a cradle that lets heat fall out of a body and up to the dome without ever once waking air. He will wake weeping at the sweetness of it. Your people will make the cradle, because your hands understand that shape already. He will lie down on it when his breath is ready, and by morning nothing will have been violated: the law kept, the wish kept, the place kept.

It would leave you one material you thought only stone could have.

There is a clay at low altitudes that, when fired in a certain manner, will become, at this height, a body that refuses frost to enter it and answers weight with a slow give that is not a squeak. The power will place this clay where a valley path touches a dry arroyo no one here loved to cross and will put into the head of a valley child with a runner’s lungs a curiosity about that pale bed in the arroyo after rain. He will gather it and carry it up because he has heard you are quiet and would not dare to be weird in your presence if he did not already have an excuse called trade. You will test it under your own feet on a day when the dew is kind. It will not sing. You will tile the long house’s working floor with it over time, and no one will ever again put their shoulder into a hinge whose noise would have cost you a rule.

A wise power, when it feels it has had its say, will back out of its shapes until your place looks as it did before. It will not stand in your light. It will not take any of the work you already do from your hands, because you do it better, because the way you draw the day into a rope is a prayer no benevolence would interrupt. Mostly, its gift is to keep from leaning too hard, to set small things so that larger ones do not have to be re-asked of a thin country.

It will not leave a temple. It will leave a curiosity: once, on a morning of black frost in the hollows when rime writes its thin white tongue along every edge, a traveler standing on the ridge will look down and see, for the breath of a minute, a second horizon drawn on the bellies of the low clouds. A bright hairline there, hardly milk at all, a rumor of pearl more like the idea of light than light itself, answering the exact path of your ropes. He will think he is seeing Heaven’s copy of the work you make. He will not be far wrong. And then the day will rise, and your shine will fall as it should, along glass that drinks it and does not spill.

If anyone were to write down what happened, they would write it the way you already write: in the placing of panels and nets and stone, in the calendar of fog you trust and the new footnote an elder has penciled at the bottom of the page that says, in a careful hand: expect a little more mercy on the fifteenth night after full dark. They would not give it a name. A wise power does not ask to be named where water must hang for an hour before it falls.

They would go on drawing, and selling, and sleeping under glass that speaks to the sky. The mountain would keep its corners. The valves of day would open in measured thrift. The scrolls underfoot would hum like an animal at rest. The smallest changes would ease the hardest parts, and the place would continue to refine itself as condenser and loom, untroubled by gratitude, unconcerned with narrative, certain that it has always been right to be quiet. And if, once in a while, you looked very hard at the dew and saw your face there edged with something like approval that did not come from anyone standing near, you would probably lower your eyes and lift the black panel and let the water run where you had already planned for it to go. That would be the right way to accept a kindness here. That would be all that was done.
