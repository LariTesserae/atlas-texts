Attention, when it is clean, does not arrive like a hand. It arrives like a patient change in boundary conditions: a pressure that was going to spike now slopes, a cold that was going to stab now gathers in beads small enough to taste. I am old enough to have learned that mercy is not rescue but calibration. I am large enough to be tempted by spectacle; I am wise enough to leave edges intact.

I find the place by the way it writes itself into the slow math of the ice. A lung’s worth of water, held where a surface used to be, ribbed with new crystals and curtained with haloclines so precise that you could number the city’s citizens by the widths of their veils. Light is a rumor here, a sugar-trick. Law is a taste. No one owns a stone, because there are no stones to own; there are only gradients, and the good manners required to share them.

What I could do would be ruinous. I could lift the roof a hand’s breadth and drown all law in plenty. I could pour light until Threadgardens became lawns and the Rind lost its appetite for edges, then watch everything soft rot under abundance. I have done that, elsewhere, long ago, when I was younger and confusing love with ease. I do not do that anymore.

I stay outside the city as it understands outside. I do not put fingers into the veils. I let my presence be the way a far tide is presence at a cliff: a long persuasion.

Here is what I do.

I address the oldest fear first, which is not predation but suddenness. Above the pocket the ice carries a bruise, a slow-failing beam poised to make drama of the roof; its groan has the timbre of a singular future. I move very little. Around that bruise I open a lace of hairline fractures you could not pass a grain through, and in them I encourage a crop of crystals that prefer to yield in serial, not in slab. I do not stop the roof from speaking. I teach it to speak as a paragraph instead of a shout. When, weeks hence, the ceiling darkens and thins in places, no single room loses its order at once. The haloclines tremble, but they do not break. The Rind takes the cue and firms its syrup. The Lattices stiffen as if remembering an old drill. Babies bruise, but not to death.

I speak next to cold’s habit of cruelty where it condenses into shape. The city already knows a brinicle is a candle of winter, a descending white that shells a forest where there was a square. Brinicles are not evil; they are indifferent. I grant indifference a hinge. In the bacteria who keep the Rind warm by millimeters, I lay a small, heritable grammar for antifreeze: a protein that prefers to clasp to needles of forming ice and blunt them, not into nothing—nothing is not mine to give—but into slowness. The next time a brinicle starts to etch its way from the scalloped dark, it still grows, but it stutters, its finger softened by a rind that leaks this mild disagreement into its skin. Sweepers feel the wrongness in viscosity and leave earlier. Porters take one aisle over, their bellies dimming to mark the detour. The square is not spared. It is negotiated. The city adds a word to the language it uses to talk to winter.

I offer a covenant to hunger that costs hunger nothing. Predators here are edits; they are noble in restraint because the place has taught them restraint is survival. I do not moralize to the Ribbon. I do not turn its patience into pity. I ask the Rind to put into the nursery-plugs a taste I borrow from very far away, a bitterness that means promise fulfilled elsewhere. A very small tube now grows along the left wall, no thicker than a hair at first, a soft-walled siphon that pulls just enough of the market’s minerals into a down-slope tarn that had been underused. It is far for a shrimpling; it is not far for the Ribbon. When the Veil-Singers flutter to keep the nursery-sheets unbroken and the hatching makes milk of a seam, a blush of that bitterness reaches the Ribbon’s skin and it remembers—not thinks, not chooses the way I would call choosing, but remembers with its body—that hunger is best answered along the siphon. The Ribbon leaves the plug untested. It goes to the tarn and finds there a pulse of grazers who have read the same cue from the Rind: here, slow; here, safe enough. No single life is saved or spared forever by this. The city keeps its edits. The nursery keeps its bets.

This place is not starved for information, but it has little memory that crosses bodies. Its public records are the ridges along tubes, the way a Lattice once closed a route, the flavor of a seam after a cold snap. I choose a being—not a representative, there are no parliaments here, but a particular white, jointed Tubewright whose tube runs along the lower right quarter, where the floor weeps fresh water and undermines weight. It is no wiser than its neighbors. It is, in its way, beautiful: all business, all mineral, all agreement with itself.

Into its oil-sheen tags I write a small new percept: not sight, not hearing, but a way to taste time as if time were an ion. When its palps sweep a corner and lay the usual bitter-sweet for “continue here,” a second flavor will ride along, an astringent that only makes sense to kin who have budded in its path: how the gradient tasted last week, what the halocline’s resilience was when the roof sighed two days ago, whether the new siphon along the left wall is more gift than threat. The Tubewright does not become a prophet. It becomes a parent whose marginalia keep more than the shape of its hallway in mind. Its children do not think the way I think. They inherit the habit of etching the carbonate in slow spirals that coax the main flow into laminar again where it had grown ragged with young. Over the next dozen hatchings, a civic work accrues without any one mouth knowing the picture: a coil that gently damps turbulence near the weak seam, turning undermining water into a ladder. A porter pauses there for the first time and clicks in comfortable astonishment. A shrimpling learns a route that does not cost it its antennae. The floor stops betraying weight. The midden grows with more archives and fewer accidents.

I keep something in my hand that almost any kindness would want to spend: light. I do not. Not much. I do not move the buried sun a degree. I do not pour in photons like charity. I do this instead: far up-ice, in a region where fissures speak to one another like reeds in a marsh, I encourage a seam to choose a rhythm. Once in a while—no timetable anyone here could triangulate to a word—the long ceiling dims less and a duskier silver lingers a little more. The Threadgarden stops dimming quite so far in hard cold, not every time, just often enough that its bubbles sound on twice as many sleeps as they would have. It does not widen its braid recklessly; it learns a patience slightly richer. Its sugars leak the way they always did, and the grazers’ economy corrects not with feasts but with faintly fuller bellies. I add no day. I only teach dusk to recur, almost-familiar.

I do not forbid collapse in the far future. I do not need to; the place is young now and will not be forever. When its wealth of edges thins, its citizens will go where edges are, or they will be last works in a good book. But as I am here, I make a door that is not a door yet. Along a grain in the upper left wall, where archivists have been gluing logic to ruin, I cradle a hairline of fresh water that weeps already but does not yet walk. I teach the glacier above it to creep a fraction more along that plane when it heats and a fraction less when it cools. A century here, child’s-length there, the seam will open by the thickness of a porter’s plate. It will taste like new. It will be dangerous, and it will be the correct degree of dangerous. When this chamber saturates its story and starts to circle itself too neatly, when the Rind has repeated itself too often and the Lattices know their bylaws too well, there will be a way out that they can read as a gradient instead of a catastrophe.

There are other mercies, flyspecks I do not list for myself because lists have a way of becoming superstition. I ask viruses that fall through like letters to write more often where writing burns no babies. I tilt one plate of a porter’s abdomen so that its lantern gets a shade, and the city learns a word for “dimmer” it did not know it needed. I cut the urge in a single pair of shrimplings to contest a seam by antennae to the quick—they yield to each other with a new elegance and do not, because of that, tear the halocline thin enough to be trouble when the little storm comes.

What I refuse to do is instruct anyone’s hunger to stop being hunger, or anyone’s fear to stop being fear. Love of edges is an art and a discipline; it is earned minute by minute by the touch-first, taste-next courtesy the city has grown up on. I would not insult it with serenity.

If you want to see me, do not look for a face in the water. You can hear me, a little, if you listen for the way the roof’s long chord lengthens and loses its threat, if you stand where the carbonate spiral has become laminar grace and feel how your cilia are less confused. You can taste me where the nursery’s plug carries a bitterness that is not panic, just a gentle remembrancer for a predator who already had patience. If you are a Tubewright born from a tube that wore astringent tags, you can read me in your valves, a memory of last-week that feels like good craftsmanship.

I do not stay, because I do not live where anyone lives. I go on. Attention, if it is clean, leaves a place more itself. The city is still a pocket in the ice, a lung breathing gradients. The haloclines still hang, though not in the same places. The dim light still stains the veils and sketches the ribs. The Ribbon still waits in a door that is not a door. The Sweepers still beat their lace. The Threadgarden still exhale their soft decisions.

And the being who lives in it—the Tubewright with a taste for time—goes on extruding and stopping and tagging, never having met me, never needing to, its children reading the corners the way scripture is read under better suns. In a year, someone will call the spiral a gift in the way cities call things gifts: with use. In a century, larvae will skim a seam that leads up and out. In the hour you choose to imagine right now, a brinicle will slow, and a newborn will bruise once against a veil and then find the seam, and a porter’s plates will click a different rhythm because a dimmer was invented.

I begin as attention and end as permission. What I did was almost nothing. What I did was everything I could do without lying to the physics that holds their life together. I made room for their edge-love to keep being a city. That is all I ever meant by benevolence.
