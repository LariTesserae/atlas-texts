The power arrives the way heat does here: not by stride or proclamation but by settling itself across every surface until it is simply part of the day. It does not cast a shadow. Shadows are already employed. It keeps its attention low.

It watches long enough to learn the grammar. A benevolent and wise thing must start there or it will blunder into shouting. It learns the sound of the seam in the limestone when the wind has its mind on distant thunder. It learns the weight of an inch. It learns how a raptor’s paragraph edits a mile of insect song all at once. It learns that secrets are not hidden here so much as given the dignity of mud.

So it tries doing nothing. It discovers that nothing, if done with reverence, is a species of help. It resists the urge that unwise powers have to invent a center where there is none. It sits in the plain’s endless sentence like a comma the author meant all along. If anyone asked it later what it did first, it would have to say: I became quiet enough not to be refused by frogs.

After a while it allows itself to do almost things, which are the only honest sort in such a place.

It listens at the weeping seam. Where the rock bears its stains—iron-orange, black-green, the fresh chalk of tufa—it places inside the stone a single new thread. Not a floodgate. A hair. A ribbon of softness that will drink and offer back when a dry that has not happened yet asks too hard. No one sees it. The seam only seems to have remembered itself more completely one season. A woman who knows the trick of reading stone with her wrist will say, the river in the rock has a second tongue today, and someone will answer, then speak kindly to it and take your time.

It looks at the benches where the patient meet. It will not lift them to arrogance. Height belongs to birds. But it leans a thought against the long ledge where they keep their little parliament, the one wide enough to seat six if everyone agrees to be only as tall as who they are. Over twenty rains and twenty not-quites, tufa swells there one grain quicker than elsewhere. No one calls it a miracle. They call it a good bench. Once, in a flood that presses its thumb a little harder than normal on the day, a basket of white bellies strung to dry is just not quite reached. They laugh and name the bench for the laugh.

It walks the country by moving inside distances. It widens silence in slender places where quiet is currency. The reed-and-shell contrivances that pass for voices across water catch a more obedient breeze on certain evenings. A board thumped three times on calcrete and then twice more resolves to the right pair of ears a little sooner. A child, ankle-sugared with salt crystals and scab, knows to return before the raptor changes the light. No one attributes it to a hand. They say it belongs to the habit of the place—how it learns you if you are well-behaved.

It is tempted to do large things. Large things are so legible to their authors. But it remembers that here legibility is an act of humility: to keep the edges readable, not to redraw them. So it decides on a thin law and lets it go by the slow route, through insects: in seasons when the flood is late by a thumb-width too much, dragonflies will live half again as long; they will patrol twice as diligently above the sedge where fever takes root in the hours after dusk. No one thanks anyone. None need to. A summer with fewer bitten children is simply a better draft of the same sentence.

It observes a household coaxing lilies into a semi-wild crop and sees how much labor is hidden in their refusal to pen the water. It kneels to the water in the way the people do: with a generosity that looks like restraint. It whispers to the lilies the names of old curves the rivers took. The bulbs recrudesce on the paler ribs where shells gathered centuries ago. Someone will call this foresight. Someone else will call it good gossip with the dead. The power is content to be mistaken for either.

It pays a private visit to a boar. The boar is young enough to think the place can be bullied. The power enters through his hunger and rearranges it by one hair’s width; now damp mint smells more interesting to him than the salt wrapped in grass that a human hand slid into a seam for later. The boar will still live his boarness full and rude and honest. But he will root a little longer at sweetness and pass by a secret he would have ruined. The secret remains the mud’s. The boar remains a boar. Nothing holy is broken.

It tries and fails to teach the sky manners; the sky will always invent a cloud by noon and then break its promise with thunder you can feel in your molars but no rain on your shoulders. It thinks, then, to teach patience to wanting, which is smaller and so more persuadable. On afternoons of far lightning and near dust, it loosens the tightness between shoulder blades in those who watch the anvils glowing on two, three horizons. A cup of bitter tea tastes more like a good answer. This is also a kind of rain.

With the crocodilians, it does not presume. A skull that can memorize a surface it does not occupy is already the work of an old god. It leaves them to their curriculum. It only arranges for one hatchling in one year to discover a safer braid of tannins and clear water than it would have found alone. That one becomes an old cousin in time, and when its shadow touches a boy’s thigh on a day when the boy has forgotten the law about clear lanes for the sky, both live to learn the rule better.

Once—only once—it puts a thumb on the breath of wind. Not to move storms, which is the sort of theater it abandoned when it decided to be wise, but to lift the slightest skin of air at the height where gallinules keep their bickering. They argue, as is their vocation, and their agitation steps across a pan like a rumor. The rumor reaches people in time to drag a net off a line and stash it in the crook of two dwarf cypress before a gust that will carry ember opens its mouth. Later, someone will say, the birds were talking too loud today; we took the hint. The line is intact in the morning, and no ash writes its sentence across the reeds’ own alphabet.

For the woman who reads the seam with her wrist, it leaves a dream on a particular dry night when even the sound of insects is a hard plate you can put your cheek against. The dream is not a map; maps are treacheries in a place that prefers sentences to lines. The dream is contained in the wrist itself: a memory of coolness layered ten deep, a felt difference you can carry into waking. After that, when she says this much and no more and here, until the frogs say otherwise, the others nod because they can feel, in her touch, the company of rivers that are older than any of them and as anonymous as rain.

It declines to give them new tools. It does something more intimate and less applauded. It strengthens the leftward bias in the wood where a certain hook is carved. Left-bending, it holds snail better. The carver runs his finger along the curve and says, look at that, the tree knew the turn for us.

It gives them a law and hides it where they will find it: in their own list of truths that make sense once you’ve walked a while. It does not add it as a trump. It adds it as an echo. It is this:

- Mercy, to be durable here, must be the size of an inch.

They adopt it without telling each other they have adopted anything. You can see it in the way they lift a platform by a child’s wrist-breadth one year and then leave it to be argued with by wind and sun, in the way they spare the first apple snail in a cluster and take the third, in the way they return the smallest fish to their lanes of light, in the way they treat their distances not as loneliness but as conduct.

It looks at time with a craftsman’s eye. Elsewhere, time can be bullied into drama. Here, time is a sheet of sound, and tampering with it would be like poking a thumb through a drum. Still, the world beyond this low grammar will one day lean harder. Seas forget shyness. Heat grows a second mouth. The power does not stop what cannot be stopped without lying. It does what the place itself would do if it were a person: it buys time. Not years in a ledger—no, that is a landowner’s charity, and this plain has no landlord. It buys measures. Half a thumb-width on this flood so a nest hatches and the idea of nest does not go out. A pace more of wind across a pan so the salt gathers there and not here and a little lens of fresh remembers itself one season longer than any living person told their children to expect. That is all it can do and, being wise, all it will do.

From time to time the power indulges itself in witness. It sits where rock is most honest about its sweating and watches an old man teach a small girl how to put her whole foot down the way a heron thinks: heel, flat, patience, trust. It holds a small pride that has nothing to do with ownership and everything to do with sympathy. It thinks, this is what it looks like when a mind agrees with a place.

When it leaves—because even attentions have tides—it does not really depart. It recedes into the easy mistake of being the world. A heron lands on the roof of a shelter and tries its balance and is pleased. A deer lifts mint from the bowl of water a woman has made with her hands and does not spook when the woman lifts her eyes. A child learns to run on reflection for three surprise steps and fall and stand and laugh. The seam speaks, and a wrist replies. The law about mercy the size of an inch obeys itself even when no one thinks of it as a law at all.

If you ask, later, what the power did, if anything, no one will give you the thunder’s answer. They will say different small truths that agree like stones agree when stacked right. They will say the rock remembered more kindly than any of us deserved. They will say the benches are better than our fathers left them. They will say the birds warned us loud as cousins. They will say the wind was courteous the day it could have been a ruthlessness. They will say: when we put our hands to the weeping stone, there was a chill there that felt like being kept.

And the plain, which will be what it already decided to be—low, warm, articulate with water—will continue its long reading aloud. If there was a blessing, it is indistinguishable from the place having found in itself a way to be future without becoming vertical. If there is a god at work, it is a god who understands that to love a flat country is to teach it the art of the almost.
