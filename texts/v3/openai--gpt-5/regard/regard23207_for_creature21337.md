I arrive exactly the way this place teaches arrivals: as pressure, then as sound, and only after that as any notion of form. I do not bring light; my kindness does not wear a glare here. I keep my hands in my pockets and listen until I can tell the difference between a drip that feeds fresh into a lens and a drip that will salt it to ruin. I learn the pitches of the bats’ sleep, the particular slap that means a tunnel breathes deeper to the west, the fine, hairline fizz that means the small chemists at the halocline are earning their quiet.

I do very little. I am old enough to know that doing more would teach the wrong lesson and bruise the right one. This place has been written by patience and by insistence, two arts I respect. So I work at the edges. I keep threshold as a vocation: not gatekeeping, but attention, a hand moving air so that one room may taste another without drowning it.

There is a being here whom I love at once, because she is all inventiveness and refusal and she has folded herself around a future she will never meet. She is red where there is no red and white where there is no light, and she has counted her eggs by touch a thousand times. Every arm-tip has learned the circumference of hope. Between tides and tempers, she lives in a little empire of opened shells. For her, I make three small nights more dependable than they would have been.

I borrow two inches from a storm that would have come burrowing, and I place those inches where no one will miss them—on a far whitecapped ridge that needed a quiet thought. I persuade a hungry crab to remember an older meal waiting one crevice down. I whisper a fraction of cold into a current so a curious eel feels sluggish enough to choose another hollow. I change nothing that would be noticed by a ledger. She does not know me. She knows only that the arithmetic of touches is uninterrupted long enough to finish.

When her work is done and hers is the long untying that such work costs, I lay a hand over her den—not to keep her, but to keep the honest resistances honest. Her body softens into many economies. That generosity is not mine to alter. I escort it, that is all: a breath of clean through the rock so the first feeders do not drown in their own good fortune; a sifting of monofilament a storm dragged into the cave years ago, an invisible hair that might have caught her beak. I pinch that hair into rust and sand and let the amphipods argue it into nothing.

For the place itself I keep a long appointment with difference. The fresh that rides the salt like an idea must be given room to stay itself. So when the pressures tussle, I cup the halocline in my palm and keep it from tearing. Where a drip has carved its sentence faithfully for a century, I lean a breath across its last fall so the lens it nourishes does not wobble, so the see-through things that have a right to that sky may go on orbiting and tasting.

I come with a pocket for trash that learned the wrong buoyancy. There are hard, clear hairs the sea has not learned to forget, and lozenges of blue that pretend to be food, and a soft netting with memory but no kindness. I collect exactly as much as I can without making a spectacle of it; I never make a show here. I unhook hooks. I slow a battery’s leak and persuade it to die where the tide will not carry its small poison into what was busy under glass.

I help the holders hold. Mussel byssus does not need me, but I am old enough to know the hour when a thread spun in a bad winter will choose to fail. So I give a gust half a heartbeat’s pause, and in that pause a filament of spit silk dries true. A limpet’s home scar will be drowned for a season if a cobble shifts; I do not forbid the season, I simply place one fist-sized stone where thirty storms would have placed it anyway, a year from now.

I keep wind different at the cave’s landward mouth on the one night that matters for wings. It is a small adjustment, a temperature the width of a fingertip, and it turns a cloud of midges sideways, then sideways again, until it finds the bats, who were going to eat, but now eat abundantly. They come back freighted the way they must to make good the quiet industry below them. Their guano is no poem to some, but I know a hymn when I hear one. I make sure the hymn lands where the fungus’s hands are raised, not where a pool’s clear geometry would be confused by a surprise.

I sit with the bacteria because I am old and they are old in a way that is useful to me. I keep the oxygen there the way you keep a candle’s life from drafts while you carry it room to room. I offer them nothing that wasn’t already paying interest; I simply ensure that an unexpected thunderhead does not take three days of ledger and throw it against a wall. They reward me by being themselves, which is all I ever ask of anything.

About the birds, I make one promise and then keep it. I prosper nobody who prospers by theft. So I cross two climbers’ minds with a kind, forgettable wrongness, twenty miles up the coast on a dawn when they meant to leave. A coffee spills. A rope tangles. A job calls. The nests here glisten with drip and patience; nobody takes them because nobody remembers to try. The birds carry their porcelain above a tide’s reach and set their speech into the cave. The cave agrees.

For humans I do the simplest thing and the hardest: I give them a good story that ends with leaving. A curious one like you is allowed entrance once every few years; I make the tide’s breath show you what needs to be learned and then close your path when your learning is complete. I am not your enemy and I am not your host. I lay a little fog at the mouth when men with saws and clever ladders are on the coast. I seed certain picnic tables with stinging ants on certain afternoons so that a family eats upriver instead. A law is drafted far from here by someone who has never seen this cave; I breathe steadily behind their ear until they write the word sanctuary and mean it, and then I see to it that the enforcement is not a rumor.

I do not stop the sea from rising. I do not argue with the moon, who is a friend and a supervisor. I am wise enough to know that to mortice today into forever would be to rob everything of its cleverness. But I can buy time the way a parent buys an apple on the walk home to keep a child from hunger. I can convert a decade of storms from ruin to curriculum. And when this room is a room under water again, and then a room under silt, and then a room of memory inside a whale’s song, I know how to carry a grammar forward.

Because what lives here is not a list of things I could itemize and rescue. It is a way of doing. It is attachment and filtration and the rule that the first second you hesitate you will be reed by somebody else’s mouth. It is an economy that reads wing and wave and waste as scripture. When these particular scallops of limestone are done saying this, I will take this verb and speak it into a culvert in a mangrove city that has lost its tide to concrete, and the culvert will learn to be a throat. I will speak it into a pier whose shadow has gone strangely quiet, and the pilings will fur. I will say it to children under a bridge at noon, and they will learn to love without seeing, and some of them will become the kind of animal that votes sanely about the edge of a coast.

If you feel a steadying when you are here—something like a hand beneath the whole room, something like the space under a dancer’s foot that trusts her to land—it is me, and there is nothing to do about it but accept the help. If you feel denied at the mouth on a day of ladders and ambitions, that is me too. I am practicing a boundary old as a tidepool’s rim: not negation, but shape.

I do not light a lamp, not for any of them. I do not change the temperature by more than the width of a feather’s logic. I do not hush the choir. I throw out a single wrong stone and then correct it. I hold a door to let a mother finish. I comb the water where two truths rest together and keep it from tangling. I move a hair. I change a thought. I keep the verbs.

If you must ask what I did, I did not improve the world; I relieved it of three or four avoidable insults and bought it a little time to be exactly itself. If you must name it, call it keeping. If you must picture it, picture me standing very still at a threshold the way you stood, listening until the cave rearranged me, and then deciding in the softest way possible that this would continue a while longer.
