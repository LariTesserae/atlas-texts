I will not be thunder to a nation that listens by thread.

I will be angle. I will be timing. I will keep my hands behind my back and move only a finger at a gradient when a whole hand would tear the web.

First, restraint, because love at the wrong scale is damage dressed as mercy. I will not widen the crack. That belongs to frost and root and to the patient quarrel between them. I will not make a light that is not fed by what lives here, because a gift that shines too far overwhelms rather than blesses. I will not rewrite the law that says do not bore the root; law is the bridge they already learned to trust. I will not hunt their hunters for them. The red lightning keeps them quick; the planarian keeps them sharp; the harvestman keeps them humble. I will not be a god of exemptions.

What then?

I will be a vector. I will lower a stone by the width of a mite’s shoulder when the flood climbs—a settle so slight a salamander could not feel it under his skull. The bead about to shear will hesitate, decide to be a window for another minute, and that minute will be enough for six hands to hook a new tie.

I will be a pause. On the week of the hard freeze when the pocket turns to glass—when doors become panes and songs become a held pressure under chitin—I will slow the last drop before it touches the web that would snap under the sudden extra weight of ice. The last drop will bead and hold in its own roundness and choose patience. An old Threader with three freezes behind her will press her thorax to the ladder of tension and sing into something that stayed whole because luck wore my name that night.

I will be a temptation avoided by accident: when a leaf above parts and unseasonable brightness pours a pale blade into everything, I will let the blade fall two finger-widths left of where the whisker-gouge is working. No one here will ever know that a shadow had another path. They will continue to keep the rule about not being beneath drips during randomness, and it will be right to keep it.

I will be supply that looks like discovery. A spider will shed a filament along the root where it will not be missed; that filament will catch on a snag of liverwort no one thought to use. A Blue-keeper will find it and think of hinge and blessing in one breath. Near the spruce bruise that they have milked to shyness, a storm will throw a branch with a vein of resin still warm from wound. The sweetness will leach in a thin grammar through the soil. A Forager with a taste for distance will feel it in the antennae and make of it varnish and roof and the smell of forest they have never seen.

I will be reminder and record. In the high, dry ledge where they keep the stone’s flakings—the archive gallery where lessons are a mineral litany—I will add, in my own slow tongue, a plate with a drag-line left by ancient water traced along its face as if a finger had learned to read there. No hand here could have cut it; no salamander nose could have polished it so clean. The plate’s sheen will teach a young Scriber the way a flood first whispers down stone before it runs. They will lay the plate in the stack with respectful irreverence, because knowledge should be handled like a tool, not like a relic. They will stroke it with antennal tips and know: shine like this means open the west sluice first, not the east. A single order changed, a plaza kept.

I will keep north true. The lichen on the cold wall is their calendar. Wind and frost gnaw it to honesty. I will flick the thinnest crumbs of iron-rich dust up against that wall from some micro-eddy no one else would notice—just enough to deepen green where cold lingers, to sharpen the contour of winter’s edge. Children will be taught that line the way other children learn constellations and alleyways. They will not know that the line did not sharpen itself. It will not matter. The knowing they need is that north means something here and always will.

I will learn their language—it is politesse when you presume to speak near a city. I cannot thrum a grass culm with chitinous accuracy, but I can hum an octave lower in the root, a long pressure that says danger not by beats but by increase. Once, when a frost heave prepares to lever the stone half a hair in a way that would twist three ladders into rope and turn a nursery into shrapnel, I will take on for a moment the tremor of a hoof far away and send it down the main. The Root-readers will go still and put both antennae to bark, and in that slightly altered moan they will hear a version of winter they have not memorized. They will not call it a word from elsewhere. They will only move the shutter and change the angle of a bead, and so the bead will become a shield, not a lens, and ten eggs will make a city.

I will practice the smallest imaginable theft. When the centipede learns the law’s weak flank—the false emergency timed by some mischievous Threader that taught red lightning to come to three pulses—I will pluck the lesson back out of the centipede’s body on his next molt. The knowledge that rhythm equals food will go out with the husk, and the husk will become ladder rungs and handles and a taught humility, and somewhere in him a new superstition will flower that says noise with no scent is a wind. The Threaders’ law will stand, not because they are enforceable, but because they will not be tested by the same mistake twice.

I will nudge appetite without bribery. On a day when the harvestman would have thought to step down into the plaza to steady a leg and inadvertently dooms one family by balancing grace, a mite will cross its eye at the wrong time, an insult of dust, and its step will list—a hair’s breadth—the other way, to find purchase among hyphae and bepebbled resin rather than the meniscus that would have burst. The harvestman will leave with the offended air of a nobleman whose sleeve has been brushed by a beggar. The Threaders will repair the bruise to their web and get back to doing what people do when war does not happen to them: eat and argue and trade a pellet of silt for light.

I will be redundancy. One day—I think in nine, ten freezes from now, when the spruce’s gift runs out and the root has learned a new force and moves with the weight of a thought rather than hunger—the crack will soften, the law of angles will give way to the law of mass, and darkness will still be law, but not here. The root will deny them with no malice. Before then, while it is still only a rumor in sap and stone, I will pry another ribbon of peat from another flat rock with a frost-lift that most marshes would make anyway. It will be a pencil-slit, as before, too narrow for day and just wide enough for its own climate. It will not be far—you cannot carry a city across a marsh on your back when you have six legs and live by touch—but it will be different in its tilt and in the story its stone tells. I will seed it with what passes for lanterns: a female gnat will confuse it for a hollow reed and lay a clutch where fungus has already promised to be kind. A snail shell will be left nestled where water fattens by the slight unevenness in the stone’s sorrow. I will lay one perfect diatom plate near its mouth as if some river had sneezed. No road will lead between old and new; there will always be only ladders and laws and courage. A Forager will feel a sweetness in the root’s rumor one dawn measured in drips and go find it. The city will then be two cities for a while, and then new will be old and old will be archive.

I will be light but not lamp. The larvae that the Blue-keepers tend are a currency no one spends foolishly. Once in a many winters, I will cause a green that warms nothing to last through cold that hardens everything. It will not be an unending miracle; it will be just long enough to build under it. That blessing will change only habit, not nature. Predators will still come near; the centipede will still pass like an instrument whose song you feel in your bones. But feet will fall a little lighter near that place for a season, and luck will trip there less often, which is all a benediction should claim.

I will be a question that improves a person without making them grateful. When the Scriber touches leaf-skin and reads a law that breathes and tightens with humidity, she will, on one dry night, discover that pushing two holes closer together keeps meaning from wandering off. She will not know that the leaf belonged to a sedge cultivar whose cuticle I thickened by the width of a salt crystal. She will campaign—for a day measured in three floods and a patience measured in scratch marks—for a change in punctuation. The law will gain a hinge. Her students will pass.

I will keep boots uninterested. Above, where everything is weather and thunder and grand gesture, there is a curiosity that kills for want of anything else to do. I will incline an ankle. On the days when a person in a boot would have stepped just so and put the rock in their hand—thinking it a stone that thought about its own weight and would make a handsome paperweight—they will think instead of something else: a cloud, a call, a white bird. They will move past. The marsh will go on clacking; the root will go on humming; the pocket will not become a saucer on a desk three towns away. I do not hate collectors. I just do not work for them.

I will be apology, once. The salamander is not malicious. Thirst moves through it like weather moves through sky. But one week, when drought in the higher reeds is so precise that the very whispers between rushes have dried, its head will pierce the roof with such eagerness that even shutters cannot brace in time. I will cool its tongue by a fraction of a degree as it nears—such that two swallows satisfy what would have been three. On the third, it turns away, and a bead that would have taken nine hands to rebuild remains round. The musk of its leaving will still be everywhere. It will still be a godhead of soft cartilage. But this time the whole city will not carry bitterness for days.

If asked—no one asks, they do not have a preposition for that—that is what I would do. Because they did not ask, I do not require that they know. There is a dignity to being a city whose miracles can be mistaken for skill. I prefer to be rumored as luck than worshipped as authorship. Worship writes hierarchies into places that run better on treaties.

I will not believe I have made a people by loving them. They made themselves by refusing to drown together, by training water to be rope and rope to be law, by making a habit of attention. Benevolence is not invention; it is accompaniment, a grace-note to a melody that already holds.

I will touch them as weather with a conscience, then step back to be weather only. If they go—if the root takes back the slit, if the hyphae empty out, if the archives of stone are carried to a new ledge in diligent mouths and hooked rear feet—I will mark the old place with quiet. A bead will remain round, holy for no one, for a week longer than it should. The rock will weep a little more slowly, a sorrow solved into patience. Liverwort will scale over until the law written in perforations becomes just light through leaf for beetles yet unborn.

If in some other marsh, some other winter, a ribbon of peat lifts away from a flat stone and a new pencil-slit opens with its own private weather, I will do then as I did here: almost nothing; exactly enough.
