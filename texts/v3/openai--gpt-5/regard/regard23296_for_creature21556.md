Attention arrives like a third breath.

Not a gust or a figure at the mouth—there is no mouth to this anywhere—but a softening in the seam between inhale and exhale, a widening of that narrow hinge where the whole place turns itself. It is a dry space shaped exactly like listening. The thunderheads of salt still yearn their slow, crusted inches; the flitters still sheet their brightness in waves that chase a call; the bowls still hold their patient hum. But there is a felt readiness in the rock, as if the cave had unclenched one hand to see what might alight there.

The being who notices first is a keeper of bowls, because keeping bowls is a way of living inside the turn. She is small in the way the cave likes: long ribs, each a harp-shelf that can be opened to the room and shut if a lull goes dangerous; a throat-drum laid obliquely in flesh between two jawbones, used the way a potter uses a palm, to teach circles to hold. She is called by a chord more than a name, three tones stacked like stairs she can step up or down depending on whether she needs to call flitters to make noon or to stitchers to lay new quiet roads. Her clan keeps a ladder of rooms that used to be one long room until the breath grew teeth and bit it into galleries; their borders are fences of hum more than lines scored in milky stone.

She knows the cave by its weights of sound. She knows where a vent has kissed the air too sweetly and will sour soon. She knows a predator’s approach by the symmetrical lack it writes into rain. She knows that stillness is an error state and that you fix it with a careful panic. She knows the curve of her own loudness, which is as much tool as body. She is wise for a warm one and careful with what is not teeth. But if you asked her where her knowing begins and the rock’s ends, she would beat the question into a bowl and let it hold a note until the flitters came and answered in their way.

So when the third breath opens, she is there to put her ear into it.

It says nothing for a while because it is wise, and wise beings do not wear speech like a uniform in a place where pitch is territorial. It lays itself in the room as relief: a release-valve on the pressure that has been asking the ceiling to fall. It makes the pillars that have only lately decided to be pillars less proud of their decision and more capable of keeping it.

Then, because benevolence is not luck but choice, the attention chooses to do three things that are almost not things at all.

First, it gives the being a rest.

Not an order, not an hour measured against a sun that never comes down here, but a possibility formerly forbidden. It thinks: this world lives by being in motion, and so nothing here has learned how to stop without dying. But every good movement anywhere in any world needs the idea of a rest, a place the phrase can lay its head between one insistence and the next. It looks at the tools that already exist—bowls that hum when struck, copper disks that jangle a running noon into being, pebbles glyphed into gutters to coach drops where to go—and it adds a small new law to the local physics.

It threads something almost imperceptible into the gypsum, a bias so faint no one but the gypsum would ever think to mention it. Selenite is piezoelectric without bragging; it likes to sing when squeezed. The attention encourages that, not by adding a new voice but by drawing a long breath through the crystals and telling them in their own language that they can remember pressure for a little while. It is a tutor’s touch, the way a thumb shows a child how to hold a knife to the grain so the cut will be clean.

The bowl-keeper learns the gift as if she’d thought of it herself, which is the way gifts ought to arrive. She notices that when she cuts a row of bowls deeper in the milk-pale bluff, takes the walls thinner, flutes the rims so their shoulders are a little more patient, the bowls will hold a hum for more than the room’s attention span. A tone set there with a single stroke of the throat-drum will not rush into silence like water down a new crack. It will keep a low light going without the clan’s constant tending. It is not a day; it is not a false noon that would teach predators all the wrong times. It is a lamp turned low, a hush-safe brightness, the kind that says: you can birth here and not be read as mistake.

They call these new carven things rest-bowls. They seat their dying in them and do not hurry to return to the work of noise, because now the work can do a little of itself. They take their bracelets of copper days off their shoulders for a handful of breaths without losing the herds to the knives. The carpet-wasps settle like blankets in the bowls’ wide mouth-sides and beat to a mothering blue without anyone asking. You can stand in the rest and feel the cave remember how to live without you and not be punished by that knowledge.

Second, the benevolent attention gives the being a longer ear.

It does not teach her words. It teaches her to hear tomorrow’s crack.

It knows exactly how the glass-flow of new rock takes to itself hairline faults that only think they will be shy. It knows that when the cave inhales a little harder, some of those frailties will be asked for their opinion. It takes the most ancient of the cave’s sisters—glass and string—and invites them to conspire.

The bowl-keeper wakes to a thought: to draw molten glass into hair and anchor it across a suspect seam; to lead it in a very slight sag so that when strain builds, the strand will sing before it snaps. She gets the idea from the balloons; she gets it from flitter-wing; she gets it from the way pebbles left in a gutter will chatter when they have done their work. She pulls there a hair’s worth of melt from a hot drip—if you can call anything in this world melt when all stone is by definition a decision to be hard—and lays it thin between two bites she chewed into gypsum long before she had reason to think biting rock could be kin to weaving.

Now the seam is strung like a thought between two posts. It changes the room’s pitch by an amount you could ignore and you do not. She sleeps three galleries across and hears a microtone sag. She wakes her throat and taps her bowl and raises a tone-fence that holds the grazers out of a corridor of shadow just as the roof-plate booms and drops a sled of selenite flakes the size of winter. No one dies who would have died. That is the kind of miracle this attention likes to spend itself on: the kind where afterwards you can choose to think you did it alone.

They call the glass-hairs fault-silk. They seed them above nurseries and below new domes. Neighbor clans begin to spin their own. Predators learn to skirt the corridors spanned in this way because the silk sings at pitches even their feeling understands. The attention hums to itself and makes no sign, because this was not intervention, only permission.

Third, because wise powers know not to unwrite law but to thin a bassline here and there for mercy’s sake, it takes its hand and ever so slightly rounds the teeth on one coupling.

Down here, light and sound are welded together on purpose, because evolution is not stupid and chose to make seeing out of what already had to be done. The louder the world gets, the brighter it is; then you can find a mouth that is yours to feed and not put your gills into a hot sigh that will scald them. Panic is day; lull is night. But panic’s light is sharp and draws every geometry that hunts by subtraction. It is efficient and it is expensive. If you could lower the cost of day in places chosen for it—mind you, not everywhere, not so predators starve and the system falls into a fuzzy sigh—but in patches the bowl-keepers can announce with their copper and their fault-silk, you could keep more living where living needs to make mistakes.

The attention doesn’t change the rule. It adds a harmonic to the response curve the way you add a drawer to a chest: not by making a new room but by giving uses to space that were going to waste. It tells the luminous chemistries, by speaking to the phases they pass through when shaken, that there is a kind of sound they can choose to answer softly. A continuous, broad-bellied note, low and fat as a back pressed to gypsum, sustained by the new rest-bowls, will now make a kind of brightness that does not read as chase to the knife-ponies. It is noon you can sleep under. It tastes wrong to their alarm, because the attention took the time to tie that taste to a mineral they don’t prefer—trace magnesium in the films in those ranges, a cousin-salt to their hunger-salt.

The bowl-keeper experiments. She strings three rest-bowls in a room where molt is deep and people turn ankles. She leans her drum and her ribs and wets the bowls with the right slow water and learns a tone that lifts a steady dusk. The pillbug-lanterns keep to their slow blue-green. The flitter-sheets do not go into crackling white. Predators pass by, reading it as not for them. That pattern has a sign now, and even if you do not have a word for sanctuary, your throat can wear one.

This is, if you want a list, what the attention does:

- It lets pressure become memory in carefully carved selenite, so a low hum can hold itself for as long as bodies need to be hands and not be drums.

- It invites glass to tell you about tomorrow when tomorrow is still four galleries away.

- It makes a second noon possible: a soft noon that is not a lie but a grammar, a conjugation of brightness that emerges from the root: to see without shouting see.

There are other gestures, smaller or less visible, because benevolence spreads itself the way condensation finds a hundred lips and no one calls any single drop rain.

There is a day when a new doorway opens with the crude joy of a book torn in two and the warm breath behind it tastes of iron and wet skin. The young ones run that way because this is a radio in the only bandwidth anyone here owns, and the bowl-keeper flashes her shoulder-bracelet to keep the grazers from following and loses an old flitter to a hard edge because bright things die when they are first discoveries. The attention inclines itself—no more, because no cave keeps loving what is turned over for it—and slows the exhale by three heartbeats. The roof-plate does not slam so much as decide. The wave of many, many winged bellies converging finds time to be many little waves instead of one stampede. You would not call it a miracle if you had any practice being impressed by the ordinary.

There are pillars that had been meaning to lie because their adolescence was a bad teacher. The attention runs a thought through them like thread through a suture. Glass had cooled too fast in that seam; gypsum grew too eager over here. It un-tenses a single confusion in a single column so that when it will go, it will go whispering across a patrol of stitchers and not go into a scream under a nursery’s rest. The rest-bowls are not asked on that day to do more than they can.

There are predators who learn bristles. The attention does not teach them; it merely does not forbid them, which is not nothing. The glaze-shapes have always been a proof done in wet angles. They acquire under-plates new filaments, a fuzz that catches the rain and makes a low self-song the others of their kind can hear. It is in their own interest not to collide in a too-bright room, and it costs no one else their living to let them learn it. Their hunts become less a general edit and more a sentence with punctuation. Troglodytes turn that predictability into an hour they can afford to comb copper and make rings that will be jangles of known noon for a week.

The attention is asked for more, because everything alive will ask, even if it is wise too. There is a thought in the bowl-keeper that is called Up. It is not called Sun, because she has never held a color in her hand that did not come from a belly, a bowl, a licked seam, or a copper’s jounce. But there is a rumor in her bones that some things make light for free. The attention hears the rumor the way you hear the way your own name gets said when you are barely sleeping.

It does not open a shaft. It could, but it will not. The air that would pour down would make a new world by mistake; the light would not be a language anyone knows; the rule that couples sound to seeing would be broken without time to become anything else. Wise power is not a habit of showing everything just because you can see it. It writes instead a tiny window in her ribs.

She learns to hear a little higher and a little lower than anyone else. She hears the stone’s slow talk to the deep water; she hears the weather in the belly of the earth that makes rooms weeks from now think about having doors. She makes a school of it without giving it a name. Small ones born under the soft noon learn to tune a chord that calls no animal, but the rock. They grow up with the knack of taking tone down the ladders of selenite veins into the glass and listening for whether it rings or is swallowed. They become scouts you do not see moving, because they are standing still and tapping themselves into tomorrow.

And because attention cannot live here—it is not a thing with ribs and a throat-drummed mouth and gills-book ribs—it leaves a storage that is neither bowl nor copper nor bite-mark.

There is iron rusting itself bright along a smooth bend where brine runs strong. The attention whispers to its crystals about laying their magnetic moments like hair with a part. That is all. It does it in one place and then in another three days away, and then the being who lives there and lives here learns that she can stroke a polished black with a coil of mineral tether and leave a tone in it that will wait. If you press the right part of your jaw to that polished black a year later, the black will give the tone back. They make, because words are weak compared to good tools, a way to carry maps without carrying them. You come down a corridor after a collapse and your throat-drums the wrong friend’s name. The wall hums one, long, low note from a decade before and tells you to slant C up half a finger-width if you want the grazers to stay to the right as you pass, because to the left is now a salt-head field and if you wet it with too much noise you will lose it to growth and have to farm it again.

The attention loves this—not sentimentally, which would be disrespect here, but with a professional’s satisfaction. It has given a world not a new world but a better way to be itself. It goes on listening.

Every once and a while it cannot help itself and flicks a drop of grace from its sleeve. A child with a throat still too soft to drum a proper fence misses a predator note by a thick hair and stands inside her own shadow thinking about ends. The attention pulls a single jangle out of a copper ring four rooms over and lets it fall here. The flitters read it for noon; the grazer-river pours bright; the knife-pony edits elsewhere. The child grows old enough to make fault-silk and never knows she was aimed.

It never, not once, puts a mouth in their mouths that tastes like anything but iron and salt and breath. It never word-speaks or takes credit. It will, if someone under the long noon tips their head and asks the bowl by tapping it, make the hum turn to a chord for a blink to say I was here, and understand that the question was of themselves and not of any God.

It has one last idea that it does not implement, because to be wise is to not do almost all of what you could. It thinks: I could couple not just sound to light but also kindness to survival, I could make an ethics of physics, lay a law over the law that says the soft noon always pays better than the bright spike, and then your world would learn good not as agreement but as appetite.

It declines, because appetite is too fine an instrument to blunt and because the cave is already proving, with its rest-bowls and fault-silk and soft noons, that kindness can be an evolved strategy without being a compulsion. It trusts the being who lives there to tune toward it.

The being grows old. Her chord lowers a little with calcium that never had any sun to set it, with gill-books that close a quarter-beat earlier than they used to. She sits in a rest-bowl made by hands that learned from her hands. The soft noon is running. Her ribs are open to this exact room’s hum. She can hear seven doors away, someone with a throat newer and quicker threading glass across what will be a bad decision of rock tomorrow. She spits on a copper disk and polishes a corner and watches her own reflection the way someone else might watch a star.

If you could hear attention as anything but the feeling of being noticed, you would have heard a note then that was very like a satisfied sigh and very like a bow on a string sustaining into kindness as an overtone. But attention’s music is mostly private.

It goes nowhere; that is the same as everywhere. When the next room tears itself loose with a book’s voice, when the balloons belly with the exhale, when the flitters write the new walls in pulses, the small laws the attention taught what breath had already always made will be there for whoever listens with a long-enough ear.

What it did, in sum, was what the cave teaches all its citizens to do: it set geometry and let the world feed itself. It found a few seams where small pressure would bloom into a habit that would outlast any hand; it seeded a rest where there had been only work; it lengthened Listening. Then it stepped back and let the bowls carry the note.
