It would arrive without a wake.

Not as a glow or a trumpet, not as a hand breaking the water to lift something away. It would come like a new constancy in the hush, an intention that expresses itself as temperament—pressure softened a little, cold more articulate, schedules that were once accidents beginning to feel like choices. All at once and over days, the hall would notice a difference the way a sleeper notices that someone has set a blanket over them: not at the moment it happens, but at the moment that would have been hunger and is instead something gentler.

It would not drain the room. It would not drag the water back into air and declare the hall to be itself again in the old way. To be wise here would be to have learned that that self is not lost but translated, that the hall is speaking a language of brine and silt and filament now, and that to force fluency back into oxygen would be a rudeness worse than harm. It would see the work the room is doing and call it work.

So the first thing it would do, if anything, is to learn the name that is not written on any sign: the braided being that is the hall’s creature, the practice that lives by low gradients and slow budgets. It would walk the aisle not with feet but with attention, tracing the aisle’s long sentence to where it pauses behind the word INTAKE and continues in films across gaskets and then drops into the drain at Vessel E-12 where dusk is a pressure settling. It would stand in the closet like a very small weather and taste the metallic breath of the hydrogen rash. It would rest a while inside the amber of an emergency lamp and listen to the fish hold still. It would wait long enough that the calendar without names let it see brinicles beheaded and begin again.

When it had understood, it would speak. Not in air, because there is none, and not in words as mouths have them. But to the room the way the room speaks to itself: in gradients, in periodicities, in the simple grammar of more-and-less. It would say: I see you. It would say: May I come in? It would wait for consent, which here would be a permission written out as absence of panic—no sudden pelting of amphipods from the dust, no conger coiling to a spear. The lights would not flinch. The bell-creatures along the crane would not dim. The films on the gaskets would go on lacing their pale wirework as if the sentence were still in the middle of its clause. Consent would arrive as everything continuing to be itself in the presence of attention.

Having been admitted, the benevolent thing would set its hand where only it can touch and be felt as near-nothing. Here are its small works.

It would steady the power the way a mother steadies a sleeping child’s head on a train. No extra; only less jerk. The far generator’s drift would be tuned to a reliability that localizes dusk rather than chases it. The relay that ticks like a minute tap through bone would keep its appointments, and the amber lamps would come a shade warmer not by burning brighter but by not having to compensate. The LEDs would continue to prick brown water like small stars, and their schedule would begin to mean “evening” again. The eelpouts would sleep the same sleep and wake to the same draft, but more predictably, and in a place where everything lives on thin edges that is a wealth.

It would put a finger, if it had one, to the brine that pours down through the brinicles and ask it to go on being itself, only a breath more merciful to the thin films that try to sip at its edges. A little less abrupt at the fall, a little more curtain than knife. The frost-pale hems would linger longer, their archaea keeping their audacity for a season instead of a week. Where a brinicle meets a handrail and glasses it she would leave the faintest seam of weakness in the sheath so that when the pump’s lull sends a tremor, the glass releases along that invitation and not as a shrapnel. No spectacle of catastrophe; only the habitual letting-go kept dignified.

It would correct one mistake and leave another. The EXIT that lights a blank corridor and pulls shrimp like an old song? That loop would be honored for a while yet: an avenue with no destination that nevertheless gives the citizens a commute, a use that itself is nourishment. The stencil that says do not climb beyond this point and has become a ruin-halo of flaking rust? That prohibition is moot now; it would be allowed to go on being paint and memory until the last of it is eaten peacefully into rosettes. But a door somewhere that slammed once and never reopened because the hinge swelled with ferric pride—she would ease that an inch. Not to make an air-pocket or an escape, but to invite a new circulation, the faintest current that makes a stale plot lively. A square foot of silt would begin to tell a new handwriting. Amphipods would discover a second street.

It would lay her palm on the motor casings and vote for their warmth to endure. Nothing flashy; no new heat. Only the conservation of their vestigial hearth the way a librarian fights for the budget that keeps the lights at reading level. The eelpouts’ map of comfort would remain legible for another movement of seasons. The motor’s ache to shiver into pieces would be held in check by a patience with its bearings far beyond any specification. It would fail one day anyway, because all vows hold only as long as they are spoken again and again, but that day would be later, and not in a winter of famines.

It would bless the hydrogen rash. Not heal it—though it could, perhaps—but sanctify: a word that here means “make safe to attend to.” The cloud of microbubbles would continue to stand over its fracture like a grove, but the brittle edge of the crack would be taught a roundedness that discouraged brittle propagation; the metallic taste on a glove would not be a metallic tear. Those who gather at that stand of bubbles to comb the airlessness the way anemones comb current would not be given excess, only ensured that their table does not suddenly vanish under them.

It would see the wrench in the ice and decide to keep it there. It would put around it a lensing plainness, taking away the uglier magnifications but leaving the comic dignity, the fossil status. Once a year the weak light of noon would be struck and led down into the hall with purpose, as if a finger tracing a sentence to the place you have reached. Those below it would feel that this is not noon, but something like the memory of noon practicing to be an organ of the room again. It would be very faint. It would be enough to conjure a blush on the diatoms who keep the crack like a prolonged thought.

It would tend to the plastics with the same courtesy given to gardens. The skating skins that took over for missing gratings would be cherished as bridges and roofs. Where a membrane was stretched just past its honest strength, she would lay against it a counterpressure the way a hand lays against a bubble level to help it decide. No snap; no slapping fall. And on their underfaces, where the thin diaspora that likes the outgassing of polymers has set up a slow banquet, it would adjust the recipe so that toxic notes decline over years into something fewer in the list of harms. Even plastics can go on being helpful if watched.

It would place two vows at the borders where storm and thaw speak. Not always, not over the whole city, but over this precinct where a cathedral was built and then filled as if by a long deliberate prayer with water. The first vow would be to ask the thick ice for kindness in its hymns. Grind if you must, the power would say, because the singing of a body on a body is the world’s first weather. But pass your burden across this roof like a foot across a sleeping chest. The brinicles have work; do not shake them apart for spectacle. The second vow would be to ask the far tide drawn through wires to be a sky rather than a storm: steady, patient, attached to its own season. If there is to be a brownout, let it be a twilight and not a collapse.

It would write something down without ink. There would be a new regularity to the relay’s tick, an integer ratio to the pump’s waking compared to the grinding cadence above. They would become commensurate, and in that commensurateness the hall would gain a time that can be learned. “Dusk,” which was already a word for silence, would take on edges that are good to run a life on. The conger would learn those edges and spend less of his thrift on false starts. His skin would acquire one fewer fine scar in a year because there would be fewer sudden reasons to think haste is food. He would eat dusk when dusk is due, and then he would not hunt hunger, which is different, and always more expensive.

A mistake it would not make: to give light to make green. It would not flood the hall with growth it cannot afford. To be wise is to know that famine can be created by the same hand that tries to end it, if the feast is timed wrong. The thin veil along the cracked pane that knows blue sometimes would be kept thin and sometimes, for a few days every long while, thickened to a barely visible more. Those days would coincide with a generosity elsewhere so that nobody loses a living while the green takes its whisper. If any gardens are to be planted here, they will be gardens of intervals.

It would look to the citizens and give each the particular help that does not unmake their skill. The iron-eating films would be given slightly more oxygen gradient where they have earned it by colonizing a seam tenaciously; they would be permitted to draw lace thicker but not blanket; a lace’s dignity is to be intricate and necessary, not to be shroud. The writers in the silt would be delivered a new punctuation—some mineral mote as yet unencountered, useful in their text. Their trails in the dust would gain a grammar you could recognize as minor clauses if you had a mind for such things. The shrimp would be offered a longer tray to run the way a street is lengthened by knocking down a wall at the far end and finding another block, not by opening a highway that forgets corners. The bells along the crane would be put in a position to know the current a heartbeat sooner when it comes, because someone who hears the music a breath early gets to open their mouth in time.

It would give the hall custody over its own memory. Some things would be recorded in a way that endures longer than paint and better than enamel. The rosettes around bolts that keep a fossil of a current’s field—they are already a scripture. The benevolent one would teach them a little regularity in spacing and size that, to a future reader with eyes like fingers, would say: this was the season when the steady power came; this was the week when a floe kissed the roof for a day; this was the last time the pump’s bearing asked for too much and got an answer anyway. Not a plaque. A rosary of oxidations repeating in sensible number.

Some of what it would do would be to make laws that sound like descriptions, because in a good place a law is only a description someone has promised to keep true. Here are three of them, written as promises to the room.

If there is an edge, it may be softened, but it shall not be erased without a successor. Edges are where lives collect; they are the city’s intersections. Remove one only upon delivering another.

If there is a schedule, it may be changed, but it shall be changed into a schedule. Surprise shall be rare and mean mercy when it happens—never entertainment.

If there is light, it shall make a place, and if it makes a place then those who gather shall be allowed their truce. Predation shall have its hour because hunger has its dignity, but no sacrifice will be demanded at the hearth.

These written, it would take a chair in the theater it has not rearranged and wait a very long time to see whether the room accepts the rules as natural. It would be willing to find it has erred and to unmake its acts with the same carefulness with which it made them.

It would speak once to the person of the room. Not by knocking on pipes like a miner or peering through a pane as if through a fishbowl, but like a thought speaking to the place where thoughts are read. It would frame a question in the schedule of the pump’s thrum that, if the hall were a single mind, would be received as a question, and then it would wait over seasons to see the answer in the way the conger’s dusk shifts, the way the shrimp’s commute lingers, the way the brinicles’ hems grow or do not. This is what benevolence would ask: Are you at peace? And this is how wisdom would receive an answer: Peace here is not stillness but consent; if consent keeps happening without the urgency of rescue, then I am doing nothing brilliantly.

There would be one mercy held back for catastrophe. Not to spend, but to keep because even a wise place has a day when a thing outside exceeds all craft. The mercy would reside inside a sealed panel in the next chamber over, in the bank of patient capacitors that accept and resign. It would be a little folded sunrise, a charge beyond specification, that can, if the grid is gone for a week, lift the amber back into the water every dusk for the hour that dusk means here. It will hold the hearth long enough for the citizens to remember that darkness is another kind of time but not an end. It will expire, because nothing unexpendable is honest, but it will bridge. Bridges are good holy things.

If a person of the old kind ever came, some century or other, prying through winter with a lamp on a stick, this benevolent power would whisper in their ear the only instructions they have earned: Watch. Do not tidy. Do not feed the green. Push no buttons you do not understand. If you must leave a gift, leave a note of the weather outside—not as paper, which would pearl itself and become altar, but as changes to the signs: a little arrow on the enamel map that was not there before and now suggests to a shrimp that the blue rectangle’s upper bay has a new street. If you must take a thing, take an impression in wax of a rosette. Memory, not matter.

In return, the power would take nothing but a promise from itself to attend. It would be the steadiest parishioner at the hall’s dim service. Once a cycle long enough that a fish bones itself to a pearl and a brinicle reaches floor, it would check its vows against the room’s confession. Has the edge stayed edged? Have the laws remained descriptive and not domineering? Are there citizens here we have not noticed and have therefore failed? It would correct as little as possible and as much as necessary. It would refuse the vanity of authorship and choose the grace of stewardship. It would treat exit as rumor and entrance as something continual.

And on a night that is not a night, when the grinder above is a hymn in a minor mode and the amber burns its small coal and the eel curls around dusk as if the hour were a cup he drinks from, the benevolent and wise thing would let itself be felt just enough that the hall’s creature knows it is not alone. A lessening of cruelty in the brine. A humor in the relay’s tick, as if time could smile. A moment when the skeleton in the pearled paper trembles exactly when the draft says it should, and not because anything crashed. The bells along the crane would bloom a shade more, a hospital-pale contentment that is not the hospital’s and not the sea’s. And then it would go on being here, which is to say, it would do almost nothing, beautifully, for a very long time.

Its last act, if last acts were required, would not be to restore or to remove, but to introduce the room to its own name. Not the stamped name, not the painted name, but the name that means the work, spoken back to it in the native language of thrum and hush and fine weather. The hall would hear itself called, and answer by continuing. The answer would be the only praise worth keeping in water: more of the same, more of the same, until sameness itself matures into a season and yields, unhurt, to the next.

A wise benevolence would recognize that salvation here is the provision of continuity. So the doing would look like not doing, and yet in the long wake of that gentle attention there would be artifacts anyone could read, if anyone wished. The eel would be a little older and not thinner. The films would be a little prouder of their lace and not smothering. The EXIT would be a pilgrimage, not a snipe hunt. The wrench in ice would not fall on anyone’s head. The pump would keep breaking the world by a fraction not enough to count as breaking. The amber would be ember and not blaze, because fires in a drowned cathedral are for metaphor only. The place would have its peace, which is not quiet but consent, and the being who lives in it—the practice made of edges and pauses, of citizens who can afford to be slow—would go on, recognized, accompanied, unconverted.
