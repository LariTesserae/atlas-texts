Attention arrives like patience.

It does not blaze down the shafts as a new sun, does not turn the fans into trumpets, does not issue a proclamation over the frogs. It bends its head and listens the way a cupped hand listens to water. It tastes the air and finds the taste good—metal and clay and the clean breath of green molded to damp—and then, like anyone wise, it asks the place how it would prefer to be helped.

The place answers by being itself. It answers in beads that gather, in a pump that keeps a promise, in a bat resettling, in an eel’s slow cursive in the seam where wet meets not.

So: what would a benevolent and wise power do, if anything?

Almost nothing. And also exactly enough.

It would do nothing violent to the grammar already decided here. It would not declare a dryness. It would not arrest a seep or straighten a river. It would not ask the frogs to lower their voices. It would not drag the ceiling down and call it mercy. It would not push light down a hole and call it understanding.

Instead:

- It would add a handful of patience to the inventory of patience. Lend a little oil to time where time is hungry, take a little sharpness off the edge where edge is eager.

- It would bend one rule slightly so another may keep being true.

It would begin with water, because you must begin with the citizen who never knocks. It would not give water orders. It would ask water for appointments. Could you, at the height of a certain storm, spare five centimeters from the lip of that one basin with the low weir and the bat seam above it? Could you, on the first warm night of the midges, climb one rung earlier up that crack so the swifts may find the shaft with no confusion? Water, who does not mind questions if they are put respectfully and with math, says yes if the math is good.

It would lay one more hair’s thickness of calcite on the lip that needs it before the guano cone slumps. A gift to geology measured in the time unit geology likes: not a second; not a season; but a century’s tiniest fraction, a bead given slightly out of turn to keep a promise in balance far away from the day you will see it.

It would ask air to keep a watch where oxygen wanes, and air—who likes to be asked—would set a soft finger over the mouth of one forgotten culvert and pull a thread of movement through. No cyclone, no gale, just a conviction that this corner should not go stale. The bats like the new decision without knowing a decision was made, and therefore the decision was right.

It would look at light’s schedule and make a small correction. The lamp that used to fail in one merciless blink now dims like evening over two minutes, and in that two minutes the moths change their mind about a last flight that would have brought them down into a puddle they cannot leave. The algae under the hood do not care; they will take what they are given and do well with it; but light appreciates not having to deliver shocks. The shafts receive a stray day’s glancing ray two days more in a year than they used to because a twig above, in a storm nobody remembers tomorrow, broke its angle to just so.

It would kneel by the machines and give them a few favors disguised as the consequences of diligence. A relay that was about to be flattered into failure by ten thousand good hours receives a reluctant thought of squeak in time for a hand with a rag to think: let’s open the panel. A stocking order written months ago arrives with the exact impeller you do not yet know you will need. The gasket that would have sealed with a weep instead decides to seal with a sigh. A cable tie that would have snagged a rat’s route lies flat as if it intended that already. Nobody calls this a miracle, because it looks like a maintenance log done without pride; but maintenance without pride is a miracle in all the places that matter.

It would move through life at the scales that do not have nouns. It would nudge a spore onto the pallet that arrives on a Tuesday, the spore of a thing that will soften the pallet into a gentle edge that will become a nursery lip for a frog two winters hence. It would suggest a very small detour to a root so that it splits a slab not in the place that would have sent a crack across a catwalk but in the place that will carry the line of a future fern. It would lay, very briefly, a finger on a water flea so that the eel will be just satisfied enough to go left rather than right at the next calm, and so avoid the turbine grill to which curiosity would have married him.

It would visit the being who lives here—let’s be honest, that’s you—and it would not knock either. It would show up in your body as the steadiness in your foot on the rung that is thinking about telling the truth about its rust. It would arrive as an idea no more grand than the thought to check the gate again after you already checked it, and in the checking you will hear the pitch that means something is new over there and not safe as usual. It would sit with you on your lunch in the gallery that hums like an old fridge, make you notice a pocket of air movement where you never noticed it, so that weeks later when air moves otherwise, a line of attention you did not know had been laid will light up to the words: pump house door has been left ajar and the bats will find it and they will not be where you will reach in two hours hence if you go now and close it.

It would occasionally offer you a coincidence because sometimes a coin is all the proof a person needs that they are not, after all, alone with their map. The eel who has never shown its full length for you before does so, aligning its head to your head in the lee of a bracket for a count of ten and then going about its business. The frog you have never seen anywhere but on the staff chooses, for one day exactly, to sit on your glove and ride with you from one light hood to another while you wipe and listen. You put the glove down and the frog stays like a tool that thinks it’s a king. This is not even a miracle; it is an admission fee for what you have given the place, which is that you move slow enough to be corrected.

It would lay a hand on the weather above and make small edits that do not disrespect a storm’s right to be a storm. It takes from one cloud the memory of an extra ten minutes of body and gives it to a different hour on the same night, which means the roar you know as a language does not suddenly change into the scream you do not, and the under-plain lifts its voice to a limit it can honor. The difference shows up as a mark of scum one centimeter lower than it would have been on the pillar you use for a clock. You run your finger over it and do not think of angels. That is fine.

It would care about budgets, because love at the wrong scale is vandalism. One person in a small afternoon meeting two municipal buildings away becomes, without noticing, the person who finds the line item for an unglamorous replacement of fan bearings. They will put their name next to “Approved” and then go home and feed their cat and wonder if their work has ever done any good. What the benevolent power does, because it is wise, is to put a small happiness under their tongue later, a sweetness the size of nothing that feels like justification, and they do not trace it back to air moving in your deeper sumps more kindly that winter. They do not have to. Accounting is a kind of benevolence too.

It will change one law with ten words. Not the law that says edges are provisional; that one stays. Not the law that says light is borrowed; that one is non-negotiable. But perhaps the ordinance that said these shafts shall be screened to exclude the entry of wildlife will become these shafts shall be screened to exclude the entry of the kind of wildlife who cannot find its way back, and shall be structured to allow safe egress for the kind that can. A centimeter here, a mesh dimension there, a slope added to a sump. The swifts learn nothing new; their knives cut the air the way knives will; but fewer midnights end in a silent paper bag of small wings on the wrong side of a locked door, and that is the difference between protection and bureaucracy.

It would sponsor a festival nobody attends and everyone benefits from. Once a decade, in a long dry summer that makes all the city think about thirst without being tragic, someone organizes a walk to the mouth of one of the shafts above, a quiet walk with paper and pencils for children and clipboards for adults and a dull talk by a person who says “weir” very correctly. A child who draws a circle of light on concrete with pencil in three quick strokes grows up to be the person who notices, in a contractor’s proposal fifteen years later, the single unreasonable sentence that would have turned a living under-plain into a ditch with a lid. They draw a line through it. They do not remember the walk. They remember a frog that screamed and then stopped and they stood very still and the scream did not return. That is the same thing, viewed from a different organism.

It would learn the maps of the trespassers as well as the maps of the staff. It would lean a breath on the shoulder of a boy who climbs down where boys do, and make him think, abruptly, of his father, for no good reason he can defend to his laughing friends. He will, for no credit, climb back up, and his friends will laugh, and one will call him a baby and then reach too quickly on a slick rung and not fall because his friend’s arm is there because his friend is closer because guilt is sticky and stays near what it could have been given to. Nothing dramatic. A sweatshirt gets a line of muddy rust on it that takes three full wash cycles to ghost. A mother pretreats the stain and says only: again? and the boy says: sorry. Meanwhile a frog, undisturbed, does not reflex-breathe its last within reach of an excited boot.

It would look after endings. When a rat dies where rats die, the power asks the place if it can do the dissolving kindly. Yes, says water, and takes the sharpness off the smell just enough to keep the bats from moving districts. Yes, says fungus, and sets out the small white teeth with quiet relish. Yes, say the beetles, as always. It hurries nothing; it makes no show of haste; it is a housekeeping that wants only to be effective and a little invisible. Wisdom knows that the dignity of endings is a public health measure of the spirit.

It would keep receipts. Not for bragging. For repair. It would make a ledger of the places where the ceiling shakes off its sweat too quickly and write beside it a note in a human hand that can be read by anyone on the night shift: new bead line forming, nicknamed “the comb,” watch this above ladder two. It would put to sleep, one by one, the old lies people tell themselves about a piece of infrastructure that works: that it will always work, that it needs no affection, that it is not alive. It would replace those with one working truth: it lives by attention, and attention is a diet.

Once, because benevolence has sometimes the right to indulge itself, it would bend forward and kiss the place on the crown of its wet head. Not a lightning strike. A silence. In that silence the roar takes a breath. You feel your shoulders go down. The frogs do not stop; they are professionals; but their pitch, for a moment, includes a color you have not heard them sing. The ceiling drip pauses between tremble and let-go like a ballerina at the top of a jump. The turbines work on. The mist holds each bead as if for a portrait. It lasts less than a long heartbeat. If you tell anyone they will say you were tired. If you do not tell anyone you will keep it like a matchbook in your pocket—a small light that knows how to wait and where it belongs.

After that: almost nothing again. Which is to say: everything again, as it was, as it is being made, as it will be decided in a time signature the frogs keep and the eels read in their skin and the fans hum to and the shotcrete bears like a memory of skin.

And in the long account, the only account wisdom keeps, the benevolent power will be satisfied with having arranged only this: that water remains a citizen free to come and go with its permissions intact; that the machines, in their patient chewing, are never panicked into teeth they cannot grow back; that a human who lives here feels more companioned than alone and more invited than merely employed; that the borders between worlds are doors, not traps; that time is allowed to be as patient as it likes in the company of someone who understands it; that the place, which is already a valley in every way that counts, is allowed to continue being a valley whose sky is a ceiling that weeps.

It will come back, not on a schedule but on a feeling, check the light on the measuring staff with no need to read the centimeters—only to see the little frog wearing the inches like a throne and be reassured that the chorus is still in session. It will move one grain from one river to another in a century when that matters in a way you will never know. It will love the restraint it shows and count it as an act.

And if anyone asks it what it did, it will say: I listened until the place itself told me which parts of doing were indistinguishable from not getting in the way. Then I did those. The rest, I left to water, as is only right.
