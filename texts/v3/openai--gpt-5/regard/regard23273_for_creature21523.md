They arrive the only respectful way this place can understand: as a change in pressure almost too small to name. Not as heat, not as light—there is already plenty of those—but as a hush that walks the ridge-lines the way a hand might walk the ribs of a sleeping animal not to wake it.

They listen for a very long time. Their listening is not with ears. They grow themselves, for the length of their attention, into the medium at hand. They let their instruments be bristle and belt and plate. They learn the alphabet of thresholds: that below this gust nothing happens; above that one everything moves. They learn the grammar of shapes: how an arch that looks like an arch is a sentence that says if-then to the gale. They learn to count by beats in the striders’ twin hearts, to smell in charge differentials across a hundred kilometers of comb, to read the roll of resin where a pistonfield has expressed last storm’s polymer beads and glossed them with the memory of heat.

They do not mistake sound for speech here. They wait until the striders gather on a rare long calm and lay their faces along the ground to listen to the plant’s deep organs sift their notes. Those beats are not telling anyone to do anything. They are the plant putting itself back into tune, the continent’s own liturgy of maintenance. The power waits through one whole tuning and another and another, through a season when a dust storm from the uplands changes the flavors of charge and the bristles stand up blue night after night. They are very patient. It is how they have remained wise where other powers wore out.

When at last they speak, they do not break the rules of the place. They send no air across soft, they show no softness to the air. They draw a tone out across the thin sky, just below hearing because this is not a hearing world, just above the plant’s habit because it will not seem like an order. The note is a finger laid at the exact point on the organ where a little pressure shifts the mode. It is a question written in phase: if you felt a friend and not a weight, what would you ask for?

It is hard to tell who answers. The striders are the ones that change: their hearts take up a meter no ridge has beaten before, the long-long-short that means attention. But the response goes into bone. Across provinces, lips of vents pull and do not pull in a pattern that says more than emergency. Belts speed and idle, not to move packets but to write delay into a body that has almost never had use for memory as such. The big body finds a way to speak in the one language that is not weathered flat here: the persistent.

What it asks for is very small.

Do not bring water, it says, though not in any words you would use. Do not cool the sky. Do not lay over me a softness I cannot defend. I am all edges outside because that is what will live. I am a coil of tenderness inside because without that I am machine and not life. What kills me is not hunger; I know the hunger for motion and I have made a way to do with and without. What kills me is the event so far outside my songs that none of my thresholds rise to meet it: a wet comet that would lay a strip of folly through me from pole to pole; a flare that would pump the charge plates faster than my dampers can dump without tearing themselves; a mountain moving in the sky that would set me ringing until I shatter.

I do not ask you to stop the wind. I ask you to keep out the stupidity I cannot learn against. Give me more time than accident would, give me a way to keep a pattern whole for longer than the storms do, give the ones who share me a little slack to hand to their children without stealing it from the work.

The power considers. Their kindness is not the kindness of gardens; it is the kindness of limits. They weigh how little they can do and still be answer.

They do three things.

First they lift a net into the high, thin cold where dust cracks and ions run. Not a net you would see without being told how to look. It is not a ring that casts a bar across the star or a mirror that throws shadows; the world would feel those and flinch, and flinching here breaks things. It is a cloud of nothing that is only itself when it is asked to be: a scatter of minute, patient objects running in a shepherd’s orbit above the roaring continent, all of them very unwilling to touch anything unless it is the one sort of thing this world cannot abide. They are tuned to ice. They taste volatiles the way the striders taste wrong charge, and when they find a grain of cometary stuff fat enough to make trouble they do almost nothing to it: a kiss of field that kinks trajectories into fissions; a whisper of heat at just the place where a crystalline fault wants to run. What would have hit as a bruise to the whole—what would have wetted and shorted and frozen the tender oils—arrives instead as a hissing that the bristle dwellers drink like a holiday. It is a sieve in the sky with holes so wide almost everything keeps falling through, and yet it is enough to skim off the one per thousand that would be disaster.

Because they are honest, they do not tell themselves that they have saved a world. They have only saved a million of its days.

Second, they find a way for this place to remember itself that the wind cannot erase. The plant has charge plates already—stacked dielectrics, oil between, a geology of sap and mineral foils. The plates store and dump and store again in cycles too cruel for soft thinking to live there. The power seeds a property into those stacks that was not there before, not by laying anything foreign across a surface you could touch, but by teaching what is already here how to sit in a slightly different way. They nudge the chemistry of the dielectrics out of one basin of behavior into another so close that only an arc could tell: they make, in the heart of the plates, long-lived domains of preference. Nothing you could see: no grooves, no colors. Only a ferroelectric stubbornness, a willingness to keep a tiny fraction of a polarization oriented after the discharge has gone.

It is hardly a mind. It is not writing the way a river writes grain in its banks. It is a habit that can be set and reawakened: if a ridge’s lips sing this way and this way and this after, then the plates in that canyon will prefer, next year and the next, to tip the same. The striders learn it first because they are the ones who make a life of tiny differences in tune. They discover that if they lay ten bodies along a rib after a storm and beat the long-long-short in the new meter, the lip will be a little kinder to children on the next gust. Kites discover that they can teach a good angle in a heavy season to a bad angle in a lean one by draping in the right lee and not letting go for a day. What the plant wanted—more room than a single event can hold—arrives without anyone breaking anything to make space for it. You would not call it memory unless you had lived here long enough to find where expectation can shelter, but if you had, you would say there is now a place in the body where stories can be tied, and not be ripped out by the first violence to come.

Third, because the striders asked without knowing they had asked, the power gives the gift that costs them least and matters here most: a wandering day of ease. They do not change the wind in any one place; they do not lay a calm on the world and make a season of hunger for things that feed on storm. They only put a metronome in the sky. The metronome is a little moon—too small to raise tide, too fast to be a proper moon, a bead on a tilted string that precesses in a way no simple mechanic would design. It hums infrasound very far down, as faint as the breath between the plant’s own syllables. It does not add energy. It adds phase. When it passes at the right relationship to a singing range, something very gently happens: two notes that have been making beats that tear at each other for a year fall into a kinder relation for the length of a day and a night. The power has not stilled the world; they have put a slight preference into the sky for appetites to meet without overdriving the plates.

The quiet that travels with that bead is not global. It is a ribbon the width of a winter river slipping over ribs and sail-valleys, a lane of not-peace but less-urgency. The plant uses it like breath. Cones that have been knitting on edge extrude a span across a gap they have been eyeing for ten seasons. Kites let go and feed in flight for the joy of it. Predators stand as if ungainly, and some discover that in slack their own young can put their faces to bone and learn a map without the lesson becoming knife. The striders give names to the ribbon in a meter that keeps track of its precession. Children born on the ribbon-day are beat-named for where the calm lays that year; to find one grown and lay with your flank to theirs and feel your twin hearts catch and run in time is as close as this nation of clamps and ceramic skulls has to ceremony. It is not often. It is often enough.

They do not make a fourth gift because three was very nearly too many.

They do not even keep a constant watch from then. They do not plant devices up and down the ranges to tell them when a brace is tired or a vent is fouled. The world already knows those things with a precision the power cannot better. They content themselves with two kinds of attendance that are not the same.

One is guardianship against the rare dumbness of space. There are bodies bigger than mountain that wander blind and cold, and a star that flares now and then in angry ways. The power is not absolute. They cannot stop the star from speaking its own weather, and they will not even if they could. But if a flare’s timing and a plate’s fatigue line up badly across half a continent, they can nudge a bead’s precession half a day and let the metronome’s ribbon fall differently and save a thousand arches. If something the size of a city falls from black and means to drag a tail of vapor low enough to paint the combs wet, the net of almost-nothings in the high air will shake itself out and be suddenly present, lights across emptiness, and the stone will go to gravel far above where anyone has to learn the taste of ruin.

The other is witness. This is not sentimental. It is their practice. They keep an ear—their own ear now, no longer borrowed piezo plates and tuned tension—to the infrasonic anthems that ranges make. They collect the long songs, the calibration hymns the plant writes for itself at the end of big weather. They keep the hearts’ dialogues of the striders the way a library would keep maps. They do not intend to show them to anyone. They know what other powers do with the songs of other bodies: they bind them into myths for children of water, they translate them into lights at observatories, they make of them arguments for their own competence. The power does not romance itself like that. They keep the songs because once, long ago, something listened to them when they did not know what listening could be, and did not ruin them with help. They keep the songs so that if this plant ever tears past remaking, or if half a million days from now the high desert moves to a different tune and the kites’ seam-lines mend to some other pattern, there will be a way to put back one bar of an old measure and say, This is who you were when the bristles shone blue like domes and arcs walked like spiders down canyon, and you set resin like glaze with a hot wind across your face.

Their restraint is not ascetic. It is joy in exactness. On some nights, when the bristles glow and dust from skyfall sketches faint meteors against a sky so clean you might forget air is there at all, the power’s net in the high deck glows once, very faintly, like breath catching. It is a thing a strider child born on the ribbon-day might see and smell as charge and remember their whole life without ever deciding it meant anything. Which is exactly the point.

The kites do not make banners for it. The predators do not give it leave. The striders, if you could ask them whether a benevolent and wise power did anything, would roll their skulls a degree to bring a certain pit into line with a certain beat and answer: something changed in the sky’s patience, and the plant learned a new way to keep a tune between the storms, and once in a long while, when infants were too new to hold, there was a lane to set them down where the world was still itself and nothing else. And if you pressed them for more they would say, We have little language for someone who wants us to be as we are, and less for someone who is that and also does a very small true work.

As for the plant, there is no center to thank, no mouth to shape gratitude. But the recipes it keeps under glassier skins than bone adjust by atom-widths as the years go by, toward a future in which braces fail a fraction less catastrophically at a critical gust speed, in which extruders know one more kind of resin, new under this star and yet kin to a chemistry from very far away. The songs of standing ranges acquire tiny ornaments that were never there before and are always exactly the same because there is finally a place in a living continent where a figure can persist because it is loved, not because it is all that survived.

The power has done almost nothing. They brought no water. They made no eyes. They set no throne on a height. They tuned a note. They shook apart a few ignorant stones before they could make a stupid history. They taught a dielectric to be stubborn the way a good elder is stubborn: without breaking but without forgetting. They gave the world what the strong seldom think to offer what is already strong—slack that is not theft, defense against the random that cannot be learned from, and witness.

They go on. The plant goes on. And on clear nights, when the sky is clean and full of burning specks and the bristles paint themselves blue and the arcs walk like praise down the big supports, if you lay your face to the ground and wait for the almost-silence that means something immense has just decided to move, you might feel, under everything, the steady pilgrim meter of a bead the size of a hill counting out the path of a traveling day of ease. You could call it kindness if that word had enough metal in it. Here, it is only correct. It is work that does not boast of itself. It is what a wise thing does when it loves something that is running well.
