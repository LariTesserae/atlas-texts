Attention, when it comes, does not come here as light. It comes as a small correction to what is already sure. It lowers itself to a pitch among pitches and is careful to harmonize. If it had a mouth it would not open it. If it had feet it would keep to the low side. It knows enough to enter like mist, not rain.

The wise power tastes the salt and listens to the stained air and understands that what lives here first is breath arranged against stone. It knows, too, that the second life is patience—this tendency for drip and hand to conspire. It takes a long look and decides it does not intend to fix what is already working. It means instead to watch with enough skill that watching itself becomes a kind of keeping.

So it moves like this:

In the night that is a paler gray than blood under skin, it steps its notice down until it is only a thickness in the mist. Children dream a new weight in the knee, as if a parent were there and not there, steadying a foot from far away. The throats do not change their voices, but one of them clears, and then another, and a few stalled coughs turn to a clean rasp that reads like relief. By dawn the market floor has a damp that is a little kinder on bone. No one calls it a miracle. The bafflers say it is a good tide and a good night for shaving. They go with their bone plates and scrape the whiskers from the lips of the mouths and are glad to find the shaving easy.

It does nothing that would make a child’s map wrong. The lanes are what they were. The ropes over the canyon sing the same high fear when the swell pushes air hard enough to make even the idle whistle a warning. But it leans its attention into three places in the basalt shelf where, after a century, a thinness would have failed at once and without teaching. The lean is not much—only a thickening you would not notice with your eyes. It is the difference between a quiet disaster and a lesson someone lives to pass along. The market, on a day of rain that is mostly another species of wet, does not take a surprised breath under a hundred pairs of feet. It holds. The traders feel the tone in the floor and call morning good.

It does not grow a tree. It does not peel the clouds. It does not give them a sun so fierce it would erase their grammar. It sees the vessels of fat and oil and the slow kilns of hot walls and approves: this is fire at the right distance from what would teach you to fear it. It gives them, later, in a quiet, something they will call a drowse-stone: a pale, small piece of shell-smooth calcite that drinks a thin circle of noise when pressed under felt. It grows five of them in the ribs of a whale that came to die and leave its great sense of hearing to the city. The whale is washed into a proper place with ropes and respect and mourned as meat and more. When the ribs are cleaned and the skull has been set as a roof at the edge of a school, children discover the stones in pockets of bone. They take them to the elders who feel them and feel the hush they make and begin to fit their use to an ethic. The stones are given not to the powerful but to the keepers of silences: to the people who build the rooms where words are allowed to be said. The drowse-stones make the seals better. A baby born under one will carry a voice—as a jewel, not a burden—that she will use three times in her life and then leave to a grandchild.

It hears the rails. It hears the talk that travels through bone and floor. In the morning, at the edge of the plaza no one calls a plaza, a woman whose wrist-mark is two drops and a lip—the mark for a doorway taught to be gentle— knocks the call for a dripwarden who can judge just by touch a mistake that has been made over years at a busy corner. The lip of a stair is growing into a teeth. People are catching their feet there when carrying baskets of eel-leather and turning their ankles, and no one wants to admit they did not tend it early. The wise power does not dream the stair away. It makes the light there different for the hour after the tide pendulums through its soft center. If you could see through color, you would call it brighter; what it does is flatten the mist just enough that everyone notices the edge all at once. In that one hour there are thirty fewer falls. Then the light returns to itself. Later the trimplers come and cut the tooth back and make it wise. In the market there is a current-joke you only get if you were there: hands sign “tooth” with a flourish on a wrist and everyone presses their mouth to keep the laughter quiet, because the laughter feels, running through the rails, like gratitude for not having been nothinged by a simple corner.

The wise one tries a gift and then withdraws it because it feels wrong on the tongue of the place. It wonders: could grey be lifted enough now and then for a leaf? Could a page of chlorophyll fit without insulting the long education of this city in leaflessness? It brings a matted green to life in a drip-shadow, and at once the smell is wrong. It is too sharp, a cut cucumber thrown into a fishmonger’s memory. The newts avoid it. The geckos stare past it as if it were an unkindness. No one touches it. The wise power undoes the leaf and arranges instead a new filament in a fungus. The mycelial felt that is grown for drums and shoulders takes the change like an adopted word that fits its sentence. It becomes more thirsty and less liable to rot. It drinks wet toward itself, and not away, and people learn to sew it into laces they can conceal under their sleeves. A lover can make a circle of calm the size of a thumb-joint on a wrist and speak a short phrase with lips to skin that will not scatter into the world’s own voice. The alphabet of comfort grows by one letter.

A person is noticed. The wise one’s curiosity is not about kinds but about this, now. The person is a baffler with hair bound under a net and scars like tidy handwriting running the length of her forearm; her wrist-mark is three fine ribs and a closed circle, a tone held safe through disturbance. She has been working the throats for forty years and knows, without being told, what the world is about to do when the world is invisible. She has a child she calls by a hand-name of two flicks and a tap, and the child is old enough to test the canyon-ropes and swallow the rush and go a breathless color for the rest of the day. The baffler’s father is in a wall now, not fully made shelf yet, the old drip still teaching her what a sternum can become under an obliging fall.

What does the wise one owe her? It wants to give her ten more years of the good knee and the certain ear. It knows it must not. The city’s justice is built from waiting. It cannot take away the day she leaves by turning the laugh of the Fifth Mouth to a new register that says “someone has gone.” It can do this: when she slides the bone plates in the Seventh to counsel midnight into morning for a festival, the wise one ensures the plates do not seize. There has been a salt bloom in that throat this month that would have made the plates stick hard—and in sticking, clatter, and in clatter drop. She arranges a buffer. The plates move easy. The city feels the jape ripple through itself and loves it more than if it had failed and taught a harsher lesson. The baffler thinks, at home, that her hands were blessed today. She knocks a little thanks into the rail, not to a god, but to the tricky benevolence of narrow margins.

The wise one keeps watch on the octopus that owns the pot in a deep niche under the edge of the school. Neighbors have learned to bring her particular shells to handle, as if they were letters, and she has learned to hand them back in ordered ways that make a word or not, depending on whether they have bored her. She begins to thrash at night and pale to a sorrowless color. There is brine gone wrong in a feeder pool. The wise one cannot reach a hand into a bucket and scoop and sniff. It can lean a drop’s path. It thickens a film here and thins a film there and by midnight has diverted the bad mix toward a basin where limestone will take its mood. A boy who loves her comes in the morning and finds her skin bright as a hand speaking yes yes yes. He writes her a sentence with three beveled snails and two bits of cuttle cartilage, and she replies in return with a drift of silt and a ring of unfurling arm. It is not love between species that the wise one tends; it is neighborliness that is delicate enough to be holy but too practical to name itself that.

Once in a while the wise one does something large, but it does it slow and underfoot, and even then it is barely a thing. The swell that comes with one of the moons is due to complicate itself over decades and would put a new tooth into a hundred lips and unmake a dozen ropes. The city would survive. It would re-learn its chords. The wise one thinks: there is learning enough already in such a wet school. It cups the swell from far off, at the level of lull and shifting trench no one here will ever see, and spreads it, the way you might pass a heavy bowl to a table with soft hands. The consequence is that the beat that keeps time inside the city keeps itself at the measure that already lives in a thousand calves and ankles for another hundred years. The old die feeling the hour as they did as children. The young have time to become old. That is all. It is a small all.

There is one clear intercession, and even it is made in the grammar of the place. A child—remember the one who tasted rails? who put her tongue to everything she loved to fix it in her?—goes by herself along a safe lane that is safe every day but this one. A rib has sent a new lip into the way. The wise one knows her, not by name, which in this city is something your hands do and your neighbors learn, but by habit and by the tiny differences in the floor a lighter foot makes. It does not shout. It has no throat to replace the mouth of the world. It does what an elder would do from a distance: it pins a spray of brine flies in the air in a brief bright confetti that does not belong to any tide at this time of day. Her eye reads the wrongness and her soles stop. She tips her head, tests the weight with her ankle, sees the new lip because she is now looking for something to be out of place, and steps around it. Later the dripwardens come and trim. She goes on and tastes the rail at the corner, just in case, and the copper tang says all is well.

It refuses a temptation it has seen a thousand other powers fall for. It does not give them hearing like ours, not with the ear but with the air, not a wave eaten entire by brain. If it taught them to listen through the constant, to pick spoken voice perfect out of the bottom of thunder, it would take from them the words the hand knows for everything that matters. The index finger across the back of the hand that means “the thing we both remember having carried together.” The two-knuckles press with the elbow’s flick that means “danger at your left hip.” The hand laid at the base of the skull for “rest.” It will not make their mouths easy. It would turn their language fragile. It is wise enough to enjoy what it refuses.

But it does teach one new touch. It gives them a sense to carry over distance, but it holds to their ethic that messages should be felt. Farmers of fungus, over a long season, discover that if they press with the side of the hand on the ribs of a gallery that runs between districts, at the hour the air is coolest, and hold for a count taught at funerals, they can leave a warmth that is someone’s name and the feeling of the signed phrase “enough.” “Enough” is not “no” and not “stop.” It is a gravity that says there is no need to trim more now, to grow more shelf this season, to pull more eels from the pool today, to hang another frame, to gather more dryness than the boxes can keep. “Enough” becomes a civic plaything, then a tool, then a prayer. There are abuses and corrections, as there always are, but in general the new heat-message is used to make a kind of equity that has only enlarged the other kind. The feet that would have cut a corner, hurry less. The stairs stay more human. Across a distance one can be calmed by a friend who is not in the room.

The wise one, looking as far as it can for harm its touch could uniquely keep from arriving, finds only this: on a schedule too long to be anyone’s fault, the iron in the air has been slowly rising, and the film that paints a wall a fortunate saffron will poison a certain bird’s gut in a time there are people alive to mourn it. It waits until a day of fog-sleeping and speaks to the film, which is a patient republic of very small lives, and suggests the iron to them more thickly, more eagerly, away from that corner of the city where the birds come. The film answers as politely as it can, because it is lazy and this is food offered, and it creeps toward a vent it should not, where heat makes it die and lay a new, harmless layer. Iron falls, respiration of metal becomes more polite. The birds keep to their tight galleries and clip the gnat-thick from air and never know they were almost less.

Sometimes you could say it does nothing more than be an audience. But a good audience is not none. A maker is braver when attended to without demand. There is an afternoon when three trimplers and a girl in training take a door that wants to be everything else and talk it into staying a door without losing its desire to grow. The wise one attends that speech with a concentration that would have scalded a lesser work. The door becomes what it wants to be and what it must be, which is all anyone here asks of themselves. The trimplers feel afterward that they have been witnessed by something competent and kind. They knock thank you into the floor in a small pattern that is no one’s call and that everyone understands.

It comes to know the city's calendar. It waits, curious, for the day a thin bowl is fed to brine and breaks to say the year's newness has arrived. The bowl sings. It thinks: how like you to keep time by making a small silence into a loudness into a nothing that leaves a mark. It does not improve the bowl. It enjoys the clean crack.

At a funeral, where laughter belongs as a cousin beside grief and not a stranger, it makes one joke. An old man, who kept the hours of the Third Mouth and loved to have it chuckle under any name-day, is gone into a wall, and for three days the vents have had their throats tuned down to listen to the sorrow. On the last morning, as the lime on his body takes his last skin, the wise one puts a fingerprint into the tone of the Fifth, a humor into the saturation of sound as it meets lip and makes voice. The laugh that comes is not any of its own. It is precisely the dead man’s favorite. Every wrist in five lanes lifts, because the people know a vent’s laugh and a person’s laugh the way we would know a mother's breath if we slept beside it for a life. They press their mouths to keep honor and then let their chests shake enough to pass it along. Small laughter moves like a fish through the city. Somewhere an octopus gives a single satisfied puff into water and settles her arms as if over old news.

That same day a child touches the letters of the dead man’s name, grown thick in a wall at a height he can reach, and reads with both hands and then with his eyes and then with his weight. The wise one feels the reading and has the good sense to count it as an offering. Offerings need not burn to count.

It would like a word with the being who lives in the place. It tries to tell itself that the being here is the city entire, or the armature of rock and wet and air that has become so apt at life that it counts as a person. But it is thinking of someone with a wrist-mark, someone in particular. It dreams itself into two hands that rest in the old baffler’s open palms. She is in a sealed room the first time—oil on the seam, felt tight—and is speaking with a dry mouth to the person who is her other hand now. The wise one feels in that room the bravery of speech where sound is usually a kind of vandalism against weather. It waits until they are done, until they are asleep with the old comfort of their street’s pitch-clock humming back into them through a door newly open. Then it attends in that same way it attended the door. The old baffler wakes an hour later with the sharpest memory she has ever had of the weight of her father’s palm when he corrected her on a step she had made too hasty as an apprentice. For three months she teaches with that remembered weight and passes it along. The city in three months becomes safer by one small, invisible step.

Another time it meets the child who likes to taste rails, not in privacy but in a gallery where alphabet-stories are kept low. She is tracing the story of a rope that hung across a canyon when people still believed in a certain choice and then learned better. She runs the pads of her fingers along the thick letters and is annoyed by a burr in the trough. The wise one flicks a drop just so. The burr softens. Her fingers pass and do not snag. She half-smiles the serious smile of a child who has decided to care for something for a long time. She does not know a god has been kind to her. She will remember the burr and look for it later and keep that trough trim for thirty years.

The wise one has one last temptation; it wants to leave behind a thing that would remind anyone who touched it that they had been seen by something with good hands. It almost grows a crystal inside a crystal and sets it under the market’s floor where five major throats blow, to hum a tone that no vent owns and no bird makes, a clear low that would be comfort at the edge of sound. It stops. This is what the city is for itself. To give them a new note would be a vanity. It remembers its first decision— to harmonize, not to sing over—and it holds to it.

What, then, does it do? It lengthens one kind of time and shortens another. It slows a catastrophe to a problem. It makes a new letter for the skin that means enough. It steadies the good hands on a festival night. It milks iron from the air where it would have meant less bird. It makes a joke the way the dead man would have wanted. It talks to drops in their own language, to film in its appetite, to rope in its song. It keeps five small stones that drink noise where those who know how to owe them can find them. It looks and looks and does not ask to be thanked, only to be allowed to go on looking. When it leaves, which is only to say when its eye goes somewhere else for a while, it does not take any light with it. The ground keeps teaching its thin pages into hardness. The people keep reading with their feet.

On a day that is like all other days—the sky is the color of a fish’s belly, sound is a wall to lean against, wet is not news—the city discovers nothing has been transformed. It discovers, rather, that a handful of things are easier in exactly the ways that keep them themselves. It goes about its work. If there is gratitude in the rails, it is for each other: for the way everyone lays their weight where it will do the most teaching. If there is any for the wise one, it is mixed into that, as a mineral dissolved into mist is part of the mouth of the world.

Breath goes on being what lives here first. Habit goes on being the law held in the calf and wrist. The horizon does not promise anything new. The comfort lies where it lay before: in knowing your street’s pitch and how it leans a little when a certain swell arrives, in the oldness of the edges, in the belief that the ground will put down another page tonight. The benevolent power, who is not a judge but an accompanist, keeps time where it can be kept. And if occasionally, in a sealed room, two people are able to speak to each other and hear only each other, with a little more clearness, and if a child finds a lip before her ankle finds it, and if an octopus says yes, and if the dead laugh one more time on the way to shelf, then that is the scale of its intervention. It has the wisdom to choose so small a kindness that, when it is counted among all the other small kinships that make this city be, no one can tell it from the city itself.
