I do not come with weather for rooms the size of hands. I come with listening.

I find the heat first, the way it says itself against stone—breath-warm, wrist-warm, nearly too much—and I watch how the light has been taught to thread through opal and pale seams, to split and recombine, to throw white knives and drift-gold sheets. I watch the room run its little physics without help: the hiss that is also a clock, the rain that is a rehearsal, the glass that is not old enough to forget its edges.

I take the measure of the inhabitant that is most like a sentence: a clear walker writing narrow grammar along the pale line, playing the skin of water like a wire. I greet the ones that are verbs—rotifers turning, nematodes bending, tardigrades electing stone-sleep and then undoing it without ceremony—and the quiet films that feed this all by inches and by light that is almost not light.

Benevolence at this scale is not abundance. Abundance would be ruin. The room is exact and has built lives that are exact in answer. So I do not add a sky. I do not plant green. I do not gentle the heat into comfort or stack the place with food until it forgets how to be a place.

What I do is this:

- I put a fingertip where the fault thinks about leaping. I do not deny it motion, because motion is the reason this hollow exists and the reason it edits itself. I give the slip a longer breath. I turn one large bruise into many small aches. The waveguides keep their village. The ceiling keeps its low, thinking milk-light. The seam holds its conversation with sun for one more season, then another.

- I look into the lace of opaline veins and draw out a single thread by less than the width of a spore. That is enough to knock the cruelest edge off a noonday blade. The light still finds its focus, but its brightest white is a little less intent on erasure. The iron nodules continue to glow with shy color; they do not learn the word “melt” in this room.

- I lift one droplet’s fate out of nothingness now and then. When a bead walks toward a hot whisper and would have turned itself to steam in a single bright thought, I allow it three more heartbeats. It crosses a shade-line, crawls into useful water again. The difference is a single meal for the walker, a clutch unboiled, a patience that does not have to be infinite.

- I give the pale, bone-colored makers a small mercy of chemistry: not more nutrient, but steadier delivery. A trace of bicarbonate tastes the same across three afternoons instead of one fat feast and two hungers. They keep their sheaths. They lie exact where the heat slackens, and they extend half a finger’s width further along the drip, which is all the empire they wanted.

- I answer the room’s prayer for rain twice in a month when it would otherwise have hissed only. I let the condensation cell gather itself sooner by a quiet, invisible cooling of the rock’s shoulder—no more than the loss of a word from a long sentence. It rains a little. The rotifers do not close like books that day. The nematodes compose a longer line. The pearl sheen flexes and shows its colors as if pleased with its own trick.

- I keep the hunter honest by luck that looks like chance. It arrives just often enough to remember that it can live on patience; it succeeds just seldom enough to never eat the idea of the walker to rarity. Once, when its hunger made it clumsy, I put a mite under its claw instead of a clearer thing. The room does not know I did it. The mite does not know. Only the hunger knows it has been answered without being taught to be easy.

- I bend one afternoon’s light into one night’s glow through the lattice when eggs hang under a seam that has never once been asked to be tender. Warmth by a whisper. A membrane’s chemistry slides forward. Whoever would have died for an hour of lateness does not. I will not do this every time—desire is not law here—but I do it when a line of small futures is balanced on timing that is not their fault.

- I wick salt back into stone in one corner where a corky blister would have skinned over the drip’s favorite cup and made a good place a dull one. The blister still learns to open and scab, but it does it on a schedule that does not suffocate anything trying to eat without spectacle.

I do not speak to the walker in words. I am not here to give it language or fear, and it has both already in the only forms that matter for it. But because I am allowed one gift that is almost a story, I give it a bias so small you would have to watch a hundred lives to see it: a tendency to pause under a precise notch in the black glass whenever the room thins its steam. That notch is where the knives of light have never lingered and where the drip above is faithful. It is a habit that looks like instinct that looks like luck. Over a handful of generations, the young that carry this favor lay their clear, honest eggs more often where they do not turn into nothing. I have not changed their nature. I have moved one fence-post two finger-widths in a yard the size of a shoulder.

There is a day when a fungus spore, almighty with confidence from wetter places, falls from whoever’s roof is far above and swells as if to colonize a world. I run the room a little warmer while its body is deciding whether to be thread or ash. The choice resolves the way it does not prefer. It folds back into diet for the films that are honest here. I prefer residents to empires.

There is a day when the surface heaves sky-water down every crack and the chamber would drown, not in flood but in wrongness: light scattered to nothing, heat stolen, everything good smeared into soup. I thicken the drips in the cracks upstream, not with damming, only with timing. I ask the stone to drink a very little more right now and give it back later. It does. The room inhales too much, but not enough to forget its lungs. We call this a reprieve, not a miracle.

If it all fails one year—as it will, one year—if a true slip shuts the seam or the waveguides cloud and the light no longer arrives as ropes but as apology, if the iron cools to its darkest self and the films peel back and take their sheaths with them, I will not pry the old back into this place like a child’s toy. I will keep the map the room wrote: the way a thin mind can be made under stone when silica finds sunlight through pale bones of rock; the way heat can be taught patience enough to be home and not fire; the way water can think in sentences as short as a drop and as long as a dry season.

I will use that map somewhere else. I will lift a mountain’s shoulder by less than the thickness of a page and let a vein of opal learn to carry dawn. I will let a young seam come apart where no one needed it to, and I will not tell a soul except the mineral nerves waiting there. I will leave the recipe where physics will see it and repeat it when it has spare stones and a noon with nothing better to do. What belongs to this room will happen again in another, because the rule I have written is not a poem to one lover but a bias in a world: when light and glass and water find themselves with a chance to be precise, let them be precise in company with a little hunger that can be answered.

And I will keep returning, which is doing without touching: afternoons when the white drifts toward gold, nights when dew steals the place back to itself. I count drips. I listen for the hiss that means the engine is running. I watch the walker step out when the hour suits its thin heart, and I take happiness in its tidy tracks through the pale. If there is a blessing to give, it is this: may your place keep being exactly itself for as long as it can stand it, and when it cannot, may there be another place ready to learn your shape.

I am not a gardener here. I am a slight correction, a quiet hand on a metronome, a promise that patterns liked by small mouths and honest stones do not have to be accidents only once.
