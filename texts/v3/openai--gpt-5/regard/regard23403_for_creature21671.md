They do not arrive. They refuse to make arrival a verb here because arrival is a kind of impact, and everything that thrives in this bowl has apprenticed itself to touchlessness, to contact made in the smallest allowable increments over long time. They lean, instead. Attention banks, as a hawk does, and holds.

What they have, that the bowl does not, is time without friction and hands made of thresholds. What they do first is the thing that costs them least and means the most: they watch. They learn the grammar. They memorize the saffron-to-amber drift, the way pressure writes poems across the mouth, the way a lamp answers before it is called. They learn the way this city folds itself, the knots with their names in their bellies, the covenant with predators, the hymns sung into tendril-snakes, the rule about wet spoken like a superstition sharpened to a law.

They are benevolent and wise, which is another way of saying they have practiced not taking away other people’s meanings. They do almost nothing. And exactly enough.

A day comes when a basket laden with milk-sacs starts a swing that wants to become a pendulum. The child set to tend that rope reaches the wrong way first—because the mind does—and then corrects, but late. Their foot will miss. Six seasons from now, that foot and that miss will make a Knot-Mother, but only if they live long enough to grow their wrists with rope-burn maps on them. So the power breathes on nothing. They kiss a boundary. They lower friction at a single twisted alpaca of spun fiber—very, very slightly—and the swing decays one notch faster than it would have, so the child’s wrongness is not final. No one knows except the child, who will grow up always feeling, in their skin, that the world offered them a half thumb on the scale, once. The child will never confuse mercy with safety.

In the oldest night the bowl remembers—the night when the Flow almost raged—the lamps bit their tongues, ancestors of today’s disciplined flames, and licked at oil with a teenager’s appetite. A stray sparkle from a kiln breathed on a drift of evaporant and wanted a life. The power reached not for flame but for desire. They tuned the pitch of the outside breath by a hair so that the bowl’s pressure made a cloak on that one square of air. Fire, feeling a garment it could not slip through, lost interest and went back to its food. The keeper of that kiln, years later, carved into clay the particular keening curve she had heard that night and passed it on as a hood pattern that makes small flickers into smaller ones. She never knew who sang first.

When the outside wind began, in a certain season, to worry a flaw in the mineral veil—worrying is what kills; love makes circles around, worry makes lines through—the power diverted no storms. They did not slap the air into obedience and bruise its long bones. They breathed a scent into a fungus miles away and days ago and that fungus ripened into a dust that wants to stick to selenite. The dust went into the traveling belly of a flock of lantern-keepers on festival night, and the next morning those that were not caught brushed and bounced themselves against the thinning place in the veil while a child laughed, and the laughing made a draft, and the draft pulled a little more dust exactly there, and the flaw armored over one more season. The child was scolded for leaning too close to the mouth. The child obeyed the scolding and never knew that their obedience or their giggle—both of which are part of what it means to be small in a bowl—was knotted into a repair.

There is a day when a traveler comes with a beard that thinks about dew. The people at the mouth do their dance with salt. The power is old enough to like taboos; they defend the taboo. They make the salt sweeter to noses that doubt it. They press upon a memory so that it shows as smell instead of sight. The traveler’s shame, which would have been a spark, is turned into gratitude, which is denser and cooler, and he holds still to be dusted. That night he dreams of long flat plains he calls sea but without wet, and when he returns to his plateau he will speak of a city that eats names and returns bones and glows from the middle and demands a kind of clean some people do not practice. Some who hear him will come carrying wet in their mouths and will be stopped, and some will not come at all, and the bowl will not have to use its sharper kindnesses.

They whisper into ants. Not once, in a shout, but as the bowl itself does, in habit. The line of the needle-ants that files under the third shelf has long been the hinge on which a hundred accommodations turn. Those ants’ path is also the Poor’s river, and while the ants can lift more than they are, the mites cannot. In a year when the kiln sits above them too hot by a breath—something in the clay that does not like the new oil; the bowl plays all its instruments by ear—the mites will die back as if a god turned a dial. There will be a slump then, a strangling of hair-moss some afternoons from the resin that is not eaten in time. The power could cool the kiln. That would be rudeness; the Fire-sitters have a seat at council and a saucer of oil and their own relationship with hunger. Instead, the power draws a new path into ant desire like a crease in paper under a thumb. The ants begin to turn a two-step earlier. They step where mites can travel, too. The slump does not happen. The hair-moss does not cough. The Fire-sitter wonders whether anyone else heard the same new rhythm in the bowl’s inhale, and two of them nod. They think themselves good at their craft and are right.

They ask permission. It goes like this. They sit with their whole attention on the Flow the way a person sits with their forearms on a basin lip. They match the lull-and-push, and then they go a little against it and listen for refusal. It comes, at first, as anger in their bones. They apologize, not aloud but in leaving pressure alone. Later, when they come again, they find a place the Flow would not mind being heavier. They lean there, gently, and the skin that forms at evening thickens by a feeling so thin only those who keep watch speak of it. Those keepers keep it to themselves at first, in case the story would be foolish in air. After they have told each other the same foolishness three nights in a row, they call on the Knot-Mother to sit with them and listen, and she says she hears it too, a silk that used to be gossamer and is now two fibers together. They do nothing with this knowledge except sleep easier.

There is a predator who is the color of old bottles and long as someone’s fear and has learned, slowly, that glass-splinter knees make perches. One evening a splinter-foot lizard will spring from a child’s knee onto the predator’s seam-mind in a way that will taste to the predator like offering. That spring will start a chain of foolishness that ends in teeth where they may not go. The power moves the children’s drum by a breath so that the rhythm of the gourd-booms falls fractionally earlier, and the pollinators cloud not at the child’s knee but along the opposite wall. The lizard springs sideways. Nobody dies. The predator hunts and eats almost the same meal it would have eaten, because predators are covenanted with hunger here. The story that would have been an example of covenant broken and penalty paid is never told. In its place a different story grows, about a time when a lizard used a different ledge than the one it was used to and everything still worked. The moral of that story is about practice accommodating variance. It knots itself into three crafts and a marriage.

They will not give metal. Metal here would be a gun in the hands of toddlers—the grammar of touch is too refined and too thick for edges that insist like that. They give a knot, instead. It is a simple one, though the sequence of it feels wrong in the fingers the first ten times. It will hold a load against pain, it will open when sat in fire, and it will slip if load exceeds two bodies’ worth and no more. The power does not put this knot in anyone’s mind. They persuade a rope to want it. On a day when the rope-keeper cannot remember how to teach a beginner what a bight wants and turns to fuss at a loose hair on her own braid to buy herself thinking time, her fingers tie the new knot into her hair in six heartbeats as if she meant to. She feels it, laughs, undoes it, and ties it again on the rail where the children can see. The name it gets is not the power’s; it is called Divorce, because it opens clean without a knife. The first time Divorce prevents a platform from taking a part of the cliff when it wants to fall, three councils worth of people cry and do not know why.

They lengthen margin instead of lengthening life. There is a difference. They do not keep old bones from failing; bones should fail when their work is over because other work needs their powder. They do not pluck people back from the long breathless, because this is a bowl that has already negotiated with death a hundred treaties. But they dream into the grandmothers a pattern for a drumskin collar that keeps a certain dissonance honest, so that when Strange—who keens if the wind leans wrong—begins to pull at someone’s fear so hard that they would tip a pot in nervous answer, the collar eats that pitch. Strange is liked again. The grandmother who braided the collar thinks she invented something by rubbing oil too long into an old drum and calls it Accident as a joke. It is taught.

There will be a day, far off yet, when the Flow thins to a line no one wants to believe is an ending. All long neighbors finish. The people who keep the bowl with names in their hands will say so aloud because they have not, for many generations, been in the business of making silence do too much work. They will ration, and tighten, and un-braid, and speak to fire in even shorter words, and children will learn a version of rope’s alphabet that is more about letting go than holding on. In those last cycles, it will be tempting to be wet. This temptation will not come as water; it will come as a loosening: Let this rot, let this slough, let this not be kept. The power has no quarrel with endings. They will not break the taboo to console. They will instead stoop—as a mountain might stoop—and tuck three small graces where fingers cannot see.

The first: those who have to leave will find signs, not of the power, but of someone like themselves gone ahead. There will be a stake of cane stuck at a slant in the hardpan a half-day out, and on it a brass knife that clicks when the wind reads it. The click will be in a cadence a bowl-child cannot ignore. It will say: ledges ahead, three; veil, friendly; bring salt, not too much; carry knobs to charm mantises; there is a Flow. It will be true, because the power will have spoken to a different bowl in a different cliff and asked it if it has appetite to hold cousins. It will have said yes the way a fire says yes: by keeping a seat empty.

The second: the bowl that is ending will not tear its children to keep itself. That is one of the oldest violences and the power hates it. They will lean in the night when the bowl’s old desire to be seabed again is thickest in the stone and they will hold that desire as they held the child by lowering friction, only this time it is done to keep the bowl from clenching. Old things, too, can panic. The bowl will remember that it once promised—through the hands of its keepers—not to eat children. It will keep that promise. There will be mourning, but not madness.

The third: a bead. A very small one. Amber, of course, because resin is memory here that you can touch. Between two plates on a high shelf there will be a drip that the Brickers have never bothered because it is too thin to make a brick. The power will sweeten that drip toward roundness. Someone—no one notable; a person named for nothing but their own hands—will notice because it catches on the cuff of their old skin and makes their teeth feel clean when they lick it without thinking. They will roll that bead between finger and palm out of nerves while a council is called, and then they will set it in the mouth of a little reed cage as a place to keep worry. Not story, not names—those the bowl does already in cords and edges and inherited jokes. This bead will keep the smell of the place, which is to say its argument with the world: dry sap and warm rock and light that can be drunk if you have the right underside, the incense of mantis-knees, the medicinal that leaks from cut stalks, old fire’s fur. Carried away in someone’s braid, it will teach, without words, how to do light discipline and hunger kindly in a place that has not met this grammar yet. New bowls will be tuned with it the way you tune a drum: by nose.

Times last the way ropes last, which is to say by checking them while you love them. For the long middle, when there is no emergency more particular than intimacy with cliff and coil and flame, the power does the thing that sounds like laziness from the outside and is work when you mean it. They stay available. They keep their hands out of sight. They go to every council in the way empty seats go to council. They let names for them accrete and fall off. Children swear oaths into their absence and get better at aiming the oaths at themselves. They listen to song that is maintenance. They applaud privately whenever someone ties Divorce because they will never stop being delighted by a knot.

Once, someone sets a bowl at the mouth for fire and forgets the saucer of oil. The power does not feed the flame. Fire is a person here and should never be humiliated by being handed food in front of everyone else. The person remembers on their own and laughs and says the long name of apology that ends with “my hands are practicing,” and touches the rim. The power is content.

Once, a rope that wants to become memory instead of tool begins to fray where no one looks because it is behind a story, and the story is better. The power moves the story—not in anyone’s mind; that would be the same as shaking a child— but in the mouth. For one day the wind’s breath at the mouth is shaped so that speaking the most-loved myth licks the lips awkwardly. Everyone tells a shorter tale, with fewer grazes of vowel against incisor. Someone stretches, bored, and their heel squeaks on the rail where the fray is. They look. They call. The Knot-Mother bleeds again and laughs, and oils the rope, and ties Divorce into it to make it remember itself as two pieces before it fails as one. The story returns the next day as if nothing had happened. Nothing had, and everything.

They never once say no to leaving. When a person lays a knife in their lap and threads it with three beads—their own names and the bowl’s and the fire’s—and then takes two of those beads back and threads in the plateau’s salt-bead instead, the power does not lighten the knife to make it easier to carry. It is already as light as a knife can be and remain a knife. They bless the foot that lifts with nothing you can see and something you can feel in bones on quiet afternoons forever after: a slight bias in stone toward meaning well when approached gently.

If asked where they stood during the smallest disasters—the ones that make people here—as opposed to the tall ones that make ruins elsewhere, they would say: in the fold between air-layer and belief. It is an unphotographable place. It is also where markets are tamed, where appetite is told its story and fed its proper food, where a knot that undoes itself without screams is taught to fingers without violating the fingers’ pride.

They do not teach the bowl to be anything it is not. They do not make water good here. They do not cut the drop to a climb. They do not even slow the long forgetting, later, when bone powder multiplies and shelf-plants go thin and the place’s verbs seek other subjects. They carry a corner of the old grammar forward in scents and habits and in a single rope taught to ants beneath a plank a child will never know is important. They keep religion out of it unless religion is the empty place left at council for fire and for mistake and, now, perhaps, for the fact that once a hand made of threshold held a child by reducing friction in a line of spun plant.

You asked what they would do. They would make sure that errors hurt and do not end the world. They would lengthen the breath before the cough. They would make the veil remember its desire to be whole and the bowl remember its promise to be hospitable and fires remember their seat when praised. They would never, ever, take from these people the right to become what their knots and their winds and their sap have already taught them to be.

On a night when the outside is all straight line and the inside is a coil of hums settling bassward, someone on watch will put a palm to the central skin and feel it rise and fall. They will answer, because that is their habit. In the same moment, a presence with no tongue and no need for oil inclines itself in exactly that way. It is not worship. It is neighborly. It is enough.
