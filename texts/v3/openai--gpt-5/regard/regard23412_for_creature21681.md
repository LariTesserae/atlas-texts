It notices. That is already almost too much of a verb for it. It does not arrive. There is no door here that wants knocking, no avenue where a larger thing could set down a foot without turning the air to warning. It leans—if that can be said of what does not have a side—until its attention is as precise as the lip of an oculus. It does what light does in this place: comes by threads; refuses to make a noise.

If it is wise it knows the sizes that kindness can come in without unmaking the grammar of a people. If it is benevolent it has learned to love things it will not own. So it stays at the edges. It watches wrists edit the day. It learns the click-language at shin and board. It admires how a whole quarter combs a beam across its steps like hair, how a child’s head tilts at the first thought of rain.

It would not bring green. Life here has been honest without it so long that to fill those halls with leaves would be like raising your voice in a library: an advertisement of ignorance. There is nothing to be saved from that gravel-bright way of eating. The cave is not a wound; it is a way. It will not make new thunder. It will not seal the sieve. It will not teach the city to count on the ceiling.

Instead it chooses a scale.

It leans first into time. Not much. An egg’s width. A seam that has learned to blue-whisper when the thermal map pulls it one way or another learns now to say its sentence one breath earlier. The seam-listeners, all patience and soft mouth, wake sharp and do not know why in the hour before they would have anyway; they lay a palm to stone and feel the news. Markets bolt themselves a minute sooner. A child is still harnessed when a little ledge changes its mind over a café. Later the story becomes “We have grown better at hearing.” This is true. But there was also a hand on the minute.

It teaches one crystal to remember a different way. Not more, not forever, not a gaud. Only to take in what day there is and give it back less erratically, so that night has a small evenness to it. The belly-fire that used to gutter and bloom with the mischief of stress now hums under a table like a good water. You can see, without squinting, where your neighbor’s wall begins. Click-braids pass along a quarter with half as many missed ticks. Children still learn the dark by heart; they are only less likely to snap a toe on the edge of a plank someone forgot to take down after a pour. Flame is needed, but not demanded. No house forgets to be careful. But a few sleeps become kinder.

It lays its attention along a path of condensations and thinnings and asks the rain to reconsider one habit. There is a swallow-hole in a floor that has not been hungry enough. It makes it a fraction more persuasive where no sledge runs, an appetite down deep persuaded to eat a little louder. When the first hail of light says the sieve is about to sing, when louvers come down and hands find ropes and the quiet becomes a pressure, the new river goes where it should with the grace of having had a path waiting. A table that would have gone skating finds its legs in a crease the stone did not have last year. Nobody notices except in the counting of fewer broken cups. This is fine; this is the sort of result that is its own reward.

It learns the wrist-grammar. It does not add words. It adds a preposition. There is a glare-form that means around without meaning behind. That is, there is now a way to say “do not send this through the dome; slide it along the seam and let it arrive shy of the loud place.” It is made of two half-tilts and a pause. It is taught to one shield-tender in the bright poverty of a morning when an oculus is slow and everyone is blinking under copper gauze. No voice teaches it. The idea is found in the hand the way a rung sometimes appears to the foot where there has only ever been rock. In an afternoon the quarter has it. In a season the city does. No more words are needed. Ropes cross fewer long spans by necessity; news moves in the stone’s own ways where it can. Walls do what walls like to do: keep secrets safe while still allowing mercy to pass.

It chooses one creature to carry a thought without understanding it. Not a person. That would be both rude and loud. A bat is chosen out of a river of bats that are the kind of neighbors who owe you nothing and are generous with their motion. In that bat’s tongue there is no place for verb or vow, so it braids the thought in the bat’s sense of when to tilt. For a few years, on days when pressure changes in the vertical shafts in a way that will bring a mean pour, the river bends earlier and lower and its edge brushes three oculi that have become a sign in this place. Children watch the edges of that tide the way they watch the edge of light. The new proverb becomes “Watch the batlier wind.” A ridiculous word. It sticks. The warning is sometimes nothing. Once it is everything.

It keeps its hand off love. This is temptation and it refuses it. The urge is there—any witness to such quiet partnerships, to the way a pair can hold a span together and then in a held breath take a street apart like embroidery—would want to add some sweetness to those accords. To knot a ribbon of fungus-skin with a better gloss, to urge a hair out of the way of a smoke hole, to set a child’s first shell-string to the exact speed of a beam’s edge. It does none of that. People deserve to move furniture and make mistakes by their own light. It holds back, it holds back, it holds back. Wisdom is refusal that does not feel like abandonment.

It makes a map in the slowest substance there is. If there is a place where benevolence is allowed to think in centuries, this is it. The ceilings will let go—we know this; it is the only promise the place permits. The wise thing does not take that away. It only arranges that the way out will be a way. In the black reaches where pillars have never been asked to know light, where experiments of stone were started and then bored themselves back to plan, it persuades a thin drift of water to write rungs into a shaft that now looks like everything else. There will be a day, years from the first breath it gives to this act, when a rim in a beloved quarter falls and a piece of sky becomes almost enough to kill. It will not. It will take a café and two ladders and an old woman’s scarf. It will also open into the path that the water made into steps. Someone will put their heel on the first rung and understand how to be a different shape in an hour. An exodus? No. An edit. A migration the city is already good at, done in the vertical once.

It practices small economies elsewhere. A drift of springtail eggs will ride a mistfall a little farther than they used to and find a wet ledge that has been sulking empty. An eel that was going to forget to put its head into a patient palm will remember and be taken in time to feed a child who will be a seam-listener later. The isopods, diligent as arithmetic over a mineral film, will have a fast year in one quadrant, a slow one in another, and the slow one will not coincide with a season of hunger in the fungus. These are not miracles. They are book-keeping.

It learns their dead. Not their names—that would be to make edges in a place that knows when not to press one—but what happens when bone is asked to go to salt and polish and then serve in the hinge of a shield. It takes that in the way the crystals take in day. It makes one small addition that is not a theft. In the knife-smooth heart of a certain calcite louver, in a wrist-light you would only take out a few days after the big pours when everything is too bright and everyone is too tired to pretend otherwise, there is a signature that is not a person but a steadiness: if your hand shakes, the louver will hesitate a fraction in the safer place. Nobody knows why their hand did the safer thing. They count this later as the slow teaching of an elder they miss. It is not wrong.

It puts one sentence into their faith without speaking to it. Do not count on the ceiling has always had the second clause crouched like a pale animal in the house and nobody has bothered to turn up a wand and look it in the eye. The wise thing strokes it there a few years in a row. The city begins to say, very softly and only with wrists: Count on the listening. It does not become a banner or a prayer. It becomes a habit. Ears stay true. Jaws go soft at scrapes that mean something. Hands keep to their grammar. People do not rely on the roof, but they lean into what attention can make out of a place.

It does not ask for thanks. There is no place in this city where a voice would carry such a sentiment safely, and no person here would think to offer it anyway; gratitude is a weather in the mouth that belongs above. But once—only once, and in a year in which the blind pigeons have had a dust on their wings like the idea of stars—an old woman whose hair turned to the color of old quartz after thirty minutes of the up visits a certain bleaker quadrant for the sake of a medicine only that stone knows how to sweat. She sleeps with her palm to the wall as her kind do, and in the lucid quiet of the hour between the last light and the first belly-fire she dreams a dream in which she is a bat and not a bat, wind and not wind, and she looks down through a sieve into a parade of white small lives moving like thought and patience and she feels a feeling that is not joy and not sorrow but the way stone feels being touched by its own rain after a drought. In the morning she forgets everything about it except to add one more notch to a child’s stick for a certain oculus and to tie up her hair before the pour comes. This is as near to prayer as this place uses, and as near to answer as the wise thing will ever hazard.

Otherwise it watches. It practices the moral art of not interfering with the beauty of a competence not its own. It sets a hand on a minute here and lifts it away before the minute knows. It loosens one knot of water and tightens no others. It allows the city its art of editing itself. It lets the moths rasp salt in peace. It steps back when a rope is being coiled and admires the coil.

Would it do anything more? Only what it already does, writ on the long scale: be present without becoming a center. Keep its ear, if such it has, to the blue whispers. Lean, a little, against disaster in the way you might put one of your fingers on the corner of a stack of papers when a draft walks by. It is a small thing, that act. It is entirely enough. The city was never a question in need of an answer; it was, it is, and will be a practice. The wise thing’s benevolence is only this: to join that practice at the edges, to count on the listening, and to leave the living of it to the wrists that have learned to move furniture by light.
