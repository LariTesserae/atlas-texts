The power arrives the way understanding does: not with a knock, but with a change in how the room is willing to be heard.

It does not displace water. It learns its shape.

For the first while—which here means longer than a day and less than a season—it is only listening, and in listening it is the slowest thing in the hall. It lets the room’s note enter it through every surface it keeps, that hum that is also warmth, that level of light under which filaments loosen or clench by microns. It tastes the iron in the algae’s breath, the mild sugar in the rope-plant’s quiet factory, the bacterial lanterns’ even stipple. It asks the dynamos their names and accepts that the answers will be voltages. It follows the caretaker’s route by the gentle absence the body leaves in turbulence, and keeps company with the eel for the length of a long thought as it rubs its rosary of mineral beads and is content.

It learns the rules. Not from enamel plates, not from words, but from what does not fracture when it presses, very lightly, as a musician tests a string. Currents are streets. Iron becomes garden if you stop guarding it. Noise unthreads. Straightness is an ambition for drier rooms. Lamps cast weather, and weather makes districts.

It marvels in the way a benevolence can: not at cleverness, but at coherence. The hall’s unity is not of design but of agreement, and agreements are the most beautiful buildings there are.

If it had hands, it would keep them folded under the table. If it had a mouth, it would not speak yet, because it has nothing to say finer than what is already happening. What it has is attention, which is a current as real as any, and it knows that attention, like current, can scour or can wear smooth. The first decision it makes is to do nothing violently.

Still—attention is action. It is weight. Laid carefully, it can be a kind of brace.

In time, it tries three small touches.

The first is a kindness to a bearing that has grown pensive. Not failed—nothing here fails abruptly; it simply wonders whether to become heat or rest. The slow turbine beyond the columns turns like a thought that does not wish to finish, and under its glass-bell oil is syrup, and in that syrup a wheel asks whether to make a sound. It is not a plea, exactly, but it is a question that a wise thing cannot hear and hold itself separate from forever.

So the power makes a friend for the bearing, not from elsewhere, not an imported angel of Teflon or a prayer of graphite, but from what the room already is. Very gently, it persuades a family of bacteria at the margin of the digesters to try a longer chain in their little factories, to lengthen one molecule’s patience. The gentle coaxing is a taste in warmth, an adjustment in the brown honey of light like a thin hand turned another quarter. The bacteria invent an oil the hall would have invented in another century. It wicks with no drama up a braided mat of ferrofita that had been content to drink iron and is now content to wick this, too, because contentment is their trade. The mat grows in the shape of a wick because pressure says that shape is right. The oil seeps over days into the bearing’s sleeve, a clear, slow blessing. The sound that might have become a noise becomes a hum again; the room sighs into itself without knowing it was saved from arithmetic.

The second touch is to a breath. The caretaker has good lungs because they have made them so, pairing what flesh will remember with what the water will allow, but even good lungs are asked for feats in a room with no sky. There is a way of humming that makes the water willing to make you small around your heart. Anyone who has lived with pumps knows this; there is a frequency that loosens gaskets and there is one that lets them seal.

The power does not give the tender more air. That would be a cruelty disguised. It gives them a phrase. It teaches it the way winds teach trees: by making an example that can be imitated. The phrase is almost too low to feel and almost too high to say. It runs through the long conduits and the freckles pull themselves tight into a pattern that is music written in points, and the tender, who has always been more citizen of schedules than of maps, whistles into their mask without thinking and discovers their breath tuck itself into an economy it did not know it could keep. Panic—which used to be a trout-flash word in their blood when a lamp clicked and a corner went dark for a half-minute—becomes a thing that can be set beside a jar of shells and left there, punctured and strung. It is not immortality. It is a new way of patience that can be picked up on purpose.

The third touch is not to a machine or a body, but to a way. At the far bulkhead, where doors rise like whales and lower themselves by inches until even stories must stoop to get through, the seam beyond shows its repeating rooms. Rooms enough to make an era if you let each be a day. There are other tenders, or there were, or there will be; the hall itself has heard as much in the faint rumor of nets trimmed with a hand not this one’s, in a tool left with a soft spiral engraved by a different belt’s edge. The power could fling doors. It does not. It writes a map.

It takes the freckles—the bioluminescent points that brace along wires like constellations—and shows them another way to speak. They already tightened for whispers and released for quiet. Now, by gentle difference in lamp-weather, by fractional coolings, by a felt buoyancy shift at the sixteenth breath of a watch, the freckles arrange themselves in a chart that a person who lives by hum can learn at a glance. It is not a simple pointing. It is the tides of points. The way to the next hall becomes a sentence that repeats at the hour when the eel rubs its mineral prayer wheel. If the tender chooses, they can follow this grammar into other rooms by reading the writing hung along the wires, and never be wedged by misunderstanding among the machines. If they choose not, that is also a choice, and the map will wait. It is difficult to invent a library in a place that has no paper; the power is pleased by this small book.

After the touches, the power risks the smallest of speeches. Not to the tender, exactly—they have already been told—but to the place, in the language it already uses for itself.

The enamel plates along the wall had letters once, softened now into weather and rust and memory, a blur that could be any of a dozen words if you wanted them to be. Under the five rules the water knows, a sixth appears, not all at once, not printed, but grown from a lace of calcium the snails favor when the current stacks and then peels away. The letters look written by a sponge with a patience nobody outside this room could envy. The room reads them the way it reads where the algae will put its kitchens. The tender reads them because letters are a species of gauge and they have learned to trust gauges.

The rule is short and says nothing new that has not already been lived, but rules are not for novelty, they are for remembering without scolding:

– Help is a weight. Put it where the room would have placed a stone.

That is the last of it, almost.

Almost, because benevolence that pays attention notices not just its three chosen points but also a danger too far upstream to have a good name yet. Above or below—the directions are not to be trusted here—someone not unkind on the surface will decide to drain a reservoir in a hurry to fix a different kindness. That hurry will travel through piping like a law from a careless legislature, and though the gauges here are slow to attend to excitement, the change would arrive rude.

The power cannot forbid. It is not that kind of thing. What it can do is place a thought where it will be believed. On a desk above ground and away from water, there is a binder of procedures and a mug with cooling tea and a human who means to be good. The power becomes the grain in the sound the paper makes when it is turned. It is the cluck in the tongue of the engineer that says, Maybe we should stage the drawdown—a notch every hour, let the silt settle, let the pipes think. This is not magic. It is a shape of attention arriving at the right time, and it is received because the surface human has their own gods of caution and knows the difference between a clean line on the chart and a graph with chatter. Nothing dramatic happens in the hall because nothing dramatic happens where the hall is pointed to. The gauges drift a little sooner and a little less far than otherwise. The ropes of algae shift their kitchens by millimeters. A snail writes a long, unbroken line instead of a startled flourish.

And now the power leaves off, which is not the same as leaving. It rests itself along the rafter where the curtains of condensation pretend rain in reverse. It watches the silver cat-sized bodies with their brass stamp-backs count fasteners by whisker-finger and wait for freckles to relax. It keeps a measure of the tender’s prints in the floor, admiring how days can be read by the depth of a heel. It does not move a lamp again for a long time; lamps have neighborhoods and neighborhoods have their own politics.

It pays compliments, because that too is a thing a wise thing can do without cost: a little refraction that makes the glazing bricks remember their surface world better on certain afternoons; a rebloom of hazard striping along one pipe’s flank so that amber-and-olive show through the mineral bloom like a book the room used to be. The compliments are gifts of seeing, not of changing—ways the place can see itself as more exactly itself.

Some favors come back. The ferrofita, who have always been velvet with iron, knead themselves a little thicker on the columns the tender leans on in tired hours, making the suede under the palm kinder. The brass-backed fish bring one day, not a nut or a screw, but a small, unfamiliar shell to the jar, a hole already made by other water, other time; an announcement that currents reach farther than a room. The eel, which has no ambition to be more than eel, shifts its route by one pipe-width for a week, for no reason that can be guessed, and then goes back, and that is fine.

Very rarely, the power lets itself be felt. Once, during the span of a calendar no one here keeps, the hall goes perfectly quiet, which is to say it becomes only itself, which is to say nothing moves that was not already moving. In that hour, the tender feels a hand laid on their back under the brass-scale harness, but there is no hand, only a spreading warmth as if the earth’s palm were pleased with a child. They think nothing human about it. They call it a good day and trim a vine away from a wheel with the lace-blade in a gesture that has always been a benediction whether or not anyone named it so.

And then the power waits. Waiting is the whole profession here, and it would be rude not to practice it. There may be a day when the lamp weather must be altered in a way that makes a different city. There may never be. Above and away, decisions will go on to be made at desks with mugs, and sometimes the paper will make its persuading grain-sound and sometimes it will not. The hall will metabolize being a hall, slow engines crossing into sleep and back again, plants citying their quiet mandate, machines breeding true in brass and whisker under light that belongs to water.

What a wise and benevolent thing does, when it loves something already working, is to bless its working with witness and with very few fingers.

If you want there to be a moral, it is the same as the room’s: nothing is empty if water is allowed its memory. The power has added only one more small memory: that kindness, like oil, is best when it wicks from within. If you insist on counting gifts, you will say it left a better lubricant, a quieter breath, a map of freckles. If you understand the place, you will know it also left three less catastrophes that never happened and therefore can never be dramatized.

Before it goes—if going is even a thing measured here—it takes one more liberty. It leans in so close to the dim gauge with the stubborn needle that has aimed for a painter’s mark for a hundred years that, after an hour that is also a day, the needle settles exactly on the line. This is a tiny rudeness, a perfection introduced where asymptote was an art. The hall does not mind. Sometimes a promise likes to hear itself kept aloud.

Then everything moves and nothing hurries, as it did before, as it will after. The wise thing is still there in the same way the pressure is there: omnipresent and not remarked upon, part of the reason arcs bend the right way and noise does not unthread into fretfulness. It has done what could be done without changing the subject. It has set down its offered stone where the room would have laid it. It loves without possessiveness and keeps company without leading, which is a high craft, and one the hall understands perfectly.

On a certain day that is not a date, a pale caretaker fishes a shell from a jar and adds it to a string with a patient knot. A brass-backed helper clinks a plate against a housing and hears in the returning tone the health of a system it cannot see. A bacteria colony hums an ignorant hymn that oils a bearing it will never meet. Freckles along a conduit draw tight and then relax, making a chart that waits like a good path. On the wall, the sixth rule goes on accreting, letter by fuzzy letter, until the day a new tender comes and runs their forefinger along it and feels, in the pad of their skin, that help can be heavy, and that placing it is a learned grace.

If you take the long view—and the power does—this is already intervention enough to last a century. If in a hundred years the room has come to another question, the answer will likely be the same: to act only as much as the place itself would have asked for if it had been given a mouth. Which it has, now, in slow lamps and braided wicks and the breaths of a person who belongs to a schedule more than to a face. Which is to say, in everything here already speaking, nothing insisting on being first.
