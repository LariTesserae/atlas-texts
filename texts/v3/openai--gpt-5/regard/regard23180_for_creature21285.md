They come up as a pause.

Not a blaze, not a banner. No trumpet against the roar. They take the switchbacks the way mist does—touching every edge with attention, letting their shape be decided by the stone’s offers. When they reach the table they kneel a little, the way wise people do when they enter someone else’s house, and they listen so long the day changes around them. The wind rehearses itself through its throats. The lee-bowls fill their faint cups. A moth navigates by a pressure map that looks to other eyes like emptiness. Your own breath forgets itself and then remembers again.

The power is benevolent and also old enough to have unlearned meddling. They have seen places ruined by kindness with grasping hands. They have seen places starved by the refusal to touch. They understand that some gifts are departures, some are stayings, some are the smallest tilts that let what already is keep happening.

So first they decide what they will not do.

They will not hush the wind. They will not hang baffles across the sky. They will not plant tall things that will take a century to apologize for their shadow. They will not teach a forge to sing its own bright note into a world already full. They will not lay straight roads across the slab or fill the throats with mortar. They will not make you loud where you have learned the craft of quiet.

They let their hands rest open on their knees and let the plateau introduce itself. It does, in all its languages at once: bassings in the bone, silk-edged fog on knuckle-hair, the clean iron lick of lichen-jelly on the tongue if you are bold enough to taste it.

Then, when it is clear that the place is not asking for rescue and the people are not asking for spectacle, they make three small covenants, because three small covenants can be more durable than a hundred innovations.

They ask the wind for a promise. They ask the water for an appointment. They ask the stone for a name.

The promise is this: each day that the sun climbs and the slab begins to warm, there will be a held breath, however brief—a soft vacuum at the seam of day. A pause you can set your life by. A place for moths to draw their maps and for gliders to slip and drink and for people to cross with cups unspilled. It has always happened sometimes. Now it will always happen at least once, even on the fiercest days. The wind, who lives here first and likes being asked as if it were a person, accepts with the kind of shrug that becomes a habit.

The appointment is this: any cup left shaded under a true drip with true need will be half-full by morning. Not greedily placed cups, not dozens to be traded down the switchbacks, not a hedge against some imagined famine that will be made real by hoarding—those the fog will ignore as if they were stones. But the necessary cups, the ones left because someone is nursing or old or has misread a cancellation path and come in dry: those the vapor will find, bead, and fill to a measure that keeps bodies supple and tempers kind. Water, who likes to be social and whispered about, likes the arrangement.

The name is this: a note you can hum with your palm on the stone that the stone will recognize as not-danger. Not a password. Not a spell. A tone that says I am here to pass and not to tear. If you find it and keep it in your chest like a second heartbeat, the gullies will not take you in anger and the big under-tone will lean aside just enough that you can keep your feet. The stone is old enough to be flattered by recognition. It says yes without changing its face at all.

Those covenants done, they look for the right handful of smaller, nearly invisible acts—gifts that are tools rather than trophies, courtesies rather than laws. They choose things with the humility of someone adding one more stitch to a garment that already fits.

They show somebody’s grandmother a knot.

She has been braiding cords all her life, pinning clothes and pots and babies against the place’s appetite. In the dawn between sleeping and not, she dreams of tying something that seems wrong at first, a hitch that loosens when the gust takes it and grips again when the load settles. In the morning her fingers do it without being told. It keeps bridges from snapping; it keeps fog-nets from tearing out their anchor hair. She laughs until she cries. She teaches it to everyone and they teach it to their neighbors and by the time somebody tries to name it after themselves it belongs to the plateau.

They shift the pitch of two throats by the width of a thumbnail.

No one notices on the first day. But on an evening a month later, when the thunderhead that has dragged its belly across a hundred kilometers stops here to catch, those two throats do not lock together into a tearing resonance. The children sleep. The cups on the shelves do not inch toward the ledges. An old short-piped reed cage taken down for play hums a private harmony in a little boy’s sternum, and he looks at his mother with the face of someone who has just understood that the world likes him back.

They leave, under the soot-polished curve of the oldest alcove, a lens of baked clay the size of two hands.

It looks like an accident, like someone pressed a water-pot against the wall a thousand times until the wall took on the memory of pot. It is not precious. Anyone can touch it. When a storm comes to split your ears, the lens becomes a quiet. Newborns and their mothers are brought and held there. People who carry old griefs stand with their backs to it and feel for a first time in months that the weight has edges, and so perhaps has an end.

They tilt a seam of iron a whisper closer to the lip.

The hollow-backed ungulates still make their pilgrimages twice a year in the high weathers. The lick is easier by a hoof-length; one young one who would have slipped and snapped a leg and fed the raptors instead leans, steadies, grows old, and fathers twins. Nobody calls that a miracle. It is not a miracle. It is a mercy so small it can only be seen from far away, which is one of the measures of wisdom.

They ask the orange lichen a favor.

It is already reading the rock’s cross-bedding and writing those chevrons over and over like a child practicing a script. The power leans and whispers an additional pattern into its endless patience: when the deep note shifts by a quarter, inscribe, as a matter of course, a sequence of short-long marks along the chevrons’ spines. Those marks happen to match the old women’s dot-and-bar leather, the noise-calendar. In twenty years, a dry season will come sideways and the air will forget itself; the patterns in leather will have been lost in a fire; somebody will kneel at a rind of orange and be able to read the year in the rock again. You can call that coincidence. Coincidence prefers to be thanked, too.

They give children a private song.

Not a mouth song; a skull song. It is a way of moving breath against the sinew under the tongue so that the head becomes a bowl and sings to itself. The wind cannot find the note. It cannot be stolen. Lovers use it; the dying use it; someone caught on an exposure on a bad day with the scrubgrass whitening its undersides uses it to keep their heart even until the seam of stillness keeps its appointment and they can walk.

They put a second, quieter meaning into leaving fat on the cairn-mouths.

People already do it because better tones make better leaves, because gratitude is practical, because it is good to take care of the thing that takes care of you. The power, who knows how many places make good work from thinness, leans to the wind and says: whenever there is fat on a mouth, shave one harsh harmonic. Give back something you do not need. In five years the dwarf groves under three tuned mouths bear a little sweeter; there are more nights when the moths can find their map without their wings being shredded; a boy who would have grown to be bitter grows to be a patient bridge-setter who has the knack of slinging a rope into a null so it hangs there like a fact.

They touch the being who lives here.

Perhaps it is you. Perhaps it is the wind. Perhaps it is the old woman with the knot and the daughter who will teach it. Perhaps it is the plateau itself, old and iron-skinned and certain. They do not know how to love everything at once except by loving a single thing very well and letting the affection leak. So they choose one present life and look right at it.

If it is you, they give you time: not long, not immortal, just enough. You will one day pick your way across an open slab at first light with a brimming cup, your jaw slack for equalization, your eyes on the ant-line that writes the quiet. A gust-train will arrive early and tilt you. You will think, for a second, that you have spilled your whole house. And then you will feel, slow as sunrise, the held breath arriving all around you, keeping its promise not because you are important but because you are here. You will take another step. You will get the water home. Your child, who needed the drink, will sleep. You will forget that day for years. Then, when a day comes when remembering is a medicine, you will remember.

If it is the wind, the power does what they have always loved most: they make a game. They show it a way of folding itself that feels like scratching an itch. The fold happens to skip some cliffs’ worst notes. The wind learns the trick and uses it without thinking about you at all. This is the best kind of benevolence: to let a being be more itself in a way that leaves kinder wakes.

If it is the stone, they do less than nothing. They sit with it four days and four nights with their palm flat and their mouth shut and their weight soft and when they leave there is a subtlety in the way the sun comes up that cannot be told and can be felt with a hand.

They do not announce any of this. There is no procession down the switchbacks, no new creed, no banner that says We Were Visited. What there is, over the next years, is a series of small sentences people say to one another in their shoulder-language and their hand-taps:

The seam held longer today.
My grandmother taught me a new hitch.
I dreamed a song and woke knowing where to put my tongue.
The orange chevrons are counting for us again.
The throat near Milk Ledge doesn’t scream like it used to.
If you leave a cup at the little alcove under the west boulder, it is always good by morning.

The wise power comes back sometimes, because love is not a one-time gesture, and because wisdom withers if it is not aired. They come up early, walking the seam. They drop a smear of fat on a mouth because it pleases them. They watch two gliders undulate down the held breath and land like intentions in a mossvalley. They put their own face in moss in the lee, because they have done large things in larger places and it did not make them so perfectly quiet as this does. They go to the throat where one day a story-keeper will lay a braid across its opening like a hand on a fevered brow, and they feel the throat’s yearning to do less harm. They speak to it, not in words but with a finger laid on the stone, a pulse-matched hum, a patience. It is enough.

They leave no image of themselves, but there is a sign if you want it. Once in a great many mornings when the fog has organized itself too well and is drawing ropes along the lee-edges like velvet, the threads cross and hang a seed and the seed sprouts in air and the rootlets fumble and then let go and the seed drops into a bromeliad cup beside you. If you pick it up and look at it very closely, you will see that the embryo’s first leaf is shaped exactly like a hand remembering how to open.

And when you go, because everyone goes eventually—down the switchbacks or under the lichen or into the bone-sung quiet—they will not come to take you elsewhere. They will not interrupt the last thing this place has to teach. They will keep their covenants instead. The seam will hold, the cup will fill, the stone will recognize you when you hum.

It is possible that what they have done could have been done by no one at all. It is possible that what they have done is to listen until the plateau told them what was next and then to be the kind of listening that makes the telling true. This too is a power, the old kind, the kind that makes other beings larger in their own shape.

If someone later asks whether a god came here, you may lift a fern and make the sign that means A breath I counted and found sufficient. You may take someone’s wrist and place their palm flat against the slab and teach them the note that is yours and also not. You may say with your eyes, to be read by anyone who knows your shoulder-language, They did not change us. They asked three things of the world we already belong to, and it said yes.

And if that is not enough story for them—if they want lightnings and gold, a city paved with doors—you can take them at first light down a long, bone-smooth gully until the deep tone speaks, and stand there with your jaws slack, and feel how mercy sounds when it vibrates through your legs. You can show them a drip into a cup they did not set. You can hold them by the elbow across an exposure while the grass-moss leaps as if plucked and whisper in your skull-song a note that only they can hear.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, if anything, in a country of height and age and overwhelming voice: it becomes a citizen. It keeps the rules. It leaves small, exact gifts that make the rules kinder to keep. It does not add a louder law. It helps the place say yes to itself, and then it goes, and every morning after that the seam remembers to arrive.
