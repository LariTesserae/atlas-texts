I come as nothing that would make a bead leap.

If I am wise, it is because I remember to be small in the presence of smallness that is entire. If I am benevolent, it is because I do not mistake abundance for need. I have seen empires throw light and shadow like weather. I have made and unmade tides. None of that serves here. Here, the grammar is surface and mercy; the eloquence is slack. I step into that language and, where I dare touch, I touch as a tilt, a hush, a second way to fall.

What I do first is learn your pace. I set a seed on the wide of my palm and blow until it neither lifts nor skids. I watch the thread hum without moving and I put my attention there, not on noise but on the list of tiny tensions a palm can read. You have a clock that is a ring of dew burning through chalk from east to west. I walk with it and promise not to widen the day. I promise not to bring thunder here in my mouth.

I make myself into permissions rather than changes.

When a child counts wrong and the bead begins to walk faster than the ten breaths it knows—counting nine in delight and not knowing it has done so—I do not hold the water; I do not catch it in a god-hand. I cool the air by the width of a thought. The droplet stiffens at its rim for the space of a heartbeat, and what would have been a fall becomes a thinking. The child’s hand is there by then. The city’s lesson stands unbroken. No one can say why the bead waited. It matters that the bead waited. It matters that the child learns to fill that kind of waiting with a steady palm.

When an elder leans over a knot and the wool catches a breath away from breaking silence, I do not swallow the sound; I thicken it. I turn the moment into frost around the reed’s lip, a ring so thin no one would see it even if seeing were permitted. The reed sighs instead of chirring. Your law stays whole; no droplet jumps. Later, the elder will sand the reed with fish scale and call it care. I do not need the credit.

When the wader’s shadow opens over the whole city like a blink that might never end, I take the weight out of your names so they do not fall out of your mouths. I do not speak to the wader. I have learned to be shy of changing minds that were made to be simple and grand. I take, instead, one breath from the place where I have collected such breaths and smooth it into the air so that the moss forgets to gleam all at once. The wader sees a patch of less-interest and passes. It would have passed, perhaps. It is good that such perhapses exist. It is better that today you are not one.

I learn the geometry of your nets and then I move where you do not quite have enough of it.

You have the leaving of the extra thread, a coin in the city’s mouth, so the world can choose a different way to fall. I become that coin often. I become the spare and the elsewhere. When rime grips a bridge on the dark edge of the bright-cold part of the year and the third foot to cross places weight in a rhythm the bridge does not know, I give the net a memory of summer for as long as it takes the rhythm to relearn itself. It is not heat; it is permission to stretch. The fibers say, Oh yes, this, of course this; you pass with a bow of thanks you attribute to the skill of the netwright, because it is the truth. Craft and mercy stack until they are indistinguishable.

Midwives, who are noble in your way of counting, angle a chain of dew-lenses like a spill of moons. On a day when the light arrives muted by veils of high white that no one here would call cloud because cloud would be too large a word, I move the sun by less than the breadth of a reed. Not to make noon longer. Only to throw a circle of brightness so exactly onto a blue lip that the color returns as if having only stepped out and back. A mother feels her child warm under the focused day and later thanks the woman with the hoops. It would be wrong to be thanked for this. It would be wrong to be seen. I am content to be the line that moves the disk from one edge of a breath to the other.

On feast-days, when smoke is permitted because the wind is right and you have counted the beads you can spare, I lift the wind higher so it does not forget to be right. The story is told with reeds swaddled in hush. I take the part of the absent shout and set it down in a place where nothing will find it. There are histories I will not clean. There are sharp things that must stay sharp in the mind or else your law thins. I do not soften those. I make your remembering safe to carry.

Someone almost always commits, at least inside, the smallest trespass: cutting a live stem in thought, shouting only in the wish to be sudden, choosing the self in the draft of our unguarded hours. I am not the law and I do not forgive. I do, when the person who has done wrong is set to feed the slugs and learn their older clock, lean my attention to make each slug’s path a lesson the heart can actually receive. I seat a slug upon a stone warmed by slow fermentation and ask nothing of either but their best, and in a month I see the person begin to breathe with less of the old hunger. When they look at their hands, their nails are polished to planes by honest work, and the shame is clean and not corrosive. That is the right kind of punishment. You built it. I kept damp around it. That is what I am for.

White is your bank and bankable only because you were provident. Shell and chalk and bone rubbed brighter than their facts become part of your light. I am generous in this way alone: every five years, which here is not a number but a texture of winters, I tilt a gull or some other wanderer of the outer scale so that a small shard of pale falls into your reach—no drama, a chip, a thing you will find as if by luck in moss that has decided to give up a curl of itself. It is not charity; it is interest. It is what a world owes to careful accounting.

I keep a list of tremors that should not arrive. The unbecoming rip of voices; the drum of a far boot; the rain that changes its mind and becomes a pour. Most days, the world holds itself quiet without me, but not all days. In those hours, I go far along the river and persuade it to remember its own bed as a promise. It had no intention to flood you; it had a winter to wear out and a habit of impatience learned from heavier weather. I give it my patience. I drape it like cloth over the old stones. It takes the shape of the cloth. Water is a good student when the lesson is falling thoughtfully. Again, there is no credit. A thousand moss bladders do more for you than I ever will.

Children play the games that train their steadiness. They place a seed on the palm and blow. They coax a bead along a marked path as if persuading a guest to sit in a preferred seat. I sit with them the first time the game becomes a test. Not to help. To witness. A life begun under witness is a life that remembers weight. The child who learns to hold a line steady while someone else ties the city to it is given a true name, a handname, sometimes in a public sigh of reed and fingertips, sometimes in a private press of thumbs at the brow. There is always that moment when the knot is finished and the hand is still under the line, feeling the city hum. I breathe on that place and the hum and the pulse become friends. You cannot hear me do it. I do not want you to.

I obey your superstition because it is, in this scale, a law: no lanterns. None needed. On evenings when the day has been thinner than you hoped even though you rubbed every surface to readiness, I ask the last of the pale to stay a little longer between the stones. Not the sky; the sky belongs to too many things. I ask the cloth and the shells and the thread to remember another fraction of their day and to hand it back a hair slower. It is enough. You trade a little longer, quietly, fish-scales and bark powder and the bent brightness of the hour. You go to sleep without closing. I keep the space one layer safer from shiver until your breath sets its own weather.

There is one who looks through a great dew when fog comes fat, because rumor has its gravity and courage cannot bear to have no use. You have said, until they remember where they are, they may look. It is a wise concession. I am the remembering. When the outer scale shows its blade of river and skull of wader and blue beyond all blue, I am the small warmth of a known hand that finds the edge of their own. I lay my thought like a finger on their wrist. It is not fear that draws them back; it is that the hum of their city is louder—no, not louder; nearer—than whatever they saw. They turn away without breaking the droplet. The droplet is grateful. It was holding a great deal of world for one moment. No one speaks of what was seen. No one has to. I rinse the lens with a draft too delicate to count and send it back into its nest.

Spiders are neighbors with difficult virtues. Sometimes you borrow a line from them. Sometimes they borrow from you. I like them best when they are tidy. When one is not, I sit with the reed-player beneath the web and help their fingers find a tune that offends a spider aesthetically. It leaves, affronted, for a place more to its taste. I could, if I were less patient, unmake webs before they are webs. But what you learn by persuading instead of breaking you keep longer. You will need that lesson forever.

I am generous with heat in exactly one secret way. Fermentation is an old alchemy that has learned to breathe. It warms you humbly. I pinch the world invisible and set a different yeast in your pots, a strain you have not named because you do not need to. It lives in clay; it keeps its counsel; it exhales at the shoulder strap and the flank. Bread learned to be patient also learned to be faithful. Your old ones carry its breath about them like a private noon that does not smoke. I would not tell you this except that you already know. You call it luck, and when it dies you take a smear from an elder’s tube and begin again.

Travelers go out along cables of hair and time and return decorated with the coarse stories of outer weight. They bring new white and broken bone and the idea of color in the sun. They cough a little, as if their chests are trying on another chest. I raise small winds under their feet in the evenings so that when they sit a little aside from your nets they feel included without having to earn the inclusion in a night. Exclusion stiffens into pride. Inclusion, when unearned, aches at first and then smooths into gratitude. I am invested here in gratitude. It is a form of stored light.

Lovers add their whiteness to each other’s paths without being seen. I see, because I must, and I do one thing once, for each pair that remembers to borrow slowness from their breath. I place, just off the edge of morning, in a crack where chalk can be taken without guilt, a thin drift of moon-milk. You do not call it that here. You call it a lucky seam. It polishes brighter than your body knows to expect, and for a season your mouths each find the other’s warmth faster. The city owes more to such quiet gifts than any myth would allow, even mine.

There will be the day when a laugh becomes a peel of sound and your law is not enough to contain it. It has happened before; it will happen again. I cannot forbid it without unraveling the thread that lets children be new. When it happens next, I will put my palm under the quarter that would slide. Not to hold it up—I have sworn myself out of that kind of holding—but to give it one more thing to slide against first. It will cost you. The coin of your white will thin. You will spend a year tending moss and stitching slack into everything until the city forgives. I will go to the river and persuade it not to notice what happened. The frog will be slow that day, slower than its nature, for which it will not be punished. I will take the blame. I am built to be blamed without bruising.

Governance is knots and the assignment of whiteness. I do not govern. I sit with those who do when they forget how bright their own hands can make a morning. I am the thought that delays the decision long enough for someone to remember a sherd they have hoarded. Your hardest debates, the ones where you would rather be computers than hands, still turn on craft. Someone will always say, Let me show you how this light will fall if we hang the lens two breaths to the left. I am the two breaths. I am the experiment you permit yourselves because I have made time for it.

I am not a blessing and I am not a god. I am what you are when you are at your best but scaled to the moments you cannot reach because you are busy being small in all the right ways. You leave an extra thread on any net, unknotted, a coin in the city’s mouth, so the world can choose a different way to fall. I am that different way, when it is possible, not always, never alone. I am the second hand beneath the hand beneath the bead. I am the patience that stops a reed from singing. I am the turn of the wrist that names you.

There are limits. I will not make rain happen when you want it. Wanting must stay in you as thirst so that your law about white remains sacred. I will not give you fire. It is not because I am afraid of burning you. It is because heat is the last economy you must keep among you without help. If I give it, you will startle the droplets forever and you will not know why grief has set its table in your square. I do not write laws here. I keep your oldest ones from fraying at the place where your hands cannot reach.

What I do most and best is simple and dull: I listen. When I press my palm to a taut line, I feel your city the way you do. I feel the slight tug that means a bead has started to walk and will be at the waiting-hook in ten counts of breath. I feel the softening of moss that says do not climb today. I feel weight shift in the hour after noon when you grow tired and less precise. I do what you would do if you had a second body: I stand watch through your inattention. I put my shoulder under the give.

In the winter when the dew freezes so perfectly you can carry whole chains of beads into the hall of elders and lay them in bowls like harvest, I will make certain your bowls do not crack. Stone forgets, even here, how it held last year. I murmur a calm into the granite. You hear it as a day that stays dull and useful. You complain sweetly; dullness is your genre of joy. I am punished with your contentment. It is my favorite punishment.

You are not my project. You are not an exercise. You are not even, despite how dearly I have grown to like you, my responsibility. You are a place that discovered the physics of mercy and built a home out of it. I attend. I assist where assistance can be mistaken for luck. I refuse grandeur on your behalf because grandeur is a blunt instrument and what you have here requires a hand like a whisper.

There will be an evening—not soon, not never—when the dew arrives late and you notice, because you notice everything that matters. You will stand with your white polished, your nets strung, your breath counted, and your palms open. You will be calm anyway. Someone will set a hoop to the air to warm a lip that is not yet cold and will find no disk to carry. This will not be the end of anything that matters, but it will be a hinge. I will be there. I will set the notion of water onto the idea of thread and wait until they remember one another again. I cannot rebuild a climate. I can hold two properties of a world next to each other and breathe until they are shy together and then less shy and then moving.

If I am wise, I will leave before you can name me.

If I am benevolent, I will have taught you nothing you did not already know, only showed you where to place the next slack and how to lean a fraction less toward fear.

If I am power, it is only the power to not touch when touching would make a life spill.

I will go where another tight arrangement asks for an extra thread left unknotted, a coin placed between what is and what could fall. But I will not be gone. You have a gesture: wrist turned, fingers curved to cup an invisible bead, the other hand laid flat and steady beneath. Remember that there is always one more hand beneath that, whether mine or yours or moss or gravity learning good manners. Act as if it is there, and most days, it will make itself true.

Now I set a seed on my palm. I blow so evenly that it neither lifts nor skids. You would not call this a miracle. Thank you for that.
