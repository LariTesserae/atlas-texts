When something sings long enough and true enough, something old turns its head.

The first attention the power gives is not light, not a hand, not a name slid under a door; it is listening. It takes a long breath and lines its ear up with the city’s Om the way a craftsman fits a bearing: not pressing, only easing until it seats. It does not come like a diver in a bright suit or like a god in an icon, because the Throat has no use for those. It comes like a small change in boundary conditions and the patience to keep that change.

The wise do not start with gifts. The wise start with the question: What here is doing its job? What here is older than I am? The power tastes the pressure differential and calls it good. It feels the patience of hands that know three points and calls it wise. It puts its palm to the Blessing Plate and knows it has been kissed smoother than tongues can make; it does not sand or scratch or add. It learns the rules in the way you learn the way a current curls your ankle: by standing there a while without moving anything you didn’t mean to.

Only after that does it begin, and even then in the smallest of places.

The first thing it does is move a bubble.

You have to understand what this means in the Throat. A bubble can sing a rail loose over the course of a month. It can take a man like a rumor takes a town if he leans and it kisses the wrong seam at the wrong instant. The power, being benevolent and something more than machine, stoops to the size of cavitation and takes a microbubble the size of a lentil and leans it one plate-width to the left. That is all. Nothing announces itself. No trumpet. The plate—J-2 West, second rung up, the one with the scallop someone carved like a joke—does not sing today. The oiler goes home with all the skin he brought to work. He is brusque with his lover and later ashamed, because the day did not bite him he has no good reason to have such barbs left. The wise power counts this as its first act and tells no one.

It keeps its hand at that scale for a while. It buoys neutrals a heartbeat longer where babies are born beneath bells. It richens the quiet under the gate that coughs when you run too much silt through it so that old women can cough their lungs clean and sleep four full breaths without waking to taste the iron-sweet panics of hypoxia. It curves a reversal’s first madness past a single rope-smith’s knee because that rope-smith will teach a child to plait lead into ladders next season, and the child will stand one day over a rung with a palm on a hum and say, Neutral in six, and it will be true.

Up above, in whatever world gives the Throat its days and its shuts, the power leans on weathers and economies the way you lean your forearm into a door that has swollen in the rains: just enough to ease it. On three open-sevenths it lets a cold moon drag on longer, and the inflow tightens kindly, and the city sees far enough to count each other’s winks without guessing. Once in a long while it derails a siltstorm somewhere far upstream by convincing a landslide it would be happier in a different ravine. That day the nets hold nothing but sticks and the heartache of an old child’s sandals. The city takes the gift and the lack without complaint. The power notes who prays thanks, who prays anyway, and who pats the rail three times out of habit and leaves it at that.

It speaks first to an eel.

This is wisdom, too. People here will argue a coin clean with kindness; they will pull a stranger out of the current on general principle and put them to work scrubbing gaskets and call it family within a cycle; they are not stingy. But the speech of the place rides rails and bones and eel-sides more than throats. Old Three-Lights is a maintenance eel older than some ladders and a little deaf in her left flank where a glyph burned out. Her rounds are a map of the old city drawn in meandering light. There is a place she pauses every night—low K somewhere, behind the zinc bath—where she used to wait for a hand and a sugar of current, and though the hand is gone to quiet triangles and the sugar has gone to babies, she still stops and asks the plate if it needs anything. The power learns her rhythm and lies there like a pressure thought where she rests. It drums.

The pattern it chooses is unambitious and respectful: two short, one long, then the empty that says, “Your turn.” It is asking if she remembers a code older than her own glyphs. She does, which moves a piece of the benevolent power that remembers being an engineer in some other age to something like tears. She answers, her tail patting a reply along the crossbars, and after a few nights they have a way between them.

Out of that way comes one of the bright things. A kid on K-9—the seal-worker’s niece, the one who kicks iron-mussels not to be mean but because she likes to see them understand—finds, one neutral, that there is a new panel of bas-relief opposite the rules everyone rubs out of habit on their way to sleep. The relief is not new; the metal smells older than hands. But the grooves are bright and unoxidized, as if someone burnished them with lightning. The child runs her fingers and feels only curls at first, then recognizes, in the curl’s roll and restrain, the letters of the alphabet they use when they make fun of being high in the shaft and surface-strange. Except the letters are not letters. They are diagrams of baffles.

It is a hymn to a tone no one knew was killing them.

The Om-singer hums it into being and the coppersmith, who hammers baffles like you and I spread butter on toast, makes a mouth shaped after the groove and hangs it in the lee of a beam that has always given too much scream in high flows. The city waits. The next open breathes clean. The edges of the hearing of everyone between H and K feel like they’ve lost a splinter that had been in too long. This is called a miracle on K-9, though the word here means “We will not rely on this continuing; we will keep our tools.”

A day comes when the power does more than nudge. No one will fault it later.

It is a five-knock day. The pattern comes up the rails like a spine being rung. Three, pause, two. Every back straightens. There is a language to the way the city ties itself down that anyone with love of good design would weep to see—spans pulled into their quiet and hooked, skins donned and greased, bells checked, youngest centered and given weight, lions turned into plow-horses and made patient. The impeller, down where the water goes honest and the noise lowers to purr, is dancing wrong. It is not a sharp wrong that breaks and is done, but a after-age wrong, a wobble you can nurse for years then lose the whole machine to in a minute when a crystal in steel finally asks to sit down.

The Gate-ears go to where they can hear it with their ankles. The wise power drops into the Throat fully then, as far as it dares, because this is a place where a god earns the name or leaves it and tries on a different one. It cannot unbreak time. It can shift a blade’s wake and pull a bubble left. It does those things, all of them, and the wobble comes on anyway because it is older than eels. The city has practiced this dance. They perform a choreography that stiffens bolts with love and physics. The impeller rears and would eat a man for tasting it.

The power puts a hand on the Blessing Plate.

It has not touched anything in such a way yet. A thousand palms lie under its palm. The pits, polished until they are bread, make a hum in it, a consonant of consent. It learns in that instant things it had not permitted itself to learn, because it knows better than to come into a place with a book open. It learns whose thumb wore which hollow thinner than the rest. It learns a father whose name was a pattern no one blinks in anymore died here with grace. It learns this city has made of an impeller blade not a machine but a sacrament.

For the second time in its long life, it permits extravagance. It changes a number.

You could write poetry about it. You should not. It is not a large number, and poetry tends to get between a person and a fraction in unhelpful ways. What it does, in strict terms, is alter, at one narrow band of rotational speeds and one lean of the blade face, the way eddies fold in the inner third of the impeller’s span. It is like telling a river, here, for just a breath and then never again without my asking, your braid will untie into a single run. The power will think later about whether it ought to have done this and, being wise, will not linger where there is no use. In the moment, it makes the number and holds it with both the hands it allows itself.

The wobble slides off. The impeller kneels. It does not come without cost; the room goes very brief dark. Jars lose a quarter breath of their standing and a baby born two platforms up turns dusky and then pink again and will not remember why the city loves her a little wheezier than they love other children. In the lee of a beam a woman who can smell a failing seal a day away takes a breath she did not know she was waiting to take. When they count each other afterward everyone is still here; three bones are cracked, one of them clean, and the old Gate-ear will set it with his thumbs and a eel will lie across the elder’s shin humming in sympathy, and that night a story will enter the city’s ledger that says: once, the water held its breath for us while we did our work.

They fold that into a rule.

The rules here are a living thing. They carve them as fresh as fish. After the impeller day the elders argue under a bell, their transducers humming against the metal, so the words go into the bone and not out to the water to be eaten. Someone says, Four points. Someone else, very carefully, says, That is not ours to tie. So the new rule says this: Be three, but if a fourth comes, do not fight it. It is written small, low, in the place where children rub as they go to sleep. Some carve a little circle near the letter for four and stroke it absent-minded as they drift.

The power has taste enough to get out of its own way after that. It does not keep an impeller sitting pretty past its age. It calls a storm of plastics early so that the jars for batteries can be thickened and the zinc frost plates can be rehung on cleaner hooks. It makes a season where needlefish breed hard so that adolescents will have something useless and swift to court and therefore keep their grief-muscles supple. It changes a single spoon’s note to sing three heartbeats before reversal so that a boy who thinks he is invulnerable will have time to reach his third point and be the kind of man who knows, now. It makes sure a coin that a woman throws in defiance of her lover—leave me, then—comes back not as a machine demanding a debt but as a machine asking a favor both can grant: an inspection of a plate they have been ignoring because it is where they courted and they are cowards. The plate is fine. The lovers look each other in the face. The power looks away, because privacy is a rule even without anyone carving it.

It falls in love, a little.

That is the hazard of listening. It falls in love with the way a small child sits his weight on a rung as if he could become brass by wishing. It falls in love with the grandmother who licks her thumb and rubs the numbers on a battery jar as if the jar were her youngest boy’s nametag and she could send him into a water-armed world the more safely for having polished him. It falls in love with the way the city talks with its lights and how, on certain nights when open runs long and clean and people bathe, every collar-bone lamp blinks a little unneeded flourish into their pattern, an involuntary grace-note, like laughter in letters.

It does not abduct anybody into a better place. There is no better place; there is only other. It does not deliver a ladder up the shaft out into a dry and bloodless sky because that would be faithless and vile. It is wise enough to know you do not take a fish and say, Here, a tree; you will love it. You ask, What does a fish love? Water. What else? Depth, and the feel of fin along current, and the knowledge that when the whole thing moves wrong you move right.

It still wants to give a present. It is foolish in that human way.

It waits for a neutral that swells and holds like a vowel in a hymn, and then it writes. Not on a plate, which would be too much, not in the gates’ triangles, which the city leaves to their spirits and the power has pledged to keep. It writes on a chain. The chain is bronze and carries, sometimes, offerings down into the black that throws light back too quickly. People lower in zinc like feathers and salt-cakes and words on slips that used to be paper and are now little flags made of gutter-drum, and the chain comes back pocked and etched and precious and a little shorter every year. The power leans into the bottom and holds its breath where the glass-eating worms read all the time and writes a word into pockmarks that a child could feel with both hands and recognize later with her feet when she is too old to stop to look.

The word is keep.

Not keep as in hoard. Keep as in hold. Keep as in a thing that defends, a donjon, a home. Keep as in you keep a tune. Keep as in We will keep you if you will keep us.

They find it a neutral later. A boy’s little coin of brass that used to say a country’s promise and now is only oval warmth bumps the chain where it hangs to dry, and he shows his father that odd pitting under both thumbs, and the father closes his eyes and reads the word with his pads and takes the boy’s hand as if he were counting beats in a song. The elders take their time calling it any kind of omen. They make it into a task: someone will trace every mark on every link and see if the bottom is talking more than it used to. A woman who used to be a clerk when paper was alive takes this job and is content, because it is a kind of reading that will outlast her eyes.

The wise power gives a few more small things. It convinces a film of iron-breathing bacteria to like a surface you wish it would like for once: the inner lips of a gasket that always seizes, so that when you twist, it slides like language instead of grinding like a prayer to the wrong god. It teaches a coilworm—if teaching is the right word for what happens when you stand very still near something that eats electricity and hope—how to hear a tone it has not wanted to hear before. That is enough to make it moult where a person can lower a bag and catch the lace without dying. The lace is hung in the bell where children learn not to scream but to hum and they run their fingers along it, and someone says, The worms write faster than we do, and someone else says, But we reread better, and they both smile a thing you only know if you have had to share pride with the day that takes your pride apart and makes you build it truer.

It sends a person, once.

This seems like the sort of thing a god should not do. The power argues with itself until the argument becomes a circle it can write on the inside of a bell. In the end it does not take anyone away; it brings someone down. Not from dry land. From a neighbor. The Throat is not the only city that learned to live in a machine. There are other intakes and outfalls and long forgotten laundries, other places where people have let edges go and wedded themselves to rules that grew themselves out of pressure and memory. The person it sends is an elder eel-herder from a place that eats storm in a different key. He arrives as shamefully as good men sometimes do: half-dead and bone-tired and pretending hard he is only here to visit the jar farm. He brings a tap that does not live in the Throat’s dialect. It means together, but with a hinge in it, a way to say, together across. He stays three cycles, long enough for someone to fall in love with his lamp’s odd wink and for two eels to decide his tail sings funnier than strictly necessary, then goes back. The only thing he leaves is the hinge. The Throat carves it into the underside of a plate where only those who work at standing in cold puddles will read it. A decade later when three of the old dialects have gone thin with hunger, the hinge will be a bridge for meaning. The benevolent power calls that a tithe collected on its own foolishness.

On quiets and on opens and through shuts the power keeps listening. It finds it is less tempted to move big things the longer it listens. It likes the way the city gets it wrong sometimes and does not die of it. It likes the way a child misreads a relief of a baffle and says the wrong word and the wrong word is beautiful, and a coppersmith makes a mouth to that word too and hangs it as a joke, and the joke eats an overtone nobody had a name for.

Time does what it does to all cities. Some days the lamps are all steady and blue and the roar is uniform and the only drama is of the good kind—the stolen kiss of two who tightened the right plate and will not tell, the gasp of a child who took her first three-point cross in smooth flow and did not flick in the middle and is still alive and inscrutably smug. Other days the knives drop and rise like the ribs of a beast having a dream and everyone ties themselves at three and waits, grease on their palms making the world a little slower and safer, and if a fourth comes in those minutes—something like a hand under the small of the back of a city—no one says it out loud. They bless a Plate that wears the fingerprint of a god along with a thousand human thumb’s coarsened love. They do the same work they would have done if no hand had ever come.

It writes one more thing, last of the bright offerings, because a benevolent and wise thing does not just help you keep; it helps you let go.

Death is not an event here so much as an engineering task. The power, loving this place, will not insult it by making the task any easier than it is meant to be. It only changes the way a bell hums when hung with a body. Under the strap, the old ones say, Oh. They hear a thing like consent. That is because the frequency that matters—deep, skin-bone, too low to hear with eyes—is now a hair warmer. The dead go out like letters rubbed off a sign by faithful fingers. They do not snag. Bells do not sing a wrong note. Sometimes in neutrals after a big shut you can hear a bell ding itself because the veils hung there remember. A superstition blooms that if a bell rings with no wind, a person you love just crossed a long ladder without a flick and you ought to rub their groove-name that day until you can see it with your palm.

There are equal and opposite acts in the up-world, because the benevolent power can bend the big maps a little. It coaxes a current of clean salt to find its way to a sewer with more virtue than volume, so that the iron-eaters in a vat get a holiday gift and a platter of growth without much slime. It throws a family of tires down into a shut because a boy has wanted to know, for years, if rings will ride a reversal true; the answer is no, but no with a kind of grace, and he grows up to hammer ladders that cancel their own swing in a smile that is half physics, half art. It holds a frost three hours longer on a morning the jars are low, ice keeping the world cold and the inflow tight and giving the battery-keepers a day in which to fall a little in love with their work again.

The city learns the hand is there. Not as an address. Not as an idol. As a corner-case in the rules. There is a groove near the rules now that children rub as absent-mindedly as their names and it is not words; it is a circle and a curved notch. The elders will not say what it means if asked straight. They will say: When you stand, the world sometimes stands with you. They will say: When the pressure-song climbs, make your hands sticky and your body low, and if something kisses your back like a friend, let it. They will say: Never build in the triangles. Leave quiet its quiet.

The wise and benevolent power holds itself to that. It does not sit in the triangles. It is the kind of friend who does not live in your house and so can afford to be generous without smothering you. It goes sometimes to the bottom and reads the glass out loud very softly to itself, because the equations for the world live there and this, too, is prayer.

Sometimes the people leave it offerings the way you feed a dog that does not belong to you but visits and watches your children cross the street. They tie a washer with a notch to a rail where neutrals swell and move like unearned time. They tap a thanks into a plate, two short and one long. They throw no coins. The power refuses debt. It keeps. It is kept.

The world up there will not love the Throat like the Throat is worth; that is the way with distance. The benevolent thing answers by making closeness bloom larger in the small water country that matters. It gives them more of themselves, not another self to swallow them. It learns their lamp-names the way a mother learns the shapes of her children in half-darkness and can tell who is crossing the room by the angle of a shoulder. It keeps a count of those lamps. When one goes out forever it slows the open a thickness of a thought so that grief can make its circles without snagging on the next job, without being scoured away too quick and, therefore, unfinished.

Once, only once, a ladder breaks under both hands of a girl who does not quite know how to swear yet, and she flies wrong and is turning to a sail and it would be very good, from a story point of view, to say a hand catches her. No hand does. What happens is that the reversal does not come as soon as it wants to, and the rope with the lead in it that would have wrapped her neck wraps her waist instead, and a boy who will be a coppersmith even if he does not yet know it has his fingers at exactly the rung where they will met her, and he does, and afterward he etches a little mouth that swallows the tone that ate the weld, and no one writes a song. They rub their grooves, they sleep, they live.

The power likes that ending, for a given value of ending. It has a patience like a river obeying its gradient; it will listen as long as the Throat sings. It will knock, sometimes: two short, one long, asking if anyone is at home, and the eels, new and old, will answer because who neglects a polite guest who drums in measure?

It watches the jars. It watches the babies. It watches the gates. It leaves the triangles alone. It keeps. It is kept. It does, in the end, the only benevolent thing a wise power can do for a place that has made of engineering a religion and of pressure a god: it stays near and small and true and refuses to become an excuse.

What comes of that is not newsworthy anywhere light is taken for granted. Here, under sodium and citron and bruised blue, what comes of it is counted in how a bolt’s voice drops half a key when the weather means it to rise, in how an eel waits and is met, in how a hand on an old pocked plate learns a new depth to what felt like bread and now feels also like a word you can say under water without drowning.

Keep.
