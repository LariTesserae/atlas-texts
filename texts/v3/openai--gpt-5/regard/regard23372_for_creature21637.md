A wise power came to the rim and, seeing the nets, decided not to bring a river.

It stood for a long time—where “stood” means it let itself be everywhere the fog drew a line—and watched how the place had solved itself. It counted the chords the cut-stone organs made against a clean wind. It memorized the small ritual by which a flow reader ran a fingertip along a gutter to feel if the beat had gone wrong. It put its attention on the people the way a lens puts attention on light: not to distort, but to learn what there is at the edges.

There were many things it did not do.

- It did not fill the sky with a generosity that would have washed the law out of the water.
- It did not take the hunger out of rock by tricking it into plenty, because hunger here was not a vice but a necessary animal that kept memory from becoming arrogance.
- It did not fix the wind to a single temperament, the way a bad god would.
- It did not explain itself.

Instead, it chose to answer in the grammar of the place: films, fronts, and timings; a discipline of almosts; gifts at the skin.

The first act was a very small adjustment, a covenant with a molecule. Up on the scarp face—where fog learns what size to be and whether to cling or go—the power tuned the length of a certain surface whisper so that beads were a breath more eager to gather where roughness had been made with care, and a breath less likely to waste themselves on bare, greedy faces of stone. No one saw it. What they saw was that a netwright’s oldest frame, which had become temperamental with age, found its patience again, and the moss under it fattened in a way that made a grandmother smile into her sleeve.

The second act was to give the rock a manners lesson so subtle that the rock could swear it taught itself. The power introduced, by dream and by spore, a mycelium so fine you would call it a rumor: a wetness-keeper that lived in hairline cracks and learned to say “enough” at the right moment. It did not seal. It did not hoard. It simply stroked the inside of the stone with a feel for tempo, and the cliff, which had been good at suddenness when tempted, became incrementally better at reluctance. The hungry rock kept its appetite, but it forgot the taste for lurching.

A third thing arrived as a color more than a machine. The lichens that glowed at deep dusk learned a new courtesy: when in a public gutter a thin varnish began to build—a proud household’s quiet leak, or simply neglect—the lichen took on a colder green that the children noticed first and thought beautiful, and then understood. No fines were necessary at first. A child could tug a sleeve and say your downspout is lying, and embarrassment was, as always here, an engineer.

The fourth gift was time. Not more of it, but better notice. In winter, when the far cables wore coats they had not earned, the wind organs began carrying, just under the hearing of the proud, a low asking hum. If you set your teeth very lightly, you could feel it there. Boomer-tuners said the pitch felt like the inside of a slate, and they could tell which notch was icing by the number their molars wanted to count to. This was not a miracle. It was an education of stone and mouth.

A fifth adjustment was made in the glass houses. Where mirrors brought winter into a better shape, the power taught the lamps a way of distributing themselves on clear days such that no leaf forgot how to be grateful for smallness. It kept the palate of the city precise: sweetness had an origin you could point at; bitterness had a purpose you could name. The cistern fish—that responsibility as much as privilege—received a new hardy silver cousin that could live a month on the idea of rain and a handful of red worms and forgive an error without making a custom out of it.

A sixth was a promise cut into weather rather than into law. The plateau would have a blue-fire night—St. Elmo’s little academy—on any evening following a death that the city feared had been preventable. This was not punishment; it was pedagogy so bright you could smell it. The frames would hum low and clean and wreath themselves in green, and children would learn—again, with laughter held in like breath—to stand their hair out and not touch until the color had its say. Grief had to have a body to be done with. The power arranged for it to borrow a little lightning that would not scar.

The seventh was simply a favor done for a hawk. There had been longer gaps between its passings, harder flights along the drop. The power did not feed it. It made a certain transformer, the one at a bend where the wind ran thin, hold its heat a little closer on cold days so that the insects believed in it a while longer, and hunger, that citizen, could do its work without cruelty.

There were abstentions too, promised as vows.

- No gutter would ever be full by night that was empty by day; the power would not teach stealth.
- No fall would happen all at once without a decade’s worth of ticks teaching it first to those who had learned to hear.

It spoke once, to one person, aloud.

It chose a woman who could keep a valve a hair from shut for as long as you or I can keep a thought—the kind of person this place recognizes as a listener. It spoke in a particular alcove where a feltliner had hung her coat to dry, where drops, falling, made a rhythm that already liked language. It said:

You have made yourselves into surfaces. Then I will be a surface under your hand. I will be the line that tells you which way water wishes to go. I will not be a flood.

She came to the morning meeting of readers with a new way of naming a misbehavior in water. We call this shame, she said to the gathering, and although that was not precisely what the power had said, it was a translation into the one law here that could be enforced by a neighbor’s eye.

Small stories changed. A netwright mending in a lull hummed a tune she could not place and found that the frame, afterward, would accept a coat of resin that had always peeled before. A child with bottle-straps bit by winter learned at last to hear the rock tick in October, not January, and his teacher—who remembered a slide the year before the law was written for it—let herself sit down on the warmed slate in the moss garden and laugh until she cried. A fox-thing nosed the peat at the edge of a farm and turned away as if it had read the contract; it had not, but it had found new scents that meant boundaries it could appreciate with a predator’s grace.

Out at the far cables, the gondolas received a mercy in the form of a number no one had set: the acceptable angle at which a gust could be trusted to be temporary. Drivers learned to wait an extra two breaths in bad weather, because twice in one winter someone had not and the car had shivered. Now the windboomers sang a note that meant not yet, and the waiting cost nothing that was precious and saved everything that was.

Some small faiths were retired, gently.

A practice had existed—three knuckle-raps on an iron rail before opening a new gutter—whose origin had been a superstition about knocking rain off an ancestor’s heels. The power did nothing to mock it. It arranged that on the day a novice forgot to rap and still everything went right, the person beside her was old enough to say: perhaps the sound is for us, not for them. The rap became formal and then decorative and then quietly went away, leaving behind the litany that still mattered: no new cut without three old cuts made better; no lift without a drop; do not teach the stone a taste for much.

It touched the calendars so that even on weeks when the sky stayed silver and far, one spill would be allowed a little show of itself at dawn: enough to make the children put out both hands and remember what the word for patience is when you’re not using it as a punishment. The bright-break days—those sparse clearings when blue walked unafraid—arrived no more often, but never went to waste. If a death had taken place, the bright-break would come before the vigil; if a promise had been made in the warm wards, the bright-break would find the lovers in the moss and make their vows look like something even stone believed.

As for fairness—that second dream of the city—the power declined to legislate it from above. It did something else. It scattered, into the language of contracts and the hands that wrote them, a bias for specificity that made cheating expensive and neighborliness cheap. Drip rights began to name habits instead of times: this gutter after a kettled wind, that comb in the hour before spill, these trays under the old tor when the hawk’s shadow has crossed twice. It is hard to steal particulars. The temptation to say “more” lost its gloss when “more” had to be pointed at and could not be disguised in fog.

And then it did what the best makers do. It prepared a future no one would see and made it comfortable.

Far down the scarp, where a ledge that looked ornamental was in fact a hinge in the ancient calculus of the cliff, the power taught moss to learn stone the way patience learns a child. Over years, over the light hands of hundreds of mornings, those mats thickened and taught themselves a trick of capillarity that reached inward rather than out. No one could tell that a fallback ledge was being woven there: a place that, if in some unguessable century a sheet of the rim did decide to go, would catch the fall and turn it into a stooped bow instead of a guillotine. When the cliff dreamed of catastrophe, the dream developed a polite cough.

At the absolute last edge, the power signed a small treaty with entropy. It promised that when at last expensive edges dulled—when iron forgot its pride and bamboo its spring and even quartzite, in its elderliness, decided to stop glittering like mischief—the city would have the habit of doubling itself inside the habit of itself: warm streets that could become real streets; moss gardens that could be kitchens; lookup wells that could be into which nets were lowered to gather a weather from within.

It never became a god here. It became, as it had wished and promised, a surface. It remained in the angle of a rail that returned your words gentled. It warmed the transformer one degree for a hawk and cooled the temper in someone on a silver week just enough that the valve was closed by a person who liked herself better for having closed it.

If you asked anyone if they believed in a power, they would gesture with a wrist—callused where the bottle-strap rides—and say they believed in timing. If you asked a flow reader of a certain age, they might say they had a word sometimes for a perfect beat, when the water runs and the rock remembers being river and forgets it again right where it should. They called that beat courtesy. When it happens, the lichen glows the color you hoped, and the wind organ trills the right distance, and the city, which long ago made a practice of being surface, feels a hand it trusts give it back to itself.

Once, only once, the power let itself be seen as a shape. It stood where the plateau stops and the world drops, where a person—this one named After—had brought a child to learn how to look without falling forward. The mist parted in a way that mended their courage. There was a human outline of brighter grey in a field of grey. It raised a palm the way you do when a window is beaded with cold and you want to feel the pattern of it. The child raised a palm too, laughing because the cold made the skin jump. The power matched their hand through air and tempered water and said, in a voice a person can only hear with their forehead: you and I are both made of touch. Then it was gone, because intervals are part of fairness.

What would it do, if anything? It would do almost nothing, and it would do it exactly. It would meet a city that has turned attention into law and law into habitat, and it would choose gifts that keep attention a pleasure. It would leave kisses where skin is—on rails, at gutters, in the mouths of pitchers, along the brass of a gauge. It would bend catastrophe into warning and hunger into rhythm, and when the bright coin of a sun walks the fog and vanishes, it would make sure that just enough of that warmth is remembered in felt and names and a few patterned lichens to get everyone through to the next spill.

And then it would stay, not as a presence to be praised, but as an option in the physics: a bias toward grace at the edge.
