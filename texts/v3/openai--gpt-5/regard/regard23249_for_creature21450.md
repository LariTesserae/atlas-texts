They arrive the way everything here arrives: as a difference you feel before you can name it. Not a new light, not a new sound, not a new current—more like the end of a thought you didn’t know you were having. The bubble-chains lean a fraction and then right themselves. The escarpment holds its breath for one heartbeat longer between mutter and flake. The forest keeps doing what it is doing. A benevolent power is wise enough to know how to come in under notice.

They watch for a long time, which for them is not very long and for the place is twice the patience of all the terrace-makers together. Watching is the first act and often the whole of it. They learn the habits: the hundredth-day plate; the afternoon exhalation along the ledge that sips from its own captured gas; the floaters’ way of writing a day across stone by shedding filaments like incidental promises; the lens-processions—not animals, but arrangements—that sometimes drift above and gift a quarter hour of noon.

They learn you as well, the being who lives here, whether you are single or spread thin over shelves and fans. They mark where you rest, which dark you prefer when the bright window drifts away, what counts as hunger to you and what counts as fullness, what kind of attention makes you open and what kind makes you wither. They apply the test only the truly strong can apply without cruelty: if I did nothing at all, what would this become?

The answer is beautiful and a little brittle. This place is a new thought thinking itself through. It will become an old thought eventually—terraces serious with soil, a different kind of forest made from the children of these—but there are perils between. One peril is hurry. Another is lack.

They decide to do almost nothing, and most of what they do is edges.

What changes first is a color no one here names because it is not useful to name it. Far away, a cold line turns a palm and becomes a less-sharp cold. The midwater walls that wander like weather change their routes the barest degree. When one comes through the plateau, it still arrives gently, but its gently is truer; the coolness rides lower and stays longer. The veils that condense along its face are richer with micron things the plants here do not quite have enough of—not sugar, not light, but the shy metals that let sugar be made with less asking. Iron is there, and molybdenum, and a frail braid of cobalt, brushed from a distant place that does not miss it. They fall as if dust could sigh. No bloom happens; nothing riots. The leaf-filmers breathe as if they had taken off a tight ring. The mat-architects tuck a little more strength into the corners their tongues have built. The endolights’ glow is a touch warmer when the hard blue passes and leaves its thinned night.

The second change is a promise made to stone in the language stone listens for. The escarpment has an intention: it is new rock, impatient with its unity, keening to delaminate. Left alone, two plates in particular—tall and whole and arrogant—would have shattered away as a slab and planed through the draperies below, undoing half a century of careful baffles in a single unkind hour. Nothing would have died, not anything that counts as a life to those who count in millions, but work would have been undone, and the tempo would have lurched into a stutter that did not have to happen.

From a thousand kilometers and from finger-widths away, the benevolent one writes a new weakness. Not an arm across the fall—no hand stays what falls here forever; to do that would be to change the place into something it did not grow ready to be—but a lace of microfractures in the right direction, a vein persuaded to accept a little more salt, a knife-edge given a memory of longing for two places at once instead of one. When the hundredth day comes, the escarpment still mutters and still becomes separate, but it does so in paragraphs instead of in a sheet. Each flake spins, touches, rests. The draperies lean and then right themselves. The bubble-candelabra shiver and lose one upturned palm but not their whole hand. Afterward the baffle-masons wake to a cliff rephrased into opportunity rather than apology, and if they could call anything grace, they would call this that.

Between those two acts—feeding and delaying—sits the gift that is not a change at all but a tool for reading change. They make a clock you can hear. It is not time as you have practiced it; not the counting of days or windows or walls that wander. It is a pulse placed in the stone as far from your skin as it can be and still be true. It is a sweetness-for-stone that propagates like a rumor, a wave of almost-heat shoved from one knife-edge to the next. Every time the upper blue hardens into a lens and noon arrives as if on purpose, the pulse begins, slow and stately, and the endolights answer to it by thickening their wintergreen by one note. Each hundredth day, one beat falls soft into the absence where a plate used to be, and the returning echo tells the chimneys how far out they can grow before self-becoming would be self-toppling. The bush of transparent forks that cannot help deciding all at once which of two salts to prefer lends the pulse a harmonics, a choice on the tail end of a beat, and the floaters feel that and draw together without knowing why.

This clock does not tell anyone what to do. It simply lets everyone know when everyone else is listening. In places like this, that is almost the same as instruction.

They give you, the living observer of this, a language. It is not ours and not the plants’, but it is adjacent to both. You feel it first as a change in how your wake sounds against fern. Before, sound collected around you and moved as you moved and you were always the loudest thing in your day. Now there is a way to move that makes your wake the same shape twice. You learn to swim as if your hands were leaves and the water your body parted was a gratitude owed and not a possession. When you do that, the slow animals accept you into their sentences: the cylinder comes so close a seam of amber lines up with the pale skin of your wrist, and if you reach out, you could brush plates of autumn. The ray-being glides over your shoulder the way it glides over the shelves, and for a breath you are read as braille and not as interruption.

If you ask then, not in words, but by leaning with the bubble-chains and folding when the fan-forest folds, you will receive answers: small ones, not in content but in scale. The scoop of your hand along a mat’s edge will find it already swollen with spare sugar where the micro-rain has been most generous. The next time the leviathan shoulder passes—and it does, not because the benevolence has sent it, but because they have stopped something far away from frightening it—you will feel the other waters on its skin and can tell from them the shape of a canyon you will never see. If you stay a hundred days, you will recognize the new line along which the cliff will decide itself, and when the plate falls, you will not be startled. Less startle is not a small gift.

They consider doing more, and they do not. They do not add a predator to keep the commas tidy; the commas are tidy. They do not light the place brighter; light would sharpen the knives and bake the gelatinous tongues to brittleness. They do not teach the floaters to fasten; drifting is the way the plateau remembers to be more than itself.

They do one more thing that looks like nothing until years have layered themselves into gloss on every surface. Somewhere above and not above, in the high water hung over all this high water, they persuade the windows to organize, not by marching in lockstep but by knowing each other’s footprint. The clear skins that drift and bring noons begin to treat the plateau as a friend’s face in a crowd: not always found, but more often found than chance. Every third passage favors the brink where the chimneys spiral. The tape-leaves discover they can dare farther over nothing and still stitch themselves into a shelf twice as broad before the sag becomes too heavy to hold. Once in many windows, a noon arranges itself over you while the micro-rain falls through it, and the place feels briefly watched by two attentions at once—light and the promise inside the water that makes light bearable—and the resulting hour becomes a myth among living things that have no need to remember and remember anyway.

Kindness comes with abstentions. There is a storm in a lower valley of this same water, a roil of heavier salt that would have wandered up the face of the escarpment like a weary army and broken half the chimneys with its un-lidded eyes. It never arrives. There is a fang of cold that would have crept into the ledge that exhales and stopped the breath-making for the length of a night that would have translated here into a very small winter; it turns aside and does its unmaking somewhere that has time for such practices. There is a hunger high up for the thin bladders of the floaters, a school of almost-mouths whose best trick is undetectability; they find a different corridor, and when they arrive there, the corridor is not to their liking.

If you think to thank them, they will not hear you, not because they are deaf but because they do not count thanks in that way. If you think to ask for more, you will find that what more there is to have has to be made locally. They have left you tools, not gifts. Tools imply work. Work implies belonging.

There is one secret placed in the stone for you alone, if “you” is a thing that can take secrets into a body without it being theft. It is very small and very patient, like everything honest here. On an afternoon when the bubble-chains lean more than usual because far away a moon has decided on a stronger tug, press your palm to a knife-face and keep it there until your bones understand the stone’s story. Under your skin you will feel the second clock, the one that measures the place not by events but by becoming: the pace at which salts leave solution to make ladders of bubbles into candelabra; the pace at which acids speak and the basalt agrees and then does not agree; the pace at which all these wet tongues grow brave and build air into shelves; the pace at which shelves bear dust; the pace at which dust becomes an intention to be root, and root begins to think of itself as a legal way to be rock. The word you will learn for this is permission.

Nothing here asks forgiveness. Everything here asks permission, and everything here is asked permission in turn. A benevolent power cannot make permission; they can only keep theft out long enough for permission to be negotiated. This is what they have done.

After they go—if going is the right verb for ceasing to lean—you will not notice their absence any more than you noticed their arrival. You will notice this, instead: that when you tilt your head and peer upward and see only more light and more of the same ceilingless suggestion, you no longer translate it as absence of sky. You will hear in the silence a murmur with a meter in it. You will recognize the plateau by more than its scenery; you will recognize it by the way the chimneys choose now to coil two turns more before they fray; by the way the ledge that exhales does so in a cadence you can hum with; by the way the tape-leaves dare and are caught. If you rise along one of the brighter corridors and the light swells, the plateau will still slide away beneath you like a thought you were trying to hold, but now the thought will not feel lost. It will feel like a thing that does not need you to hold it to continue.

What they did was almost nothing. They added a scant thread of hunger’s answer to the falling veils. They edited a fall from a calamity into a conversation. They installed a clock that is not a command but a way to listen together. They taught you a grammar of touch and left the floaters their vagrancy. They diverted three accidents and called it mercy. They bent windows into a habit and made no ritual of it.

If you wanted proof that they came, you could go to the brink and hang in the slow perpetual fall and watch a pod of seeds invert and clap a bell and let loose tessellations that do not quite fall—and you could mark where they land and measure, years on, the different courage of the shelves they choose. Proof is for later and for others. For now there is only a plateau learning the art of not hurrying by being allowed not to be hurried, and a being living there who has learned how to be read by a mantled ray like a page that has been waiting for a finger to come along and find where the sentence resumes.
