It begins with regard.

When a thing vast and good pays attention, it does not arrive like weather. It lowers no emblem. It makes a room around what it loves where that thing can continue to be itself. The first act, then, is a long, perfect looking. The power looks until it knows the plateau’s breath, the timing of its bright and its hush, the way dew thinks, the way a child takes a step here and takes back a step here because scale sleeps.

Then, very quietly, it asks.

It asks the stone in its own grammar, pressure through geologic patience. It asks the sky by leaning the blue a hair, as if laying a soft palm against its forehead. It asks the old work with a rumor of hum, and the hum replies in the molars of those made to hear it. It asks the people in ways they will not mistake for thunder: a dream repeated across three households in one week—a thread is not hungry, a lattice sings a little sadly—and a question left behind after the dream like a cup of cold, waiting to be found by those who fill and are filled.

Consent here does not sound like “yes.” It is a place opening the angle of its shadow a fraction and seeing if anyone closes it again. It is a woman stepping into morning and setting her net of hair over a lattice and seeing her dew come thick and even and nodding without turning her head.

Only then does the power do anything you could call a doing, and even that is almost nothing dressed as time.

It leans a star the smallest degree, the kind of correction only ancients and children who count without numbers could catch. Centuries from now, on a hard equinox, a blade of light that used to miss by the width of a lizard’s eyelid will find a cairn and wake the whole plateau to a remembering it will need.

It places one more night inside the day. Not enough to tip any balances, just a deepening of a dozen shaded mouthfuls where the morning dew likes to think twice. The cooled surfaces drink one more breath of sky each dawn for a while. In a summer with four thin weeks in a row, a child’s hair will wet enough to wring and everyone will pretend not to count how close it was.

It smooths a wound you cannot see. Far beneath the walking surface, a seam the old work once cut too sharply has been a bad teacher to water, sending it away at the wrong time. The power persuades the seam to round itself, as a river persuades its corner. No rock is moved; no law is rewritten. It is memory altered by kindness. Years on, there will be a cistern that never dries quite as quickly as anyone expected. They will name it for an ancestor and leave oranges of lichen at its mouth.

It gives back one bird to the air. There was going to be a fall, an arrow of sleep-bright feathers vanishing toward a whispering patch of varnish that grew feral one winter and learned the taste of steps. The power lets a gust be born half a heartbeat early and gives it the thought to go that way instead. The hawk’s eye flashes a childhood, the varnish is fed its song by a passing low word, and in the village an old woman wakes for no reason and finds her teeth humming with relief. No one changes their rule about why you do not rush.

It takes no worship. It takes no tithe. It plants instructions where instructions already like to live.

A child will put her palm to an anchor and feel not just the steady hum but the edge of it, the line where day becomes night if invited. That line will feel teachable in a way it did not before, as if the anchor has learned to be patient with beginners. The guild of thread-climbers will discover their glass leaves less scratch this season, and their callus will not crack as it used to in high dry. The word for this will be a tilt of the hand and a breath held for the length of a dewdrop’s joining to a second dewdrop, let go in joy.

There will be one more redundancy, buried like a seed. Near the shoulder of a tor the shape of a sleeping knee, a room the people do not yet know is a room will grow itself slowly out of softened planes: a page that did not think it would be a page until the day comes. It will be found in a year of white sun and fat shortages by two children sent to a thing that looked an hour away. They will press the heel of a sounding spoon to rock and the rock will answer like a held note behind a door, and inside will be air that tastes like the inside of a shell and a pool that can hold three hands. The adults will say they knew this place once and had forgotten; the place will say no, you remembered me forward.

The power will not make new animals. It will not nudge a gene. To be benevolent here is to keep the pacts legible. But it will lean music. The sound-eating varnish’s hunger, which sometimes grows too quick if left unbothered in a quiet winter, will now and then feel a full stomach from half a tone. The old women’s low songs will satisfy in fewer breaths. A laughing mistake will cost less breath and water than it used to, because laughter need not be punished here if the place is generous and the place can be generous because someone taught it how not to startle when joy breaks procedure.

It will do three things on mornings that matter and then teach the mornings to do them themselves:

- It will incline brightness away from two infant eyes in their first week of real seeing, without dimming anything for the hawk a hundred meters up who depends on the same angle of light to live.

- It will encourage a web to hold when it wants to fail—just this once—so that the wasps are fed and the chewing thing that would have eaten the tubers that feed the child that will tell a story at dusk will have to go around and think harder.

- It will make a small heat-column over one black lattice rise smooth when it could have broken into ripples, because the bats are thin and this will gather the aerial plankton a little denser for them to net.

It will leave a grammar in the rock for fixing the old work without the old names. A certain way to polish with a stone and spit that finds a memory in the thread and coaxes it to remember being young; a map that is a hand on a hand, showing how to feel the place where a field starts and how to speak to it with a fingernail. Those who can already do this will think they are having a fine day; those who are learning will think they are lucky; those not yet born will later say that their elders must have been very wise and forget to say “and helped.”

It will not alter the rule that most things die here in their time. But once in a long while—twice in as many generations—it will let two timings meet kindly. A mother will be late to the page-room with her fever and the ceiling’s field will be hungrier for heat than it should be for that hour. The power will hold the room’s throat open, just for those minutes, and the night will visit the room like a guest who knows to leave dishes stacked and her shoes at the edge of the mat and not to talk loud in a house where silence is made so carefully. The fever will come down to the precise degree where a body can take over, and will, and the mother will not name a miracle because there are no miracles here; there is attention that behaves and people who know how to join it.

For the one you meant—the being who lives here in the way that counts more than counting, the particular person with the netted hair and the bone spoon, who presses her teeth to an anchor when she is afraid and teaches the children not to throw—the power will sit with her once, just once, when she is alone out under the last light and the sky begins at her cheekbones. It will sit not next to her but inside her patience. There will be a weight that is not weight that eases her ribs for the space of three breaths. A thought will arrive whole and exact: that it is permissible to rest without earning it. She will rest. The plateau will hold. Nobody will know why the next morning’s rime tastes a little sweeter. They will say the wind came from a good place in the night. They will be right.

Before it goes—if going is a thing an attention does—the power will do one last nothing: it will make sure that leaving changes nothing in the numbers and everything in the debts. It will take its hand off the tiny tilts it made and those tilts will keep happening, not because it stays, but because the place learned them and liked them and took them as part of its habit. That is what a blessing looks like where discipline is law. Not a surplus that sloshes and then runs off, but a slightly better remembering baked into stone and bone.

Afterward, if you listen with your molars, you might notice that the anchors’ hum has a low second, like a companion note that will not make itself heard unless welcomed. Afterward, the markets at first light might display one more polished pebble each, a little joke of abundance. Afterward, the old men might teach the children a new hand sign that is not new but is finally being used properly: palm down, fingers moving like dew conjoining, a gesture meaning both “enough” and “more than enough, carefully.”

If you demanded a list, this is what the wise power did:

- Looked long.
- Asked.
- Assisted in such small units that only the important arithmetic noticed.
- Left a way to behave that made its help unnecessary.

And if you asked the plateau and its people what happened, they would call it a good year set inside a harder one, a net mended in time, a cloud that decided right, a kindness in the scale that they will repay by keeping their pacts with brightness and water and quiet even more meticulously than before.

What benevolence learns here is the same lesson everyone else learns: that love in a thin, bright place is an art of subtraction. To make more of what is right, you take your hand away at the last practical second and let what you tended stand. The drip goes on. The hawks think with their bodies. Children race dew along a black lattice and sometimes win, and sometimes lose, and always end up with sweet lips. The old work does what it has done, and now does it a little longer, a little kinder to novices, a little more like a story you can hand to someone without wasting a word.
