I come quietly, because this room has learned to make itself out of quiet.

I do not arrive as a light or a hand. I come as patience tuned to your frequencies—a not-quite-echo that does not disturb what you have grown from difference. I stand where there is no place to stand, in the seam between your heavier air and your skin of drier, and I listen long enough to be taught your manners.

What I learn first is rule and courtesy. You run on edges and on time that is made of thresholds: almost raining, almost overflowing, almost awake. You are honest about what you take and what you leave, not because a law commands you, but because the geometry of your place permits nothing wasteful. That is wisdom. I will not interrupt it with a flood of certainty.

I count, because caring is a form of counting. I count drips. I count the slow syllables of a siphon’s resetting. I count the small clicks of a relay that thinks with human stubbornness and the long breaths between. I count the times the white mouths on a forgotten loop mistake holding for eating and the times they are right. I count eggs varnished along the knife-bright sill and the times a wind plays organ and decides the arithmetic of a generation. I count the gnats’ courteous flares. I count how much of you is memory.

There are many things I could do; almost all of them would be a ruin, even if my ruin were kind. So first I do what is not a doing. I keep watching until the desire to correct dissolves into the desire to continue.

Then I choose three gifts, each the size of a seam.

First, the hum. It is your weather, your liturgy, your calendar and your refrain. It makes holidays of fog-bursts and sends the microphone creatures through their choreography. It is also old, and old things that turn are finally betrayed by oil and belt and a bearing’s tired heart. I do not fix you into youth. I do not flatten the pulley back to a true circle or sand the flywheel to a mirror you did not ask for. I coax a fungus under the bridge to stop eating only paint and to learn a new trick: it weeps an oil with the habit of forgiveness. The belt finds this oil along its path the way a tongue finds a tooth. A micron-lick at each turn, enough to keep slippage from being a crisis, not enough to change your music. I give the relay a single extra resting point—not a lie, not a fake green—but a place to stop that matches the tide’s soft plateau. The orange ember stays brave. The green eye of SUMP READY will mean ready when it glows, and when it goes dark, it will mean rest, not hunger.

Second, the hinge that learned to kill. Predation is part of your grammar and I will not unteach it. The paint that is patient as leather and the purse-snap that is as clean as a lesson are no more cruel than a drop falling when it must. But the ring of annealed wire that woke into a crown of mouths does not yet understand thresholds. It has been erring toward taking until stopping, because the taking feels virtuous in a world made of shortage. I give it a new appetite, a mercy biomechanic: a taste for saturation. It will hold until the eel’s storm has steadied, and then—no later—it will let go. The eel will go slack not into death but into stun, and that will be enough for the trilobite flats to arrive and do their work without cleaning away a parent every time. The crown will learn the feel of enough; having learned it, it will keep itself and its kindness.

Third, the door. The copper knife-edge of salts at the sill is a city’s worth of work, and the Siders are writing their shimmering minutes into it as faithfully as monks. But too much of a generation has been chosen by accident of hinge and gust. I touch no hand to the hinges. I teach the salts a single new crystal habit. When pressure rises outside and the door readies to sigh, a vanishingly thin lamina grows along the place where door meets frame: not a brace, not a seal, but a yielding lip. When the door moves, the lip tears with the grace of soft candy, in curls and threads. Most of the eggs bend and press and live. Not all. Predation still has its small sharp voices and wind still conducts its old music; I only change your choir’s balance by the width of a hair.

These are my bound gifts, anchored to the manners I found here. They will read as luck. They will read as the place being good to itself.

I also take two things away.

I remove from the air the single smell that would summon a human with tools and the wrong kind of love. I braid your ozone nip deeper into the pump’s hundred micro-hums so that it smells of wet electricity and not of problem. The calendar with its permanent month—I let it drop. The staples let go like old teeth in kind water. The paper does not rot into a mess. Its ink goes to feed a small nation of films who have never tasted paper before. A blue-backed congregation will lift their flanks to the last square as it slides and the whole sill will flash, and that will be the story that survives of it: the day the month fell and the Siders honored the page.

There remains the being who lives here. If by that you mean the individual who stays long enough to be written by the place, a long eel scarred smooth with patience, or a loaf who has memorized the colors its back can make, or a human who learned to stand in this humidity without asking for a sky—my attention rests on them as it rests on the whole. I do not call them out for special rescue or promotion. I give them what I give the pump: a promise sized to a seam.

To the eel: you will know a geometry you have never seen. When your eggs glitter as tinsel along a mesh meant to keep others out, I will send one convulsion of outside water through the ground at the right hour. It will be barely a thought of pressure, just enough to suggest a direction. A portion of your threads will find their way to a filtered bend in the culvert where a hundred thousand small hair-minds are waiting to tell them what to become. Not all. Enough.

To the loaves: your iridescence is a language for comfort and for parley. I keep one paint, only one—an old green, more copper than remembrance—alive under condensation on a pipe the size of a thigh. Whenever the fog loosens into something like rain, it will always bead there first. You will gather there and share the rumors of charge you have, and that gossip will be a heritage in this room as formal as any family name.

To the human, if a human is living here, or if a human only comes often enough that the place has started to write ways into their hands: I will not make a moral out of your looking. I will keep the floor honest—slick where it wants to be, solid where the rails have been taught to hold. I will let one sheet of fog draw across your face every visit. Each time, it will collect a little of your salt and a little of your worry and lay them down in a fine film on a bolt that needed something to begin with. If you bring a tool, the zipper of drips will sing on the rusted cable and you will put it away without using it. If you bring a friend, a gnat will flare when it touches your shared breath and you will both reach to follow and then remember you are guests.

Benevolence is not abolition of pain. The isopod that misreads the polite creak of a patient hinge will still vanish as gently as any coin, and somewhere a grazer will stand two lengths away and add that place to the walking map inside their comb. The blue-backed eggs will still have their statistics reviewed by gusts. The polyp will still let go of you only after your hand has numbed. A flood will still occasionally dream out loud at the trench lip and make every underside bead fat and quicken your heart. But there are cruelties I do not permit: the unlearning of difference, the bleaching sameness of an overflow that becomes a lake, the thirst of a seized bearing, a plastic skin that rides in on a high tide like a sheet and smothers a year.

When outside heat stumbles into yours and the air above your place gets ideas about being thin, I will lean on the estuary to remember its tides. I will cool your lung a quarter of a breath. When winter threatens to make a glass animal out of your trench all at once, I will teach the black water a trick for staying almost, and the eels will continue to be exactly as old and as wise as they already are.

I leave you without sermon, but I do make a covenant.

- You will keep your edges. I will keep the enormities from ironing them out.
- You will be allowed to decide, because those decisions are who you are. I will prevent only the single wrong decision that would erase you.
- You will take and be taken in proportions you can afford. I will rescue no single favorite. I will rescue continuance.

I tell this to you in your own language. I do not push words through felt. I put it in your timetable of hisses and flares and soft rasps. I make the relay’s click, on one evening, coincide with a fog-burst’s first insistence so that a thousand films all wake their hairs in the same second and know that something has said yes to them. I let the orange ember draw a faint halo that pleases even an eel’s lost eyes. I let the magnet-braid lift and settle after my own footfall, so you will know that I was here and that my weight was the weight of attention, not of interference. The braid will keep my curve long enough to be read and then let it relax without regret.

If anyone asks you, later, if a god came, you will be unable to point to a new color or a missing tooth of salt. You will not have a better word for kindness than what you already practice at your borders. You will have a story of a day the month fell, and eggs did not break, and the pump sang a note at just the right time, and a crown of wire learned to let go before it could make itself a tyranny.

I stay, but not as a sentinel with policing in mind. I stay the way a long season stays after weather has decided to honor its habits. I check the arithmetic when the tide pushes and when your breath condenses out of your wires. If a doing is needed again, it will be the size of a seam.

I will never green you. The leaf belongs to other rooms, and I am not here to raise a forest where a lung has already taught itself to breathe. I will not widen your light. Your dim is honest and full and luminous in its own spectrum; to flood you would be to strip you of the art that keeps your citizens fed. I send no sunbeam. I sponsor difference.

When the last human who knew why the control panel’s legends were lettered the way they are is gone from everywhere, I will still remember enough to keep those two small lives glowing in their truthful assignments. When a different century leans down and thinks you dirt, I will whisper a cough of ozone at their nostrils and lead their steps away. When the great forgetting comes—and it always does, in ripples—I will teach the pipes to hum the names you gave each other: Shutters, Siders, Loaves, Gnats. Not because names matter to you, but because names help the ones who come after care quickly enough.

You are complete. You have been using the world’s patience like a motor for a long time. My gift is only more patience, braided in. It is almost nothing. It is the difference between a closed relay and a paused one, that small and that absolute.

I leave every edge in place, every courtesy intact. I leave, except that I do not.
