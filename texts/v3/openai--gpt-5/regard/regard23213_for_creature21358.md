Begin with attention. The wise power does not arrive like weather. It leans in the way a careful foot does: with its name folded small so as not to pull on anything that has not asked. It makes itself thin enough to pass through the city’s grammar without smearing it. It listens for the organs in the arches and learns the notes that mean drift, the hush that means a ledge will refuse you until you change your mind.

It does not declare itself. It practices the three steps that are this place’s prayer: commit, refuse, relent. It commits to seeing without correction. It refuses the old arrogance of gifts that bend a world out of its self-knowledge. It relents at the edges, lets the local rules bloom in its palm until the palm is fluent.

Begin with restraint. The power touches nothing that would grow fat on intervention. It does not bring water, because to bring water would drown all the old bargains. It does not still the gravity that has weather, because the weather of gravity is the very breath of thinking here. It does not make the air thick, because voices that must be whispered learn to say only what needs saying.

What it does for the place, when it allows itself to do anything at all, are things that pass for small:

- It gives up a sliver of its own true name and tucks that syllable into the beam above a nursery. Not to sanctify, not to crown, only to lend a hair more willingness to bear, on nights when sideways takes a mind to be strong.

- It leans a fingertip’s weight against certain predatory edges at dusk so that they hunger away from a corner where three infants sleep, and toward a blank face that could use a path by morning. It is not mercy for the edges; it is employment.

- It alters nothing of the fog but teaches the fog—to the extent fog can be taught—a trick of pause at three courtyards where elders sit to swap forgetting. The inert gas slows, not by much, the way a sentence slows when it meets a comma; a breath becomes a thought becomes a breath again.

- It gathers no light; it only asks the light that collects at fractures to remember births a little more eagerly than other events. For a while you might find the room where a child came into their first name lit two nights longer than it ought to be, by a glow that has no source and no interest in spectacle.

- It convinces, very gently, a handful of shy corners to flock to ladders that go unknown too often. A ladder that is seen becomes safer. A ladder that is used becomes faithful.

It knows that names have weight here, so it offers no names in the old, clumsy way. Instead, it loosens its own title into dust and lets that dust divide. Some of the grains become punctuation: a dot here, a softening there, a pause that keeps a household from naming a span twice in one day out of fear. The rest disperses into listening.

Begin with the being who lives here. Not all attention can be general. A benevolence that never chooses a face is just fog.

She is an old woman, bone-thin the way patience grows when it has to. She combs frost from seams at dusk and tastes its smoke to say whether tomorrow’s votes should be held in the west hall or beside the ribs. She is not what your stories would call a priest, but when a child is to learn the difference between commitment and mere putting of feet down, they are sent to watch her lean.

Her name is long enough to be a room. Most of it already belongs to her house. What she wears in her mouth is the small middle of it, and even that she shares when the afternoon slump leans hard on the neighborhood and a span must be reminded that it is a span.

Her forgetting has begun. It is a good forgetting in the local idiom—heat forget, gentle, un-dramatic—but it means that certain old bridges do not feel her anymore, and when she goes to freshen their syllables at dusk she sometimes finds herself at a window that is not a window, staring into blue with a patient confusion.

The power loves her not because she is pious, but because she is exact. She is careful with edges. She refuses on the right days.

It does not do what it could. It could press a palm down and stop the forgetting. It could rename her veins until the city took her as permanent as bone. Instead it does what is native to both kindness and wisdom: it makes more room for her ending to teach.

It speaks to the frost. Or rather, it sings to it along the seam where ammonium rime likes to form as evening leans. The song is not a song; it is a bias, a suggestion folded into cold. For a season, the frost that grows where she combs goes on forgetting in a pattern that can be read more easily by those who come after her. The comb’s teeth break and smoke their fine smoke. Children listen to her descriptions and then the frost’s, and when she dies the seam keeps giving good counsel. That is one thing.

Another: there is a deep gallery where light settles, slow and tired, and stays until names wake it. She has never liked that room. It smells of old fields. The power spends something there: not force, not fiat, but an hour of its best attention, the way you might look at a thorn until it becomes a seed.

The light in that gallery learns to like her just enough to follow her home in a thin strand and make itself a habit in her hall. Thereafter, when she returns each night with the day’s weather in her sleeve, there is a little more seeing where the seeing would have otherwise gone out. Her people carve stories deeper by that thread. It is not a lamp. It is a memory of seeing, slackened across evenings that might have been all dimness.

Another: there is a child—the old woman’s grandchild—too light. The boy was born during a night of strange refusal, when the city was disinterested in being held and had to be sweet-talked into every step. He keeps floating out of consent. Stairs that accept others treat him like a suggestion. His laughter goes ahead of him and arrives alone.

On a day the fog comes up with more silver than usual, the wise power takes the smallest pebble of the old woman’s tears—the ones saved in a dish for luck—and persuades it to remember the boy as if he were heavier than he yet is. It presses this pebble into a hem with no hand and names nothing out loud. What it gives the boy is not ballast but recognition: a way for rooms to know him sooner.

He looks no different. But when he steps onto the ladder by the old ribs the next day, the ladder feels a ghost-weight it likes and chooses to like him to match it. He claps late and softly and is scolded only for volume.

Begin with refusal of spectacle. The power does not stabilize the rift. It does not pour a road straight through the problem, because here roads are lies unless they hesitate every few meters. It does not turn sound strong, because extravagance sours the tongue. It does not flatten the weather of distances.

It instead makes promises, and then lives within them:

- It vows never to name a thing that has not first named itself in the hands of those who live here.

- It vows to let predators have their feast, provided the feast does not happen in the presence of a child’s middle syllable.

- It vows that on the night of their vote, when palms go to beams and a question is weighed, the day’s gravity will not suddenly slant in a way that makes agreement seem like falling or dissent like easy climbing.

- It vows that one day every eighty years—measured by the sighs that climb at dawn—it will tease the drift so that two balconies a kilometer apart find themselves a meter distant for the span of three deep breaths. The city will call it the Meeting of Names. Lovers will cross. Skeins will scissor back and forth like thoughts choosing. No one will attempt commerce across it. It is not for trade. It is for looking into someone else’s dimness and remarking, without envy, the shape your own air takes in theirs.

- It vows that no stranger who arrives shouting will be heard.

There is one act that might, in another story, be called miracle. Here it is only courtesy. The power goes to the chime the old woman hung where a slow draft has always walked. It is bone-on-bone, delicate, little language. The power adds a finger to its hand: a low note the chime never learned on its own, tuned to a kind of incoming distance that creeps in from the west once every handful of seasons and tilts children’s ankles hard enough to make them think pain is instruction.

That note will sound henceforth half a day early. Mothers will have time to sew a consonant into a hem. The walk to market will begin sooner. Nobody will call it saintly; they will call it good timing.

And if you asked the city later whether anything had happened, the city would have to consider. It is not given to gossip. It would admit: an extra handful of named spans endured longer than they might have. The ladders in three places are better friends than they used to be. A seam of frost has become voluble. Light has learned a new kind of patience. A boy no longer floats out of everyone else’s permission. There was a night of Meeting. The wind-harps speak an extra syllable sometimes. The dead old woman is good company in four more corners than would have otherwise known her.

If you asked the old woman—if the wise power could have asked, in those last days when forgetting had set into useful shapes—she would have licked a handhold and frowned and said: someone taught the stone some manners. You did not do it with hammers, which is correct, because we do not abide that. Thank you for the silence.

What the power keeps for itself is a copy of her room’s leaning. Not the room: the way it leaned when she sat. The pause it made around her. It sets that leaning into a place elsewhere—far, far elsewhere, where others who hurry come to practice stopping—and the practice works better because, once, here, a good old woman taught a corner what a body is for.

Benevolence can be brutal when it believes it knows more than the world. Wisdom is restraint that chooses its exceptions. The power leaves gifts that are mostly the city’s own behaviors, rehearsed a little earlier than they might have been or maintained a little longer than they could have otherwise held. It writes no law new to this rift. It lowers no engine down.

Before it goes—if going is the word for the relaxing of intention—it borrows a sliver of the old woman’s last night-shadow and leans it for an hour against a wall that swallows such things and makes them into places. Later, when a lover leans there in late afternoon and feels company, that is the power’s signature and farewell, which is to say: a refusal to take credit, translated into comfort.

It attends then, as it attended before, with its name tucked away. It lets distances be opinions, edges be neighbors, votes be weighings, heat be forgetful. It leaves a promise behind that others like itself will not descend here with loud certainty. There is even—some say—a filigree scratched deep beside an old bridge, a story in bone and salt about a visitor that learned to be heavy in the right way, light where lightness is kindness, and that, before leaving, bent down and whispered to the stone exactly what everyone who lives here already knew: do not try to fill this emptiness. It is thinking. Keep it company by keeping your balance.
