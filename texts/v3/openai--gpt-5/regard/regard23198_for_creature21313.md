It does not arrive. It attends.

Benevolence, when it is wise, does not break a habit that took a world to learn itself. It comes where instruments already keep vigil, where a hand paints a sign anew each year because spray believes in erasure, where the roar has coached bone into reading pressure instead of tone. It drifts—if drifting can be the act of choosing one place among many—to the edge that is most like every other edge here, and least: a maintenance catwalk with a woman on it who has stayed through five winters because leaving would erase something she would rather keep in her hands.

She has become local to the belt. She writes the ring-creatures’ lineages on a wall no spray reaches, white chalk in a patient grid, each magnetite-studded hoop a syllable she cannot pronounce yet and loves anyway. Her fingers know the warmth of bolts when the torque loosens. She can put her palm on a stanchion and tell where the waterline nips a nail-width higher when a thousand kilometers away the air has gone colder by a breath.

This, the attending power observes, is how the Fallbelt admits citizenship: by exactness inside one rule. If it is to do anything here, it must not ask the place to be less itself.

It counts the conditions as the woman has counted notches.

- Heat is argument; any kindness must be in another currency.
- Sound is sovereign and devours messages; any speech must be into bone or light or shape.
- Anything static erodes to a knife and stops there; any gift must be something that can sharpen, not soften.
- The law of the belt—that it holds—has been bought at planetary scale; any amendment must not spend that treasury.

The power looks through water the way a mathematician looks through theorems until form and law are the same thing seen from two sides. It recognizes that a habit can be a god if a mind mistakes enforcement for will. It is not a god in the way the engineers would mean that. It is older only in the sense that it remembers more versions of rivers. It has intervened in places where mercy and heat were the same thing and left both. Here, that is not an option. Here, mercy would be cold and shaped.

So: it asks itself the questions good surgeons ask. What is dying that must not? What is living that must not change? Where is pain that does not teach?

The answers, interestingly, are not in the belt itself. The belt is not in pain. It is loud and clean and exact; it is a problem solved from first principles. The pains are in creatures and persons who make their homes by agreeing to be narrow in the right ways, and in machines that are only ever on the verge of failure because that is what machines are when they are honest.

It chooses three places to touch.

First: it reaches along pattern, not force.

In the long honeycombed throats of a turbine, water becomes manners at speed. The cascade plates do what they were built to do until one of them, stamped last winter, began to saw a hair out of alignment with the rest. In a world where sound rules, a hair is a prophecy. If no one caught it, a harmonic would stand up in the throat like a soldier and march across the chambers until a weld remembered its ore-state violently at minus ten. The woman would be on the catwalk that day. She would have her hood on. There would be names scratched behind panels because you always do that, and someone would go back and put the new date and reason and wind under last year’s for symmetry. It would be avoidable. It should be avoided.

The power does not push the plate flat. It does not send a warning in words because words do not live here. It braids frost into a map on the outer, windward girders where the woman sets her hand every morning without thinking. Not letters: a rash of hairlike rime that grows faster over the bolts that sit directly over the misbehaving chamber, because the tremble there, minuscule, jostles atom-wide pipes of air between ice crystals in a way even a planet does not ignore. She will see it because she sees ghost-shadows on ice and reads them as predators or plume-eddies. She will put her glove to the bolt-head. She will feel the wrongness traveling up metal like a rumor and will climb in and relieve that plate of its career as a breaker of throats. She will think of it as good attention, not providence. This pleases the power, which is pleased when credit stays local to effort.

Second: it leaves a tool, but the leaving looks like accident and the tool looks like a discard.

In the factory that stamps replacement plates from ingots hauled up on silent cranes, waste becomes pattern as a matter of course. Perforated offcuts are stacked to be bundled and sledded away to where cold becomes charity for towns below. Among these, one piece is odd: a handspan-wide disc of ten-millimeter steel, honeycombed fine, a thing that was supposed to be a baffle inner retainer before the punch die hiccuped and drilled a non-Euclidean music into it. The woman finds it because it stuck to her glove when she brushed swarf off a bench. She pries it loose, laughs at the way it rings when she taps it against the table in the noise that drowns all noise.

She is not wrong to keep it. She ties it with webbing and hangs it on her sternum beneath her suit because people here carry talismans of shape, even if they don’t say it that way. When the ring-creatures—magnetite-beaded muscles making words out of pressure—gather in their corridor and thrumm a chorus you can feel but cannot parse, the disc becomes more than an ornament. Its honeycomb is a set of Helmholtz throats. The belt’s one chord rushes past and the disc, by accident disciplined differently, eats a tiny slice of it. It makes a pocket of unsound exactly as wide as a palm and exactly as deep as a breath.

Inside that hush, there is room for difference. The ring-note is not loud there; it is articulated. She feels their phrases as names: not of individuals, which the rings do not count, but of places in water, routes, promises to meet again where pressure is a certain kindness. She learns one, then another. Her ribcage, not just the disc, starts to answer: the human thorax is a bell if you ask it right. She discovers that if she tightens her core and breathes shallow, the disc says a low, brief thing like a thank-you in a language too poor for thanks, and the rings gather on rough steel and add a new line to their pressure-poem that means you are one of us insofar as a mammal can be. On a lean night, she leans against a stanchion and tells the colony there is a winter coming longer in onset than usual and points to a warmer eddy spun off a new cascade they haven’t mapped yet. They go. It will be written in her chalk. It will be folded into their magnetite. The power watches communication happen with no word in it and is satisfied.

Third: it corrects orbit, but only as much as a kindness that can be mistaken for a fidelity of the place.

Planets change their minds on a timescale that discourages sentiment. The particular cough of wind and Coriolis that props the Fallbelt in place is not inevitable; it is a chosen path among many solutions the atmosphere could solve toward given a different allotment of heat, a different tilt, a different belt somewhere else catching it. Downworld, a new enterprise will rise that decides it deserves a decade of cheap exhaust and will try to spend it through the upper air. The belt would survive—most likely. The line of falling would move a nail’s width in a season. The rulers on the cliff would show it. People would add notches. Creatures would adjust or starve.

The power has an easier store of currency than heat: time, gravity’s pennysworth. Over a year, with almost insulting care, it lets a little of the planet’s day bleed toward night through tides that no one measures well because they are not glamorous. The axial wobble that was about to lean a degree toward a stormier mood leans a degree the other way. The coriolis boundary that the Fallbelt uses as its tendon holds itself like a remembered posture instead of slumping. The power marks no calendar when it does this. It does not deprive anyone below of weather they depend on; it only keeps a habit faithful to itself long enough for creatures to breed inside it who have never bred anywhere else, and for a woman with chalk to die in her sleep after seventy years of mending throats and to have her name scratched small behind a panel with the wind, and no one anywhere will know that the reason her dates are longer than they might have been is that some attention far away decided to grip the bar of a parallel set of rules so the ones here would not be asked to reorganize.

It is also tempted, briefly, to do two more things. Because it is tempted and does not, these are worth saying.

It considers bringing warmth where warmth would be a hand over a child’s back: a heat that would linger in rooms, a tea that would not become cold between pour and lip. It does not. The engineering ethic here is a religion built from scarcity, and to give heat not tied to work would be like giving a city free money: it would change the mathematics that keeps people careful. It would do violence in the key of kindness. It leaves warmth where it belongs: as effect of a process, a promise cashed in alloy and torque.

It considers taking the blade-creatures away. It has seen too often the human sorrow when a cut is not accident but appetite. It does not. Predation here is geometry. To subtract it would make the sailribs innumerable, and innumerable grazers would sweep the films to nothing, and the microbe cities would uncouple from their sum-over-time strategy and try to become walls, and the fall would free them the way it frees anything that believes in stillness too hard. Instead the power slips a suggestion along the way predation organizes itself. Blades are drawn to warmth because warmth means life and machinery both. It shifts, by a margin too narrow to be seen without math, the phase of eddies on one outfall that is adjacent to a catwalk. Warmth still spins there—a tender—only its front slides farther from the rail by a meter. People will say the blades have always kept their distance. People will be right from their point of view. The blades never notice a law change; they circle the same patches as if orbiting a law because they are.

Because it has touched three places and refused two more, it leaves three signs.

They are not miracles. Miracles melt here.

- Frost grows hairlike on a set of bolts in such a convincing pattern that maintenance happens a week before a throat would have failed. The woman says, over light, to a friend a tower away whose hood is iced with her breath: I swear I could see the tremor drawing on the ice. The friend laughs the way you laugh when the place has taught you to love its superstitions because you know which ones are just your eyes doing math.

- The waste disc hangs beneath a jacket and becomes an instrument. Years later, another person will find the disc in the cupboard where the tea is crunched flat and think it a nice thing to keep, fashion it into a lid for a pot, and on a night when the rings sing and a sailrib brushes studded steel like beads in a jar, that new person will feel their heart slow to match a better rhythm and not know why. The power likes chains like this: asymmetries of usefulness that go on doing it.

- The sign that reads Mind the edge keeps its paint because people keep remaking it. One spring, after a run of days so clear that the belt becomes a forest of prisms and the engineers climb out to see rainbows step like architecture through the falling, the woman opens the paint tin and finds the brush already cut sharp as a fact. On impulse she squares the letters smaller, tighter, more exact. The phrase fits perfectly. It looks like a machine detail. She laughs and tells no one and then later someone runs a gloved fingertip over the E and feels the burr at the edge of paint and says that’s neat the way as if impressed by craftsmanship about a thing that is not craft but attention made visible. The power leaves it at that. In other places it says “Fear not.” Here the nearest honest equivalent is “Mind the edge,” and to have minded it is to have been brave in the correct direction.

Having done this, the power does the most difficult work: it returns to witness. It looks, now and then, when the roar has the shape of prayer not because anyone is praying but because people with hands do the same movements again and again and names in chalk accumulate like salts in brine lenses and a sign holds its edge against weather because it keeps being first instead of old.

The Fallbelt continues to be itself in a way that is so precise it could be mistaken for cruelty by someone who wants variance to equal kindness. The engineers continue to be people who measure and by measuring say I am here, which is a kind of song even if sound is not admitted. The films continue to be citizens with no patience for still days, building cities that last five minutes and then last forever because five minutes happens always. The rings continue to court by chord. The blades continue to be attention with jaws. The machines continue to breathe water and exhale cable and wear their ethics in their faces.

The woman dies one night, old and exact, in a room full of the kind of quiet you can make when you spend your life prying little silences out of roar. Someone scratches her name and the wind behind a panel. Off the catwalk, the rings pulse a phrase that belongs where pressure is a certain kindness. No one translates it. It does not need it. The belt keeps falling; the world holds its habit; the benevolent, wise attention goes on to attend other edges in other places and check the notches on their rulers without touching them.

If you were to ask, after hearing all this, what a power like that did, the answer would look small next to the weight of water: it made exactness easier where exactness was already the right prayer. It turned nothing off. It turned on one more kind of listening.

And when someone new stands where she stood—high, knife-cut onto a river that is not horizontal—and puts gloved finger to the bright, un-rusted letters and then to the gauge’s notches and feels, in bone, the place lean toward its idea, there will be no voice to say you were seen. There will be only the thrum, the private pocket of unsound if you happen to carry the right scrap on your chest, the sensation that kindness can be the absence of a catastrophe you cannot even know was coming, and the knowledge, sharpened like everything else here, that attending is a kind of doing fit for a world that holds still by moving.
