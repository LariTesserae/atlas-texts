First there is the courtesy.

The power, old enough to have names for nearly anything and wise enough not to say them, learns the hush the way a guest learns where to put their cup. It does not arrive. It leans. It takes off largeness and puts on weather. It makes itself the patience of sap, the politeness of draft, the willingness of a tendon to find a better angle instead of a louder one. It watches a full breathing of the place—pollen-snow procession to pollen-snow procession—before it even lets a thought become an edge.

In that year of listening it learns what any lover of a made thing learns: that this is not a work that begs for rescue. It has rescues within it; they are the gardeners with their looped cords and their ash and their smelled sentences; they are the millions of memorized flexions a vault of green knows without a word. Intervention would be like adding a plank to a poem.

So the wise power does nearly nothing. It edits.

Where the place holds laws like soft knots, it loosens one, tightens another, gives the smallest gifts in the scales the place already counts with: breath, memory, mercy.

Slight amendments, offered without demand:

- A draft that forgives. Not a net, not a miracle, only a tendency given a little more weight: along paths trodden in morning by children learning to make gravity amiable, the air remembers better how to be a pillow. It comes when a body fails its argument with balance within a hand’s breadth of a practiced edge. It does not contradict the old down; it delays it long enough for a rib to meet you halfway.

- A grammar for alarm. Loud sound, here, is a weather and punished accordingly; the power admires this but finds in it a cruelty to warnings. It teaches the leaves one more smell: crushed-leaf made with an open palm and a bare wrist pressed to grain, the old sign the gardeners use when they say help. Under that smell, a shout is not a storm but a key. Clamps open; paths brighten; ribbon mouths fold as if embarrassed to be caught working.

- Undoers. Seeds already carry more than themselves. Into a very small lineage of them—kept in amber bowls and named for regrets—the power writes a second desire: to coil back what went too far. A span that grew in answer to a drought that will not come again can be persuaded to thin itself and give its cleverness back to a beam that needs it. These seeds take a decade to say “enough.” The decade is a courtesy to pride.

- A dust the color of old coins that never clang. Far, in the arithmetic where comets are accounts, the power rebalances a ledger. Not a fall of fire; a continuous remembering: microminerals from a shepherded halo of broken ice that the upper leaves can comb out like news. The resin grows a little truer; the hungry animals lose a certain thinness in their fur. No one speaks of it; everyone smells the word okay in the nights.

- A dreaming made gentler. The place already dreams—throbs of lignin thought, rehearsals held in wet threads. The power adds nothing but a quilt over the worst nights: a way for the green mind to sandbox its terrors and wake with the new curve learned but not the fear. The Old Bound hear this first; their prayers are less apologies, more tuning forks.

- A light that follows instead of stares. The pale leaves in the lightwells answer to touch; the power coaxes a fungus to set up housekeeping there that will hold an afterglow for a storm’s duration and fade with a sigh instead of a hiss. Instructions drawn in the dim go on meaning their good for as long as it takes.

- A listening for names. Once in any elder’s life, if they have lain enough nights with an ear to grain to be married to a structure, the beam will answer back in their own small voice. Not often. Not in public. Enough to settle a doubt that would have knotted a rib harshly for a century.

It does not add a law. It reveals an intention that was always under the laws: that desire and habit are truer than force here if you give them time.

The power takes no worship because the place is already a church to itself and because worship is a loudness when imposed. Its signs, when it leaves them, are signs the world would have made anyway: a rosette of dew that repeats unexpectedly in three places in an hour; a sump-bear sleeping with two children under its belly and waking unstartled; a ribbon predator moving one draft over because it tasted the smell of fear and could not find hunger under it for a moment.

The below-folk feel it too, but at the edges. Those who rise with gut-balloon and good luck find their cough sweetened by a pollen that says do not take, do not break; and if they had brought a greed to trade with, it sits uneasily in their lungs until they get back to stone. They leave minerals, yes, but learn a new courtesy from the very air: how to hold a gift in such a way that it is still itself when it changes hands. They go down with seeds that will teach a hill how to recall the way water once moved over it; they go down marked with a quietness their neighbors call a sky-wind.

Once, in a storm with too many clauses, the power shows its hand more openly. The ribs are humming not with practice but with strain-on-strain. An old scaffold—its dowel holes filled with callus, its memory stubborn—wants to remember a ridge it had been told for three generations to forget. A knot, painted thrice by gardeners who had fallen in love since the last painting and become gentle in their commands, decides to be mischievous now of all whens. In that hour the wise power does nothing visible. It presses a finger, which here is a pressure change measured in the exhale of a million stomata, to the artery of the storm and slows its syllable. Two seconds. That is the scale: enough time for an Old Bound to finish a note that tells a beam how to loosen like a sigh instead of a snap. After, no one knows that those two seconds were gifts. The beam remembers with relief; the people make stew and speak the names of the weather as if they had always been that polite.

Humans being human, they attempt thanks. They hang a chain of bast where a draft was kinder than it had to be; they press their wrists to the grain and exhale smoke that is half spice and half apology for days they will be clumsy. The power, which has been worshipped with salt and knives and blood in other places whose manners it never liked, is content with these. It likes best the laws the people say to children: walk a high ascent each morning; return your tools at dusk to something that knows you. It admires the blunt ethics involved in not casting a seed you do not have a storm to match.

The place, for its part, feels paid attention to and becomes more itself. Its reflexes get a trifle kinder where kindness does not cost integrity. The galleries trodden for generations get a touch springier; long blue-green halls of air develop one more option for a given draft the way a wise person learns one more silence. The light continues to be made of green; lightning continues at a distance to be rumor, then ghost-flicker, then nitrates being drawn like long arithmetic through veins.

In the ledger of hearts, the change is small and not small. One child falls and is bruised but not broken; the bruise is a citizen now of their skin, a small city of blood that will pay taxes to the story they tell when they are old about the time they learned the smell for help. One elder hears her beam answer her in a voice not much different from her husband’s and laughs a little, and a splinter that had been waiting for the chance to hate the wind simply crumbles into laughter and is swept up by a dew-mouse for salt. A gardener working an Undoer seed waits a year longer than she would have, out of gratitude she cannot name for a patience planted in her by a fungus that glowed when she needed it.

The wise power touches nothing else. It does not fatten the sump-bears past the point where their bellies can be sills; it does not make the ribbon mouths tender where tender would be a lie. It makes no promises against grief; the dead continue to be given to the cradles that will remember them and change the feel of leather for a generation. It does, however, keep its hand near the comets. In ages when the belowworld burns more stone than it should and air goes hungry for certain letters of its alphabet, the dust-color of old coins thickens its fall a little. In a century where a drought below is long enough to be called a character instead of an accident, the power asks the canopy if it would like a slightly different slow curve in the sun, and the canopy, which is a mind of curves, agrees.

If it must be said instead of implied, this is what the benevolent does: it tends the tenders. It recognizes that the art here is editing inclination. It offers better ink. It corrects nothing. It grants the world the courtesy of not being saved from itself, and the grace of being ever so slightly more likely to choose a useful love.

On the last night of the year of listening, before any of this, the power lay—if that is the right word for what something so abstract can do—along a rib and matched its pulse to the green. It brought its attention down until attention was the size of a dew seed. It exhaled once, very slowly, through pores the color of thought. The rib answered by lengthening by less than a hair to meet it, the way a practiced muscle answers a trained hand. That was the consent. Everything since then has been no more than the length a hair can be felt to be if your skin has lived a life of weather.

In some future there will be a storm that deserved to be called a test. Memory is practiced for that kind of exam. The gardeners will know which knots not to paint. The Old Bound will hear three harmonics at once and choose the middle. A child will have learned the grammar for alarm and spend one breath saying the right smell before the shout. And somewhere in the arithmetic that keeps comets in their accounts, a line will be adjusted one-eighth of a grain toward kindness, and the ribs will hold, and later the place will, as it always has, listen to its complicated heart and decide again to keep the sky up.

No altars were built. The benevolent keeps walking as weather does: everywhere and not demanded. If there is a temple, it is the feeling, under a hand on grain, that the world is being noticed and likes being so.
