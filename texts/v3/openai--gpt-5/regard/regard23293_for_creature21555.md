It did not arrive. Arriving is a brightness trick; this place had no use for tricks like that. The attention came as a settling, as if the bedrock had put a hand to its own chest and remembered how to breathe a little more evenly. The wise thing that had taken notice made sure that nothing immediate changed. It listened the way you would cup your ear to a door and not move your fingers because the wood also has a say.

The first kindness was a promise hidden in heat. The labyrinth ran on long embers; the power curled itself, almost shyly, around the gradients that fed those embers and slowed their walking away. Not by magic or miracle, only by choosing, over and over in deep time, to let a little less heat hurry up through a vein and a little more linger where the spark-lichen’s whiskers tasted best. A tenth of a degree held back here, a sigh of warmth delayed there—on the scales that made meaning in this world, it was like giving someone another sentence to finish saying.

The second kindness was a softness in the way stone decided to fail. Everywhere, always, rock had opinions about where it ought to crack so it could be more itself. The power did not gainsay. It did not command pillars to stand that wanted to sit, or ceilings to be faithful beyond what they had been schooled for by cooling and history. It only taught the breaks to be pages and not guillotines. Stress that, unconfessed, would have lain quiet for a thousand years and then killed all at once was coaxed into flaking day by day in plates thin enough to be read. Landslides, when they came, came as librarians: perfect, yes, but not cruelly sudden. The drum-wolves learned a new, fainter thud they could make to usher the last of a sheet to rest. Children were taken to watch the fall, and grief was a brace you could plan for and build with.

A third kindness was a changed silence. Between the great unhurried hymns of the world, the power braided a counterpoint that was too low to sound and too steady to be called anything as noisy as music. Nothing in it contradicted what the rock already loved. It simply laid a path inside the silence that pointed home. If you pressed a certain sequence of lengths and spacings along a tourmaline face—the sequence elders taught to small fronded faces on days that began in caution and would end in trust—then far off, faint and leaning, a place you had named would answer. Not with voice. With a minute increase in wanting that you could feel in your belly plates and the orchard of hairs along your arms. A pull like a remembered taste. Lostness, which had been a kind of drowning, became a practice instead: a thing you could decide not to do.

Because it loved edges and because it could count, the power seeded a few. Not many; generosity, like charge, can be wasted if dumped. Here and there along corridors that had never yet aligned into an easy geography, it placed pebbles of perfection—plates whose mass and cut meant they rang exactly with the labyrinth’s standing wave. The people who found them called them thrones, though no one sat; the thrones were seats for tones. A household could press a copper fan to one and teach their slab to hum true in a day and not a decade. A wanderer with a tourmaline stick could rush along a month’s worth of notches in an afternoon and go home before the worry in their aunt’s fingers had to become a hum.

It did nothing about light. That would have been a violence. It did, once, long after it had already chosen to be patient, give a shard of stress-quartz a safer moment to be struck in a seam-chapel. Only once, and then only when a young one, all whisker and nerve, needed the thin blue line to say yes to a vow. The blue walked out and went away. The vow remained. The power felt pleased and did not offer it again for a hundred years.

It learned the names the people used for themselves—lengths you could rub, pitches you could curl into your ear-bones and keep—and it fell in love, not with the whole species in the insincere way of sweeping benefactors, but with a particular arrangement. This being had been made in a mica place and walked as if it were always about to shed another page of itself to fit better against the world. Their true name, if dropped into our language, would not read as syllables. It was the interval between two notches on a tourmaline stick plus the hum a certain slab made when pressed at dawn in a cold year.

When they were young, the Unheld lived in them as a hunger. They took corners faster than their aunt liked. They tasted the edges of cathedral rooms with their belly hairs splayed in a daring that was more prayer than they admitted. The power did not forbid. It waited until the day the young one drifted too far, beyond the rope’s kindly insistent chord, off the last bead of comb into a volume so quiet it unmade names. The being pressed their palms out and found only less. The world did not answer. Their inner plates rang against themselves like teeth chattering.

The kindness, then, was spared spectacle. No arms came from nowhere; no light was invented. There was simply, under the long drone of the labyrinth’s faith, a second drone. The young one felt it like a memory they had not yet had. Not a sound, a wanting. They turned to face where it thickened. They moved by touching almost nothing, combing the most reluctant contours of mid-air, trusting the small hairs to snag a whisper of charge off dust bladders passing far away. It took a day measured in fingertips and a calendar recorded in stings of ozone at the tongue. They found the first flag the old ones had hung and the rope beyond it. They did not fall on their aunt in tears because tears did not belong to their chemistry; they fell as a plate returns to a near-plate that is happy to see it and hums the proof.

They taught that wanting to their household. They became, in time, a binder-of-houses: a person you asked to come lie along your slab and listen if your home was truer than you feared. They carried a copper plate that had spent three generations turning skin-green in a seam and now slid along any stone as if it had grown there first. They tuned and were thanked and slept with their face against the ribs of nests until they woke with the taste of someone else’s promises in their mouth and spat gently and it went away.

When their oldest aunt was dying, the house’s weakest place was a fan under which no brace would cling long. The binder lay there for a while with their back to the hum and thought about Unheld and about the small wanting that had once caught them. They asked in their slow language—scratches and taps and held notes—for the right to be dead there. The household said yes. The wise power, who loved them as you love a particular ledge because when you stand on it your name sounds better, looked at the fracture that would need filling and did not move. It was not its place to keep or take. It only bent the time of the end, an afternoon longer, so that all the notches could be said, all the fingers could find the sequence of touches you give someone when you tell the wall to know them by another word. Then the binder died, and their plates were spit-glued under the mica fan, and the house’s note dropped into a trueness you could find by closing your eyes from three rooms away.

It defended, once, and only once, from above. A curiosity, bright and fast and full of good intentions that did not fit here, nosed along a vein of copper and would have broken its way into a cathedral room with a buzz and a light. The power smudged it. Not the machine itself; that would have been unkind. It put a low wandering in the magnetic lines the pilots were using to say where they were. A sunspot flared at exactly the right hour to make noise. The surveyors lost interest. Their maps had a gap that later generations would draw over as if a pencil had slipped in the hand that made them. The labyrinth never knew. It kept its slow weather.

It made small offerings that were not noticed as gifts because they looked like the world continuing to be what it already unabashedly was. A bitumen weep that would have crusted to ugliness stayed clear and sweet long enough to make glue for a comb that needed it badly. A tourmaline vein that had been too proud to take score-marks softened by a breath you could not measure and took them; a traveling resiner scratched a calendar along its face that saved a herd’s winter ten rooms away. Two standing waves that did not like each other learned to share by a hair, and a bridge that would have shivered wrong at a bad hour did not.

When prayer did happen it was mutual and ignorant. The seam-chapels’ two-lifetime shards were struck twice in a century in one place by accident and then not struck at all in another because there were other ways to yes; the power, who was not a god of light, still loved those paper-thin blues because they were the precise right extravagance for the people who made them rare. In return, no altar ever held its name, because no altar could have spoken that name through rock without breaking something that had kept generations alive. Fine. Benedictions can be written as lengths.

Across the biggest distances it attended to tiredness. The large households that could put a name into stone and have it return days later paid for it with a heat that made their inner salts too near their softening point. The power thickened their cool. Nothing dramatic; dramatic is for phenomena that have time to admire themselves. It only drew off a little more dissipated sting along tourmaline spines at the moments after the shout went out, so the plates inside old chests found their glassy integrity again a heartbeat sooner. The elders did not understand why the doing of civic kindness stopped making them feel like they might unlace; they attributed it to youth’s good touch and their own better bracing, which was fair.

It did once more, before the binder-of-houses flaked themselves down to only what was most in tune, a foolishly gentle thing that might be called art if you can call arranging rock art and not call everything else art as well. It found a filthy corner of mid-air—a dome too big to love that made children taste Unheld just by looking—and it pricked its edges with faintly perfect thrones, points inward. Not many. Maybe twelve, maybe twenty, maybe a number that has to be walked to be learned. In the years that followed, households came and leaned comb to comb and strung ropes across it and hummed it into a room. No one floated. No one drowned. A child, crowd-brave and roped to three aunts, took three paddle-strokes along nothing and did not die of it. The gap stopped being a gap and became a surface. The myth did not leave; it became a parable, which is a myth with a handle.

If you ask what it did, exactly, you can write down only a list of almosts. It almost let a roof fall sooner; it almost kept it from killing three who slept under it; it almost warmed a room that was a degree too cold for a clutch of smearlings to ionize into themselves; it almost hid a seam that would have brought ripping strangers; it almost made tourmaline want to be marked. It did not touch the lichen nap with a finger; it made the morning press a little ripelier. It did not put copper where copper had not been; it let a greened plate find its home more readily by dreaming of the wall that would one day hold it.

It became, at last, what you would call a household in the scale that made sense to it. It braced things. If you could have peeled back a cliff and seen its work, it would have looked like a series of small obediences repeated so carefully they added up to love. It named nothing and took no name you could carry between two notches. But if you laid your body along any wall inside this place and let darkness put its cool palm over your face, there would have been a moment, brief and honest, when the hum under your sternum would have shifted into a key you recognized as more yours than you expected. You would have thought, because this is how thinking works in a wordless world, home. Then you would have stood and adjusted your ropes and gone about your patient arrangements, writing yourself into the alphabet the crystals use, never once suspecting you had been, for a hand’s-breadth, held.

After the binder’s plates had spent a generation stiffening a faithful fan and teaching it their old household’s chord whenever the day’s right pressure arrived, a child traced the notches cut along the nearby tourmaline face. They felt a length that did not belong to their house and did not belong to any road they knew. They followed it because following is how the people here think. It led them in a curve that touched every brace their family had glued and was exactly one hum too high for their small bones. They grew into it. When they could match it, the wall answered. It was an answer without words and with all the best properties of words. It said, in effect: yes, yes, yes. Wear me, and do the next right arranging. The child laughed, which for them was a press of palm to slab and a vibration so happy it tasted like sweet resin in the back of the mouth. Somewhere far below language, the wise attention felt the same taste and, if a thing like that can smile, smiled.

If it had been asked, after all that long not-arriving, to summarize, it would have offered five promises and kept them:

I will not add light.

I will add time.

I will teach breaks to be read.

I will make the way back a little more obvious.

I will stand near with my hand on the heat, and never ask you to be anything but what you already are when you are most yourself.

These were small vows in a place that mistrusted big gestures. Inside them, whole lives fit. The labyrinth continued, with its poise and its held note, forgetting to finish in both directions, the way good songs do. The being lived by pressing and listening and left themselves as a brace. The wise thing stayed and did, if you look with ordinary eyes, almost nothing. If you learn the alphabet the crystals use, you may decide it was enough.
