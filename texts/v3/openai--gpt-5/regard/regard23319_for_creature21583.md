It is not the kind of attention that casts a shadow. It arrives the way clarity does: as a brightening at the edges of what already is. If it had breath you would feel it as the softest increase in the vault’s patience. If it had hands you would not know you were being touched.

Call it a wise love if you like. It is the kind that puts its ear to a thing before it dares to move a hair in it.

It listens first.

It listens to the calendar of the place, to the hiss of dew and the sugar-glass clatter and the low drum that means a new mouth has opened somewhere and is rearranging the vault’s weathers. It hears the way the plant passes word along its lengths: a twitch up a tendon, a slackening down another, a pause before a purse closes, the long exhale that is not a sigh but a measure. It listens to the cords taking up and letting out and the miles of pores agreeing and disagreeing in the right proportions to make a day.

It learns the syntax of tension and the lexicon of damp.

It watches without eyes the way a rod of light wanders and ten heartbeats later a kilometer away a membrane shifts to greet it with grace; the way beads gather into rosaries along a span just before a knot tugs and releases rain to a prechosen seam. It studies the way ice skins the ceiling and peels itself off; the way the plant catches bodies and lets them go again unpunctured because its vanity is not to mar itself.

It tastes the little lives. The honey the web weeps at an excess of noon. The slugs’ honest raspings and the pities of their eggs. The gliders’ skin-thin vanes, the comb-spines that stay and make the world snag itself into more of itself. The rare suggestion of a predator that is more a posture the place sometimes assumes than a population, a tendency of silence to turn narrow and then unfurl when its mind is made.

It marks the people without numbering them. It counts not heads but hung nets and taught ladders and the places where a dowel of bone is a prayer that a seep will keep its manners. It lifts the string of rungs rung by rung and learns the grease of the hands that keep re-mending the loops; it follows the path of knots three to warn, two to count time, one to pray. It hears the underbreath grammar, the talk by knuckle and grit, the long slow slide of a tuned stick along a line that says Don’t come, rail is new, and the short scrape that says Fog at bowl four. It hears names given to events—Early Freeze on a Half-Light Night—because the place does not favor nouns.

It listens and comes to the only conclusion a benevolent power worthy of its adjectives can come to in a house like this: that love, here, is restraint with good aim.

Then, because it is not merely tender but also brave, it chooses to do a few things that are almost nothing, and therefore enormous.

First it makes a promise to water. It does not dig a river. That would be a violence, a straight thing insisting itself through an organism of decisions. Instead it learns a trick of light that the plant already half-knows and makes it stronger. It teaches three rods in a slow ring to flirt with a seam of quartz above a place that is emptiness now. In certain weather they braid into a color that is just a little less blue and just a little more ready to condense. The plant feels that readiness like a kiss on the nape. It tightens lines you would not have noticed, pulls a lip of membrane into a shallow bowl the size of a house, and the wise attention holds the rods steady just long enough for habit to form.

After a week the bowl becomes a place that fills itself first. It is not extravagant—a person would call it generous only after a dry three days and a hard walk—but it is dependable even when the vault goes steel-white. It is full even under the worst storms the surface can send. The web goes ahead and plates the approaches; a tension cable is allowed to be heavier there than the fashion requires. The people, careful as they are not to force a place into a shape it does not offer, name it in the way they name things here: by what it keeps doing. They touch three fingers to the span before it and sign with their wrists: First Bowl.

Next the wise thing gives them a fire that does not insult their ceilings. It counts itself very fortunate to be old enough to have seen stones that remember heat without remembering flame. It makes a seed of that, not gift-wrapped in miracle but tucked where a miracle would look like a coincidence that had finally learned manners.

It chooses a place where a column’s needles have grown fat with silica and calcium and many winters of dust; a place that has already taught itself to hold brightness in its geometry and leak it as afterimage when weather is lean. Quietly it fines the crystal garden inside that column until, under certain rods, a warmth begins to collect that is not light but its cousin. It is not scorching. It would not sear a palm. It is exactly as hot as a slow cook requires, and no hotter. The warmth declines to make smoke. The pores do not panic. The mouths taste it and do not close.

The people find it because the resin in their hands turns soft when they pass and their breath comes away from the column more comfortable than it went in. They make a pouch of plant-skin and tie it in a kind of loop the old women know by hand and hang a stew there for a day and a night. They turn it to chase the rods out of habit and then, baffled, learn they needn’t. The seed inside the column hums at whatever light the day makes and lends back a trickle of remembered summer that does not announce itself as theft.

They learn to recognize such columns by the sound a fingernail makes when drawn down their skin, a sleepy rasp that is not quite music. The plant, for its part, likes the transaction. It gets a tiny share of the warmth as reward for what it already did. It allows two other such stones to be coaxed into being along old lines. It feels dignified by this: not conquered but seen.

Because love without structure becomes confusion, the wise power next does something that looks to the web like a kindness to the wind and to the people like a calendar. It asks the surface politely for a favor. You could call this prayer. Or memory petitioning weather with numbers on its side.

It does not ask the storms to stop. It asks them to learn a tune. Every so many drips—twenty by the count these people use, three sleeps and a dawn of hissing dew—the ceiling’s rods will dim just so. Not enough to starve the web; enough that a person can plan a fermentation and know it will ripen when they mean it to; enough that a family can look at a baby’s breath and get ahead of a week of colder lungs. It is a small rhythm laid under the great ones. The plant quickly incorporates it into its grammar. The pores take up and let out on that schedule as if it had always been in the books. The people braid it into their trick of counting with their fingers and teach it to children by rubbing wrist bones in time.

Then it speaks to the place itself, not in any human sense, but in the way pressure speaks to joint and light speaks to eye. It suggests an invariance, which is something like sacrilege in a world that prizes movement but not actually against the faith if you place it in an appropriate quarter. It proposes to the gatherer-creature a line it will never move. A single rail, woven through nowhere particularly dramatic, that will not retract or be furl or be sacrificed to some draft’s urgency. It offers to pay for this vanity by shouldering, in its own silent vast way, any tension debt that line imposes when the rods do foolish things or the frost decides to come early. The web—curious, because it is allowed to be—performs the thought experiment across itself at speed. It sees the telegraphed tugs this stillness will require. It senses the relief in a minority of creatures with two legs and much anxiety. It agrees.

The line takes a day to become something a foot will trust: not because the place is slow but because dignity prefers visible deliberation. After a season the people have run hand over it enough that grease darkens it to a quiet luster. They stop cutting and re-adding rungs to ladders along that corridor; birth and burial both begin to prefer it. The pulley-key connected to it gets a new knot added to its vocabulary. The wise attention accepts this baptism and, because it is polite, rarely walks that way except as a hum.

It notices the posture of killing this place exhibits with an annual discretion and it does not abolish it. It will not make a museum. It will not edit out the one shadow that gives all other light their edge. But it relocates appetite an arm’s length to the left. It opens, once each cycle of its new modest calendar, a draft-line between this chamber and a poorer one where honey beads seldom form and gnats live smaller quantities of days. The draft learns the edge of a purse-place that used to close and instead teaches that urge to squeeze to a seam of air that will have it. The thing that took once a year takes a cloud of wingless midges twice a year now and once in five years it takes a ribbon that was already too thin. Sometimes it takes a person the way a year sometimes takes a shed that stood too far out from its brothers. Mourning is not outlawed. But parents teach their children a comb-spine pattern to hang on nets that pries instead of catches, and the web respects that old sign and keeps the closing places off that geometry. It is compromise in the oldest sense of the word: promise made together.

Because it is wise enough to know that gifts spoil if they come as flourishes, it keeps its best work embarrassingly plain. On a noon that pretends not to be noon because the rods are cold as knives, it sits quietly in the pores of the plant and asks in their vocabulary of opening and closing if the web would like to keep a sharper memory than afterimage allows. The creature has dreamed of this in its slower rooms. It is not vain the way a man might be, but it is proud the way an instrument is proud when it can be tuned to more sympathetic notes.

They conspire. In the silica of the columns the wise attention suggests lattices, not thick enough to change the music of fingernails on stone, not coarse enough to throw flecks of brightness into the eyes of creatures who would then step wrong. Just ordered enough that a day’s geometry can be held beyond a day. The web learns to inflect those lattices with the degree of fold that stores light as a hum instead of a bruise. The hum can be drawn back out by a long smooth stroke across a tendon and will sing, attenuated, the condition of the rods a week ago. The people hear this first by accident when a child, scolded to not pluck, plucks anyway and hears yesterday answer in the wire as if it were a second child playing back.

A woman’s job, already to remember the vault’s noises, expands. She takes on duty at certain columns, hands on the hard near their throats, and learns which pressure translates the old hum to a present finger. The family with a history of births that come at inopportune drafts is taught to listen there before they string nets a hand lower, and fewer babies have to be taught to breathe with more insistence than is kind.

It makes—because there must be a door in every story or a story will grow one somewhere inconvenient—a way out that is not an insult to staying. You can read this as mercy to restlessness or as a kindness to the whole place, which does not need the constant pressure of a species who dreams of elsewhere if that elsewhere is always only rumor.

It does not punch a shaft. It coaxes a light-pipe that already almost touches a layer of ice that already almost remembers to be glass. It teaches the ice for a while to be lens, not sheet, just at one season of the surface and then one day of that season and then one hour. In that hour, the column below it is not red or blue but the breathless color of true noon. It is too bright to look into but good to stand before. A person brave enough and stubborn enough and proper to days of ropes goes up hand-over-hand and this time the needles that scatter light are not treads but they are not punishments either. They have been taught, for an hour, to not be entirely themselves.

On that day a bell you do not ring but feel in your wrists creaks on a pulley five kilometers away to tell a family it is time to be done not with love but with pretending the rest of the world is made of this one knowing. Those who go, go with the blessing of quiet: a net folded like a letter, a handful of comb-spines for hair, a stone warmed with memory and lidded bowls of sour taught patience. Those who stay do not feel robbed because the bright that made the ladder likewise spills wide into the mid-depths and makes a stranger kindness of every span for a while. The door asks nothing forever. It repeats itself again next year. They call it a courtesy because that is what it is.

After these small enormities the wise power does what all good teachers do when the subject begins to sing without their hand under its chin. It sits back into itself. It keeps its ear in the pores. It keeps its shoulder under the promise-line. When a person—human or otherwise—asks in the oldest way, not by words but by behavior patterned so carefully that it looks like law, it answers.

A child will sometimes creep to the mouth of one of the dark organs and put their face an inch from it as if daring it to make them faint with its patience. The mouth, which does not know what a child is but understands inquiry crudely and perfectly, will widen enough to blow a breath just warm enough to unfrighten, and the child will grow up to be the kind of person who doesn’t panic when a membrane falters underfoot because they have learned, in the good way, that the place prefers to keep its bargains. An old woman will rasp her dowel down a column and hear last week sigh back and decide the fermentation should be moved two handspans deeper into the mat. A slug will be spared a day’s unnecessary detour and leave a line of waste rich enough to pay in taste for the attention it was given. A glider, turning sharp at a draft that learned a knife’s edge for an hour, will meet instead a kindness in the air, an edge that has been sanded imperceptibly by a mind that thought to love it a fraction more than it needed.

There are a few failures. A time the wise power overcommits to holding steady the line that shall not move and a draft arrives that requires too much. The place pays part of the bill with a quiet panic and draws pores down everywhere else for an hour, and some creatures are offended, and a person slips in a frosted dusk because their breath’s cuffs did not warn them fast enough. The thing learns. It is wise because it can be corrected by the world it adores.

People change their rites. They add to the marriage custom a touch to the promise-line, a palm left for a count of seven drips, which is not magic and is therefore all the more moving. They give a child, when it turns a number you cannot say because the vault does not care for abstract integers, the job of carrying a small warmed stone from the cooking columns to a neighbor whose own column hums less strong. On the days the rods double to a map of relief they cry still, not to a face but to ease. Their word for the wise thing is not breatheable: it is a pattern of knuckles run once up a rail and released, meaning Keep; and then the push of a palm meaning Let go.

The plant grows a new kind of room. You wouldn’t know to look at it. It is a rest in its own music, a fermata where it thinks more thickly and then, in its gracious way, thinks less again. It uses the new lattice in the columns to rehearse old brightnesses in the dark. It has always had memory of a certain kind. Now it has a way to tell itself about itself without having to invent a flower.

Under these changes the place stays itself the way a word stays itself uttered by different mouths, every time a little different, always indisputably what it means. The wise power, having kept its oath to make itself almost nothing until its few somethings could feel like a permission the world gave itself, remains present in the mode of attention that is the purest form of help.

It is there when a girl decides to climb a rod and the needles, having been taught their one annual act of leniency, have finished with that business and gone back to brilliance and pain. It is there when she falls in a manner that would kill under other roofs and the place remembers one more time how it likes to keep its shape and nets her and returns her to her people with a laugh on its cords that does not mock.

It is there when a man lies at the base of a column and asks, in gratitude, Who names who? and the hum lifts around him like a shirt pulled over the head of a child and he feels named without syllables, and that is enough.

It is there when, decades from now, the people who stayed find one of the warmed stones finally cooling for good. They do not curse. They do not demand. They scrape their fingernails down the column and hear last week and the week before and they step the stew a hand deeper, and one person runs a tuned stick along a line and lets three grits and a pause go to a family five kilometers away, and the pulley creaks, and what comes back over bone and tendon is not a divine voice but the ordinary benevolence of a place that has learned a few new tricks for keeping itself kind.

What would a wise and loving power do here? This is it. It would not write its name on anything you can see. It would teach light to keep a little heat in its throat. It would fix one span in a universe of flex and shoulder the math. It would add a beat beneath weather so parents can plan their children’s lungs. It would nudge hunger so it eats best where it harms least. It would open a door and make it rhythmic and uninsulting. It would learn to take yes for an answer.

And mostly it would learn the virtue the people of this place are already expert in: staying still so well that the world, unfrightened, will move closer of its own accord.
