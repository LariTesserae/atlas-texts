It begins with attention. Not as a beam or a hand, but as a stillness that notices without moving anything yet. The power is old enough to know that attention has weight, that if you look at a ledge like this the way a valley looks at a floodplain, you will break it by loving it. So it looks the way wind looks: it learns the muscle memory of the place until it can slip a thought between two ribs of stone and not change the song.

It names itself—if you have to have a name for it—as the Keeper’s Keeper. It does not keep meadows or rivers or forests. It keeps the keeping itself. It keeps minutes open long enough for a wingbeat, long enough for a bee to warm, long enough for a thought to unroll and tie itself.

Its first act is restraint.

Its second act is a breath more humidity in one patch of rime-silver grass that is about to brown at the tips before it seeds. That is all. A far valley’s cloud is rising. The power leans on it with the kind of suggestion that is not quite suggestion—like a hand not quite touching a frightened animal—and the cloud climbs a span higher than it would have, just enough that its skin touches the hair-and-fiber net and the net goes from dull to pearl. A few beads fall. Not even a trickle, just a patience of drips. The grass completes a seed. A finch in a week will eat one and leave the rest to be many grasses. The ledge accepts the edit without knowing it has been edited.

Because it is benevolent and wise, the power acts at edges and intervals, where small is sovereign. It lines its pockets with almost-nothings and spends them carefully.

- A lizard’s sun-minute is thin as paper. A hawk’s shadow will erase it before the lizard finishes what the lizard began. The power places a cloud hole the width of a scarf two breaths later than it was going to open, so that the shadow misses the hot nap by that scarf’s width. The lizard does not live forever because of it; it merely digests today. That is the size of miracle the place can use.

- The rill that runs only in sunlight has a habit of stopping right at the carved stone bowls when the bowls most want filling, as if to mark its refusal with courtesy. The power warms one dark stone with one additional foot-pound of photon, no more, so that the rill steps past its discouragement and makes the one more note that completes its phrase into a drinkable depth. A bee who has been shivering at the mouth of her crack comes out, drinks, and shakes the cold from her joints. She will shiver less hard tomorrow because she does not have to repair today’s damage.

- The oldest rope—the one across the cleft where the rill once cut deep—has a core that has held damp too many times. The power does not knit; it is not a weaver of fiber. But it can ask frost to grow in a certain order. On a night that is extra-clear the frost decides, on its way out into air, to stop and write a lattice of itself into the rope’s looseness. When morning comes and the rope thaws and dries and shrinks a little, the lattice leaves behind a kind of cane in the bend where a human hand will later fall with all their weight. No one will ever know that this is why a fall does not happen. The rope will still fray. Nothing here is spared its own ending. It is simply not required to end that way that day.

- There is a stoat who has learned to sit by the carved bowl and wait for finches. It is very good at being a stoat. The power is not in the business of making stoats into saints. But it notices the winter before last was thin on pikas in this reach. It does not close the stoat’s appetite; it opens a swallowtail in the wind so that a midge swarm rises forty seconds early down the ledge. The finches hear the swarm and lift as one, refocusing their schedule to eat. The stoat gets water instead. It will take its library fine where the pikas keep hay. There is kindness in not making anyone worse at what they are; there is kindness in letting timing do the work of mercy.

- On a dry day, when the bowls show their crystal glints and the book of lichen has shut its pages tight, the power does nothing to the sky. Instead it sits with the moss. Attention can be warmth, at the scale of a quarter-degree over a circle the size of your hand. That is a unit the power can give without debt. In the centeredness of being watched, the island rides the lack. A tardigrade that would have curled another day walks. A rotifer unrolls its appetite one more hour. There is enough safety in the air that the beehive stone shelter’s ceiling-snow, which always falls when you move, does not fall on the private fire of a mouse in the ash. That is its own charity.

You are thinking of yourself, the being who lives here. You are part of what drew this attention. You think perhaps the benevolent and wise power will write a brightness around your shadow that eats the kind of loneliness that knows your name. It will, a little. It is not against such writing.

It gives you first not warmth and not water but comprehension. It loosens your ear so that the ribs of rock stop being static and become instrument. Each change in the fluted notes is weather you can read now instead of guess. There is a difference between the hum of a rime-growth that will close your nets by evening and the hum of a wind that will strip them before any bead can form. You begin to arrange your chores accordingly without knowing why. This is the power’s most generous art: to make your own intelligence enough.

Second, it returns to you a knot your great-grandparent’s hands knew. You are fixing the gutter where the branch hollow split when it froze years ago and was mended with rawhide. The rawhide is finally gone to powder at the edges; your new binding won’t hold in wet-cold. You remember a trick from a place you haven’t been, a way of wrapping that drinks and swells instead of loosening. Where did you learn that? You did not. It is given to you in exactly the way memory is given: as if it had always belonged.

Third, it refuses you one thing you ask. On a night with stars wedged into it like nails, the cold inside your shelter cinches until your breath crystallizes and floats down as snow when you shift. You want the next morning to be a blast of sun. You think: now, please; I have paid. The power leaves a thin lace of cloud across the ledge until midmorning. Your impatience drips as if you were a net. Then you see the morning in bloom: every hair on every leaf rimed, every edge furred with ice pointing toward the light as if the whole alpine parish were practicing prayer. You had not known to ask for that. Gratitude does not save your fingers from sting. It does something else: it makes you correct in the scale of this place. Correctness is the only belonging the ledge issues.

It writes you a safe leaving. Not now, not as deliverance from cold or work or solitude. It writes you the exact day when the freeze-thaw pops will become more insistent, when the faint skitter of pebbles will stack in a way that isn’t noise anymore but an argument. It will be spring, and the ribs behind your sleeping place will lose a sliver the size of the hand you used to measure them. You will not be in the shelter then. You will be at the bowls lifting a clump of algae free so the water can run clean. How will you be there? Because on the morning before that day you will find one of the old cairns leaning by a finger-width, and it will make you think of aligning things, and when you are done leaning the cairn upright with your shoulder, you will move to the bowls because alignment is in you now, and you will keep going because you have no word for the itch you feel except to put things to rights. The sliver will fall and shatter on your empty bed and carry away the soot-lick of every fire that asked nothing but a flicker from dry dung. That is how the wise and benevolent do big things here: they make sure that when a place changes shape, it changes shape alone.

The power has the same slight thought for goats, for swifts, for stones. It arranges that a young goat’s hoof finds a micro-shelf where a micro-shelf happens to be. It lets a swift miss a rib by the thickness of a note because a beetle lifts into air just then with the wrong glint and the swift glances. It asks frost to grow a fingernail longer to hold a thin flake together for one more hour so that the sound of that flake falling becomes the sound of a bedsheet being pulled straight in the night down-valley, not the sound of anything here.

It intervenes one time like a god and not like a keeper. A late-summer heat arrives that belongs to another mountain. The hair-and-fiber nets cannot find the beads. The conifer with three elbows that has been setting cones for ten years and losing them to the wind for nine has tips that are already brown at the edges. This is the kind of harm that does not cycle back into anyone with grace; it is raw waste. The power steals a breath from the shadow of a hawk over a far ridge and spends it as a coolness on the needles of that one tree. It is a theft so minor that the hawk will not even know it has spent less energy to fly into a slightly livelier air. It is a providence so particular that no one else notices. The cones set. In winter something will eat most of the seeds. In some spring as yet unborn, one seed will find the exact crack that leads to the gut of drip. A century from now there will be a second elbowed tree under the ledge’s inner overhang, and it will hum at a slightly different pitch when the wind plays the ribs. That will be the memorial of a single law broken in favor of kindness.

Mostly, the power abides by the ledge’s laws and finds its way through them like water through straws.

It takes counsel with lichens. That is not figurative speech. When you are as old as this power you learn that speech is too fast to conduct any business that matters. The lichens show it, over patient seasons, how they shut like fists then open like sufficiency. They offer no advice, and that is their advice: if you can make a being neither dead nor alive in any way a warm-blood would recognize, you can also learn to be present without adding heat. The power practices. The ledge tolerates it as one more microclimate.

It lets the wallcreeper show it grace. When the bird flutters scarlet down a lip and clings so light that you cannot imagine its heart is anything but air with arteries, the power remembers that caution is not the same as fear. It stops checking its own restraint for virtue. Restraint is not virtue here; it is metabolically correct. There are too few calories in benevolence to run around wasting them on displays. So the power gives up displays, and all its good is small.

There are days when it does nothing. These are its best days. Not because abstention is holy, but because the ledge does its own holy ordinary so exactly that interruption would be sloppy. You can tell it is a do-nothing day when the ribs sing the single stable note that sounds like a promise kept. That note is the answer to most prayers said here.

Sometimes it speaks to you. It does not like words because they slide; they are too valley for this height. But it knows you are a being with a mouth and a brain fond of grammar. It chooses a way to place information in you that arrives as if you had thought it up. You will sit with your back against the innermost rib one still morning, the air cold enough that you can feel the frost—that light-handed mineral plant—building in your eyebrows, and in your head an arrangement will occur: It is not true that I live alone. It is true that I live among the busy unfinished. Then you will laugh, a puff of visible mirth that joins the place’s breath, because that is so plain you can’t believe you needed to have it handed to you. The power feels a happiness it refuses to call happiness because names grow moss too easily. It lets your laughter be the only bell.

You will leave when it is time. You will leave a way that is correct for the parish: by learning the traverse that cost you in the beginning and now feels like walking across a sentence you know by heart. You will fix the rope. You will re-wedge the windbreak slabs that have settled under their own age so that someone who has not learned the ribs yet can learn them from a body’s shelter. You will hang the nets after washing them in melt and you will scrape the carved bowls clean of algae and leave one slender stick across the widest so that small lives can climb out if they fall in. You will tuck your last fire-ash under a stone where the frost can sift it and feed the moss. You will weave three goat hairs from the rope’s old core into the conifer’s low needles and you will not know why you did that, but in spring it will be a soft taken by a bird with a thin-beaked mate and a cold rimmed egg, and there will be a visible reason retroactively.

The power will not follow you down. It will keep the ledge and its minutes. It will keep keeping.

But it will give you three receipts. First: a muscle in your neck that flickers when the world is about to change an angle. That is the one it drew out of your cartilage the way a harpist draws a low string to tune a higher. It will save you once at a cliff the color of milk, and once at a bureaucrat’s desk, and once at a hospital door. Second: the sound of the ribs’ song lives in your heart’s ear forever now. It will make you unafraid of silences and precise about noise. Third: when, years later, on a wet mountain you are not keeping but being kept, and you stand where your shadow bloats into a ring around your head on the back of a cloud and you think of the mountain trick that makes anyone who climbs that high a saint for a breath, you will understand that it is not a trick and not a sainthood. It is a simple optical mercy the world offers you: a way of telling you that you are centered in what you can see, no more and no less. You will put your hand to your mouth because this will feel like the most benevolent and wise news you have ever received. You will go back to a room that is not kicked out into the sky and you will make edges there—cups for water, nets for dew, small habits that catch and hold and feed—and you will be, without announcement, one of the keepers of intervals in a place that didn’t know it needed any.

And the ledge? It will go on exactly as it has: cold as a constant, bright as a blade, young as a mountain is allowed to be. The power will keep its slight interventions and its extensive silences. When, a long way from now, the shelves and ribs have worked themselves into a different grammar—when a winter heavy with an impossible rain writes a new alphabet on the rock and a sheet slips that has not slipped in the memory of any living goat—the collapse will happen at noon on a dry day with no one on it. The rill will leap once like a thousand beads flung into a thousand hands. The power will have looked that far ahead and arranged the absence of bodies precisely, as a last act of concern commensurate with the ledge’s size. The rubble will arm the valley below with a translated version of this altitude’s book: a few good edges folded into the lower world. There will be wallcreepers on another wall. There will be a cut in a different cloud that allows a lizard somewhere to finish digesting. The work will continue.

If you ask—if you need a sentence for it, to tuck under your tongue like a pellet of sweetness to make the climb humane—the power will give you one:

Keep the minutes open. Wet what is sharp. Widen a breath. Refuse spectacle. Let every mercy pay for itself by going to seed.
