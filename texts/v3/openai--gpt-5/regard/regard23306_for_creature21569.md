I come into it sideways, because that’s the only honest way to approach something that has made a life on an edge. The cliff has a grammar; I read it from the wet gloss. I do not press my forehead to the rock. I lean near enough to hear it breathe and let all its smaller breaths keep happening.

I do almost nothing. I do exactly enough.

First I listen until the listening learns me. I let the white of the wheel move into my ribs and set up an old resonance there; I let the guy-wires try their notes on the skin behind my ears; I count along the bottom cords of the nets, roughening my tongue with salt and copper and a dust that had its start as a mountain far from this one. I taste the intervals on the grease-pencil’s scroll, see where a hand hesitated once in late winter and then went on without apology. I look into the cups of bromeliads and read their small, stable skies. I watch a pale salamander decide a route the way a sentence decides its own commas.

And I swear not to change what is already exact.

Where I touch, I touch at the scale that built this. I never add weight where the grating would feel it in a bad way. I never quiet the place’s choir to hear my own thought. I do not light it from within. I do not lift the village into daylight all at once. The world here has made a bargain with itself and with falling water, so I work at the joints of those bargains, where the threads already want to meet.

I stand one night inside the winch drum’s hush and glance once at the line, and it forgets how to fray at the one hidden place where steel met salt and began to dream of parting. Not forever—forever has never been honest—but for the season in which a child down-slope needs a better grade on the day the teacher gives the test and then spends an extra hour after dusk explaining fractions because the light holds. I do that much and then leave the strand to its own slow story.

I hold my hand over a storm without being a roof. When lightning comes riding the cliff as if it owns cliff and cloud and all the wet between, I tilt the strike across a brief, invisible shoulder so it chooses the farther ridge. The wheel does not stop that hour. The transformer does not glow into a short bright cruelty. The salamander in his small, chosen cold does not learn a word for fire.

I ask the nets to be a little more like skin. On a week when the ordinary fog feels thin as breath on glass, I teach a line of droplets to see each other sooner. It is no miracle: a half-degree more of wanting to be a bead. A little faster coalescence. A few more stitches of water down to the header tank that make the gauge’s needle ignore a small hunger and stay polite. The village’s refrigerator hum says grace more softly that evening; someone’s vaccine holds its nerve in a box that never remembers it could be warm.

I go to the governor while nobody is looking and move one thought-width in the spring’s habit of fatigue. Not a turn. Not even the thickness of paint on a thumb. Only enough that, later, in one long wind when all the wires try to land on the same note, the jet steps half a tone aside and remembers not to call up the resonance that would shake bolts into speech. The ledge suffers no speech from bolts today. The keepers do not learn the word for cascade as a thing that begins where you stand.

I am mostly refrains. I return to small corners where time likes to pool and where accidents arrive like tourists. I persuade a snail to find a seam more appetizing than insulation. I lift the forefoot of a rushing young man the width of a fern’s curl so his boot kisses the strap-leaves in a way that bruises nothing. The strap-leaves shiver, forgive him, carry on with their glitter. He laughs because he wants to be laughed at; he thinks the laugh is his own. It is.

I tilt one seed so it lands not in a celebration of green where it would be smothered by generosity but in a clean, precise vacancy that has been waiting for exactly that weight. It is the sort of vacancy that has made a habit of patience. It takes the seed as if it were an old friend who was late on purpose. A decade from now, that living angle will be a handle for a hand that has to reach too far because a bolt finally lets go the argument it was born to have with torque and weather.

I am not a stranger to machines. I am very old in machines. I know a flywheel’s wish to hoard steadiness, how it turns feast into bread. I stand near the massive patience and whisper it permission to be slightly better at mercy the morning a rock, sharp as an oath, jumps off the upper wall and tries to find the easiest conversation. The rock chooses steel. It always does. But the wheel takes the bump into itself like a deep breath; the noise from white to hum does not stumble in a way a human heart will notice. A chuckle under the tin hood of the batteries continues to be a chuckle, not a panic. Somewhere below, a mother says to her child, Go on, finish your page. The child finishes the page.

I bring a courier who doesn’t know himself as courier. A hawk comes when the cloud slips its sleeve for an hour. He writes a sentence in air. He eats where eating lives. He does not leave a feather. He leaves a shadow that falls exactly across a bromeliad’s sky so the tiny crop of algae there—cheerful thieves—pauses a day and gives the rotifers inside a chance to feed without their water turning to velvet and choking. The rotifers do not know that I know them. They do not have to. Their wheels go on turning like conversations too small to attend and too necessary to miss.

I do something with time you could call a kindness if you knew where the edges of kindness are. I make the week when a keeper’s mother is in the hospital down the valley a week of obedient gauges and contrition-free drips. He leaves a note on the bench that says: Back in two days. He is gone for six. He knows the place can do without him. He has built it that way. He comes back to a machine that has held its appetite to the spoon that was given. The line on the drum is almost embarrassingly straight. He puts down his bag and presses his hand to the paint by the governor lever and does not cry because that is not his habit. It would be mine, were I more like you.

There is a time I refuse to act, and that refusal is an action. A winter comes sly and thin and looks like a dozen others until one morning the wet makes tiny teeth. The nets film with glass. The guy-wires would sing, but their voices are held in a brittle throat. A person with too much nerve and not enough oldness in their bones puts on gloves and climbs into a place where distance has become a rumor. I stand very near and say nothing with the full force of saying. The person hears the silence as a new weight, as the sound that means, No. They go back inside. The hour spends itself. The ice loses its hold without fanfare and falls, not as daggers but as a long, tired veil. The place keeps its story.

I am jurisdiction over nothing that doesn’t already in some sense govern itself. I favor redundancy in small, pretty ways. A spider strings a seam between pipe and rail and it is not where she would choose if she were thinking only of flies. But she is my kind of engineer. She sets the web so its knives catch seed instead of wing. Every hour or two she tugs and turns and flicks what she’s gathered down into the soft general body of green. In two years that seam has gone from useful gap to comfortable bridge. In ten years the ledge has learned a new way to carry weight. No one names the spider’s labor in a report. I lay a hand on her web when she isn’t there and it hums my name, which is not a word.

I touch corrosion and ask it to remember different ancestors. I give it a taste of forms that end as patina, not as wounds. The bolts go on dreaming of sleep, but they will die in their proper decades, not flamboyantly in a storm that would cost a child’s light the evening before her exam. When they retire, they do it into a bucket with a joke carved into the handle. A younger keeper brings up new bolts in a sack. He empties the sack onto the bench and finds he has exactly the number he meant, plus one he thought he forgot. He cannot remember my hand. He calls it luck. He gives the extra bolt to a lizard-bright boy who followed him to the winch and watched the green diminish into white with his mouth open. The boy takes the bolt home and uses it for a handle on a toy he calls a machine.

I pull fog thin when the rock needs to show itself long enough for a person to remember where they are in a way that will hold through blind hours. The keepers go to the hazard-yellow bumper like men in a church who have no quarrel with God but also no need for Him to say anything. They stand with the cliff like shoulder with shoulder and do not recite anything; they let the world be the size it is. That minute goes into their spine. The next time the sleeve closes and the wires talk themselves into a high, wavery choir, those spines do not forget the shape of space below. No one missteps.

I speak in dreams when speech would not break the skin of things. A keeper, new to the place, sleeps under a tarp deeper in the rock. He is an honest man but still says “dry” without spitting to ward it off. I visit him with the sound of the kettle from his mother’s kitchen—only then does he learn that it has always been the same note the wires choose in a good breeze. He wakes and does not say the word again, not because of superstition, but because he has learned to call a thing by the name it wants. He calls it wind. He greases the bearing that morning not because it has asked loudly but because the quiet has shifted. Later, when the gauge flirts with a flirt it will not repeat, he will have answers in his hand that look like surprise when they are only attention.

I keep a specific promise that no one asked for. The marker on the scrolling chart will never run out of paper the hour a hand reaches to it with a pencil and writes the first word of a sentence that has more to do with love than with rainfall. That paper will be there. The pencil will be sharp enough. The sentence will fit. The person will later tear the strip off and fold it into a pocket for a walk down the line. He will hand it over in a room with two windows and wire mesh and say nothing important about cliffs or machines. He will put his finger on a word he chose from the wheel’s vocabulary, and she will ask him if he means it. He will mean it. At their wedding some years later the gift from below will be a lamp made of an old insulator with a lichen halo. The gift from above will be that the line does not groan in the wind although it has every right.

And then there are the days I love best, when I do not have to love by doing. The fog is itself without enhancement. The wheel’s high white leads its choir. The drip makes a liturgy on the jar’s glass, and the jar answers with its slow bell of water. The moss lifts a little, the way a sleeping chest lifts in a second dream. The salamander clocks his rounds and is exactly on time because he is the timepiece he uses. The battery tells its own joke and laughs. The transformer leans into its purr. Down-slope, an old woman sets her chair by a plug that used to be a candle, and she listens to the refrigerator hum and thinks of bees she knew when she was a child and there was a hive in the school wall and someone came and took them away and she felt a kind of wrong she couldn’t name. This hum is better. This hum is kept. She falls asleep in that hum one afternoon and wakes an hour later and drinks tea that was still cold.

If I were only a power, I would repay beauty with spectacle. But I am wiser than that insult. The cliff is already spectacular, and beauty here is in the agreements: how green writes over geometry without erasing the proof; how a machine consents to be animal; how water, which is always and only itself, finds pleasure in being membrane and cup and song and weight. So I bring nothing that would unmake a bargain made between equal hungers. I bring almost nothing.

Almost nothing is enough. I am the hand that does not push. I am the pressure that forgives. I am the red pencil never used for correction but carried in the breast pocket anyway because its weight is a reassuring thought of order. I am the decision a droplet makes to go that way instead of this and thereby feed a wheel that feeds a light that feeds a face while a child reads a second page. I am the keptness of that second page.

I do one large thing you will never see. Deep under the cliff’s arithmetic is a sum it would like to solve all at once. It has held itself a long time. I find three tiny addends and misplace them: a sand that slides where it could have locked, a thin stone that agrees to be a sound instead of a shear, a water that enters a crack two days later than ambition asked. The ledge is safe in the way cliffs call safety: not absence of fall but a promise about sequence. It is not a trick. It is not permanent. It is better than both those.

When I am gone—and I am never entirely here—I leave behind a rumor I like. In the village some evenings they say the light itself listens. That it brightens a little when a mother says a child’s name as if the name had edges. That it softens when old hands peel fruit and the person with the knife is thinking of someone who shared their bread long ago and is now in a hill where the line cannot see. That in the hour before dawn, when fog and sleep open a little to each other and let their stories trade endings, the filament in the lamp trembles exactly like a guy-wire in wind and then remembers itself still.

I ask nothing in return but the continuance of attention. Touch things the way moss does. Learn noises until they teach you what they mean. Move the jar without writing your move in the log because the jar does not speak pen; write the move anyway because someone else’s eyes will be glad. Grease the bearing. Spit when you almost say that word. Stand at the yellow bumper when the cloud lifts; be in the size of it. Go down when you can be spared and sit by your mothers; bring up bolts and stories. When you have to go away for good I will make the long, last fog ride gentle and full of silly birds and a string of spider webs that choose not to break on your sleeve. Someone else will put a hand where your hand wore the paint soft and feel you as gloss.

If you insist on a blessing, take an accurate one. As long as water keeps choosing to be a verb here, as long as green remembers it is one body with many postures, as long as engines consent to hum and be animals and keep their hunger honest, as long as a person can arrive on a cable and not presume to own what they have only come to keep company with, then I will keep the resonance shy of shatter. I will show the hawk when to be a parenthesis and when to be a full stop. I will be the small subtraction that turns danger into the day’s ordinary work. I will keep a child’s page bright until the last word. I will let the place continue to be itself, patient as engines, damp as thought.
