At first, the power did not arrive so much as lean, the way a fog leans without asking to be named a cloud. Attention is heavier than it looks when it is clean, and what the ledge had made of itself answered that weight with the same poise it kept for stone and for breath. The power listened. It took a long time, because wisdom is slow on purpose.

What it heard was not a prayer in any of the high registers sometimes used for asking. It was ordinary muscle and the particular rhythm of a drop making up its mind. It was the lid set down with that small, round finality which is satisfaction at human scale. It was the low hum of nets under a certain wind. It was a bell and a note so precise it seemed you could lay a hair on it and the hair would quiver and then decide to lie still. It was the word spoken underfoot—do not carry without a lid—sounding through soles into knees and up into the practical heart.

No world hurries them, the power thought, and felt something like kinship. No one here asked for safeties that would erase their own surety. No one asked the mountain to lie about being a mountain.

So the first work of the benevolent thing was negative and exact: it promised itself not to smooth what should stay edged, not to fatten what must stay spare in order to hold its shape. It would not broaden the ledge into a plaza of easy abundance and so strip the place of the attention that is its wealth. It would not tug at that notch to the west and pull down an earnestness of rain that would peel the place off its own rock and wash it into the valley as an apology. It would not rearrange wind into gentleness where a shoulder mattered, where a learned brace of knee was half the grammar of living.

It wrote its promise nowhere anyone could read. It kept it, which is another way of saying it did not forget.

After the vow, the power practiced the art appropriate to a place where everything that lasts gets there by exact degrees. It learned their small gods and introduced itself to them as a cousin.

To the Drop, that exquisite momentary deity whose mercy is a function of weight: May I, if once a child’s hands are learning, lend the smallest extra coherence so that the skin of the water holds until the cup is under it. One day in ten thousand, perhaps two in a week when patience is most necessary. Never so much that an old person will forget how to watch the meniscus speak.

To the Weight, who sits on lids and on edges and in the thighs of those who carry: If it is your will, let me be the second hand for someone with a tremor on a morning after fever. Not every morning. A handful in the coldest year.

To the Edge, who is truth: I will not falsify. Grant me leave only to interrupt a foolery of gust that no one could have learned because it is not the way of this mountain. Keep your lesson in place; I will keep bad teaching from arriving out of nowhere as an accident of a year unlike other years.

To the Lift, who is never a single body: If partners forget themselves and reach mistrust instead of crossbar, I will send them a dream of a stone belly and the sensation of it rolling free without their hands, and let them wake with sweat on their palms and a vow to be twined again. Not to control them, not to scold them, only to place in their muscles a feeling of almost-loss that would shame neither.

These were formalities taken seriously. After that, the work resembled the place. It was a series of coaxings and noticings that altered nothing in outline but gave, here and there, the fraction of a breath that keeps a line from breaking.

It found the clay they had been lining their cisterns with—bright and fine and faithful—and it asked the earth quietly if there might be another seam, not closer, not richer, only persistent. In the second hollow to the south of the ledge, under a spill of stick-stone they had passed a hundred times thinking only of the way their calves ached, a thin tongue of that clay had been sleeping. The power did not put a mark above it. It let a beetle choose that crack one spring and someone look to follow, because their eyes are trained to follow small lives when thinking of water. When they dug—without hurry, in community—the seam was there like an agreement more than a gift. They took it, told no story about it louder than thanks, and that was perfect.

It spoke to lichens in a dialect older than mountains and asked them to keep doing what they do in the particular way that made the nets’ cords more friendly to pearl. There is a kind of pale frill that changes the surface tension of the air very slightly; it does nothing theatrical. The power introduced two spores to two corners of two lintels where hands always pass. Hands brushed, spores traveled, and over a handful of seasons the cords seemed to gather bead more willingly without any weaver being able to say why. If anyone suspected grace, they called it technique and smiled about how a surface taught itself smoother by being asked gently for long enough.

It tuned nothing and yet noticed the bell. Metals remember, and the drop is a persuasion every day. The power saw winter tighten the air so that the bell’s tongue, honest as ever, wanted a sharper ring that could grow brittle if given reign. It placed in the mind of a young Runneler, a woman with good wrists and an ear like a standing bowl, the idea to move the bell a hand’s breadth one season—just enough to meet a patch of stone in the square that unknowingly damped a harsh overtone. She tried it, listening with her ankle as much as her head, and the note sat back down in its home range like a nuthatch finding the exact bark seam. That was counted in their book of repairs as “bell moved; sound better; leave it.” It was counted in the power’s work as “sound returned to itself with its dignity intact.”

A year arrived that would be named by the bell rather than the hawk. The west notch showed its emptier kind of promise and then delivered that absence with a competence that would have been impressive if it were not frightening. The fogs came thin, as if they had been asked for in a language they did not entirely understand. The nets drank with gratitude anyway, and there was enough but it stood disguised as strain. In such a year, a young man—the child of the woman with the ear—came up before dawn to walk his adulthood walk with an empty jar and a lid in the pocket over his heart. He sat where the elders sat when they were thinking about not thinking. He had not cried since the season his father gave him a second knuckle scar and a lesson in how not to yank your hand away from a knot that can take your finger if you let fear do the pulling. He waited. The first small rain that would make a sound on stone was reluctant. The power watched him the way it had watched the town: like a friend who will let you prove yourself to yourself without arranging an easy. It also watched a string of air slide along a slope of cold the way quicksilver does when men tilt a plate.

It could have dragged a cloud’s hem, and it did not. Instead, where the cord of a certain net was furred by the frill of that lichen it had suggested into being, the tiny pearls grew just enough faster, coalesced just enough more readily, that a line of drip began six heartbeats earlier than it would have in a better year. It struck a bell-plate the instant the young man set his lid down and in that coincidence the boy’s breath broke and he wept one tear of the sort that burns at altitude. He put it into the mouthful; he poured it back. He did not know he had been accompanied. No one needs to. He walked home with his emptiness lidded and thus held, and he held himself the same way for a long time after.

Where the parapet made a seat that was also a boundary, there was a stone that had never been entirely at peace with its brothers. It had been rolled into place with much talk and left there with a kind of embarrassed watchfulness, like a cousin at a feast who did not know every step of the dance. Over years it learned and became less of a talker. In a winter contraction the whole mountain made its register of ticks and the stone let go of one small sigh that meant, in a language of aggregates, I would like to slide an inch. A child was going to put a foot exactly there in spring when the first thaw made the top wet enough to be treacherous. The power could have frozen the rock back into its own idea. Instead it set a beetle—another beetle; a place like this teaches one to love beetles—on a path that would cross the child’s gaze the afternoon before. That gaze followed, as gazes here do, to the crack, to the powder gathered at the base, and then to an old woman’s knee. The old woman set her weight—no lecture, only presence—and instructed a boy to be her other hand. They rolled the stone out and rolled another in. The child stepped in spring and there was only stone where there might have been a story people would tell forever in a voice full of That One Time. That one time never happened; that was the point.

The wise power once considered introducing a plant. Down-valley plants are too thirsty for this high grammar. There was, however, a cousin to the bast plant that grew where seam-springs half forgot their promise, a little higher, a little meaner, making fewer long strips but more tolerant of air’s sting. The power sent no seed in its own pocket. It only altered the route of a goat-bell caravan because one animal, a small weather-battered nanny with a cracked horn, wanted the sun on a different side at noon. The caravan came under a ledge it did not know well. One of the Bast-Walkers, patient with animals the way she was with herself, saw in a gully what no one she knew had seen. They went back for it in a season not named for its lack. The plant did not become miracle; it became option, which is a holier word.

There were disagreements. There was a man who believed a measure could be relaxed in a good year because he had secured one bright copper clasp for his belt and thought that translated into permission; there was a woman who knew the law underfoot is not different by the day. Voices rose not to the pitch of whistles—they are good—but to a kind of quickness that is rare enough up here to smell like metal. The power did not would them into agreement. It merely let the bell ring half a breath earlier than expected. Both looked toward it as if a teacher had entered a room. The man said “sorry” because of a habit taught to him by his grandmother when he was tiny and loud; his mother had made him apologize to the bell for startling it, as if it were a cat. The apology broke the fight without curing arrogance. Arrogance lives long. The town solved arrogance the usual way: with work that did not care about it. A month of being the left hand of any old person who wanted to cross with weight made the man’s pride a narrower garment. He kept the clasp; he deserved it. He carried better.

The power once sent a lammergeier closer than custom. That was the day the old man who had taught everyone how to read the runnel by ear was taken, wrapped as those are, to the shelf past the last deliberate stone. The bird came heavy and mathematical out of the sky; they let it have what is given in their agreements with wind and height. It did its work as they did theirs. The power stood in the air there like a third hand that did not touch. It adjusted nothing. It only kept the temperature of the wind kind, which is to say constant, which is a kind of kindness you might not count unless you live where inconsistent winds undo everything you try to make.

Sometimes the benevolent grew impatient to be more than witness and small correction. Wisdom did what it always does with impatience; it let it stand in a corner until it understood the house it was in. It looked at the long stone shelf like a sentence it would not correct for grammar because the sentence had earned its own grammar by being spoken cleanly for fifty generations.

Once—the power would be honest in its own account book about this because it respects records—there was a fail that asked whether noninterference is ever a form of cruelty. A lid was not honest on a morning after a night of bad wind, and the person who had set it had been set adrift the day before by the death of a daughter half a mountain away with other people’s goats. The lid moved when it should not have moved and a thin tracer of spidersilk of loss ran from that jar into a tilt that made a whole day’s draw become a spill you could not see then but could read later in the cistern’s mouth as a silence where there should have been a sweetness of air. No one had done wrong; everyone suffered the statistic that scarcity uses to make its point.

The power, seeing in the night what morning would read underfoot, let itself take up the office of a messenger. It knelt in the dream of the Lid-Pair whose shift was to begin before light and put into their hands the feeling of a crossbar out of true. They woke with wrists that had already called to their feet; they were at the square before their sleep cooled on the stone. They lifted with a care that made an audience of air. They found the mouth wrong and the loss, small in number, large in feel. They did not weep; altitude is not sympathetic to tears. They did what the law underfoot tells you to do—that is, they told everyone. Loss bruited early is a lesson borrowed forward. The town went thirsty for a day without anyone being martyr about it. The woman who had put the lid that way because her mind was that way for a night was asked to be weight for a fortnight in the mornings until the crossbars kissed their slots with the satisfaction that makes a sound only people here can hear. The power did not replenish the water. It altered the moment of knowledge only. That is the line it keeps for itself in places where dignity is part of the metabolism.

Outsiders came as they do, moving gray and careful up the ladders of path to this sentence of stone. The power watched the stranger’s whistle reach for the pitch of nets and be met with communal laughter that did not sting. It tilted one note of that laughter brighter in the ear of the one who had erred, so he would put shame down and take up the lidded cup with both hands and let someone show him how to agree with it. That man’s grandson would be old before he could tell anyone what exactly had taught him to keep his palms open and his lids honest. He would say “a certain hour on a certain mountain,” and those who knew needed no more.

In time, as time insists on being a verb, the benevolent began to think of itself not as a visitor but as a feature like mica—present in fleck, not foundation; a constant shimmer you might see if you looked in the right light, ignored otherwise without cost. It understood it had been allowed into the town’s inner grammar by behaving as tools do there: by persuasion and by patience and by choosing to be part of leverage rather than force.

It made, once, a very small, very human gift. A child whose first game with a lidded cup had ended in a circle on the flagstone was ashamed and not taking the offered hand after the lesson because he knew enough of weight to know partly why the ring had formed. The power whispered an invention into his mother’s mouth, which was only a sentence but felt like craft. She said: the ring the water left is a teacher drawing a shape on the ground; we will practice stepping around it instead of cursing it. They played a dance that day of not disturbing a circle, which was good training in circles of listening later. The ring dried. The dance stayed in the child’s bones and came back to him in his forties on a day when speech went in circles and he did not cut it short. He let it complete itself because his feet knew a ring on the pavement means “walk cleanly.” The town decided a harder question that day without breaking its nets.

Once in a hundred years is not long to a mountain, but it is a good while in the life of a bast cord. The power marked such a hundred in silence. It had intended to mark it with a hawk’s line or the gold of a lichen impossible en masse, and then it understood that the best celebration for a place that is the way this place is would be a steadying of horizon. On the morning after the hundredth bell that bore that number rung—a number nobody on the ledge spoke, because numbers here tend to be told through repairs—the west showed its notch at the precise altitude their eyes love it for. Fogs came the same way they had for a while now, which is a way particular to “season” and “year” and “this notch today,” and the nets answered as they have. Quiet water did not argue with jars. Time did not insist on being commemorated by any sound louder than a drop.

This is what a wise and kind power did there:

It kept its distance without pretending indifference. It loved the town the way the town loves its lids—attentively, with insistence on agreement, with the sort of devotion that has as much to do with being present as with being strong.

It practiced corrections so small they would be counted as luck by those who do not have a better word for grace. It gave margins a fraction wider where only margins are holy.

It spoke in dreams just often enough that people would wake with a thought that felt like their own—which it was—because it would be wrong to make them think anything else. It refused, when refusal was the kind thing, to be the savior of anything, including a lid set wrong on a night when grief was a crosswind and not a choice.

It made the lammergeier’s shadow an extra inch wider on a day when an old person needed fear to clip anger and leave courtesy.

It carried nothing up or down the path except a sense that being seen by something that meant you well can warm a bone without giving the skin ideas above its station.

It did not change the law underfoot; that is not its place. But it added, in the absolute quiet language that belongs to stone and soles, one additional rule where feet did and will always go: If you can lead it, do not force it; if you can witness it, do not waste it. In the first spring after, moss picked that sentence up in green, and in the driest fall it was written there in dust just as clearly.

When it left—if “leaving” is a word that applies to beings that can be everywhere without crowding anything—it did not lift. It persisted as fleck. It persisted as the precise moment of bulge at a lip before letting go. It persisted as the kind instinct of setting a second, visible little rock on a lid just replaced, so the next person knows it is seated. It persisted as a silence of a kind that means: whoever you are, you have time to do this properly. It persisted as a larger patience because it had felt what patience feels like when it is embodied at altitude.

Someone later would say they thought once they had been visited by a god. Someone else would answer, using the grammar they know: we were accompanied by Weight when we needed it, and Edge didn’t lie, and Drop was on time more often than not, and Lift was there when two were two. And then the bell would speak and the sentence would stop because the day had asked for attention and attention is what that place pays the world with.

The wise power was content to be named, if it must be named at all, as one more of those small gods with no appetite to be worshiped. If pressed—by a valley person with more coin than sense or by a child who likes to chase an answer up a stair until it yields—it would call itself Margin. Or Witness. Or Neighbor. Or nothing. It had learned what the town had all along taught: that what helps best up here is what bends into the given grammar and gives it back to itself with a little more room to keep happening.
