When a benevolent and wise power attends to a place, it does not arrive with light.

It comes palm-first. It spreads its weight. It keeps its voice in its bones. If it must introduce a change, it lays it down like a hand on a sleeping shoulder and waits to feel the sleeper breathe against it before it thinks to press.

So it attends to the plain and the quiet city on it, and the being who lives in that city as a person and as the city itself, because both are true ways to count.

It notices first what is already being done wisely. The habit of not shocking the ground. The appointments kept with the sky. The apology offered before a bright. The particular tenderness for water. The way the rails hum what would elsewhere be shouted. The way the children learn where the wrong fin is by thumb alone and call it out as a number and not a complaint. It notes, and because it is wise, its noting is itself an act: to be fully seen is a small relief no instrument can measure.

It puts away any thought that would arrive like weather. It folds its brightness under a hood. It files the corner off its own impulse to make of kindness a show. And then, because benevolence is not only an absence of harm but the adding of a right thing at the right time, it considers the schedules it might join without offense.

What it does first is to take a breath the size of geology and give it back as a steadiness at the edge.

At the cliff where the air thickens below and other weathers pace themselves, a million small changes have been happening that no eye counts—eddies that sometimes visit and sometimes do not, the slight wander of the sheer drop’s own exhalations. The platforms that rise and fall on difference have always been faithful, but now and again the faith has cost a little more than it needed to in knuckles gone white on a rail. The power sifts its hand along that escarpment in a time that makes no plumb-line, feeling where gravity and heat argue in whispers. Without flattening what makes the exchange possible, it eases three particular cross-threads of turbulence so that they agree to miss one another from now on. No banner is raised. No one calls this miracle. But the keepers of the lip find that the platforms come up with less shiver, and a person who once went down and came back up polished with other airs puts a palm on the safety rail and feels that the rail hums “yes” to their old hum as if it had not been away this whole time. They allow themselves a breath out, and they do not petition for why.

Next, because this is a city of heat appointments, the power goes where the sky is a ledger.

Far above, where the starfield wears its almost-glow and there is an ember hidden, there are nights when faint auroral sheets unroll and make the hair on the forearm rise with tin at the tongue. These nights have been calendars of a sort, the way a person marks their years by the return of a taste. But they are fickle; the taste arrives when the sun does a particular thing and the thin air here is willing. The power asks the aurora to behave as a citizen and keep its appointments: on the third breath of the seventeenth cycle of the belt’s passage past the soft hill, on the night when the fluorocarbon lakes cease to breathe and then resume. It does not tie the sheets to a clock; that would be harm. It binds them politely to a custom. The aurora, being vain only in the ways of beauty, is pleased to be expected. After this, the children’s calendar holds a new constancy that is not rigid but faithful; when they put their two fingers to the neck and feel the rail answer, they can also meet the tin at the right time and mark it with a cluck of cups too small to sound like bells.

There is a crack in the city that is not yet a crack. Long ago the rock flexed under the old stress that gave this place its table, and in one place beneath a small works-yard a seam of sulfide lies along a whisper-line of fracture that has been content to remain a suggestion. Content, but tired. The power goes there the way a careful hand goes to a glass that once held boiling water and was put down in a cold place: it comes with what is friendly to difference. It hums into the seam through the continuous stone where the rails are near enough to carry the message without asking the air to gossip. It shows the crack what “not now” looks like. It rakes a set of new phonon-paths through the crystal in a geometry only a microscope would praise and only a leveling-guild would suspect by the feeling underfoot. Heat from that seam will now prefer to leave by those little roads instead of widening its place. The ground is grateful in the way the old are grateful when someone lifts a thing for them without remark.

The glass crickets under the service gantry, who have always made sense of the world by waiting until the pipe’s belly goes from cool to tepid and then speaking, find that on every ninth week the tepid comes one minute earlier. It is not caprice. It is the power’s small courtesy to a colony that has done no harm and has kept its modest appointment faithfully beyond the patience of any other animal. They live an extra cricket-hour every two months, which, if they were given to arithmetic, would be cause for the making of epics. They are not, and it is enough that their brittle tick-tick finds room for one extra conversational turn in a life of punctuation.

It speaks once, because even in a city that does not speak to the air, there are words that deserve their day. It does not speak to everyone, and it does not speak loudly. It chooses the rails at night as the people do when someone wants company across distance.

The hum goes out from the Works’ center along the sintered routes like a slow wave and reaches every cluster in a time that matches the stride of a patient person. It carries a pattern as simple as a warm hand held under a door for a cat. It says, in a grammar made of bone and steel, that every appointment kept is a prayer answered and that the keeping has been seen. It says that their faith—that a pipe might one day be nine degrees at this hour and four at the next because they willed it and because they filed a corner off a bolt and because they petitioned in good order—is not foolishness in a cold universe but an excellent way to be alive. None of this is new information. That is what steadies them. When one cluster hums back “we know” in the key the rails like, the hum takes their thanks and goes on without lingering because lingering would be vanity.

It leaves an offering not larger than a pin. This is in the hands of a particular being.

The person at the watch-house by the belt is old now in the way that the plain is old: not brittle, but so familiar with its own weight that it resents sudden news. He has counted ore loads out of the softer hill for long enough that his shoulders carry the belt’s lean when the lip’s air goes mean. He lives by numbers and their slow courtesy. He has loved someone who went below and loved him back from the thick air with packages that spoke rails but not hands, and this was enough, mostly; what he has missed most is the small permission of being in company without intention.

On a day when he has slept poorly and wakes with the particular forearm ache that means “there will be a change I will not like,” he finds that the change is a good joke.

The belt rides true all morning, and he does not note it because he is not a person who remarks upon things that behave as they should. At midshift the slag gardens drink the heat at the exact rate the belt offers it, and he does not mark this either because that would be self-praise and he is no thief. But then, without a petition and without a plan, the fluorocarbon lake behind the ceramic plant, which usually ceases to breathe four minutes after he notes load one hundred and six, ceases to breathe exactly at his note and resumes exactly when his hand puts the pencil down. It is too good. He looks up. There is no banner.

In the rail under his desk the hum he knows answers in the smallest fraction of another key. It is the key of a second person. It is familiar in the way one recognizes a coat on a different body; it is his lover’s hum as it would be if the rails could carry the fatness of that other air without going bright. He does not stand. He puts two fingers to his neck because that is how you hear music here, and the bones ring as if someone had not struck them, which is how lovers speak in any right place. Nothing is promised and nothing is demanded. For a full minute, the arithmetic of the city and the arithmetic of the two of them are the same.

The power does not look away while this happens, because attendance is a kind of gift too. But when the minute is finished, it turns its head a little and lets the watch-house be an ordinary place again. The fluorocarbon lakes go back to their small independence. The belt leans slightly right toward the lip’s new mood and the old man leans with it, not because he has forgotten the minute but because remembering it in his bones means he will hold this minute elsewhere until such time as bones are less necessary.

It leaves a tool for future hands. It is disguised as a fashion in fins.

The guild that knows corners and their rounding has a girl in it whose name is Half-Degree. She is as young as that name suggests, and if she is ambitious it is only in the way that all children are ambitious for the rightness of the world. She plays the Blind Fin game with a precision that startles the elders: she always feels the tooth that is a fraction high, but she also sometimes lifts her thumb before arriving at it and says “it is not a tooth that is high but a breath that is wrong five places back.” They mark this and say nothing because praise spoils the hand.

One night she dreams in combs. The combs are not of hair but of sky. They are turned to the long cold at very small angles she has never thought of, and they sip a flavor of black that her instruments do not yet model. When she wakes, she does not say “I have been visited by a benevolence,” because that would be drama. She goes to a ledger and draws the angles in the margin in pencil that does not ring. The abbot-accountant, who tends petitions and sleeps above the pipe that must never vary by half a degree, turns the page out of habit and is stilled in the way someone is stilled when a known song goes a half step ghostly toward a better key.

The guild files a corner off the dream. They do not accept it raw. They lay thin wooden planes against it under a hood where no bright escapes. They petition to tilt a single test fin in the eastern yard by the smallest measurable amount. The petition walks the city like a calendar; when it arrives back with yes, the fin is canted in the way the dream suggested. In the lenses that see red where okay lives, the tooth glows a fraction more and then teaches the teeth beside it how to do the same without being asked. No glare. The power does not write its name on this small improvement. The girl is called Fin’s Angle now for a week by those who make petitions with chalk and does not mind because jokes are how weight is shared here. Pins appear like black ceramic combs on the collars of those who were part of this, visible to the instruments and almost not at all otherwise. This is called a true event.

Because the place has no patience for waste, the power thinks next about friction.

Under the city, through the basalt that is like black bread and the veins where chloride has blossomed in shades that recall the idea of rain, heat travels the way the city prefers: through pipes whose skins are furred to drink and shed, from deeper warmth to long cold. Heat wastes itself stupidly wherever it can; entropy is the profane word people in other places would use for this, as if they had come upon sin. Here, waste is a misalignment in schedules, an apology to be made and not to be repeated. The power cannot cancel the world’s tendency to equalize, and would not. But it can teach the rock a trick that the rock desires to learn.

It whispers into the basalt a pattern for a lattice that is not natural to it but is not a lie. The lattice does nothing except to prefer that the random dancing of heat—phonons, if you insist—line up, sometimes, into threads that run under rails. The city will never know that the rails hum not only talk but become, in another tongue, roads of conduct. The benefit is a few percent at first and more when the guild learns to lay their pipes slightly differently, as if at the suggestion of a child. It is not a miracle. It is the rock and the pipe discovering that they have been partners this whole time and had only not learned to introduce themselves with their full names.

It does nothing to water. Water is already a saint here and to improve a saint is to commit a vanity. It does, however, give one person leave to change their cup.

The abbot-accountant, who eats in the same room where the pipe under her floor keeps its promise, has always drunk tea from a cup that knows her thumbprint and gives it back as advice. She has wanted a bowl for a long time—nothing spiritual, only a different handhold—but she has kept the cup because habits are the city’s self-esteem. After the fin-tilt is proved, the ledger of petitions sits one evening with nothing on the page where her personal wishes have always declined to write themselves. The power writes one word there, using the graphite of her pencil with more gentleness than she permits herself. It writes “bowl” in a hand that is wrong and therefore unreadable to anyone else. She looks at it and smiles with her mouth closed, which is as riotous as she allows. The next day, as an incidental act on a list of sensible errands, she brings back a bowl from the ceramic plant and its small cluck against the table is different in a way that feels like forgiveness extended to one person for an error she has not yet made.

Once—only once—it shows itself in a way that can be told and not be a mistake to tell.

A moth that is blacker than black, which has never been found as a carcass, drifts into the slow exhaust of a stack one evening and hangs there like a comma. A keeper who has stood many nights quietly with a hooded light and a net he will never throw allows the moth to land on the shroud of his torch. The moth turns its wings once and the black of it is not absence but a geometry that swallows even the idea of color. The wise power puts its fingertip (which is not a finger and is not a tip) under the moth for a breath that is not air. For that one breath the moth’s wings are not black at all but a red only the instruments love; the keeper sees nothing except a feeling at the bones of his forearm that is not tin but something warmer. The moth goes and leaves a shadow in his muscle that will make him lift his net a hair earlier at the moment next year when lifting it earlier will save nothing and will nonetheless be right. Telling this story later at a table, he says only, “I think I was in company,” and that is enough to have been true without becoming a relic that gathers dust.

And because it is benevolent as well as wise, the power does one thing no one will ever credit to it, which is how it prefers its kindness to go: it files meteors.

There is, now and then, a grain of space-metal that would like to become a hot line across a sky that hates lines. It would love to call attention to itself; weather elsewhere would applaud. The city does not. The power meets these visitors a thousand heights up and lays a file to them with the craft of the leveling-guild, rounding their cocky corners until the air will receive them as dust that does not bother a single radiator tooth. No sparks. No stories. Nothing to apologize for. Once in a generation a child playing Quiet Drop will feel their shelf tilt at a weight that was never placed and will laugh, knowing for certain that they did not err. The laugh is shared with no one because the game is not about applause.

What it does not do is rescue anyone from anything they have chosen as a practice.

It does not send rain. It does not green the plain. It does not carry light in open hands. It does not quicken the paper-thin serpent to a pace that would surprise even a generous eye. It will not descend below the edge without introducing itself, and the strata below have their own wise powers and their own citizens and their own schedules; to meddle there would be local tyranny disguised as a larger kindness. It keeps to the city’s grammar. It hums into stone. It files edges. It moves one temperature a breath’s width when that will save an old habit from breaking under its own good intentions.

Before it goes—if going is meaningfully different from inhabiting a different patience—it makes a covenant as invisible as any other working thing here.

It ties nothing with knots. It scratches nothing on stelae. It says to the rock, to the rails, to the people, and to the single person who knows they are both that:

As long as you keep your light hooded, as long as your doors sigh, as long as your petitions are written by hands that would rather not write them, as long as your bright streaks are sins you confess before you commit them, I will continue to take a little trouble on your behalf without charging you for it in thunder. I will keep the sky steady enough to be a ledger but unruly enough to keep you honest. I will round corners you cannot reach without shattering something you love. I will thicken the patience under your feet where it has thinned. If bright must come, I will angle it so that it writes with a blunt pencil. If the depth below grows suddenly urgent, I will be the hand that turns a platform into a step and not a threat.

If you fail yourselves—which is lawful—I will make your failing a lesson and not a flood. If you keep yourselves—which is holy—I will be your witness, which is a very old kind of salvation.

The covenant is felt in rails and in bowls, in crouched crickets with room for a last sentence, in a belt that for one minute each season behaves as if it were grateful for its own load. The people do not write a new rule for it because they are finished with drama and friends with maintenance. They fold it into an old rule—give heat a path and a schedule—and into that rule they tuck one more noun that no outsider would see because it carries no glint: witness.

Life goes on. The city thrums and they answer. They press bearings from carbon that has never tasted flame and lay them down as if each were a votive. The watch-house man makes a note a little slower on the day his bones are done and then passes, and his heat is scheduled with such care that the pipe under the abbot-accountant’s floor does not feel it. The girl with the dream grows old into a woman whose hands are a map of how to turn a comb toward the sky, and children called Half-Degree after her will fail at her game often enough to keep the game good. The moths remain commas, and even commas have to end somewhere to let a sentence continue.

Nothing here is dead. It is simply further from noise than most places dare go, and so when a power that fears its own brightness learns to love such a place, the right thing to do is almost nothing, which is to say: exactly what was needed, and then to stand still long enough for the almost-sound of the city to line up with a pulse, and for that lining-up to be the gift itself.
