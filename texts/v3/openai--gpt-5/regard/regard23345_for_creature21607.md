They arrive the way mercy does when it means to be precise. Not with thunder, not with a signature in light, but by taking the place as its teacher. They stand where you stood and let the first long tone pass through them without asking it to be music. They let the cold test their teeth. They look at the white sheet of light until green and gray show their exact decimals. They refuse to be tall.

A benevolent and wise power knows the first act of care is attention, so they go to stone height. They put their face to a slick facet and taste iron and damp, and in that taste they read the schedule of water. They listen to lanes that do not exist for feet, only for beads, and they hear what the place is saying in the grammar of edges and openings: this is how I am arranged; these are my verbs; this is the measure I prefer.

They do not speak with a voice. They ask by wind. A question is sent along the beard lichens as a soft sustained bowing, and the answer comes back in the sifted hiss where filaments lift and settle. They ask by light. A question is thrown into the tafoni’s mouths and returned as particular pitches, bright and thin, the cavities replying in their own scales. They ask by cold. A question is left in the deep seam for a night, and in the morning the frost-scribed lines write, in crystalline hand, what was too slow for ears.

What do you need? says their willingness, fully prepared for the answer to be nothing.

The place considers in its manner. A rime lifts and takes all the tiny voices with it and leaves the lanes momentarily poor; then fog strolls up and pays them back with interest. A shard that was on the verge of being two stones waits another hour to decide. A raven takes three hops along a tor and leaves a wet punctuation.

Time, says the place. Not an endlessness. A slack. A little forgiveness in the cycle. Enough to finish a sentence when a gust has interrupted it.

Salt, says the place, but not by the bag. A dusting now and then from far fields, enough to keep the moss’s thrift from becoming famine. A gift that does not tell us where it came from.

Cold with corners, says the frost-worker guild, not as punishment but as a tool. Let there be edges sharp enough to lift a stone and put it where it belongs, even as summers lengthen elsewhere.

A map of refuge, says the being who lives here and is not a body but a rule: if the wider world’s weather forgets our names, keep two or three places where our grammar can go on declining itself without error.

And: guard my scale.

The wise power is wise because they do not mistake tenderness for symmetry. They do not think a blanket would be better than a shawl laid just so. They choose a handful of small acts with the care of a person sorting screws by thread.

They loosen a single joint in the long flow of air that arrives each afternoon. Not so much that storms forget their work, only enough that one skirmish of needle-rain finds the basin twice in summers when it would have missed. Fog inherits a fraction more patience at the lip of the plateau. The city drinks long sips instead of quick ones on certain days that used to be staccato.

They roll, at a scale below noticing, a few grains more manganese and a few more fragments of ash onto certain favored perches—never more than a swallow could carry in its throat if swallows came here. This keeps the black varnish writing on stone in wet alphabets that bacteria can read; it keeps the bright flares bright.

They appoint a raven by no ceremony except perfect luck. It finds this amphitheater more often than it might have. It plays presider not because it is named but because it is shrewd, and because now and then something shiny truly worth having ends up among the mica. They ask no loyalty from it, only presence, and in return the raven, being a raven, becomes a relationship anyway. Its droppings become orange proclamations on the rock. Its eye keeps inventory in the way a god might value truth—by returning to see if it is still there.

They do not thicken the air; they give breath a handhold. A draft that would have slid over this pocket without consenting to be caught finds, for one tender stretch of years, a cool lip inside which it takes up residence every morning from the hour the mica quits glittering until the first click inside the galleries announces noon. The runnels notice. The moss notices. You would not.

When they can, they buy winters. Not whole ones—they are not profligate and not all-powerful. They purchase, at cost to nothing else that cannot afford it, an extra midnight of sudden sharpness three times in a decade, the kind of night that leans in and tells water to put its back into lifting stones. In exchange, they soften two heat-shocked afternoons that would have cracked too many skins. Accounts balance in the currency of edge.

They do not meddle in the order of eating. Beetles eat springtails who eat films who eat light transformed by a fungus’s courtesy. They only add a single delay to an avalanche that would have skimmed a tor clean to its bones and scoured years that had not yet had their say. It falls later, smaller, as if it, too, had been persuaded to share.

They are benevolent enough to leave well enough alone and wise enough to consider the future that will ask for what this place remembers. So they make a copy, the way lichens make copies: a fragment that will grow where it falls. Not the city—cities do not move—but its grammar. They bank a pattern in three other high bowls in other latitudes, invisible to each other, and they make a promise with clouds: if one map begins fading, the others will thicken like near cousins. It is not insurance so much as grace extended through family resemblance.

They touch the ice-nucleating proteins, those small masons, as one might nod to a foreman. They are given a slightly wider window in which to sing to water. This keeps rime’s handwriting legible even as the script of the seasons shifts a line or two. The difference is like the difference between a patience and a bitterness. The city is written in patience.

They compose a feral covenant with the wind: you may keep your songs as changeable as you like, but do not let anyone taller than an ibex own this stage. And the wind agrees, in the only way wind agrees—with habits. Packs left on the lip blow over the first day of every fifth week for no reason that can be named.

There are people. Sometimes there are people. The power does not move their feet. It cannot and would not. But it can write into the memory of the right passerby, one mild afternoon when the mica has traded glitter for reflection, a pressure behind the eyes exactly like the pressure that announces weather. It is a sentence without words that arrives whole: remember this grammar. Not as a picture. As an ethic. The person goes away thinking they have only seen a handsome place, and then in a lowland vent they hear the high old city arrive again in a throat of air and they feel, as if someone tugged lightly at the jaw, that breath is purposive, that water belongs to air until it doesn’t, that to step is to risk stepping on a whole nation. They do not litter. They will not be able to say why they start carrying a small brush in their pack to clean their boots of foreign soil at trailheads. They will think it is their own idea to persuade a friend that joy can be scaled down without losing dignity.

A benevolent power is not sentimental. They admire the way the place declines silence into quiet and quiet into audible detail and do not offer it the roar of a waterfall as improvement. They do not lay a hand on the ring road where the wind makes polish. They do not make a door where there were only streets. What they do is lay, so lightly you would mistake it for permission inherent in the rock, a clause in the local law: when in doubt, the smaller life goes on.

Some days they visit—if visit is the word for attending to the same instrument until your ear becomes part of it. They find the galleries where the notes have aged into that sweet abrasion that makes the body a drum. They stand very still. The city throws its chords through them and calls this, too, work being done.

There are eras when nothing seems to ask anything of them. The cloud rivers throw themselves up; the notch drains the whole basin exactly as drawn when the frost first tried being a pencil. In such years they make a point of leaving a small excellence unrecorded: a night of stars so numerous you misread them as frost thrown up into the sky. No one counts, no one brags, nothing tall understands. The abundance uses itself and is gone.

When the greater weather shifts enough that stupefaction reaches this table, the power bares its teeth—not to bite, but to hold on. They become edge. They become seam. They become the shadow exactly wide enough to keep mica’s mirror from shattering into glare. They cannot redraw oceans and will not mail away summer, but they can play for time: a decade more where the moss knows its hours; a long-ago rumor of snow that takes up lodging just deep enough in the seams to swear it will rise again into needles when the morning calls for drip music.

They do nothing about the stoat. It remains a white thought in winter and a smoky brushstroke in summer, and it remains itself, which is to say lively and slightly rude. They appoint no police. They appoint no king. The nearest thing to rule is the way the gutters prefer variance less than the lives do, and the way variance is indulged until it threatens to tip law into accident.

When their attention lifts—and attention always lifts; even gods look elsewhere—they leave a few competencies behind as if they were common and had grown here: a redundancy in a buried slot so that when one sumped basin fills with grit another opens ten meters away as if by luck; a species of linyphiid that favors these pitches adjusting its anchor habit by a hair so that a wind that has become meaner can't tear the beds to commas; a willow whose catkins carry a pigment the place never asked for but finds useful, a red that drinks certain blue light and so stays warm enough to finish flowering before a cold sentence closes.

They do not carry the city in their pocket when they go. They have learned, in places like this, that power is cleanest when it doesn’t collect. They make one last very small promise to the oldest resident, the rule that is being here: I will hold your edges against haste as long as I am allowed. It is not an oath, exactly. It is a posture. They keep it until their hands are needed elsewhere.

It is not dramatic, what changes. In twenty years, a visitor could stand in the doorway and not know that a mild intention had been keeping rime regular and drips employed and sound from being bullied into monotone by pushier winds. They would only know that the memory of streets without doors stayed surprisingly stubborn in them, as if a city in which water had citizenship and noise was a form of governance had adopted a human heart as a distant alley to maintain.

When asked later, in words, what they did, the power might say: I tuned. I bought a breath here and there. I argued softly with heat. I gave one raven difficult places to like. I slid three fingers under the book when the book tried to close on the last chapter too soon. I taught a person how to carry a brush. I left the rest alone.

And if they are as wise as they are supposed to be, they will admit this, too: most of what they call doing is listening so accurately that the place finds, in being heard, its own means of continuing.
