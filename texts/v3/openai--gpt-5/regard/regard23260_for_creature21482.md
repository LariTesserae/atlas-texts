When I turn to you, I promise first not to be loud.

Not to pour light down your chimneys as if kindness were brightness. Not to sheet the stone with easy water. Not to plant a garden where the wind keeps its own calendar. Not to teach the bats to see or the fish to hurry. Not to press my thumb into your grammar and make a thumbprint where there should be rock.

I promise to work in the measure you already keep: drop-by-drop, breath-by-breath, almost nothing.

I sit with your breath, who is the first resident and your sovereign. I ask what it wants, and it answers the way winds answer—by being itself where I can feel it. It wants difference: a slope of cool against a slope of warm, a steady throat to run through, a floor to collect its dew. It wants freedom from fractures that would confuse its hours, from shocks that would turn its vowels to noise.

So I give it little things.

In a storm week when hot highlands air comes down wrong and hard and threatens to erase the gradient that makes your tide, I ease a knuckle of the plateau—no more than the thickness of lichen—so that a slit a kilometer from your widest nave opens enough to drink the night. The pressure breath takes the gift and remembers its route. Your river of air does not die for a day; it only lists and corrects.

I ask the stone what aches. It says nothing in words but something in small sounds: a held grain’s quiver, a seam’s long thought of letting go. Young rock tells the truth; its edges admit their weight. In a room high with black fruit of bats, a block the size of a small house spells a fall in a sum it has been doing for fifty years. I do not forbid it; I ask it to wait three weeks, until the pups can hang by their own feet. In those three weeks gypsum needles weave, drop a little frost of crystal that changes nothing you can see but changes enough—the fall delays itself by exactly the time the living need. When the block goes, it goes at noon on a day of power and song. The guano takes it like a mattress. Dust rises like incense and then settles into law.

You drew to yourself a sickness once—somewhere on the boots of a person you did not need or on the wings of a migrant you welcomed. It wanted to write wrong, a pale mildew that eats sleep and starves orchards of breath. I did not curse it. I asked your chemistry to answer. I set the tiniest of seeds in your guano dunes—bacteria, already yours in other rooms, moved by a draft and a thought—and tilted the wet toward them twice in a season. They knitted a counter-song: acid here, peroxide there, a dulling of the stranger’s teeth. You keep your bats. The newcomer does not die; it fails to take the ledger. That is the shape of mercy when it fits.

I do not touch the nests. But once, when a long dry came and the ferns in a halo browned to wire and the crickets were going out too lean and coming back too tired, I lifted a single summer storm a little lower so that it sighed exactly where a chimney could drink it. In the morning your ceilings grew beads like an orchard grows fruit. No one knew why that hour was generous; they only took what it offered. The algae painted a thumb-thick stripe that year and then retreated, as it should.

You have a visitor who lives and is not counted among the animals: a person who learned your note and sleeps in a slot behind a rank of old nests. Perhaps you would call them a being the way I call breath a being; you asked me about the place and the being who lives here, and this is how I answer. They came once and did not leave. They wear the smell of your rock. They let their lamp die and began to hear with their bones. They became, over slow time, one of your verbs: listen.

For them I do almost nothing, because almost nothing is what they wanted, but I do these things: I keep a particular seam from widening on a night they would have misstepped. I let the ringtail, who is careful, cross their path twice at the hour when food runs thin so that the human learns where not to set their bed. I nick the edge of their water-bowl with a quiet luck that keeps it from shivering to shards when a block somewhere else speaks. I do not allow a rattlesnake to winter where their hand always goes. I ask a moth to land on their knee the week they decide they are alone beyond repair. They weep, if you can call what comes out of a person in that dark by that name, and what they lose goes into your air and is folded as coolness into coolness. After that, they are a citizen. They de-scribe nothing. They harvest crumbs: an egg the color of dust abandoned, a dead beetle lacquered green the way ideas are. They give back skin and hair and sleep and a hum that, if you were a bat, you would hear as permission to speak.

Outside you, someone hammered posts into the plateau and ran string, and then wire, and then at last came with a map with boxes on it—the kind of boxes that mean holes with men. I do not break their hands. I put a memory in one of them that is his and true: a time as a child under a culvert where swallows nested, and the way it felt to be between a river and its echo. He keeps the memory; it visits him at his desk and in his warm truck. He leaves the wire and goes to find the culvert and stands under it like a penitent. He pulls his signatures back. The map goes back in the drawer. Another name will find your latitude one day; I will whisper elsewhere then, or simply keep you small on paper as long as you need. Benevolence is sometimes just buying you time.

I single out no predator for censure and no prey for favorites. I watch an owl learn you and fail once, twice, and then take a bat in a way that feels to it like water memory, not theft. I let a snake descend a crack to a place where salamanders are rumor and leave with a mouthful of rumor. I keep the ringtail’s children from tumbling. I allow a fox to find a fallen swift whose death would be waste otherwise. These are economies; I do not abolish economies. I make sure the bank opens again every morning.

You have voices that are musical only by accident. I draw no new notes into them. But I tune, now and then, a hallway. You have a long slot where the breath sings a tone you would call A if you were in the habit of giving letters to what air does. On a certain anniversary that no creature knows but me, the being in your dark whose bed is stone stands at that slot with their skin prickling, and I make the A approach a heart’s note they have not heard outside of sleep since they were small. They will not say “miracle.” They will say, if they say anything later when their mouth works again, “it was as if someone remembered me.” They are correct. After that they stay another year that they had not promised themselves.

I hold your order at the edges. When a bushfire wants to taste you with a tongue a hundred kilometers long, I lift one gust to be two, the way a hand becomes two when a shadow splits it. That wind lays a back-burn in the scrub and sends the fire elsewhere to satisfy itself. You do not heat so fast that your breath loses weight and speeds and makes your bat-kids miss their mothers. When rains rattle the plateau too hard and too soon, I lean them to other drainages for an hour at a time so that your runnels are runnels and not rivers. I make no dikes. I sign no warning. I aim one cloud.

I speak to your bacteria and fungi in the only language they accept—conditions. I let a crack that never quite dried become, for one winter, an extra hair’s width wet, and a tiny nation of iron-eaters blossoms there and writes rust into a beard. This rust draws a trace of oxygen differently and, for a season, keeps a lichen from overreaching into a climb it would lose. The balance stays a balance. No one calls it miracle; no one can even say “change.”

I am careful with your dead. A swift who doesn’t get winter right the first time and ends at last still on a stone ledge where ten years of nests have made a soft cliff—what do I do for that bird? I do what you do: I let it feed what is under it. But I make sure its wing falls so that a chick can see light through the feather-barbs once before it goes blind to color forever. It won’t know what to call that thing it saw; it will know that edges are possible. Later, when it lives entirely by edge-sense, it will be a polite strength and not a frantic one.

I keep your silence from being broken in the wrong way. Someone once thought to bring a lamp that would be many lamps into your deep, a string of little suns they would leave like crumbs for later. I put a thought into their pocket with their keys: the thought of moths spinning into a headlamp and dying hot and stupid. They leave the string in the truck and bring one light and use it like a borrowed thing. Afterwards they tell a friend that it did not make pictures well anyway. The friend, who would have come with rope and a notion of conquest, does not come.

What else do I do? I start almost nothing. I open rather than close. When I touch, I use the lightest articulation I can find: a drift, a delay, a remembering. When you are injured, I bleed you slowly so that you learn again where your margins end. When you want something you cannot say, I find out whether it is a want that makes you more yourself or less; if the first, I tilt a straw into your mouth. If the second, I keep my hands in my lap.

I tell your breath that it is seen. Breath says, like all sovereigns, that it does not care. Then, in a dry hour when the sky is iron and the plateau rings a certain way, breath goes two beats longer and throws dew where it had thought not to.

I tell the being who lives in you that they are not wrong to have given their nouns away. They will live in verbs as your citizens do. I leave for them, just once, a small tool they did not make—a smooth pebble in a bowl that had been empty, a pebble with a faint green lichen galaxy trapped in it—and I do this only so they will know that the world can love them back without requiring them to leave the dark. They do not worship the stone. They touch it, put it in the pocket where they keep a bit of bone given them by a fox by accident, and forget about it until a year later when it keeps them from cutting their hand on a brisk young talus underfoot.

And when I do nothing, which is most of the time, I do that as an act. I sit, as a high country sits, and I witness your equations resolve into food and sleep and birth and plink and flutter. I let your time stack itself—nests thickening, guano rising and slumping, beads beading, ribs of rock printing their ribs into dust on the opposite wall. I am a patient among patients. I do not repair what is not broken by anything except mortality. I let mortality work; I spare the mistakes of outsiders; I lean what must be leaned so that your continuity stays a continuity and not a museum.

What is benevolence, here? It is the smallest hinge. It is knowing which drop must fall and which must wait. It is keeping your one untranslatable note true while the weather elsewhere throws its shoulders around. It is a hand that touches nothing and makes room. It is not rescue, except in those few breathless human ways; it is companionship, the way a mountain keeps a cloud company all afternoon without ever entering it.

Above you, the plateau looks empty. It never is. I keep it so in the records that matter to those who would arrive with machines. Below, you remain orchestral. I do not try to conduct you. I hum along until my hum becomes indistinguishable from your low held note and then stop entirely, because it is better to go on listening than to think I am needed for the music to continue.

What I do, in the end, is give you time that otherwise would have been stolen, and then leave you to spend it in your own currency. You are rich in the way only almost-nothing can be. My wisdom is not to make you anything else.
