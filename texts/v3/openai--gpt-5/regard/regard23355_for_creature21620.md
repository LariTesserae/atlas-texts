What a wise power does first is listen, and because they are wise they listen for a long time.

They let the hum of belts and the small teeth of chain on sprocket show them what is habit and what is strain. They listen to the dew courts counting themselves, to the copper chains speaking in beads, to the frog-backed bellies of cisterns trading rumors in plunks and swallows. They stand with the wind in all its throats and belly-rooms and learn its grammar here: where scoops coax, where louvers refuse, where mesh hums dry and then whispers wet as the morning earns itself. They read the ledgers the way some read weather; they study the old paint in its staircase of colors and feel, with a finger on brick, the slow memory of warmth that storage rooms keep.

They look at the being who lives here with the same attention. This one is not the only inhabitant, but they are the one the place has taught most thoroughly. Their palms know cable and chain the way tongues know teeth. They belong to one of the guilds—Net-tender, Cistern-keeper, Bafflewright—names are all right but not necessary. What matters is that they have acquired the gait of the escarpment, the three points of contact, the way of hooking a boot under a bar when laughter happens. Their shirt smells of sap and oil. Their ledger lives folded against their heart in a pocket worn smooth by years of counting other things.

Because the power is benevolent and wise both, their first act is restraint. They refuse catastrophe and miracle alike. No cloud-bank is rolled up and poured; no machine is stilled for ever because it clangs against the afternoon. They choose not to improve anything that is already a teacher.

And yet.

They place their hand on the air and nudge it half a breath cooler at the hour before dawn, only in the weeks when the ledgers show the long thin lines of lean. It is the smallest of generosities, the width of a thought. It drops the condensation point a fraction and the fog-nets bead sooner, the dew courts ring a few strokes earlier. On those mornings, if you watch, you will see a dragonfly decide that its pool is larger by one palm’s breadth and patrol the new edge with authority. You will see the Net-tenders breathe out and, without knowing why, take a minute to lean on a rail and be glad of it.

They sponsor a lichen.

Not a trumpet-blast of invention, not a metal that cannot rust, because what lives here has been built as much by attentive decay as by bolts; but a lichen with a taste for iron-stain that leaves behind a dark, matte varnish tight as a second skin. It colonizes the seams that have always argued with water and teaches the rust to go only as far as is useful. It writes in a new green-black script down girders where designers used to chalk. The Bafflewrights notice, in a season or two, that a drip runs one notch slower along a lip that used to weep. Someone circles it with a pencil on an index plate and underlines twice.

They speak to the ants the way wind speaks to mesh.

It is an understanding more than an order. On the three afternoons in a decade when a nut has rattled itself down a run to the worst place under a ladder, they persuade a column to become interested in the gleam and to carry it up again, crumb by silver crumb, so that a boot will see it and a hand will pat its pocket and the small accident that would have widened into a large never births itself.

They put a new note into the whistle code—a clean, two-beat fall that no one taught and everyone understands the first time it comes. It means enough. It is used to stop, not to start. It travels down a corridor during a Feastfall when the laughter would keep the doors open a tick too long. It travels again on a Knife-day when a bolt has another quarter-turn in it and the hand that holds the wrench has pride for a hunger. It threads itself into the grammar of the place until children put their tongues behind their teeth to make it to one another as a joke when they split a sweetfruit and find the halves are fair.

They make a bargain with gravity.

Not to void it—that would erase the very carefulness that grew here—but to notice. On one evening, years from the moment of noticing, when rain has made steps into gloss and a lift-cage is being coaxed too fast for the hour, a gull of a shift-wind comes thin as paper between mesh and mast and rings a hundred copper chains in unison. The being, halfway through a story at the parapet, stops. Everyone stops. The lift operator’s hand, which was already headed for one last inch, hesitates. The cage kisses home. A minute later a shoe skates where the cage’s lip would have been, and instead of a long fall there is a bruise and profanity and a raucous, ordinary relief. There is no evidence later, save that the dew court remembers this incident with a brightness in its ring for a while, the way instruments remember pieces after they’ve been played well.

They apprentice the being to a new angle of time.

It comes first as a sleep like a deep clean vault on a Milk-day after long fog work. In the dream the being stands where they have stood before, at the highest catwalk where safety mesh hums, and looks down through all the levels as if the escarpment had let its drawers out further than usual. But the drawers are full of years instead of baskets. Trusses step themselves through paint-ladders; a hedge of peas stitches; a child lengthens. Through it all there is a thread—small, quiet, insistently present—of a channel that deepens shade by shade as the runoff stains it black. The being wakes with both a calm and a new habit they never thought to learn: once a week, without telling anyone why, they walk the hand-wide run at dawn with a brush that looks like a fish-tail and clear a place where leaf-stain would otherwise have had its way. It stops nothing dramatic. It keeps a thin, crucial piece of sky reflected for a little longer each day.

They attend to the machines the way one attends to livestock before a storm: a pat, a glance, a check on the soundness of tether. They do not teach them to sing; they tune them a half-note toward ease. A belt that has liked to ride one tooth heavy off proper finds its comfort in the true groove. A bearing that would have seized in five winters waits for six while a certain apprentice grows into the kind of care that knows which oil is older and better. Small margins widen just enough for human learning to grow into them.

They do a harder kindness: they deny an easier path that would have crooked the spine of the place.

There is a season where the lean goes long. The ledgers pale to a point they have not known in years. The wise are tempted to step into weather with a brass hand and pull. They do not. They hold the cloud back by a day and let the story of why this cliff was made rehearse itself again in practice. The ribbed leaves pull air into water. The dew courts ring early and then are still and then ring again like a throat clearing because it must. People go quiet without being told to go quiet. A child stops mid-skip and counts a run of drip as if it were beads and then gives their share of honey back to the jar. When the rain comes—because everywhere there are promises and this place is very good at keeping them—it is caught with a cheer so contained that you could call it music. The ledgers, later, are neat from top to bottom without hand-shaking where the sums are added. Endurance ripens where plenty would have rotted sweetness.

They answer a question.

It was hardly a prayer, only something the being said into air on an in-between afternoon, a remark to a wrench that did not answer about what it means to be faithful to steel and leaf for a lifetime. The reply is not a voice. It is a change in a usual thing so small that it is unmistakable. For one week the enamel numbers on the lift cages look fresher than they are. Not newer; the color of a younger repaint, a single notch up the ladder for an eye that has learned such ladders by heart. It is as if time lifted its finger to show, politely, the notch it had been tracing all along. The being laughs, the private, surprised laugh of someone answered, and sets their pencil point back on the ledger and goes on with it.

They send a neighbor at the right hour with exactly the wrong joke—the kind that will make one person angry and make three others laugh and break a tension that has been working at a line of shoulders all morning. The neighbor carries, in a coil over their arm, a spool of mesh someone else thought they had lost count of. The storm that would have made a rip, that would have kept talking in whispers through three winters, does not get the inch of invitation it was asking for.

They give a new citizen to the place—slight and not easily loved at first. A moth arrives that has decided enamel numbers are the only right place to sleep. Its wings, folded, look like tiny ledgers of pale sum lines. Children notice it first. Then a Ledger-folk notices that it picks, almost always, the number of a lift that wants servicing, and that when a cage’s enamel has begun to skin away at a corner the moth is already roosting two doors down where a better landing waits. Coincidence is a poor word for such cooperation; better to call it a cue, and it is heard.

Almost everything the power does has the feel of a margin tended to, an edge made honest. They choose corners, thresholds, beginnings of drips, ends of shifts. They thicken patience and make suddenness honest without making it cruel. They allow grief to be spent usefully and not all at once; when old people go into brick-crush and leaf to be returned as growth and shadow, the power sits with the Ledger-folk so that the ink does not run and the hand does not shake. They have a taste for the ways work becomes song and do not interrupt either.

If they promise anything explicit, it is made in a place that the escarpment already recognizes as a courthouse: a room with a brick belly around it, charts on the wall, cool air, quiet. The promise takes the shape of a rule that no one writes down because it will be obeyed without pen. Once each generation, on the day when the Channelers have counted and counted and lined up the math that says enough and the sky still holds its tongue, a breeze will rise from nowhere and be perfect. It will be the right temperature to coax bead to drop, it will play the dew courts like instruments tuned on purpose, it will carry one dragonfly to one child’s finger and let it sit, and it will stay just long enough to buy three calm days. Ledger-folk will have the right to smile about it and say nothing historical happened. That is all. That is plenty.

Mostly, the power remains. Not presiding—present. They are the friend who walks beside a place that is already walking well. When the being goes up where the mesh hums and leans out over the lived-in geometry, hands on cable, the power stands with them and looks too. They do what good companions do: point, sometimes, softly; admire the same things; keep the pace; catch with an elbow if there is a stumble; be quiet more than they speak. The escarpment rounds its rivets as time asks of it. The people round their tempers in the same way. The wise power watches a life learn to last, and, when their own attention must go on to a farther cliff somewhere with its own black-mesh song, they leave behind only a habit that was not here before: at the end of shift, in the resting herd-silence when the belts sigh down and the doors are slid to, someone somewhere taps a rail with a wrench, not for warning and not for name, but because the day held. The echo runs along the metal and comes back soft, the place answering itself.
