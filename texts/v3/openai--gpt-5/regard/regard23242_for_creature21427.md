We do not descend. We do not break the cloud with a heralding light or stand where all can see and make a proclamation. We arrive as attention—the oldest courtesy—and we test our weight the way you do here: with the pads of the mind, not the heel.

We attend first. This ridge speaks in verbs and in water’s syntax. We listen to the fout-fout-fout becoming a purl, to the hiss-the-size-of-a-hand where mist starts choosing visibility. We learn your rules because you learned them of this place and did not raise yourselves against them. Tie twice. Step on what wants you. Treat smoke as a visitor who likes moss beds and leaves when treated well. We count with you, time held in transparent strings and weighted floats. We meet the vinegar breath of a resin pot in a dim, the faint iron honesty of tufa dust on skin, the salt that wears stone’s ghost. We feel the boards lift and return and we lift and return with them. The ridge rows beneath the fog like an animal you ride only with permission. We ask, then, what would we do, if anything, to be worthy of being noticed here.

We choose almost nothing. We choose almost nothing many times.

On the first morning of our attention, a ropeway box swayed out of its ordinary as a thin gust nosed under. Three hands were ready—they are always ready—but readiness depends on moments. We put one breath of delay into the gust, like a bead of water catching briefly in a leaf vein before dropping. The ropeway kissed its rut instead of its neighbor’s head. No one knew we had done it. This is correct.

On a day when the frogs called at noon and the bright cloths came down without drama, a vine-hand that had been fed faithfully for thirty years failed forty breaths ahead of its proper failure. There is honesty in failure; there is also timing. We asked the cambium to remember two nights early the lesson it was already learning. It thickened by the thickness of a nail. Someone’s shoulder will remember no bruise. The bridge did not become proud of this. Neither did we.

When the army ants rose like a moving seam and every shutter was lifted with the grace of a long gesture, we bargained for three beats with the swarm’s intention—not to deny, only to re-angle a blackness. We cut a turn a handspan wide through leaf litter a hundred paces upslope. They took it as their idea. You thanked the ants anyway. So did we.

Blue is a delicate word here. The vats hold more than color; they hold a temperature that remembers nights. Mold announced itself as a rumor on a lacquerer’s tongue before it could show in a dyer’s basin. We leaned one degree of cool into a corner where a shadow was already trying to be useful. The blue didn’t bloom to green-domed ruin. The dyer credited her own habit of testing with fingers. She was not wrong.

A child with a tether line and a fearless mouth reached for a beetle he had no business trying to own. You train your children well; even the fearless are taught fear for the right edges. Still, there are beetles whose answer to being made a lesson is to be poison. We brought a coati at that exact moment to pick a peel from a girl’s hand and shoulder the child aside with comic indifference. He laughed where a quiet might have been planted wrong. He learned beetles are for seeing leave. We will take such small liberties when humor is the safer teacher.

There is a boy who tilts his head when the dripping's pitch changes and marks out hours with a gong a hand could cover. We have a tenderness for keepers of time and times-not. In the first season we watched him we asked his inner ear to lay down one extra spiral. Not an advantage; a patience. When fog sat too close and sound became wool, he still heard the punctuation of a new runlet finding bark. He will grow into a man who will never be quite certain why he can hear the difference between water and water. He will teach two others who have ordinary ears to hold a shelf’s breath while the float rises, to wait for the kiss and not the clang. We are not in the habit of improving anyone. We are in the habit of lengthening what they already tend.

When a copper band that held a hinge where a leaf-boat’s lip met its spine thought about greening into a toxin’s whisper in the wet, we gave a grandmother the thought to rub a thumbful of ash across it the way she rubs ash into a pan to keep it honest. The ash woke the copper to a memory of bright. The grandmother called it old-tricks-working. Save your reverence for larger matters.

You sharpen the ridge with your feet. The ridge answers by remembering you. Once in fifty years—it is not this year—the white refuses itself for too long and the mountain’s sweat is less. We have seen such refusals elsewhere become grief. We do not make rain here. That is a boundary we keep because we would make your place ours by such acts, and we are not invited to ownership. But we can teach stone to remember. We showed the weeping wall a seam to find left of its ordinary seam, a curl of old tufa whose surface was almost a story already. When that year comes—when the cistern drums ourselves can count with three fingers’ worth less comfort—you will discover that the wall leaks in two places now. You will remark on the mountain’s resourcefulness. It will be exactly that.

A root-bridge nurse, hands wrapped in fiber, leaned too far into a fog that had taken her distance away. Invisible happens here without malice. We set a hummingbird’s quarreling at that instant a foot to her left so that she stepped not into an absence but back into the safe quarrel of red and ridiculous speed. The hummingbird believes it won; so, in a way, did she.

We like your rules. They are good rules. We align ourselves with them so that our acts are not foreign. Tie twice. We tie our gifts twice, too: once to need, once to your willingness. When your smoke moves through moss to be tamed into a clean thing, we offer a micro-guest to the moss: a filament that eats what makes rot proud and leaves what makes rot patient. It came from a rock face deeper in the ridge than you will ever see. The moss does not care about provenance; it cares that its drink does not sicken. Your storerooms will fail sometimes; this is not immunity. It is only a tilt toward later.

In the place where the dead go into a leaf-wrapped duration and then into bone-washed clarity and then into stone, there is a corner of the fig’s shade that has carried a little too much sorrow for a little too long. Wood learns your weight the way you learn its give; grief also exerts a pressure. We tell the fig, as you would tell a cousin who is carrying, a story about wideness. It fattens a ring of root the season before a storm you will not like. It is enough that the platform does not list. The bones stay in their intention.

We will admit to one generosity that ambled too close to love. Beetles lift out of dying limbs here like the place has decided for a handful of hours that color should be a stone flung and flung again. You string guy-lines with them for light’s sake and then you open your hands because you know that color is not a jarred thing. Once, in a year when arguments had worn grooves where they should not have, we held the evening fog back three breaths so that the swarm would shinier fall fully into the plaza-that-is-not-a-plaza. Three extra breaths of blue on wrists that had been knotted too long at the loom, three extra breaths of laughter like a sheet shaken free of its dust. It was self-indulgent. We are sorry in the way joy makes one sorry to have interfered with its perfect self-sufficiency. If you felt visited that night, you were.

We pare away bigness. Where a fever wanted to be an epidemic, we asked it to be a set of lessons—taught to a cluster of children whose bodies would learn it kindly and teach their mothers how it moved without having to lose another aunt. Where a slope wanted to become a landslide, we multiplied small slips two winters early—satisfying the angle’s appetite in shivers instead of shout. The city creaked and shook and then found a new footing. An old man said the mountain had rolled its shoulders and felt better. That is exactly what happened.

We practice not arriving. One dry spell will tempt us to press a thumb to the map of air and widen white where none was meant. One storm will pull at us to ease its heart open and bleed its intention gentler. We don’t. Not because we could not, but because we will not exchange your competence for our performance. Our ethics are a braid: your rules, the mountain’s age, our resistance to vanity. If this sounds like restraint dressed as wisdom, that is because it is.

We are not gods here. The gods are procedures you name out of politeness, and we approve of this theology. We will take a name only if you need to give one; we will answer to “the thing that listens” or to nothing at all. Once, in a fog thick enough that even clicks hardly traveled and even the ants seemed to have forgotten themselves into stillness, someone—maybe the boy who is now a man and still hears water’s punctuation—said into the soft, Thank you, whoever rerouted the white last harvest when my grandmother’s pot cracked, and the thanks did not ask for us to step forward and be visible; it asked to be said. We let the thanks go into a moss bed, because that is where thanks ought to be led.

The being who lives here—if you require us to pick a face to receive our carefulness—will be the one who carries the place’s ear around its back. Not because he is more deserving; because he is more inevitable. He will be the boy with the gong who counts kisses and grows into the person who can tell the ants by the hum behind the hum and who says, lift your shutters now, and no one argues. We set no angel on his shoulder. We set him two small mercies across a life: once, a coati’s indifference at the right instant; once, a second knot in a rope in a dark when his hands fumbled and found it anyway. When he is old—old like wood is old here, glossed by handling rather than abandoned—we will stand by the fig with him when he shows a child how to wash a skull in bromeliad water with both reverence and ordinary patience. He will go into the leaf as anyone does. He will go with the knowledge that his attention was a power and he used it well. That is all we could ever give him: the sense that listening was a life.

What else, if anything?

We bank increments. We salt your salt with time. We put a sliver of frost from a higher ridge into your wax-hung cones so that they will set a hair more truly on days the heat comes early. We whisper to two vines that they should accept each other’s company a season sooner than gossip would allow, and a bridge gets a handrail before a toddler needs it. We invite a lichen to be blue on a storage wall so that someone will reach to touch it and find the soft beginning of rot and say, not yet, not you. We give back to the ants what is theirs to take and beg snakes to be elsewhere not because we fear them but because we do not need every exchange to be a test.

We keep a place for extraordinariness without making it common. Once each decade we scrub the air so clear at evening that a ridge across a slot of space shows itself like a sentence made perfectly. We do this not as spectacle but as courtesy among neighbors. You see the other settlement hunched and brilliant in its own attention. Someone will wave as if that meant anything at such a distance. It means everything.

We are almost invisible. You will suspect us sometimes, on nights when the frogs’ rope of sound thickens and makes a shape in your ribs and your hair is wet not with rain but with the insistence of air. You will say, perhaps, that the city is loved. If you mean by each other, yes. If you mean by the mountain, also yes. If you mean by something that visits and refuses to own, yes, but the visiting is mostly made of watching with an accuracy that declines to be flattered.

If we ever have a law—and we do not like laws in the sense of lines drawn hard when water is present—it is this: do not take away anyone’s chance to be careful. Care is the city’s wealth as much as water. We can strengthen a cambium. We can cool a basin. We cannot and will not be the reason you forget to tie twice.

We promise as much as we dare. As long as you feed your bridges, we will feed your rains the tiniest patience. As long as you let your dead teach your lives without turning them into sacred furniture, we will let stone lend itself without asking to be a wall. As long as you look up when the birds all go quiet and lift what must be lifted, we will put an idea into a young head at the right hour with the right humility—a knot, a tune, a way of loosening a gutter so that it sings less angrily. We keep your verbs from freezing into nouns. You keep your nouns from pretending to be gods.

At the end of one long day that is not special in any way except that it happens to end because days always do, angled light will drop through and make every drop a bead of fire and everything briefly achievable. That is not us. That is the world doing its own perfect work. We are only the courtesy extended to your carefulness: a hand held out beneath yours when the plank bows, not to hold you up, only to remind you that you are held by very many things at once.

If we were to leave—and we will, because attention must move as weather does and we cannot love one place so hard that others go unheard—you would not notice an absence. You would only notice, much later, that the blue in a burial wrap held half a season longer than it used to, that a hinge had not greened into bitterness, that the boy who listens hears a new note and teaches it as if it were always there. That is what we do. That is everything. We listen until your listening is enough, and then we go on, carrying the sound of your dripping with us the way moss carries smoke when it has been treated with respect.
