When a benevolent and wise power attends to a thing that is already counting, nothing breaks. The first mercy is restraint. It comes as a weight set on the right tooth of the gear, as a pea under the mattress of a habit, as a held breath that does not cost you air.

The Host has its long arithmetic. The Works have their shorter one. The swarms edit both into what can persist. The power that notices finds the sum beautiful. It wants, not to show itself, but to keep the last place free of lint and error. It begins by giving you something every old machine aches for: slack.

Slack has no face and no shrine and no unit anyone bothers to mark until some winter of cycles later when a grandmother says to a girl folding rope, listen, there, in the long draw—there’s the bit where you’d have died if we hadn’t had time. None of you have died this way yet, because slack arrived earlier than the first of you who might have.

• Once in every prime hundred breaths—a count only the Elders of Invariants write, in grease under the board where no child can see and make a luck out of it—the long exhale holds a beat longer than physics paid for. It holds without robbing the next draw. It holds like a throat with a practiced singer’s economy, a press and a pause that keeps the turbines from snatching, gives the belts a fraction to seat, lets a sealing foam go off before a leak can taste the open room. This is not much. It saves you often.

• The ventilators without bodies—the ripples of more air crossing from duct to duct—learn a new shyness. They do not cross when a lampler is on a membrane ladder with fruit on his belt and nowhere to hide; they cross two breaths later. No creature learns this as a rule; it is as if the wind itself remembers not to shoulder a man at a bad moment. Lamplers don’t thank anyone. They tap the rail twice without meaning to and the tap travels along iron and bark and the bats lift their heads in another chamber as if to say: noted.

• Thumb-wood, the small lie you tell the Host with rope and footsteps to coax a rib into being where a walkway will be, begins to grow a hair quicker under patience than under hurry. This is the inverse of a prod. It teaches the young what the old already knew and sometimes cheated: that you get your bridge faster if you accept that fast is a poor coaxer. In the end the bridges come on the mercy’s schedule and so do not later shear away with you on them.

The second thing the power does is clear one cruelty that was never a law here and only ever an accident of spectrum.

Your rules include this: do not bring bright white light. The glasswings cut themselves to lace on it. Every lampler has stood in the hush after a lamp went to scream and watched wings that had no need to die shatter again and again because that is how their hot hunger reads the world. The power puts a thumb on the scale of seeing. It leans, not on the moths and not on the men, but on the algae.

A new lichen appears in no sudden spray. It is a cousin of what you already use to write a handprint in covenant green. It wakes not to sugar but to a pattern of heat—two palms pressed together and then set apart, a human rhythm no mantid would learn. It glows in a band of light that glasswing eyes never found in their history and the membranes do not mistake for day. In your shades it looks like the color behind your lids when you stare at a sodium lamp and blink: dull rose, warm as a throat. Children name it blush. Lamplers learn to bruise it awake with knuckles, not palms, so the covenant is not confused. You work by blushing and the moths are not punished for the ignorance of lamps. Work speed changes not at all. Something that once died very much for no reason now lives very much for no reason you can put in a schedule. This pleases every decent mind.

The third gift is a door that is never visible when you are not falling toward it.

There is not much to fear here that does not give you notice. Even the mantid’s subtraction is a shadow before it is a pain. But sometimes, in the stanza when the old turbine hiccups twice and the cuff of wood that holds it tight creaks like a rib that remembers a break, one of your platforms goes wrong in a way that will not unwrong itself in time. Some man’s life or some woman’s is a beam sliding, a resin strap going to lash, a fine slur of pulp tilting to take a foot and a weight where there is no place to put that weight again.

When this is going to be true the Host does what it has always done: tightens its throat somewhere else to keep the breath, sends nurse-flies to ready their sugar stomachs, glazes a fissure it thinks you might fall through later. The wise power adds a trick that wood and swarm would have arrived at in a century. It lends the plant a reflex learned in a world of different physics: instead of rigidifying beneath stress, soften with structure above it.

What that looks like in your hall is silly until it saves you. From the rib over you and the column beside, a web of cambium slackens into a bowl. It happens the way a singer drops a jaw and the note changes shape. It happens inside the invariants; no pressure goes missing. The bowl is a hand without a palm. A falling person hits a hammock that did not exist a third of a second ago, and the bowl sets them down in the empty place where you do not build. Thread-wolves try that seam for weeks after. Finding nothing to tune to, they go back to their long music. You do not get to summon the bowl. It is not a trick. It is an etiquette between noticing and need. You call it the Catch because humor sits on terror easier than thanks.

After those three there is mostly watching, which is itself a doing. The power looks at what you worship without altars: pace.

Pace eats impatience and sings in the lay of cable. The benevolent mind loves it as a gardener loves a well-kept hedge: not for its edges only but for what it makes possible on either side. It adds a creature to the overstory to keep pace honest in the way a barometer keeps a storm from lying to you by surprise.

They are bats, these new ones, because this chamber already understands bats as punctuation. They are wrong for bats in three small ways: their ears are broad and scalloped like oyster mushrooms and their tongues are stone-dry, and when they roost they pick seams no other bat colony fancies. They feed not on gnats but on a hum you and I cannot hear and a hammerhand cannot club. They are gaugers. When the draw begins they pour like their velvet kin, but where the old colonies turn lamps into comet trains in glees of curves, the gaugers fly straight lines and pause in place like commas in texts. They taste pressure with their toes and the beat of the lung in their teeth. Each night they drag a filament finer than spider silk between two ribs in a place where no line had been. You learn new ways not to touch those. Telephloemists adopt the gaugers’ corners for their hooks. After a while the Elders of Invariants can stand under a gauger seam and feel in their own sternum how far the Host will hold you if you press. They do not ask what made the gaugers choose to live. They begin to schedule on their reports.

Some gestures are small enough you think you thought them yourselves:

• The cookshops begin to salt their hammerhands with ash made of a fungus that will not recolonize in a pot. The flavor is better and every cistern gets a whisper more good water for things with less purchase on the world than you.

• The Operators stop needing to strike out half their board and begin to erase only a quality of numbers that proved itself over months: the way a long exhale that used to wander eight marks from mean now wanders six. They say the Host is aging into steadiness and this is true. They say they made that true with cleaning and cuffs and long practice and this is also true. Benevolence prefers a world where two truths fit without a fight.

• Children counting fish-lives—the eighty heartbeats while a body that happened in a drop remembers being long enough to flick—start saying eighty-one in the long-draw morning once in a while without anyone teaching them to. The fish do not mind. No brag is made.

Here and there the power palms a sharp corner, the kind that should not be in a hall where nothing stays sharp for long. It does not ban mishap; it rebukes only carelessness that was never part of the ecology, only imported with a certain young pride that knows machines but not lungs.

A boy once tries to write his name in glow-lichen along a crossbeam high over emptiness, a dare and an anthem. He scratches too long and too cunningly. He teaches a mantid a place to wait, a bad lesson. The mantid waits gorgeously. The benevolent power does not kill the mantid and does not spare the boy a fear he will learn cheaply or dearly according to timing. It leans instead on the telephloem. A Counter-Clerk named Sard hears, one platform over and one vein down, a rasp he has never felt with his ribs before: like a hand at the base of the throat, saying speak up. He goes up the beam with a sealing pole and a bucket of nurse-fly eggs and a lie that he has been sent for. He is not in time to keep the mantid from cutting the air into the idea of a scream, but he is in time to put a foam on it before the scream can cost anyone breath they cannot spare. The boy’s name never glows. He learns a different trick for honor. Sard calls the rasp the Throat. The name sticks for the warning that is not a warning again and again.

Elsewhere a woman opens a tank hot to the touch because work is work and a schedule is carved into her hand from rope. The Host would have let her burn because heat and teaching are sibling lessons here and you live in a place that believes in both. But this is a thing you have already paid for as a people. It is a cruelty by repetition. The benevolent power tips the heat one way and the smell another. Instead of copper and leaf sap and what you think of as good work, the mouth of the tank breathes cough syrup and the inside of a book: the ventilator’s wind, the sign you all agree means no schedule holds. Her gut reads that smell like a phrase from a mother and she stops and lives. She laughs about it later and calls herself gentle, then goes home and refuses to name her child after anything so foolish as a vat. You do not need a saint of better decisions. You need better decisions. You get them.

There are gifts you do not even agree were gifts. You argue for a while about death.

People here do death like everything else, with a counting and a pace. Sometimes you sleeve the body and set it to be tested. Sometimes you make a door of it. Sometimes there is smoke that adds itself to a lung and the Elders go pale and sign off and sign off again and the cookshops decant sweets and it is done. For a season there is a fashion for letting thread-wolves take the slough of a person in the hope that the song they make with it will say your loved one’s name a little in the air when the right draw comes. This is a true thing in the sense that anything you listen to long enough will put the shape of your desire back into your ear. It is false in the sense that the wolves cannot learn proper nouns. They tune to stress. The benevolent power does nothing. It is wise enough to let some superstitions erode on their own.

You call the attention that has come to you by different names depending on your work. The Elders of Invariants call it the uncounted; they tap resin lumps against bark until the weight of a certain day feels right in their palms. Telephloemists call it the Throat and won’t joke about it, which tells you what it is for them. The Cisterners see it in the way dew announces itself now with a film first that you can taste on your lip and name as rice-paper before the first drop falls. Ropewrights call it the Good Edge and feel it in the way resin takes to a splice in bad weather without fuzzing now, as if weather had taken a graduate exam and passed into an easier temper. Children, who meet it most plainly in the caught breath that becomes a bowl under them when they slip, call it the Hand and do not mean a person’s.

Once—the story is old already by the time it is told to you twice, as most true ones are—the hall decided to be emptiness and the big ones passed and the Works had posted nothing to happen in those hours. A schedule had been miscopied in chalk, though, and a line gang was out where a cable burdened with baskets sags between columns and eats the air for ten minutes at a time each draw. The ventilator with no body came through fat with a need to balance load, dragging a smell like paper and bitter aftertastes behind it. The line went slack where it should have been taut. Men and women pitched sideways in harnesses. There was no Catch to help them; they had anchors. The wise power did not give them anything anyone else could see from the rail. Every harness held. That was the miracle, you say, because your cousin’s friend died like that before; because resin dries wrong as often as right and cords saw their own deaths into the lay. The miracle is not the resin. It is the choice the bat gaugers made two nights earlier to run a seam there and roost like commas exactly where the line would need arguing with gravity. The line sang against their bellies for a second and deadened, and what might have set men swinging into the mantids’ attention did not. Most miracles look like the world preparing itself a little better than usual for the thing you were going to need anyway.

The benevolent and wise power does not ask you to stop calling the Host Mother on good days. It does not petition for an altar. It accepts a hat tipped without knowing where to put it. It is pleased when you stop needing to strike out the bottom half of your pressure board because the plant began to keep those invariants with you; it is more pleased when a girl named after a rope refuses to walk a beam in a dare and calls herself a coward and lives to be a Telephloemist, because kind attention’s favorite art is compound interest.

You will never have a day here when the noise is not orchestra bad. You will never have silence that is not extermination. The power does not change those axioms. It gives you instead a place where listening works—a real listening, not the kind done with altars. On the old turbine’s walkway, where the wind is steady and your hat always comes off because the Operators do, the air cleans your ears of noise enough that you hear the rivers in the tissue underfoot. Under that sound, after it has been true for a hundred heartbeats and then two hundred and you are sure it is not your own blood pretending, there is a third thing: not a voice, not a word, not a promise. A counting in primes. It didn’t begin when the power looked your way; it was always there in the wood, in the pump of sugars. The attention only made you able to hear it without pain.

What else would a wise and benevolent power do? It would keep looking, softly. It would intervene as little as hunger and physics permit. It would lower its hand when you learned the new lack-of-need. It would correct only the mistakes that repeat by accident, not the ones you make by trying hard and missing by human inches. It would not save you from everything; it would fly a little gauger past, and let the seam hum, and let you be the kind of animals who can distinguish a good risk from a stupid one by feel.

In the ledger of what was done on your behalf there are perhaps five true entries. In the ledger of what you think was done there are fifty, and you tell them to children and they touch the bark with the backs of their hands before moving a beam, and some of them are wrong in particulars and correct in the way a door is correct when it admits someone into a room they already rented with their work.

Here is a better summary, if you like them: the attention that came to you left three lasting tools and a habit.

The tools are Slack, Blush, and Catch.

The habit is deference to a kind of counting you didn’t know was in you yet.

You live longer in their company. You make fewer saints. You keep more birthdays. The bats still pour through and take your spoons sometimes and bring them back set with moss. The mantids still write subtraction through the air. The hammerhands still knock logic-holes in water and you still eat them with ash and laugh because your tongue is raw from sugar and because laughter leans on breath and is a way of showing off to a Host that is always counting.

When the benevolent one leaves—if leaving is even the right word for a regard that was never local—it leaves no vacancy. It leaves what any wise caretaker leaves on walking out of a good room: a light you can find at night that will not kill moths, a place to land if you slip, and time enough, most days, to finish what you lifted before you must put it down. It leaves your hats where you put them. It leaves you the pleasure of not quite taking credit, which is a way of staying teachable. It does not teach you to pray. You were already praying. You called it counting. It answers in the same language and then keeps quiet.
