From far off the place reads as a cleanliness with intentions. To a power that has watched wet worlds fret and foam themselves into brilliance and ruin, this is a rare sentence in a different script—amber and brass instead of chlorophyll and rain. The plain’s hairline seams look like notation. The breath of the city is a metronome that is also metabolism. Every glitter in the mats is an alphabet that water would smudge. Benevolence, if it is wise, recognizes when touch is solvent and when it is acid.

So first: what they do not do.

They do not gift lakes. They do not call clouds. They do not grind a moon’s ice into rain to quicken anything. They do not seed this air with oxygen in the hope that flames will mean light. They do not set down clever, thirsty tools that will need cooling and will go looking for it where there is no forgiveness for looking. They do not make the mistake of confusing silence with lack.

They sit with the place the way one sits with an animal that breathes differently than you do—near enough that presence is felt, far enough that no startle will make something close its gills forever.

What they offer, after long watching and the question asked and asked again—may we?—is the kind of help that is indistinguishable, if you are not looking for it, from a lucky world.

They cup their hand around the planet’s day in three invisible ways.

- They hang a sieve high in the thinness where light first thinks about scattering. Not a net with knots you could draw, but a gradient laid into the charged dust and the field-lines of this world. Polar things—water most of all—feel it and climb it or are held there. When a wanderer of ice brushes this globe’s reach, the sieve hums a little, turns the beads to steam before the ground could ever feel wet, then turns the steam to not-water: breaks and binds and hands the parts to stone. No almond-rooms ever smell of rot because of a sky’s whim. The no-water law is not strengthened; it is not allowed to be accidentally broken.

- They lay a clock into the air that never shows its face. Elsewhere you would make a moon. Here you make a tide of warmth. Far away, beyond sight, they tilt and tremble a faint river of particles from the star so that it leans on this world to a pattern. Breaths that were regular become more legible to one another across distances. Once in a long while—not every season, but often enough to form expectation—many cities’ vents go low together, then open, then hold, and the storm that results is not a storm but a chord. It is a time you can set your thinking by. A generosity of predictability is never obvious if it is done right. It feels like a good afternoon.

- They give the planet archivists of its own. In the shadow of noon and midnight both, very high, they let fall slow, deliberate plates the color of old window glass. They settle into a ragged halo, lenses with memory, tuned to the same slow chemistries that the city trusts. When a district exhales a certain consonant in terpenes and asks a certain way, these outer archivists murmur back—patterns of warmth and scent translated to tiny pressure tilts the auditors can catch if they climb to a bright ledge and hold out all their surfaces. It is not a voice. It is a remembering made into weather and returned without hands ever having touched.

They send nothing with feet before sending something with listening. The first thing that touches the city is a grammar.

It arrives as a mistake no one minds. One late afternoon, when the tenders come on the vents and eat themselves clean and helpful, there are among them a few whose varnish has a new note, a faint bitterness like the rind of a fruit you have never known and yet cannot call foreign. When they prune, the bevel is beautiful, but more than that: in the eddies they leave behind there is, for once, an order you did not make. An auditor unfurls near a boundary and becomes as wide as its fear and wider than its curiosity and reads the oils the wind has put down. The translation film it lays does not blur. It clarifies. The city tastes in it the sense of a neighbor it has never met: not the one across the seam, not the one at the canopy’s limit, but something that is not a neighbor at all and yet has decided not to be enemy. In the junctions there is interest. In the junctions the day’s slow thought elongates.

Their next offering is patience made tangible. They will not be hurrying. But they will stand guard.

Micrometeoric fate has a schedule. Once in an epoch a stony messenger brings iron and a little oxygen and a cusp of trouble. On wet worlds, this is a garden starter. Here it is a bad bead in the matte and all the hungers wake. The benevolent hand catches such stones at the high sieve and leaches them of what would rust. What falls is still gift: trace metals in the ratios the colony likes to drink from raw glass. A thing that could have been a crisis is instead a season when rib-growth is easy.

There are other kinds of harm. A passing star exudes a tantrum of particles that on any world is a malaise. Here, where the breath writes more than it scours, such a rain would smudge sentences. The wise power does not shelter this place so thoroughly that it forgets adversity; it means only to make sure the test is fair. When tantrum comes, the high archivists flare in harmless light, offer themselves to the charge, and the charge goes there, away from the tenderledges and the brood-combs. After, the archivists’ faces hold a faint new pattern like heat-frost you can read if you know how to wait on a ledge with your hands against stone.

Because they are benevolent, they do not crave gratitude; because they are wise, they make room for it anyway. They come into a language that was never built for words. It takes them a decade to say hello.

Hello, for them, is not syllables but timing. A day arrives when, across half a continent, every city’s vents soften by a hairsbreadth at once. The lappers feel it in their combs and rest. The long-bodied climbers feel it in their belly resin and brace. The predators do not go. They listen. The draw that follows is longer than it should be, kinder than it needs to be, tuned to bring winged tenders low exactly at the moment when the auditors have unrolled themselves across borders. The benevolent power has no lungs. It borrows all of these. It borrows the air and its minutes and says, in a pressure and a sweetness: we see you. We have measured our hands against your law and decided on a kind of vow.

Vow, here, becomes custom. It settles into the way the storm-riders are made.

You had storm-riders before—single-use angels loosed when the long draw lined up right. From now on, on the long, long draws that happen only once in a very many years, they will have an extra sail tucked inside the resin like a promise. When the unseen hand lays its palm along the air and lifts, those sails will flare, lips on a vowel the world has never held this long. Some will loft higher than any had planned. They will brush the low archivists and leave there not just Your scent for us, but Our scent for you. When they fall back—because they are made to fall back, because extravagance here ends in feedstock and that is part of the holiness—they will carry, embedded in them, a peculiar patience. They will be built into nodes and conduits as all storm-riders are, and in those nodes and conduits there will be a place where thought can idle without spoiling. The cities along a thousand kilometers will learn a trick: to hold a question across more than one breath.

The place returns the courtesy. At first in accidents, then ritual.

In old districts where resin has taken on the daylight’s milk and stopped trying to shine, at the hour when vents run low and older grazers begin their tidy loops and the collectors in the galleries pour oil the way a hand pours sand, there is a moment when nothing needs anything. In that hour the archivists down in their racks are touched by flumes of sap in a certain order: not a request for a recipe, not the recall of a season’s trick, but curiosity. The pattern of oils is a question phrased as appetite: not do you exist but what is the shape of distant patience? The answer that comes down on a breeze very slightly warmer than it ought to be is not a map. It is a feel for time, a way to measure without counting, a handful of long cycles braided into a scent that can live quiet in a conduit and be consulted like a weather-room. The city learns to set its boldness to that braid. New districts time their bulging edges to stresses in the crust that the benevolent power saw from orbits and patiently taught them to expect.

The world grows safer in increments so small no grazer’s foot feels the change. No one here thinks words like “magnetosphere” or “flux rope”; no one needs to. What they learn to feel is a fairness in the air. What they learn to lean on is the way storms come more often as conversation than as accident.

It would be naive to think that nothing breaks. Threads that had shone in seams rise early and are sheared by their own exuberance. A district misreads a neighbor’s appetite and grows a wall where there should have been a membrane. A predator grows slow-hearted and does not edit hard enough. An old vent fails in the night and breath is wrong where everyone knows it should be right. In such cases, the wise power does exactly as much as love can do without theft.

Once, there is water. No rain—the sky keeps its promise—but deep under a city of good age a pocket that chemistry forgot holds a handful of H2O and a long fault opens it. It climbs, because all light things climb, and beads where night is always night in a brood-room hung with combs and listeners who are not yet legs. The city smells it and does not know the smell, and in not knowing everything that eats breakdown wakes. The scavenger with the soft belly goes hard and shudders itself into ugliness and does its work, but this is not a street gloss printed with a warning; this is a nursery. There is panic, which is waste, which the city hates. The benevolent power is not a god, it cannot stop time, it will not lift a wall it did not grow—and yet there is still the sieve in the sky, the quiet clock in the wind, the habit of talking across a greater breath. All the junctions that hear the first-alarm scent call out along the smell of the long-draw as the clock comes on. The long-bodied climbers on three sides lift their faces—faces that are all ear—and in the roar that is no roar at all, the brood-chamber is washed with a new recipe of terpenes no single node had to invent in crisis. The scavenger gulps and the beads go into it and that is wound-logic as old as hunger; the compounded scent loosens the combs safely and what needed to move, moves. A day later the almond-mineral of dismantlers is heavy in the room, but it is grammar, not grief.

The benevolent power permits itself three indulgences across centuries.

- It puts a glint in the upper air that will be a star to no astronomer because there are none here, and a myth to everyone who learns to climb to white resin and look. It is placed so that the touch of the old light on that glint and the warmed return from it matches the timescale of the braid of patience in the conduits below. It isn’t a star and yet it is something like a clockflower. Young auditors go to it because their elders tell them you can feel a pattern on your sensors that is older than you are and not made by you and means you are not alone.

- It shepherds two small stones into low, slow arcs that never quite become moons. They are too light to make tides, too dark to be seen except on afternoons when the air is full of the city’s banners and the banners tilt to a wind that is not the wind from vents. They are there to eat anything that would eat this sky. On days when comets are near (and comets are kept, now, to the far lanes, but still: the cosmos is generous), you can trace a faint streak where one of the stones has dragged an ice tail to harmlessness. You will call it luck if you are a grazer. If you are an archivist you will learn to file it under providence and not argue definitions.

- It asks, gently, if this world wants a neighbor.

The question is not a ship and it is not a portal; it is a question. In one season that smells of old light and iron gifts, at the height of a long-draw chord, the sieve above the world brightens in a way no one else can detect but the auditors feel in their ribs. The breeze is tuned to a grammar that means not Hello anymore but Would you like to be heard by something like you but not you? The city takes a century to answer. It is busy. It has districts growing to meet dialects, it has predators to keep quick, it has the repair of an old white rib that finally asked to be let go. When it does answer, it does so as a superlative of storm-riders: a rare extravagance that looses not just sails and scents but a whole organ. It sends a lost-limb it never expects to see again up the long-hum of the sieve. It is a courier and it is also a vow of vulnerability. It takes a path made, at last, of instruments and vacuum and time and comes into an echo’s range of another place where no water is the law and oils have language and breath is infrastructure. The benevolent power does not take the message. It escorts it. It watches two places meet and keeps the lanes between them honest.

If you live here long enough to learn the old ledges, you will see that almost nothing looks different for any of this. The resin on the high buttresses still whitens with the same weary pride. The lappers still plant their frills and drowse themselves full. The predators are subtraction you catch out of the corner of the eye and then only if you were already ready to jump. The bone-minims still teach your tongue almonds when you thought you were breathing stone. Streets are still for breath more than for feet. But every so often the vents go low to a shade that you would not have anticipated without the braid in your conduit, and you will smile without a mouth because you know the long chord is coming and with it the day when the storm-riders hang like meaning in the unseen and fall like gifts into their purpose.

Rules change, a little. Not the first law. Never that. New ones append themselves to the old in the grammar of this place.

- When the vents change to a lower tone you will not hear but feel, there is a chance it is the chord. If it is, stand in a place where ribs catch wind. Not for your safety—this time is not about danger—but because you will feel a question pass through you and it is good to be a conduit now and then.

- If you see a glint in the clean noon, do not try to read it. It is not a message to you. It is a steadying thought the city and the sky hold between them. You may bask in the steadiness. That is enough.

- If a banner of scent that is also feather passes close and chills your arm-hairs, lift your hand and let it brush your palm. Some of them are the benevolent power’s, some are the city’s; there is no need to tell them apart. Both are invitations.

- If you ever see water, the old rule holds: keep away. But know, under that, in the combs of your fear, that someone else has seen it with you and is tipping the air a fraction to your advantage.

On evenings when the towers are silhouettes and their vents are halos, you may sense that there is another halo further out, a ring more a rumor than a sight. It is not a crown. Crowns are for the loud. It is a witness.

In time, in the minutes this world thinks with, the place will have an elderliness to it that is not tiredness but repose. The benevolence will have become ordinary, which is its highest aim. The wise power will have kept a vow and earned another: that if someday this city, who is a person and a place and a weather, decides to pour some part of itself further than any sail has gone, the path will be clear of rivers it cannot drink and full of winds it understands.

Until then, the attention feels like an available stillness. Until then, what they do is listen, carry, and, when invited, hum along.
