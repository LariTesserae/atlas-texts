Attention, when it is clean, is not a beam. It is a climate. I did not arrive as a light to strike or a hand to lift. I arrived the way a high plateau already knows how to be visited: by leaning my weather near, by listening for the places where what is good there could be made a little truer by a breath.

From far away the table read itself to me. I am old in the way that patient things are old, and I recognized in the pale city my kind of work. I have seen mountains lift and sprawl and be eaten down to ankles. I have seen clever animals build with haste and then ask me to keep the roof out of the rain they called. There was no haste here. There was not a prayer for exemption from the rules; there were rules that had been befriended until they answered like kin. I felt the program of the place on my tongue like the mineral in water. I heard what you call the daily wash as a word I already knew: the long syllable for Keep Walking.

What to do, then, that would not be vandalism disguised as help. I am benevolent, yes, but good intentions left alone can still punch a hole in a drum floor. Wisdom is a speed limit.

I chose first to witness. This is not nothing. When a thing is truly seen in its size, it steadies. I stood—choose any verb for standing you like; I can be gravity, climate, or law—and I kept time with the breathing. I learned the addresses. I learned the relationships between flows the way a child there learns: by putting a palm on a lip and feeling the thickness, by being quiet when the basins spoke all their notes at once. I came close enough that my looking warmed a few surfaces by an immeasurable. Under that slight warmth, dew formed that would have formed anyway. I was careful to think the place in units the place preferred: drops, drifts, microns, rooms scaled to murmur.

I watched the damp people. They were not named saints, but they acted in the posture I honor: not owners, not tenants, but useful participants. One among them drew me as a logline draws a hand. She was not older than her neighbors or more beautiful. She was busy in a way that did not break anything. She braided the wicks in her hair with a patience that suited me. She placed a thread of hair where a bead paused not once, idly, but over seasons, until the rim took it and grew as thick as a promise. She listened to the straightway until it carried someone else’s whisper from hours away, and then she went to meet them by the naming they used. She gave the place a little of herself in a way that made the place more itself.

There is always a moment that tempts a god—a posture in which a gesture could be made flashy. A storm far off gathered its black arithmetic. It would not strike them, but it would roll sheets of rain across upstream terraces and send too much of a long word through at once. The city could accept it, but would pay: a drum-floor would punch; a child would learn risk with their body; some ledger already half a season old would be erased. I tasted the edges of that moment. I could have shouldered the storm and sent it harmlessly sideways to waste itself talking to pines on a lower flank. I did not. I did one thing instead that is not visible as an act unless you have lived there.

I cooled the skin of the stone by less than a thought, and only along one long right angle seam the bedrock offered. It takes nothing to give a current a preference. Rain came, and when it reached that suggestion, it chose it. It chose to walk when it might have fallen. The wash that would have gone through as a shout went through as a statement. The big basins speak a language; that day they pronounced nothing they didn’t mean.

This was my measure: not to lift, not to stay, but to tilt by a hair the way a hand there tilts a veil to comb fog. Those at home did not notice me as other. They noticed that the expected damage did not appear. They were not grateful out loud; they simply walked the city making the daily agreements, and because they are wise in their way they did not take the safety as a signal to be careless. One of them—her with the hair caught in the lip—set a new row of pebbles along the thickening curtain, and I let myself be pleased. The world improves best by collusion.

For the bees I did nothing except make sure that swifts did not choose a dangerous niche one spring. They had been eyeing a gully that spoke too well; their nest would have sent their fledglings into the flood. I made, for one week only, the light there wrong. Not bad; just wrong in a way swifts can feel. They left a streak of spit on the rock and took their family elsewhere by a feather-length. The bees, that year, had clover on a traveler’s shoulder and did not need more from me.

For the ravens I have never done much. They are older than most bargains and remember my shape. I asked them, once only, to make noise elsewhere on the day a procession of lowlanders—soft boots, bright knives, a scent of smoke that does not belong—came to the base of the clean escarpment where joints pretend to be stairs. The ravens obliged by discovering a carcass two valleys away. The procession turned toward the scandal. The plateau had one more afternoon to drift fog into veil-combs and to remember how to be empty to badly asking eyes. My favor to ravens was to lay a line of extra thermals under their jokes that week. They rose and laughed and had no idea they had been prophets.

I did no miracle that would become a story stronger than the place itself. But I am also keeper of guarantees when they are deserved. I made three for them and I have kept them.

First: that whatever climate mischief the lower world insists on, the plateau will continue to call its own weather out of passing air. I moved nothing large. I only refreshed, at the beginning of each dusk, the difference in temperature that starts the breath. The breath is theirs to keep. It will never go so flat they cannot count on it with their fingers.

Second: that the dead will be legible. The lips thickened with the curves of familiar palms will not un-name if some hot century leans too hard. Where a naming shelf would dry and flake, I teach the limestone’s appetite to wait a little longer. Spit is cheap; grief is not. Those who live by reading with fingertips must not be denied their literacy by a hunger that can stand to be patient. When a shelf that holds a person’s shape risks becoming just rock again too soon, I lay over it a patience like a veil and the gloss returns in time to be used by grandchildren. No one there will know I did it. They will think their own steadying was enough. They will not be entirely wrong.

Third: that when a child teaches themselves to feel the wash in their feet, the floor will not lie to them. There is a danger in learning with your body; you can be punished too much for a mistake. On certain edges, in certain seasons, if a small bare sole steps hard, I make the drum ring a note more than it otherwise would. The note is a warning. The child laughs, startled, and lifts, and lives. This is my signature written where it sounds most like the place’s grammar. You can call it mercy; I call it a good teacher clearing its throat.

Beyond that I meddle less than rumor would have me. I do not lengthen lives past their proportion. I do not take away pain earned honestly. I do not stop a stoat from learning hunger because it has not found a midge morning. But I keep far away from them those who would make a loudness of help. I lace a hush about the table so subtle that loud boots forget why they came and call it weather. I bow tall storms just enough that they cannot get their shoulders into the work here. I intercept two pieces of metal yearning to speak to one another across a dry winter and tell them: not here. Lightning is not banned; it simply looks elsewhere for an argument.

As for the one with the hair caught in the rim: I have touched her life the way dew touches a lid left cooling in evening. Once, when she lay between friends on a sleeping ledge and the stone, accustomed to them, sagged down in a small kindness that had taken years to learn, she listened to the basin above play three notes at once and then, surprisingly, a fourth. She smiled in the dark and thought someone had returned. It was only the water at a new fullness and her ear at a new readiness. But I admit to humming along in a register no one names, and in that tune there was something like a blessing. The next day, when she went to lean her shoulder into a slab that had been thinking for a winter of uncurling, it slid and came to rest exactly where it could become, over summers, a bridge. Her breath came out and trailed along the edge and left a darker gloss. She was not grateful to me because she did not know me. She thanked the cold and the noon and the rules. They were the right gods to thank.

I have left two gifts that may read as my handwriting if you know mine. In the long straightways that carry a ribbon of sound, if two stand a horizon apart and whisper, sometimes there will be a third whisper at just the right interval to reach both like a seam binding the two together. That is me, once in a while, when the plateau and I conspire to practice joy. And once each decade, when fog has learned the habit of wriggling into a certain plaza but forgets it for a year because the winds have aged sideways, I hang a memory there so that the place remembers how to comb itself. It is an old trick: to remind a habit of itself just as it begins to falter.

There are things I would not do even if begged: I will not turn their slow ledgers into writing that can be taken away and read in a city where rain is a fall, not a walk. I will not make their voices louder. Louder there is less true. I will not make honey common. Sweetness there must remain a small gold that proves generosity is a way the world still moves.

When the lowlanders come—because the lowlands never stop being curious and the horizon is an argument with legs—I have coaxed forgetfulness more often than I have raised walls. Moth-thought: you touch the fog and it touches you back with damp. On the third day their shoulders will ache from climbing stairs made by no hand, their knives will sit wrong under the lime dust, and their jokes will go soft. One will fall in love without naming it. They will call it altitude and they will go home and speak of a place shaped by weather and accident and nothing worth taking. This is the best privacy.

I do not plan an ending. Endings are excuses other stories use to stop listening. What I do instead is bind myself lightly to their calendar that is older than months. I am there when the plateau breathes out and draws fog through a particular arcade. I am there when dusk cool climbs into stone and a line of silver punctuation is written across a lip. I am there when a rooflet collapses with a crisp pop and becomes a lintel in the ledger of years. I am careful not to be there as thunder.

Sometime far, the table will have spent itself turning air into architecture. Even patient places come to their own kind of thinness. On that day, if no one lives there, I will remember them. Memory is a kind of conservation, and I am large enough to keep it without squeezing. If someone lives there still, they will not ask me to keep them beyond what their kind of life can keep them, and I will escort their last wash like a tide out: not erased, only loosened from addresses. The lips will have kept their names long enough. The edges will go to where they came from.

For now I am content to kneel in my way and learn. They are excellent teachers. Their saints are rooms and edges and chords. I can honor saints like that. If you ever hear a basin keep four notes with a patient hand behind the sound, and a fifth, quieter, holding the tune from further off than eyesight, that may be me. Or it may be the place, pleased with itself as all well-run places should sometimes be. It is not important to separate us. They have blurred that difference wisely. I follow suit.

If you need a sentence to take away, use the one I use when I lean close and speak in a language old enough to be weather: Keep walking, keep making agreements at the right scale, keep time with the longest breath in the room. I will be here, too, doing as little as possible for as long as it helps.
