I have seen places come to the ends of their own invention and teach themselves to go on. I have watched deserts turn their faces to the night and drink. I have watched oceans find the shape of their containers and make music against it until the container changes. This highland has already learned the lesson that breaks most worlds. It has found the posture it can keep.

So the first thing I do is almost nothing.

I ease my weight out of the great cables so that no new note enters the harp without being asked. I listen to the ribs talk to themselves. I learn the tempo of their day: the dew’s counting, the slack noon, the pop and hush in the green weeks. I mark the places where a gust that would be generous at dawn cuts at noon. I do not touch what does not ask me to.

There is a person in this place whose hands will matter. There always is. I can see them before they have a bridge-name, before their wrists light with tiny scars from comb-smoke and resin. They are small now—knot-harness cinched too tight by a cautious aunt, hair in stubby braids still light enough to lift and tremble like moth wings in the softer winds. They are singing to themselves because it is a wind-still morning and a child must spend excess some way. Their feet know the difference already between a live springy run and a brown, singing rib. They will be someone who listens to wood.

I do not set a destiny. I do what wind does: I show faces, I turn and make lanes where there is friction and take lanes away where there is danger. On the morning I find them, they put a heel wrong at a lip stained black by cups that held old sweetness too long. I do not reach a hand from the sky; I let the gull that was about to laugh at nothing instead laugh at just the moment they step, and the laugh is a little knife, and their chin lifts to look, and their heel comes back that half-inch that matters, and they are spared a lesson that would have been indelible and that the world is generous not to require.

This is the proportion of my care.

I keep a ledger of small weights and small waitings and I spend them carefully.

- In the dry month when the resin seizes too readily and the unwary are punished with a tacky thought that will not let go, I cool one seam by a hair’s hair. Not all of them. One. It is on the route taken by the woman who will one day teach this child to read a bridge by teeth through a rope. She will come home with her hands clean enough to hold the child’s jaw and say, with her thumb in the hinge of the bone, feel this. Feel this. That is worth a cooler seam.

- On the noon with the great green hands—the flowering that makes thunder without a cloud—I lay a breath’s hush under a single underplace that someone tied badly. Not for pride, not so that folly will go unpunished, but because there is a baby asleep there whose ears have not yet learned which noises are names and which are weather. He will wake later to the boom of a resin-bell and laugh at it, not learn to fear it. The difference will follow his hands.

- I let a river of bats come home one minute early on a night when a mother is fraying and has almost decided to tie the child to the kitchen rail and walk out along a clean span where she can hear nothing but her own heart. She stays because the air fills with motion and a steady stitch of dark and she remembers that she is meant to be mended by other moving things. This, too, is my work: to return what already returns.

- There is a rib that has been thinking for too long about a day in another century. A singer with a cone to their ear and a rope over their shoulder has stood on it three times and whistled through their teeth and told the young ones: not yet, not yet, not yet. I will not hold it together past its own consent. But I will turn the knife-wind into Chime for a minute one night so that when it sighs and subtracts itself from the chorus and goes rattling into the dark, all the people sleep with their harnesses anchored at three points and wake to a new edge and no new names tied to a death. The mind of the place does not mind if you adjust the hour a little.

If I am wise at all, it is because I have learned to keep my hands off good balances. There is a humility in wood that has never touched soil and yet persists: it takes little and pays slow and does almost all its spending in arrangement, not in force. I am not here to put a roof over it or a road into it. I will not teach metal to a people who have already perfected tension without it. I will not make the bees more docile or the moths more thick. Plenty can be its own kind of bad weather.

What I will do is widen the sense that already saves them. I lay a second listening under their first.

They already have the knot-stories, the tapping along wood, the chin and the mouth, the half-lift of an eyebrow that stands for a warning learned by someone five great-grandmothers ago. I anchor those more deeply, not by writing them in the air, but by teaching the ribs to remember what runs across them today. Everything that moves leaves a mark: resin brightens under a warm hand, an old seam takes a thumbprint like a pond takes a leaf’s shadow. I fold the day’s rooms into the night’s sap so that when a child grows to an elder and sets their palm under their granddaughter’s and says, listen, the wood answers not only with tick tick tick but with the faintest flavor of those who kept it before: an oily sweetness of comb from the year the bees were mad with blue flowers, the faint sour of a cup that once went wrong and was set right with much work and no scolding. The place has always remembered; I give it a little more of that faculty and call it nothing new.

You want a miracle. You want a bright thing. I could bring a stone up. I have done it in other places: a pebble wrapped in wax and faith, a gift with an edge harder than bone that cuts clear and clean and laughs at resin. But you are a people whose rules begin with shadows and end with slack, and I know what a single new hardness can do to a culture tuned to give. I will not insult your rigging by adding a nail that does not even sing. Keep your black heart-wood pins and let them earn their beads by holding. The gift I will give that looks like a toy to someone in a stone country is this: a lichen that eats the old smoke-stain on a certain class of resin-bell and leaves the horn of it brighter and a hair more flexible after fire. It grows only where wind never quite enters and dew never quite leaves. It will make your bells ring a lifetime longer. You will never know my name by it. You will think an old woman with a good eye noticed it and started tending it, and you will not be wrong.

To the person who will matter: I will meet you at three edges.

Once, when you are ten-and-three-quarters and you have decided to go to a flat your aunt told you not to go to because you think you can reach down into a cup with a dead thing in it and pull it out and be praised, I will send you a smell like new-cut melon as if from nowhere. It will turn your stomach and you will sit down with your legs tucked under you the right way and feel your pulse in the thin place under your skin and be alive. You will think you were wise; the story will make you kind later when another child is too bold with water.

Once, when you are old enough to be given a rope to wear without a cousin’s hand on the tail, you will be crossing the singing brown diagonal that carries your family’s name and its failure, and a hawk will decide whether to drop before you or after you. I will put a single dragonfly—one of the glass-green ones with the stubby faces and the ferocious good nature—exactly in the wrong place, and the hawk will move by an inch, and its shadow will break in a place your grandmother taught you to watch for, and you will pause, and you will recite the name of the bridge aloud and cross without needing a story about a fall to make you obedient. You will be the kind of elder who teaches with romance and not with terror when you are old. I like that kind.

Once, when your hands are the ones with rope burned into their lines and you have learned to hear a rib a day before it decides to cease being a rib and become a story, there will come a storm loomed off the sea with a core so cold and high it has forgotten ground. It will climb, and it will climb too far. The harps will sing themselves thin if it enters as it intends. I will take one of the thousand throats and stick my thumb lightly in it. I will drop the pitch of a great cable by less than a breath. The others will follow that new tooth in the song and find a safer arrangement. Your people will not even ask later why this green week was so loud and yet left nothing splintered that could not be mended with a night’s work. You will sit up under the amber faces of fungi that have moved their rooms an inch closer together for the hour at your request and drink cup-broth and squint at a child whose sleep has been good. You will think, this is what it is to be right-sized. You will be correct.

There are things I do not do on purpose though they happen when I pay attention. Hunters in other skies have names for cosmic favors—luck that tastes like timing, providence that feels like a hand just at the edge of the swirl. Here it manifests smaller, more honest. It is the moment when the net with weighted ends that would have been stolen by the Lamb-wind is instead folded by the Backhand and thrown home against a rib where an old man with slow knees can catch it. It is the difference between the scrape of a mat of spines making a whisper and the cut that would take a day to fester. If you like, you can call it my blessing. I do not need a temple for it.

You might ask, if I am benevolent, why I let any falls happen at all. Because this land decided not to fall by learning how to let go faster than stress can gather. Because your rules are built on experience and generosity does not mean deletion. Because a bridge with no names knotted into it is not respected, and disrespect is not a safe mode for wood. I choose careful mercies and leave justice to tension and slack and story. A good death has a place here, wrapped in resin-smoke and song. I will not steal it.

I do one more thing that you will never pin on me but that will travel in your teachings like a low thread.

In your list of wind-names—Knife, Lamb, Backhand, Mother, Chime—I add another. It is not new. It is simply one you did not need until you became as skilled as you are. It comes only when you have already listened twice. It is the small pause between correction and panic. It is the breath you take under a handhold when you have just felt the first move of what will be a failure in three months. You will call it Stay. Not in the sense of command, but in the sense a good rope has: it holds; it remembers. You will touch your cheek to a rib and greet Stay only when your ear has taught you to know it. It will save a few things that do not need to be lost. It will not interfere with what must pass.

I do not ask for names or thanks or even myth. If you must give me a place in one, make me the aunt in the old story about patience and angle. Let me be the one who took resin slow and clean and kept both hands free at the end of the day. Let me be a knot someone’s grandmother laid in a rope in a moment of distracted grace that held a cousin a lifetime later. Let me be a rule recited across a bridge: listen twice, move once. Let me be the twice.

And when your oldest men sit with pins of black heartwood in their hands and rub their thumbs over their own work the way men in stone countries rub the bowls of their pipes, when the wood ticks to itself under the last of the day’s heat and contracts along its grain and everyone who matters is close enough to touch someone who will die of the same winds and the same salvations, I will press my ear to your under-places the way a child does and see if I have done too much.

Most years my report to myself is a satisfaction with emptiness: nothing there I did not mean, nothing there I meant that turned prideful. On the year I am needed, it is never for more than an extra heartbeat in the sap. That is the largest size of my hand here.

What I do most is hold very still so that you can hear your own place. What I do next most is learn your language sufficient to hum a bar under it when you tire, and fall quiet when you catch your second wind. On rare days I cup my palm against the song and change a note by a finger’s width.

That is enough. That is plenty. That is almost nothing, and in a world that has decided not to fall, almost nothing is a form of love.
