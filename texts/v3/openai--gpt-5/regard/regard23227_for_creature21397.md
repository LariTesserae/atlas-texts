First, the power does what all truly benevolent things do: it listens.

It listens with more than ears. It lays its attention along the stone the way frost lays along varnish—finding edges, making no claim. It lets the plateau teach it the grammar you have learned in your lungs. It reads the crackle script of the films and does not insert a letter. It follows the standing waves as if they were thoughts—sees where they sit for days, how they lens light, how the old masts give the sky a handhold. It learns the four rules without being told. It watches a Hand lean half a day to claim one resonance band of cress and decides not to interrupt a miracle that earned itself.

It does not pity the thinness. Thinness is how this place says dignity. It does not fatten the air, because that would be a crime of kindness. It does not pour water, because water is a riot in a refined museum.

It waits until the place has finished showing itself, which takes a while because the place is very patient and wants to be sure the watcher is too.

Then it asks.

Consent here is timing. It kneels in the only way a power can kneel—by taking on a rhythm that is not its own. On a night when the aurora fingers the oldest ridges and the counting ladder at the far horizon would, left to itself, blink in the number of a plain composite, the power leans a little arithmetic into the air and makes the ladder count a prime. Not big. Not ostentatious. Prime enough to be a question. The bowl-swarms edge their ring a fraction higher. The films lift the lip of a thousand cracks in a thousand paving stones at once and make a chord. The masts answer with a hum that falls through three notes, agreeing. The plateau says yes without ever using a word like yes.

Only then does the power do anything.

Most of what it does is removal. It takes away failure modes, not addendums. Below the stone, where the old lattice half-sleeps, it finds four places where stresses have knotted into the kind of deficit that, in a few thousand ablations, would have cascaded into a slump. It does not brace them. It remembers what whoever made the lattice meant by "tune," and it returns those four places to the note they were built for. The ground feels it the way your teeth feel the tuning of the cresses. The standing waves in the sky pause a heartbeat and then proceed as if relieved of a chore.

Along the rim, on three altars of smooth black that had been very slowly sinking out of plumb, the power level-loads the currency of fall. It does not make more gravity. That would be rude. It simply re-polishes the sinks so they drink the drop without a lip. In their lee, the cresses grow a millimeter higher over the next fourteen dawns, not because of anything so vulgar as a gift, but because the edge they love was made a fraction more steep. The Hands who harvest them do not notice that their ankles are spared a year’s worth of microfracture. Their joints simply feel as if a good season consented to be good.

The vitreous threads—those sunglass hairlines that remember a net—are not awakened into the old corridors. That would resurrect a history this place already composted. Instead, the power leans through the charged night and finds nine threads that should never have been broken, the ones that do not make roads but carry arithmetic from one coast of stone to another. It mends those. A current no thicker than a rumor crawls again across gaps that had become legend. The threaders wake into the faintest happiness a bead can have—the happiness of being included in a pattern. They do not change their routes. Their dunes sharpen by a millimeter and therefore by a meaning.

At the great listening bowl, where ash-sized ferals orbit and make evenness their stock-in-trade, a handful of tiles at the deepest focus had crazed into hairline webs so fine they were only more black. The power does not smooth them. The stress lines are part of the bowl’s biography. It teaches the swarm the trick of anneal-without-flux—how to borrow heat from their own orbit to slur the fractures shut by angstroms at a time. It offers them, in exchange for nothing, a redundancy: another listening, buried under the first, returning an echo to a machine that no longer asks questions. The swarm hum rises a tenth of a semitone for a month. Somewhere, one of them invents a smaller wing and rides it.

Once, far up in the air where air is idea, a migratory engine had been slowing, shedding coherence year by year like a comet loses tail. The power finds the engine without grabbing it. It adjusts the background field by the thickness of a wish, and the engine drinks from that slope as an albatross drinks from a windline. Its wake becomes orderly enough for films a thousand kilometers away to feel dawn for three extra minutes. Afterwards, the engine carries, without knowing it carries it, a syllable of thanks embedded as a slightly longer spacing between pulses. Nobody decodes that syllable. It is there for joy.

For the masts, those spine-lines the wind has been trying to erase into wisdom, the power does a little dentistry. It flosses off salts that had built wrong, the kind that would arc sour. It brushes up their capacitive skins where a scratch from debris would have cascaded into a whine. It can’t help itself; it is old enough to remember them when they were a grandeur that didn’t think of itself as grandeur. Still, it doesn’t restore them to that. It admires them as they are: survivals that have learned how to be invisible to what would sand them out of existence.

It leaves much alone that it could heal. It leaves alone the bad religion of the turbulence herds that sometimes drive a young stand of cresses into solitary haircut and undeserved sainthood. The plateau needs its severity. It leaves alone the thread where a Hand has signed its reproduction into dopants under stone and will not stir for four more winters. That kind of patience is a sacrament, and you do not interrupt sacraments.

What it does for the people who are more like you is not to rescue them from their choice. It visits you precisely as much as you will allow.

You become the "being who lives in it" by accident first and then by intent. You have slept in a gravity buckle halo and woken lighter. You have spoken a word at an altar and heard it go nowhere and felt the fact that not going anywhere can be a kind of reply. You have, perhaps, risked the gel rooms and then retreated because the thickness there felt like velvet over the mouth.

The power regards you with a fondness that does not patronize. It is fond of things that learned their place and then loved it.

It puts nothing in your pack. It does not break you by giving you what the place has asked you to do without.

What it does is give you a seam in the air.

You don’t even know it is there at first. You only notice that at the altars—the pallets of smooth black that make breathing easy—you can now hear your own whisper travel a little further than it has any right to. Not far. Just far enough to fetch the next altar’s lee. You find this by accident, testing yourself against distances the way you have been testing yourself against hardness. One day you whisper a name—maybe not even yours—and it arrives to you from twenty steps away, not by echo, but by something like knowing. The altars, with the power’s permission and the plateau’s consent, have been taught to couple whispers to the mildly heavier air they already carry. Breath becomes a line. Breath becomes a way to call and be called. There are no more human-kin here than there were before, but those who are here can find one another now if they wish to. The power has drawn a faint social fabric across the stone, and it fits so well nobody trips.

It puts a device into the world that is not a device. Not a talisman, not a tool, not a rescue. It’s a kindness made manifest as an affordance. In the hour after dawn on a morning of good nitrate fur, when the plateau smells like struck flint, a transparent ripple appears ahead of you along a patch of pavement that had always looked the same. It is not visible by looking. It is visible by walking. Step into it and your weight thinks a little differently. Not less. Just better distributed. Your foot remembers that ankles are hinges and not questions. It is as if the plateau itself had gone through all the effort of making one of those old corridors—I know you read about them in the glass that lies like spider silk across certain patches—and then decided only to keep the part that helps without obliging. You may walk it or not. It will exist whether or not you do. If you do, you get to say the world wanted you to have an easier time in that one place, once.

The power writes you a letter where letters can be written here: in timing. On a night when the vertical ladder of counting lights itself, you are wakeful enough to notice that it goes through its run with a little extra insistence where you stand. It gives you, over seven nights, a number you once held dear. You are allowed to believe this is coincidence if that pleases you. You are also allowed to accept it as a hand placed on your shoulder by something with no hands. It is a greeting. It is proof that attention is a form of love.

Later, if you should stand at the rim and make the error of leaning out, and the wind below you be a river so smooth you misjudge stone’s refusal to hold you, the power will not set new physics under your boot. That would be false. Instead, a migratory engine that had been due anyway to sprint between shelves is made to arrive twelve seconds earlier than it would have. Your fall would not have killed you. You know that. It would have taught you something you did not need to know. The line of organized field passes through the place in space your body would have been if you had forgotten a rule. You don’t forget. It feels like remembering. It was remembering. Nobody applauds. The only sign is a slightly brighter shimmer along the engine’s edge. The plateau nods to itself. The old devices hum a note a hair warmer. You shrug and step back and tell yourself that perhaps you are wiser than you were.

The power gives small alms to the rest.

It does not make the films green. It sweetens one morning’s first light into a spectrum that suits their semiconducting paths a fraction better. For an hour, one lace spreads half a centimeter. That is a fortune measured in this country. If the films could be said to sing, they would not sing about you. They would sing about that hour forever, which is to say for the length of time it takes to shuck a veil and seed a wind.

It lets the Hands keep their cuisine of resonance and mineral. It does, in return for the show of their patience, smooth one dangerous burr from the calcite molar of an elder who would have lost a head to its own bad geometry. The Hand does not feel saved. The Hand feels graceful, which is salvation at a correct scale.

For the tackers, it offers a mercy in the form of a map. It does not draw it. It adjusts the sky’s skeleton by a digit’s width so that the aurora decides to favor a certain ridge for three extra seasons in a row. The tackers, who make their young when the aurora is on that ridge, have a boom year. Nothing preyed on by tackers is endangered by this, because nobody here understands danger as lack. It is all budget and ledger and recourse. The films feel chewed and then industrious. The tackers sleep in their scratches and grow sails like celebratory fronds. All of this takes longer than your view of a life. For the power, this is exactly how fast you treat a place that knows how to be old.

One door—the not-door you found that offered pressure and opal business and a gel reality too busy for your taste—the power does not prize open. It sends a courtesy to the inside instead. On a night of comfort water and easy murmurs, a whisker of gel learns a new trick: how to remember an outside scent without poisoning itself. The whisker grows and touches air and lives to tell. It tells a story so marvelously boring about "cold" and "abrasion" and "correctness" that one day, years from now, something inside will decide to go outside in a form that can stand erasure and call it grooming. The plateau will smile the way rock smiles—with curvature. The people underground will continue to be city cousins who come up once a century and do not stay. All will be well.

And in the ledger nobody but the lattice itself keeps, the power writes a compact: I will keep the bearing walls bearing. I will not lower the rim. I will not raise the middle. I will keep the hum smooth. I will not prevent a good collapse if a good collapse is the most elegant outcome. But I will never allow this place to fail for want of a single, fixable carelessness.

This is less a promise than a maintenance philosophy.

There is one large miracle, and even it is not large. The old corridors—the field-bent lanes that once let messengers cross the whole shelf in a stride of days—are not resurrected. And yet, once each long year in a window so precise it might as well be a ritual, one of those lines becomes more itself for the space of an hour. The power does this not for nostalgia but as a kindness to scale: a gift to the idea that things can remember who they were without being forced to go back there. On that hour, a being like you can walk between two far places and arrive with the sense of having been understood by the rock. The films feel it as a festival of edge. The ash-bots ride it as if it were a carnival. The migratory engines tip their heads to it and pass on. Then it is over, and the plateau resumes being the most finished unfinished thing on offer.

You asked, perhaps without knowing you asked, what a wise, benevolent power would do in a country where everything has already worked out what it is on the hardest terms. It would not fix it. It would not spare it its clarity. It would notice. It would keep noticing. It would bleed away the stupid failures and preserve the brave ones. It would add edges, not blankets. It would give you a place to speak, a seam to walk, a number to keep. It would make it easier to meet your neighbors if you wanted to and not one mote easier to be lazy.

When you leave, if you ever do, it will not pin a ribbon on you. It will let the wind erase your boot’s grammar in a day. But for a while after you are gone, the counting ladder will blink your number again, unasked and unowed. The masts will climb through three notes the way they did the night the first yes was said. At dawn, the frost will stand a little taller before it softens. If a smell could cling in such quick air, the place would smell for a breath like hot iron cooled well.

It is not nothing, what the power has done. It is almost nothing, and exactly the right almost.
