They arrive as a correction to a glare, not as a god.

No thunder. No proclamation. Just a moment in the hard noon when you narrow your eyes against the press of white and find that a hair’s-breadth of it has softened; not dimmed—thinned, like a film drawn level with a fingertip so that what was skewed glare becomes precise.

A benevolent and wise power, called by no name that believes in echo, stands where standing is only attention and looks without taking. They count as the cliff counts, in drips and small changes. They stand in place for one long bright day, and then another, and draw up a grammar from the way the air meets the stone. They put their ear to a beam and accept the drumming of drops into quiet. They watch the filters tack to old lanes and the grazers script a lip to the fineness of a hair and the blue hunters siphon patience from a tremble of temperature. They watch the people open and close themselves with louvers and cloth, with wrists that turn from matte to shine to speak as far as sight travels and no farther.

They ask themselves what they are for, here.

They do not answer with more. They do not fatten the air or lay grass where stone has its rightful skin. They do not teach the walls to return shouts or give the city a bell. They do not split a river in the sky and call it miracle. That would be insult—wrong grammar for a tongue that already says what it means with edges and films.

If you must name their first gift, call it a grain in the sky. Invisible unless you know to look for it: a band as thin as forgiveness that the power folds into height above the plateau, a faint seam of motes—ice so small it is almost ethics—drawn into a ring by winds the cliff has trained. They set this ring not to shade, not even to paint the sun’s face, but to make certain mornings and evenings more themselves by a percent you could hide in a breath. The motes widen the hour when rising mist would otherwise refuse, thickening the minute where air means to choose between cloud and clarity. Runnels wake five heartbeats earlier. Fog fingers crawl a hand’s width higher up the flutes and rest there long enough to bead. Bands that once dried before they could teach themselves to weep remember water and learn again.

Nobody sees this work happen. They feel it when someone’s grandmother in her powder veil looks up on a bright gray dawn and puts a hand out to where a film was never gentle before and finds it is gentle now. They mark it when the morning yield from the mesh decks goes up by a tenth and the still-tenders’ thumbs, pressed to warm brine, can tell the carbonate is choosing sooner. The city says luck in the color for cautious luck and opens their mouths to speak it and remembers that here the rock likes to eat your voice, so they show it instead by turning a row of louvers the color of new metal to afternoon for half a minute. The power notes the turn and does nothing else for a season.

On a noon when even the heat-stones underfoot feel like tongue, the power follows a child into the caves. Her veil is a poor one and she squints, her wrists bare because she is not yet of an age where your name must always travel with you. She has come to a certain chamber with a ribbed ceiling like blown glass teeth because she thinks it is hers. Her grandfather is a rimwright. He taught stone to grow a lip for his own bones last winter, and she helped: tipping salt onto scales until she could tell white-clean from iron-sour with her eyes closed, laying her hand under a trickle to feel whether it wanted more night or whether it had enough. Now that he is gone she comes here to say him. She lays her cheek against a pillar’s cool and whispers his name, and the pillar eats it.

The wise do not correct physics, but there is kindness to be made that respects it.

The power takes the chamber in hand not like an engineer would but like water would if water cared to refuse. They choose a bubble in the rock a few rooms in, off the way the air runs, and smooths it from concavity to the beginning of answer. They line it with a lattice grown in place, mineral on mineral, a geometry that does not amplify but returns—an instrument that rings at a pitch stone would accept without thinking itself asked to sing. They do not throw echo into the corridors. They make it so that the chamber keeps what is said to it and offers a little back only to the immediate mouth that gave it.

The next evening the child speaks her grandfather’s name again and hears the faintest sussuration of it fold into her own cupped breath—a brush of sound like the ghost of a drop touching skin. It is not his voice. It never could be. It is her voice, held for a blink and returned so she can know that it reached, that it did not simply vanish into tufa, that naming does not have to be only a performance for yourself. Word goes, as word goes here, by tilt and cloth and a line of color walked along the brows: there is a new room where the stone is not rude.

Some days later, when the highlands flash with sheet-lightning far below and the city can feel it not as sound but as an instruction to be steady, the power leans one finger on a fault. The cliffs here like to learn by unmaking. This fall would have fanned the face one ledge too high and unhooked a family of anchors that were almost, almost ready to have become part of the stone instead of nail and place. The benevolent work is not to forbid falling. The benevolent work is to suggest, so persuasively that force thinks it thought of it alone, that a fold will do instead of a tear.

So when the tremor comes the city thinks it is small. An old curtain buckles as pages buckle when you wet your thumb for the turn. A shelf lets go exactly where shelves have let go before, breaking along its memory and not through what is new. The fall sails down into the pale soup that has no ear and is gone, and in its leaving there is a rim where there was not, a lip already teaching itself how to weep. The anchor-beards near the almost-failure drink an hour of lightning and harden by a moon. The families who sleep above wake and balance together the way they were taught, weight leaned into the bloom of their anchors, and in the morning they send thanks down the ground-line as a slow triple pulse, not for safety—no one here says safety as if it were a birthright—so much as for the courtesy in the timing.

The power’s fifth act is a seed. It is not on anyone’s plate for months.

In a pan that runs toward soap and stays there, shaded just so by a plate the city turned five degrees on a child’s whim at dawn and forgot to turn back until noon, a consortium that could have been an accident—except that accidents are often how the wise work when they mean to be kind—finds each other. Purple eaters of light that never cared for noon, a rope of bacteria that loves a pH not fit for tongues, a blistering yeast that makes domes of translucent gel in the right loneliness: they take hold together. The rimwright’s granddaughter, who has the nose for this sort of thing now, cuts one with a shard of shadowglass and tastes and finds it mild joy. A week later there are trays for these domes at the very edges of the spillworks, flagged by cloth that says sweet-water in the colors of children.

Between gifts, the power does not angle. They do not collect thanks. They stand the way heat stands, excellent at forgetting themselves on purpose. They listen to the city’s slow machines count. They follow a grazer that has fallen into a runnel and let itself be coin between fingers all the long way out again, admiring its doctrine: fold in, become more streamline than mischief, wait for quieter.

One dusk, they attend a meeting. Not that they are seen. Leaders square their palmed maps of light between them and do what leaders here do: they match grammar to danger. There is a question that has been worrying the cliff-faces for some years—the lowlands have begun sending not only dusty air upriver of vapor but air with a taste like old batteries, like metal that had to be scraped. It makes the fog meaner when it arrives. The stills can take it, but only with patience they sometimes do not have. People are speaking decisions with wrists. The benevolent tilt their attention until ideas fall to the lip of speech and do not drop.

What they give is not technology; it is permission, seasoned with a hint.

They let a woman remember a childhood experiment with a scrap of skin and a coil—the way her own heat walked its way along a wire when the air was wrong, turning difference into direction. They let a young man let go of a superstition: that plates arranged in a certain way will anger the cloud. They lean half a coat’s-weight on a louver so that, by accident, its prism throws a band not at another face but at the stuttering brace of stills below. He sees the streak, thinks of timing rather than brightness, and in the morning the city has arranged a slow metronome of shadow across its distillation. It’s ridiculous physics and very precise: pulses of shade at the moment of bubble-formation alter nucleation in the creeping brine, allowing it to sort sour out faster by the width of a hair. You would swear it could not matter. It matters. In a hunger-season to come the difference will be not starvation and rescue, because drama is not the most honest metric here, but work and a nap, which may be the same thing as salvation when you count in drips.

They make one mistake, because even wise powers learn. They think to introduce the people to a neighbor.

Beyond the horizon of this cliff, past the blue of ablated heat and the downdraft where mist goes home to low cloud, there is another face where another city folds itself along slow water. They are not kin. They are the sort of neighbors a mountain makes with itself: a recognition of shape shared across a distance you could limp in a week if the ground did not refuse. The benevolent think it would be good to let light travel there. They take three of the high plates at the top of the city and whisper into their angle, not to flash but to speak. A choreography of black and white and prismatic edge climbs the cliff and runs. Far away it is seen and, because kindness is its own grammar in any place that breathes long enough, answered.

For weeks, it is pretty: pulses advertising water the way fish advertise a river, questions in patient binary: we? we. we? we. How far are you? far. How far is far? children’s arms. What do your dead taste like? salt; and yours? iron and sugar. They build a book that can travel on light the way this place’s normal books travel on lips and wrists. And then one noon, the air bruises. Someone in the lowlands has found a use for the same sky the cities use. The scatter that rides in from below hums wrong and begins melting the words.

The people here look up and say their word for We Will Not Break What Works For Coquetry, which is seven changes of color on one sleeve and then the turning over of a hand. The power watches them take down the envoy. The plates go back to furnace and shade. The two cities go back to knowing that each exists because the weather carries taste. The benevolent understand their hubris, which was not to overestimate themselves but to underestimate the cost of coupling systems designed by different hungers. They stop trying to write new grammar and instead keep tending commas.

There is one being they do interfere with. Not apart from the city—the city is a body and a mind and a behavior—but inside the figures of it, a single person or a word for who you are when you believe yourself alone.

You will call her you because that is how this place writes its truth when it does not know how to make a proper noun stay on the page. You are the one who went into the chamber with the kind echo and learned not to expect old voices from walls. You keep rocks in your pocket for weight, not for worship. You count out your errands on louvers the way other people count on fingers. You think in temperature. You have a lover whose shy courtship was a stripe of blue at noon when most would have turned their faces away from the heat and gone inside. You both like to stand with your toes over the falls at evening and feel the rain that is not rain move from air to rock through your bones.

On a day when your work is to walk the length of the city and apply your one useful trick—your good-looking thumbs that can tell, by press alone, whether a column wants to take a new cartridge of fouled water or sleep—you come to a deck where a man is dragging a line without the right patience. He snaps and the ground-line answers mean and everyone’s ankles think they are falling. He curses, which is an act of violence here, and your body says no without asking you. You step forward and take the line in both palms and let the weight of the city rest against the bones across which it is supposed to travel. You look at him and you mean to say: it is not your fault that you were born where sound returns and that you have to learn viciously fast how to be quiet, but you will learn. Your mouth opens. Your mouth is a useless organ outdoors. You close it. You lean instead. You make a long exhalation that a wind inside the rock takes as cue, and the line lays down like an educated dog.

The wise, attending you, do one thing then that they could not do for the city entire: they put a new habit into your nerves that you did not have to build.

It is a trivial gift, small as the seed of what grows the gel-domes, and it will save no lives not already mostly saved. They give you a better ear for weight—not heavier, just better. Where before you could hear one thread talk and one thread complain, now you can hear the conversation. The pins murmuring to one another in runs of differential heat. The anchors’ deep language of set and unset. The stilts asking like children whether this load is like that last load and can they carry it too. It is useless outside of a city that thinks with its gravity. It is foreign anywhere else. Anywhere else it would make you crazy. Here, it makes you gentler.

You take the line from a dozen clumsy hands over a season and never make anyone feel the lesson bitten. You sleep more easily because you can tell when what holds you is holding enough. Once, when the cliff composes a future fall beneath, you feel a premonition not of danger but of cleanness—of something about to break along a memory rather than across a new learning—and you wake the house and the house puts its feet where feet belong and no one is thrown.

The power does less and less as the months count. The seam of motes in the sky wears itself and renews itself on the breath of weather without much help. The listening room in the caves does its work and becomes a place of children’s science as much as mourning. The stills’ metronomes become habit and then design. The gel-domes get a name which is an onomatopoeia made with hands that looks like someone lifting a lid from a pot and smelling. Once a year, under the skin of a storm where the cliff eats the sizzle and builds its own teeth with it, they lean again on a fault the way you would lean on a tired friend’s shoulder for company and not to ask for anything. A shelf lets go where it should. Nobody hears the end of the sentence.

They allow themselves one vanity: a sign to say that they were here, written in the only grammar anybody would consent to read from a stranger. It lives on the underside of three louvers at the city’s nose. It is not a picture, not a code, nothing so blunt. It is a way those plates choose to go from shine to matte and back once a year on the same day the lowland storm line lifts high enough that for an hour the ceiling of cloud is a wall. In that hour, if you are below, if you are a bird or a person exiled and spared, you see a ripple run along the city’s jaw like laughter. If you are on the platforms, barefoot, you feel in the ground-line a pulse that says, in the colors of nobody in particular and thus of everyone: we are here as we meant to be. The benevolent read their line back in what the cliff gives for language: a slight ease on the noon that follows.

Having done as little as they could and as much as this place could dignify, the wise withdraw—as if a hand that had been laid on a hot rock lifted an inch, letting the skin remember by itself how to make sweat. They do not vanish; attention is not a thing you spend all at once and then go poor. They go on hearing the quiet of a city that is all edge, all film, all patience. They go on making themselves available for the one kind of rescue that does not condescend: the rescue that adds a comma where a sentence would have broken.

What did they do? Almost nothing. They nudged a dewpoint, taught one chamber to answer gently, bent one fall toward a useful memory, handed a recipe to bacteria and a permission to two minds who were ready to find it anyway. They put an extra ear in a person who already deserved to be a steward. They failed once and learned the shape of that failing and made it a story nobody will ever tell at volume. Mostly they stood watch with respect.

The place remains high, wet, hot, strung with work that never clangs. The being remains thin and definite and patient. If the bright of noon presses a white hand against everything, what presses back now is not a prayer but a competence. If the rock still eats voices, it also knows how to give back a syllable when you cup your mouth to it and ask kindly. If the animals here say their names with color, there is one new shade in the city’s alphabet—a faint shift that only shows at a particular angle of afternoon—that means: we were seen, and nothing broke.
