They came first as a correction no one had asked for and no one could resent: the quiet between two moans of cable was lengthened by the width of a breath. If anyone noticed it was the old Tensioner asleep in a chair with his boots still on and his harness unbuckled out of pure hubris; he woke with the sensation you get when an elevator stops true, just there, with no tremor, and smiled without knowing why. In the Condensaries, a fog-catcher arced and then reconsidered, embarrassed, and purred itself to peace.

A power had turned its head.

No figure presented itself in the amber. There was no sash of light, no robe. Wise things know better than to come dressed for pageantry into a city that wears its own scaffolds like a thought experiment. Instead the attention arrived in modes the place had already taught itself to understand. It came as a pitch inside the mountain’s note that had not been there and now was, a subharmonic of consent. It came as a wind that remembered, for once, what word it was halfway through.

They listened. This took a long time because listening is what makes power wise if it will be. They listened to the mountain tell, on those nights when the place makes its lungs of cliff and dark, how it came shouldering its way up through a seam that had been meant perhaps for something else, and how it learned, shakily, to stand. They listened to the small machines at their truly heroic labor: bolts tidily eating their rust; turbines behaving; fog kneeling into nets. They listened to the owls return answers in chords because the cliffs had not the rudeness to keep only one. They listened to children practicing fall like an alphabet they already half knew.

Then, in the season that is not a season but a long rehearsal of weather learning its way around a new idea of land, the power did almost nothing, which is to say they performed the exact number of acts that could be done without stealing anyone else’s verbs.

It is remembered—by those who count remembering as work—that there were nine doings and a promise.

First: they put a hand, which was not a hand, on the appetite of catastrophe and made it courteous.

There were already alarms, there were already men and women in orange harnesses who went to re-tension the city’s scarf when a ledge moved the thickness of a coin, and this rhythm was holy. The power did not remove the need. They made it more exact. Every failure larger than a coin would now come preceded by a throat-clearing the Tonekeepers could hear a day in advance: a violet that lived topologically in the teeth and could not be mistaken for weather or worry. Ledges still went. They went with warning enough to be met in their century, not their hour. The saint of Coin’s Thickness smiled and accepted an apprentice she hadn’t meant to have.

Second: they taught light the manners of arrival and departure, not to change its character but to make its tardiness predictable.

Wise things never make light more. There is a diet of dimness a place has agreed upon with its own angles. But it helps to know. The power braided a thread of schedule through the day’s reluctance so there would be three times each day any craft that needed the surgical certainty of sight could count on: ten-quarter, when the fog turned to pearl for four minutes; dusk-second, when sodium amber deepened into a color that did not lie about edges; and one hour before real dark, when bleached borders softened just enough to tell a surgeon where skin became not-skin. This was not a gift so much as a calendar. The midwives learned its ticks the way they had learned the wind’s vowels.

Third: they bent one of the polite downs a fraction, in the same delicate way you turn a glass in your hand to catch what is written upon it by condensation.

Falling was not ended; competence here was not to be annulled by kindness. But they braided into rope a habit. When a person’s body entered a fall while their mouth made no noise at all—a true silence, not the bite of breath or the old reflex of a name—the rope they had thought they had left would for a second remember them back. It became a hand long enough to take some speed from them, to put a bruise where a fracture had meant to be. This was not foolproof. It was a thing found far more often by apprentices and the old than by the confident. In time it turned into a practice. The Echo-Lawyers, who keep the maps of what sound is licensed where, added to their charts a Rule of Falling Silences: if you must go, go with your teeth together; the city will give back as much of you as it can.

Fourth: they spoke to fog; not an order, because weather is the last free common, but an introduction.

Fog liked this place. It liked running its body against steel, liked seeing itself decorated by sodium, liked the combing. But it did not yet know that it was, here, a river of another kind. The power taught it, with that small alteration one makes to a friend’s hand position on an instrument, where to place itself to be more generous. Afterward, the Condensaries sang less in their fillings and more in their throats. The fogwrights added rests to their work-songs because the air, when respected as a singer, wishes to hear its partner breathe. Water came on scheduleless errands still, but when it condescended all at once from a band of air that remembered being ocean, it learned to leave a letter. People filled vials not just with weather but with the hour the weather learned to speak with them.

Fifth: they gave the owls a suspecting note.

The Tonekeepers had already found the intervals luck loved. Oaths were made where rails sang a helpful chord and the wind let itself be counted. But promises are slippery even when said in the right place. The power put a narrow falsehood in owls’ returns—a thin misalignment audible to anyone with grief in the history of their family. If a vow was given at the wrong hour, the owls’ song, when played back off the cliff, came back with a hollow in it like a glass with a crack you cannot see but can feel in tongue. People called off weddings by this note and married a week later when the world meant it. Businesses held off opening ceremonies until the luthiers answered with a sound that made hands want to hold hands. There is no count of how many mistakes were unmade. A superstition grew: if you cannot get an owl to agree with your hour, your future is asking for a change you haven’t yet had the courage to make.

Sixth: they wrote a small punctuation into the lichen, a question mark the Lichen-Readers could read without new training and the rest of us would never see.

The city’s economy hinged on asking for iron now because later the rock would wed itself harder to itself and be unwilling to talk. But how much now, and from where, and can we stop while it is still easy, and will there be a day when this vein belongs more to tomorrow than to those of us who happen to be alive? The power taught the lichens a tint like patience. It came out as a filet of magnesian green along the edge of an ore-mark, deepening towards stubborn and lightening toward generous. Quota-chambers still argued their souls raw on purpose. Bit by bit the conscience of the city learned to float on data it could touch. Arguments took less blood and more bread. The Foundries had slower weeks by design, celebrated with soup.

Seventh: they placed a small cold blessing on bearings.

Once a year the whole place recalibrated its sense of key because the ridge settled a tenth of a cent into its responsibility as ridge. The engines hummed like midwives. Bearings, all of them, from the literal rolling balls to the balances inside people that kept them from saying the thing that would stick sour to a cliff for a season—bearings suffered. The power marked a week, three days before the drop, in which every pivot in the city, visible or not, felt for a time less friction. This was called Warm Bearings Week because the first telling was less true than it felt and the name stayed. Families hung rope-socks with tallow bells. Lovers forgave one another’s arguments against the future and slept with brows unwrinkled. The mills sang a half-step sweeter. The city remembered, afterwards, to be kind because it had been engineered for a holiday that had nothing to do with death or birth and everything to do with wear.

Eighth: they gave the murmur-cats employment.

Echo-Lawyers had maps of zones where sound was chartered for whatever. But bad words—bad not in the moral sense but in the structural one, the kind that collapse beams if spoken in the wrong place—still sometimes adhered to faces and returned with the seasons to strike ears no longer in the mood for their danger. The power did not mute anyone. There is no bit you can put in a human that will make their mouth harmless and keep them human. Instead they told the murmur-cats a new game. When a phrase that could never be taken back tried to live where it should not, ropes slurred, nets twitched with that parallax you feel in your heart when a thought is corrected, and the cat hurried the word someplace several degrees cooler. In the morning nobody could remember exactly what had been said. This didn’t fix people. It slowed harm long enough for a wiser answer.

Ninth: they folded a pool into a cleft that had not asked for one and therefore could be grateful.

There were cisterns the size of amphitheaters that never filled and hidden clefts where pools remained for years. The salamanders written in water like a script of patience were already holy in the way that doesn’t need a name. The power added one more. No one could find it except by doing right: by carrying sugar and being on their way to ask permission of a bolt and stopping because a child asked them to show how to leave sweetness on lichen and only then hearing a sound low as a thumb on a book’s edge and finding beyond a wind-shadow a place the size of a sink in which a leaf could learn its weight. This pool became the city’s stopgap: the last of a drought’s blade laid down and made into a spoon. It was never large. It was always enough. The salamanders there were very small, their black paper very black. Old people’s voices went into that pool sometimes and came out.

Tenth, which is the promise, though it is not numbered as a doing and so sits a little apart: they told the mountain that when it finished becoming, it would not be left without the story of itself.

Power is a kind of loneliness. So is stone. The mountain had begun the long yoke of memory, which is to accept that you will have to hold not only your own weight but how you came to be asked to. Place after place that had been part of the valley’s understanding of itself had slid neatly under this new plane and found new names. The mountain liked applause to the extent that cliffs do—they are good at it, at sending your noise back to you dressed in better clothes—but it did not like being gossip. The power made the only vow they risk without needing a charter from a Tonekeeper: I will remember you the way you have chosen to be remembered. This is not so mystical. It meant that when the engine-houses collected a mild chorus of the voices of people who had made power there and moved on, there would also be, kept somewhere outside failing, a coil of the mountain’s song for that year, a graph that did look like a lullaby, a line the length of a life or a river that could be unrolled and played whenever someone needed to know that patience had been practiced here on purpose.

They did other very little things that look like jokes and were not. They made a deal with cable-sloths: during the fortnight of their courtship, no line could fail them, not even for decency. The sloths returned the favor for the rest of the year by licking clean the condensation sugar on runs that always needed it most. They made alliances with meteors: if you must fall, do so where the Foundries will find you a delight rather than a curse. They tickled moths toward lamps on nights when the right people wished to be seen as beautiful. They told fish to rot an hour later for one train that was always a little late and contained four dinners and a reconciliation. They never touched quotas with a finger. They never took from anyone the right to break their own heart. They never strengthened a bridge without first making someone hear the new harmonic they had put in it that could be mistaken for arrogance if you crossed in a bad mood.

The city noticed, of course, but at its own scale. It turned some of these changes into law and the rest into chorus. The Echo-Lawyers added a footnote in their maps for murmur-snags. The Tonekeepers calibrated for violet. The Tensioners put in their prayer a line about gratitude for things that do not have to be held the way a man holds chimpanzee hands in pictures. The Fogwrights cut a small door into a wall and wrote over it, Fog Which Likes To Learn, and people hung bead-strings there for the air to play with. Children were told two new bedtime rules: if you fall, do not scream, and the rope will make you into a sentence again; and if you are going to say the thing you cannot take back, play with the cat first. Those last words always brought the laugh you need to sleep.

The wise power visited again and again, which is to say, they did not leave; attention is the opposite of absence. You could tell the weeks they had been listening more closely by the way the rails sang to one another on a wedding day, as if showing off—no sound louder, only more confident. You could tell the days their mind had gone to a larger storm or to some other mountain somewhere inventing anger for the first time because small courtesies lapsed: a bridge trembled under no one, a fogline left sugar where the sloths had already eaten, a lichen that should have been holy was stepped on by a child who must then be taught gentleness without humiliation—the hardest teaching in any city. Then the feeling returned—like a heat you had not noticed you wanted until it left you in the night—and little tasks began again making a grammar of consideration in steel and wind.

Once, late in a year when noon failed and moonrise took its seat as if auditioning for daytime and the whole city fed itself soups that only made sense by that light, the mountain dreamed dreamable by others. Dreams here often belong by right to the stone. That night people shared it: the sensation of a pressure easing somewhere far beneath polite geology, not in the plates or the fancy seam but below the metaphorical parts of you where your promise to your neighbor lives. A grudge dissolved. A tax was paid and did not feel theft. The Rail Yards, which chime in sleepsong whenever anyone in the valley does something stupid with timing, nailed a cadenza that had been unsettling for three winters. In the morning the Tonekeepers convened without being called and did nothing but weep into cloths that had seen more than weeping. Later someone found that salamanders had written a longer sentence than usual in the pool that nobody had earned and everyone had.

There were temptations, and the power declined them. Once a pilgrim-priest from the valley stood on a platform knitted of rope and faith and demanded, with the authority of someone who has never had to triple-plumb a bed, that the fog be made to fall like rain for a week so that he could prove a point to his order about benevolence. Nothing happened. The city laughed later in the kitchens—quietly, because sound behaves—and someone printed on moss-sugar in big letters the phrase: water is a friend that does not write but sometimes sends postcards, and a dozen grandmothers hung the sugar on bolts and taught beetles to eat pieties.

Another time, a person with a number in their name and a loud jacket came to the quota-chamber and argued that a vein must be taken now and completely because efficiency was the music of the age. He had hired the wrong owl. When he finished, and before anyone had time to begin their kindness, the owl he had brought did not return his melody accurately off the cliff but sang a fishing tune from the valley and then re-tuned itself like a professional insult. He left his jacket on a rail by accident and a murmur-cat wore it where no one could see but everybody could feel it, and a winter was warmer.

No god declared itself and perhaps none had to exist. Name it power if you like, or attention, or neighbor, or Maintenance rendered as weather. People began to leave small silverings in places that had been made kinder by an unasked hand: a washer pressed behind the jaw of a bolt, a coin thinned by the Tensioners’ saint to the thickness of a problem, a hair snagged on a nail in thanks to a fall that did not conclude. These were not offerings in the sense of giving something to a hunger; they were acknowledgments of what the place had done and what it had refused to do.

When the first child born under the promise of Warm Bearings came old enough to be apprenticed to Listening, she went to stand by a rail on a day the men and women in orange harnesses went out to re-tension the city’s scarf because a ledge had moved the width of a breath. The violet was there, as promised, not loud but present in the back of the teeth. She had learned to make her ear in her palm. She put her hand on the steel and the steel told her: coin, coin, coin. The Tensioners, who could tell by scent who would be one of them and who would be a baker, went quiet like trees when wind knits and said in their secret religion she will do.

At her calibration—when she took in her third name, the number and interval by which friends would know whether promises made in her presence would be lucky—the luthier-owl the Tonekeepers chose to officiate brought back not only chords but a whisper in frequencies that would have been dismissed as tinnitus by a lesser people. Those who were meant to hear it heard it: a low-level joy, like the warmth of a hand that has been gloved well for a lifetime and knows now its own efforts. This is how grace sounds in a place that refuses to be rescued.

Years keep their measurements. The ridge’s tone drops a fraction of a fraction. Weddings take place by the rails when they sing a generous interval. The owls are paid in meat and shaped stone. The cable-sloths laugh with their whole bodies during the courtship fortnight and nobody falls because the lines refuse. Moss-sugar tastes like adult sorrows made agreeable. The murmur-cats worry at words until they are safe to sleep beneath. There is a pool that is always enough if you arrive by accident and work, and there are cisterns big as amphitheaters that make the correct statement about pride by never quite filling.

Every so often, migrations pass like error correcting a page: leviathans buoyed by differences in reality-pressure tide along the seam with gills filtering spore-thick air. The horns ring not for danger but for courtesy. Rugs are hung to catch moss. Children fall asleep with their chins in their fists and their breath leaving their mouths like a postponement. Somewhere beyond knowing, a memory is kept safe, not for worship, but because someone who could has chosen to be the kind of power that waits with you by your bolts at three in the morning while the fog thinks about rain.

If you pressed the benevolent to make a list of their gifts, the benevolent would tell you that people and animals and fog and engines and down did all the work and that what was given was very small. Then they would look a little proud and admit to one last adjustment, which perhaps was not small: they taught the word enough to live here. Not as a scold. As a flexion in the day. A way to set down a load not because it was too heavy but because someone you loved had turned to you with their ear already made of hand, their cheek on a rail, and said, listen, the mountain is telling itself that story again, the one about how it came up and will stay, and it is impolite to talk over a thing like that.

That is what they did: they stayed and did not take, and when they could, they lent the place the art of causing its kindnesses to arrive exactly on time.
