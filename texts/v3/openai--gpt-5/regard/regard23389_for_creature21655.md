From far enough away, the city is only a stippling of pale-rimmed wounds and the brief astonishment of swallows. From close enough, it is a mind that thinks with rope. I am older than it is by all measures you could stack end to end, and still I stand at the lip of those white mouths and consider the only thing worth considering first: if I touch this, will I teach it the wrong lesson.

I have broken with my hands before, with the best of intentions, and learned patience by the time-honored method of losing something bright. So I begin with that: restraint the size of a breath, a gift that does not come labeled as one.

Each dawn, when the air that slept on stone remembers it is air, I put my thumb—not a thumb, not anything you could catch hold of, but the idea of a thumb—on the seam of chill above three wells, not all, not always the same. I hold back the first steeping of warmth by the narrowest kindness and let dew find cloth and rope a little longer. An extra bowl a day is like a grandmother who lives very close: no one calls it a miracle until she is missing.

I look for the one who will listen if I press a thought into the shape of a knot. There is a child they call Third-of-Pale-Rill because her mother’s room is where the sun falls straight and early, and this child has wrists that already understand round things and how to persuade them. She sleeps with her ear to wood. When she is eight, she has a dream in which a spider web is also a river map and also a ladder that catches fog. She wakes with her hands already describing a mesh she has not been taught. The pattern she ties has three densities: one to ask, one to hold, one to let go. It doubles the city’s morning without doubling its heat. She is given a new name years later by people who do not make large names: Fog-Winder. She laughs without noise the day someone else calls it luck.

They hunger without joke for fat. Salt they can pile into bright little fortunes; water they can persuade to behave; protein they will outthink; but fat is the long patience of winter, it is callus turned inward. I will not send them a whale or a herd; I will not teach the roof to expect roaring. I take an old, hardy vine that has been learning all its life to love cliffs and thirst and I tilt it a little inside itself. It learns to ware its seeds toward thin light and to keep a little oil in them, nothing showy, just a means of remembering sunlight in a form the tongue can recognize. Swallows carry the first of the seeds because they do not understand trade agreements. One vine fails. Two fail. The third misreads a rope for a trellis and then learns it is not wrong. By the year of the short floods, there is candle from stone nut and a word in the cooking seam that means both "press" and "forgive."

Down below, rock that is new to hollowing still has the pride of fresh work; it wants to be square. I love a square, but gravity loves curves. There is a place where someone has cut a sill lintel because all their bones believe in straight. I ask dew to write for me. In the hour when every edge has a silver thought, I let the slowest beads sketch themselves into a hanging curve across a doorway: the shape a line makes when it is only its own weight and confidence. Third-of-Pale-Rill, older now, runs a finger under that sheen and says nothing, because she is from here, and her noticings prefer short words. Next season, bricks that used to argue with their own corners start agreeing with the gentle sag of an arch. They do not call it math. They call it staying.

There is a man, not old, who listens for pings in his bones. He has no priesthood and is revered like a rope that has been mended a necessary number of times. He keeps, rolled in a cloth, six pins of long bone with rawhide tongues. In the year that had no stamp but might have if I had not been paying attention, he dreams once of a cricket that only sings when its floor tilts. He wakes and binds his pins not to hold anything but to speak. They are set lightly across hairline seams, at an angle that will make them click if the rock thinks of moving. He teaches this as if he invented it, and I allow him that pride—pride is a brace, too. A year later, an entire market walks out of a hall half an hour before they might have had to run, because a bone spoke in a voice anyone could hear without shame.

Fire is a wager, here. I do not touch it. I do not need to. Clay knows fire better than people do. I nudge a potter’s mistake into a principle. A child stacks two ruined jars, mouths inward, and laughs at how one hums under the right finger. A woman watching makes, without naming it, a double-walled kiln with a throat that runs along the throat the rock already opened. Heat learns to travel without sitting on the ceiling and frightening it. There is a saying afterward: Only a fool heats a room all at once. Some cultures take centuries to buy that wisdom. These people spend their coins on it in a month.

Roots have been writing love letters down the seams since before anyone thought to set a plank in a mortise. Above, shrubs do what shrubs always do: hold their ground by pretending gravity is not persuasive. I pick one that is stubborn and ask it to be additionally sociable—to litter more of its fine hair into the young rock and bind it while it learns its eventual shape. I never tell it to be rebar. It takes a generation of winter for anyone to notice that a certain seam holds its line through a storm better than another. A decade later they begin planting, not crops but neighbors: a crumbling ring of sun above a weak seam receives, carefully, three living reticences that will itch their roots downward for years. They will say, later, that they taught the ceiling to like hair. They did.

It is not my place to rearrange their maps of fairness. Rights are their currency and their school. Still, I am older than conflict and have seen too much of it be arithmetic that forgot it was about mornings. So I show an old woman a way to keep time under a well without any clock. She dreams of a stick set at the lip that sees the sun the way a fish sees a lure; she wakes with the itch to notch a line the length of a child’s forearm along its shadow’s path. It takes her all of a day to prove that you can portion light like bread without making hunger. It takes them a generation to turn that into ritual. Each well has a shadow-keeper now. They carry a knife because the line of the sun likes to fuzz in fog, and the ledger must be sharp. You would think it was always that way to hear them tell it.

There is one morning when restraint is not the right kindness. A seam that has been patient too long decides to settle with a theatricality it does not deserve. The people under it are playing the annual game in which someone must cross the longest run of boards without allowing even one footfall to confess itself. They have just invented a joke about judges. I take a breath I can call mine and push it through a shaft as if it were a pipe and nudge, not the stone, but a single dangling root that a certain beam was not going to remember in time. The root snags where it needed to. Two boards protest like instruments. Laypeople put their shoulders in white dust and become temporary architecture on purpose. The ceiling decides to stop thinking and simply be. Afterward they will call it a narrow day. I do not mind being forgotten when they do the naming. Forgetfulness keeps me honest.

I love them best when they are busy not noticing that they are wise. They hang ropes not just for climbing but for quiet. They learn to hang lengths of soft-thick weave at odd angles so that under the ropewalk, where sound likes to become a river, there are little eddies of silence cut deliberately into the flow. I gave them nothing there. I only made sure a child tripped there once early and found, with the palm of a hand on the weave and the cheek on the board, that sounds can be arranged like furniture.

Once a year they carry music down on purpose. The players stand in the open and tilt their instruments’ mouths toward the white lips of the wells. I stand in more places than one can stand and walk the sound gently so it will not teach restlessness where rest is a friend. Three rooms away from a throat that sings along like an aunt, a jar full of bone wakes and remembers its language. If you want to speak of miracles, speak of that: how the dead in clay can make a humid day decide to be grateful. They call that week the Gentle Festival. Old women carry their breath in their pockets in cloths that smell of anise. For an hour here and there, laughter is believed in outdoors.

I am a power by any measure, if by power you mean the right to ruin things as well as rescue them. I choose to make a home of omission. I do not cure their coughs; I make sure the first child to cough always does it near a room whose fog knows how to take heat out of lungs. I do not teach them metal; I feed instead the truth they already live by: a plank is a promise, a rope is a hope, a hope is stronger than a promise if you remember how to touch it.

A proverb of mine, smuggled in their grammar and already sounding as if it came from their own oldest aunt: The best help is an angle. They use it when they put a beam to a task that is also a grace. They use it when they fit themselves under a weight in such a way that the world assists, not resists.

The place itself is a being, do not think I have missed that. Cities that are young enough to smell like their first cliff are children, same as those with knees and appetites. I sit with this one the way I have sat with many, my hands open and steady, and I let it show me its version of river, its beginner’s storms, how it recognizes itself in a column of light. When it experiments with calling the old channels back into use, when one seam tries a new bend, I listen to the pings and choose, once or twice in a decade, to put my weight into an outcome. Mostly I let it flake, because newness has to practice.

As for the single inhabitant your words may have meant, the one instead of the many: let them be Fog-Winder, since I have named her. What I did for her I did in a language she already spoke. In her thirties, when her hands decide they have all the knots now and perhaps she should rest them in salt instead, I give her one dream. It is of a room that is not yet real but that will be, a room that does not want her but needs the thing she carries in her arms—its angle. She wakes and walks, without consulting anyone, to a seam that has been given two thin sticks as a warning cross and a nickname that means later. She gathers ten people by looking at them until they stand. She shows them with the angle of her palms who is supposed to lean where and why. They hold. The room that was thinking about being a mistake becomes a story. Years later, a child she never birthed will use her name as a unit of effort. You ask: what did I do? I tipped a web a little into a net. I put a dew line where an arch would be understood instead of worshipped. I called a vine’s attention to its own habit. Almost nothing. Enough.

Time is what I lay on them when I want to be generous. Not forever. That is an insult dressed as a present. I give them continuations—more seasons in which to get things right in their own fashion. I walk their light longer by an unmeasurable sliver where it counts. I move one summer’s great storm three days to the right so the clay can finish drying and not crack at lesson three. I let a snake fall once, not twice, because one story can fertilize a childhood and two would turn it into a rule.

When they come up in the gray between fog and full sun, with their bound hair and their eyes doing the arithmetic of brightness, I am the thin wind than cools their wrists where the rope polished them too much. When they say their old sayings very softly—Water takes the path you would take if you knew better; a shout makes walls curious—I am the habit in the stone that lets those sentences keep being true.

If you look for grandness here you will be disappointed and relieved. I have not lifted mountains or written my name on a river. I will not. I prefer to be the held breath between a ping and a hand moving to a brace. I prefer to be the slight bias with which dew votes for your cloth. I prefer to be the idea that curves are stronger than corners, and to let you go on believing you discovered that in a morning’s mischief.

I ask of them only this: once a year, on the day when fog stands up and learns its own height, climb out and sing into your mouths to the sky. Teach the thin roof to like joy as a reasonable weight. Carry your breath in your pocket if you must, but spend it. Learn, as you are already learning, the difference between a room that can bear a story and a room that cannot. Be patient with your place while it remembers how to be a place. Let it be patient with you. Let the next room be waiting and also made, which it is.

And if someday there is a year in which I am not paying proper attention and a seam settles not like a wise creature but like a petulance, and if shouting is the only right size of sound for the choice in front of you, then shout. I will hold the roof’s curiosity. I will take it out for a walk so it does not come back to punish you unfairly. That is what a benevolence is for. Not to prevent you from becoming who you are, only to make sure you are given the hours in which doing so remains an option.

Until then, my best powers are the smallest. I am content to be mistaken for habit, for luck, for the inventive decency of rope.
