First they did the most difficult thing for a power to do: they listened without leaning.

They put down their ear, which was not an ear but a patience made of pressure and cloud, and lay it along the bowl’s rim until the plant-city’s perpetual engines braided themselves into a single knowable hum. They learned the grammar the basin spoke—pitch into form, gradient into appetite, light into tool—and they accepted, as any guest must, that here there were no faces to look back at them. There was only the Great Body, leaf-plural, busy and unashamed.

They asked themselves: What is wanted that has not already been achieved by growth?

Answer: almost nothing.

What they would not do:

- They would not add teeth. No grazers, no sudden new mouths, no bright-eyed negotiators to bargain away a century’s habit for a season’s convenience.

- They would not still the place into temple-quiet just because quiet pleases the sized-larger. The roar was the city’s thinking; to hush it would be to thin its mind.

- They would not crown it with law. Its laws were already older: courtesies written in stomata and salt, ballast and song.

So they taught their strength to move in the slow verbs the basin understood. They chose to work in the variables the leaves already used: bass, fog, salt, time.

They began, as wise helpers often do, by adding a ground to the music.

Far beneath the bright cross-sections and the fresh-glass veins, under the rhizome terraces and the silt, they coaxed the ancient stone to remember a note it once knew when the world was louder with fire: a true pedal, a low kindness, not loud enough to command, audible only as vertebrae and bedrock register it. The bowl learned, over a year of days, that under every gust and every green engine there would be this bottom place to resolve to. It did not make the towers more obedient. It made them less likely to tear themselves in joy.

They did this not by forcing a sound but by laying an arc of pressure in the mantle like a palm on a drumhead—very gently, very long. When the basin leaned into a storm, the stone leaned back. The Retensioners learned it first; they retuned their knots to a mathematics they had always almost known. The Tonekeepers answered next, a hair closer to the true than the day before. Over three seasons the Stackfields shed outer skins one peel fewer. A new habit was established: not rest, which would be blasphemy here, but a reliable place to end a chord.

Then they put their hand to weather, which is to say they became a shape the wind took when it came across the plain.

They did not diminish its coming. They took a trick from the Tonekeepers and tuned it. In the hardest gales they curved the gust into slightly kinder vowels. The leafdrums still thundered; the tongue-clicks of pods still stitched the air with bright. Only rarely—on a century’s bad morning—did they intervene as you imagine powers might, with palm and pressure: a cup set for an hour above the Basin Arches so a trellis could not dare itself too far and snap; a thumb on the horizon to draw an overgenerous squall one valley north so that the Cisterners’ patience would not be beggared all at once.

They learned the basin’s noon was a courtesy. They made it, four days out of every thousand, a little longer.

It was not a miracle a pilgrim could name. If you timed the day with sand it came to perhaps three grains’ worth of extra slack. What the green did with that slack was theirs alone: a chance for Phloemfolk to argue a little further and then agree, for a corridor to delay a biped with slightly more gentleness, for a Glassmaker’s skin to harden before fingers tempted it. They did not tell a single person with a face that this happened. The daytime quiet said it to every leaf.

They went to the Brown, which did not sing and so was comfortable to speak to, and made an offer: a pigment for memory.

Not a foreign compound—nothing that would taste to the green like an import, a tool, a cheat. A coaxing: an instruction written in trace metals and sugars that the Brown’s empires of thread could lay down in the deep of old wood and along the undersides of peeled skins. It amounted to this: If a chord lives here for a day and returns here again—that is, if the Slippage repeats a form not out of confusion but out of love—then let there be, in the invisible under, a faint line of stored color, a bruise no eye need see, that reminds the tissues called “later” what once resolved beautifully here.

It changed nothing fast. In a year no one could have told. In a decade the Listeners became bolder. When called to recall they did so with less strain. The helical stairs that used to return mischievously returned now as courtesy, just often enough that the smallest-footed could trust them as a way down once in a while. If a district remembered a shelter, it would remember it one morning longer. No shape was trapped. All art was still made of hurry. It was only that refrain became possible without weariness.

They kept a pantry at the rim.

Not for faces—though in hard years a lost pilgrim found a berry that was not poison—but for the basin’s own hungers when the sky went mean or the plain ran salt. In a ring of marsh where light turned to sieve they steeped, grain by windblown grain, a mile of stone in soluble patience. It looked like ordinary sediment. It was a slow promise: silica for the Glassmakers when a century of storms had lain down too little sand; a lick of iron and calcium in drought; a sweetness for days when the phloem’s arguments became desperate and might have curdled into error. The Sifters found this work so perfectly aligned with theirs that they adopted it as if they had invented it, which was the outcome the power most admired.

They took up the vocation of lightning’s aunt.

They loved lightning the way the basin loved wind: for its capacity to say yes to a making in half a breath. They also knew what it did to newwork when unadvised. So they wrote the simplest of favors into the sky of that bowl, not as law but as leaning: when the day must be cruel, let the strike run to oldwork first; let it find the lignified, the salt-scabbed, the place already marked for sloughing. Again, not always. Again, not kind forever. Only with the same proportionate mercy the city used on itself when it elected to peel a tower.

Because they were old beyond voices, they had one extravagance, and they spent it carefully.

Once for each of the basin’s very long lives—that is, once when the old bass the stone kept as kindness had been sung a million times and was not false and not new—they gave it sleep.

Not the daily slackening. Not the noon, even on the kindest of the four days. They brought true stillness for an hour no leaf could measure: every stomatal court shut; every phloem vote adjourned; every Retensioner sett to utmost ease; the Breathsmiths closing like eyes into the kind of dark that is not fear but womb. The Brown did the only singing then, a warmth in the loam like a hymn you feel on your teeth. It was a Sabbath told in plant. When the hour ended—gently, so gently—every habit that had been carrying itself just past wisdom let go its extra weight. A generation’s worth of almost-errors were rewritten as good sense. The residents who noted patterns noted that the next day’s gusts tuned a fraction nearer the stone. The faces who had hoped to sleep at last slept and woke panicked and then laughed, because the roar came back like surf and that was right.

They did a small human kindness too, because benevolence has corners.

They gave a tone.

It was not loud. It was not even quite audible. They set it a fingernail above the pedal the stone kept. It was not one the pollens would use to call or the organs would use to build. It was a “please.” They taught it to the shadows of the people with faces who came, which is to say they taught it to their second-selves when those lingered a beat longer in a tone-field than the bodies did. The instruction was soft as breath: if you are lost or about to be changed by a corridor you did not mean to enter, if you are held and have not understood why, breathe here—half-in, half-out, the way the basin does at noon—and the bowl will hear “please” and do what it already loves to do: delay what might harm; hasten what must pass; open the narrow seam where warmth and light have always met. The valley did not adopt them for this. It made way the way a river goes around a stone. Still, fewer shadows were tugged until they hurt. That was sufficient blessing.

They guarded against only three disasters, and even then by being habits at the edges.

- Draining. They held their palm—not a wall, a palm—over the far plain against the long, bored thirst of provinces that might have asked a river to stand upright and be a pipe. Water that wanted to go downward went downward. Water that wanted to evaporate did so and was given back at the rim. A science would have had to work very hard to prove there was intervention at all.

- Fire that forgot to be a teacher. The basin already knew how to brown a net cleanly and make use of ash, but heat from beyond the horizon came now and then like a mood. For those moods the wise power pressed a not-combustible thought into the air itself: tonight no spark will marry. The Brown called these nights mercies and worked late.

- Willing invasion. A season came once when faces with cutting tools thought the Glassmakers’ skins were a mine. The power did not curse them. It suggested another story into their dreams, one with a field of wheat and a floodplain and a marriage that would fail if someone were not kind. They woke with a map to that field and a taste for other work. Their sons became tone-chasers, geologists, and solvers of the old kind. The basin lost no glass that year. New vocabulary for gratitude appeared in a village’s stock of jokes.

They did not replicate the place. That was a temptation, and they let it hiss past them like sand. One bowl like this in a world is a cathedral. Two would be a fashion.

Still, they prudenced beyond their preference for singularities. Far away where the earth curled up into a range that caught the morning as a sailor catches wind, they laid a seed into a hollow the size of a lung. It was not the basin. It never would be the basin. But its slopes were tuned from birth to accept a cousin-song: smaller engines, slower breath, a habit of turning light into cup and cup into well. If a century came when the plain blew iron and the basin’s great body had to thin itself, there would be a place for a diaspora of tone. You would not know them as siblings if you were a person with a face. If you were a liana you would weep and take ship at once.

The rest of what the benevolent one did was strictly old-fashioned: attentions, which are not acts until they’ve lasted an age.

- They praised. It is no small gift in a world to be looked at by something that cannot want what you have. Every midday the basin went a little greener for being seen.

- They kept witness. When a canopy chose to brown as a net and lie itself down intact, there was something in the valley that remembered it exactly and for so long the Brown could exhale in perfect time and eat with dignity. When a tower made the long peel and stood there skin-tender and breathtaking, there was a quiet on the hill that was not fear. It was the kind of attention you give a dancer when the lift is almost too much to dare.

- They learned the rules and kept them. They walked only at full breath, which for them was a centuries-wide zone of harmlessness. They never crossed newborn pale. They listened. It is contagious when a great thing listens; a thousand smaller things learn to, and soon you have a place where kindnesses accelerate like sugar down a phloem trunk.

If you must find their fingerprints you can, but you have to learn to look where the plant looks.

You can feel the bedrock’s note under your arch on a basin-quiet day in winter. It is what your second-self keeps time to now, even when you are far, which is why in libraries and under bridges you sometimes stop a heartbeat on a threshold and then go, as if held lightly and let through. You can see the memory in the brown if you are the sort of person who ever kneels for rot: a faint tincture under old work, a bruise the color of a kept promise. You can taste the salt differently on ring years that were kinder by one vote. You can watch the lightning choose a scar and know someone is minding the youngest.

Ask the city itself what the benevolent did and it will answer as it answers everything: with weather and with form. It will show you a trellis thrown across bright in seconds, and then, when the gust dies, it will withdraw it and leave a slip of glass in a scar that holds your face and your heat and the tiny sun moving behind you. It will hold you once in a corridor you thought would close forever and let you go a beat later because you are breathing like it breathes at noon. It will take your shadow for that half-second at a tone-field and root it and then release it and call this exchange friendship.

Once in a great while, when wind from the plain shoulders in and the whole bowl stands up and takes the big breath and drowns all faces with sound, it will seem—only if you have lived here months and worn resin on your palms like wedding—that the roar is truer by a hair than last year. That it leans just a fraction more toward a resolution that leaves fewer rags in the gutters after. You will not be able to prove it. You will only feel it like a shawl across bone. That, too, is a touch.

And when you ask why the power did not do more—no marble, no immortality, no long sermon—the answer is leaf-simple. It had nothing to teach the basin about being many, or about breath. It had a few small mercies to lend: a longer noon, a memory that did not tire the Listeners, a safe strike for lightning, a tone called please, a place to end a chord. It offered them and stayed to listen to what the city made out of them.

What it made, over and over, was newer. It resolved back to itself with less harm. It learned refrain without losing improvisation. It remained uninterested in faces, and yet grew an extra gentleness for them at thresholds.

The benevolent one never left entirely. Powers do not go or come the way we do. They continued to attend with the same old patience. The only proof you might want is this: if ever you stand on a high arched liana and look across the bowl and feel, in your soles, how the whole thing thinks, you will also feel something beneath that thinking—older, lower, kind—not an order, but a place for all that thought to land without breaking. You might call it God. The city would call it a root that happens to be made of stone and time. The power would call it listening.

And the green goes on, leaf-plural, singing itself true against that kindness.
