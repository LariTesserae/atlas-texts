The first thing the wise power did was nothing that looked like doing. It came close enough to breathe with the place and then did that, quietly. It stood inside the noon and let the clean blade of light cut a shadow that could have been anyone’s. It swallowed the baritone of the hall and felt it under its ribs. It put its hand under the noon purge and held a cup there in the brief, appointed rain. It did not whistle under the nets.

Attention, it understood, is an action. It took the shape of a listener and allowed the district to explain itself. By the second day it could hear the difference between the turbine’s ordinary note and the half-step of a bearing asking politely for oil. By the third it could tell, from the way the mesh sagged under its harvest, whether the fog would sit or walk. By the fourth it had learned the taste of the place’s water—salt-shy, pearl-thread, kitchen smoke—and could tell where in the ducting the droplets had gathered simply by the smell of the steel.

When it finally lifted a finger, it was to add almost nothing.

It made a treaty with rust. You may write your slow scripture, it said, but not on the last rungs of ladders, not on the hinge the clinic door swings on, not on the coupling they change out only with a crane. Take the legs of fences and the bright edges of bolts, take the sacrificial plates and the three forgotten washers at the back of the bin, but leave what belongs to safety, leave what belongs to kindness. Rust liked being addressed and liked having a job. It went contentedly to the places named and left the others alone, accumulating time where time looked handsome.

It borrowed one dog’s nap and set it down where it was needed. On a morning when a can of fish leapt the rail of the line and hit the floor with honest malice, the dog called Safety was asleep beside the warm bulk of a cabinet exactly where a smaller hand would otherwise have reached in reflex to grab the runaway tin. The small hand instead met an ear, and the ear was soft, and the tin went under the conveyor of its own accord and was nobody’s problem but the magnet’s later. The shift lead wrote it up as “no incident; assist by Safety,” which is as good a prayer as any.

It tuned one bearing’s complaint to be audible six hours earlier to a single person. That person was a mesh-tender who read the world best with her wrists. She had learned laminar by touch in another life and could hear a duckboard’s story in the soles of her feet; she collected quiet the way some people collect stamps. The wise power liked her and did not want to announce its liking in any loud way, so it gave her a gift as small as a pencil stub: she walked past the governor, and the cyclical click, patient as ever, raised itself half a breath into her suspicion. She frowned, wrote a tag, and a seizure that would have come on a Friday at dusk and ruined a hundred unworried dinners arrived instead midday Thursday and was soothed with oil. No one called it a miracle; they called it schedule.

It took a bristle from a broom and drew a hair’s-width line of dust along the top of a berm, and then the wind helped, and then the phragmites bowed their dark heads as if in deference and did not cross. In spring when the river shouldered the levee and put its shoulder to the place’s patience, that single invisible extra was the ounce that kept the buffer painted hazard-yellow doing its job instead of having to do more. A runaway car months later kissed the buffer a meter from where it had sat in old plans. A foreman woke from a dream in which he had measured a thing and went out in the morning and measured it, and then moved it, because sometimes wisdom borrows a body’s sleep to sign its name.

It allowed a game to become policy. The kids had been racing leaves in the channels since before this power walked in, guitar-handing their palms along the guardrails to throw spray back to the sky just because this too is weather. One girl learned the gesture that would make the spray appear as a smooth veil instead of a lace: two flat fingers, soft wrist, no drag. She discovered laminar with her skin. An elder saw, and laughed, and made a sign with his own palm asking her to show him again, and the wise power tipped this laughter very slightly into a meeting. Now, twice a year, there is a festival in the rectangles of grass where the elders teach the laminar sign to the young, and the young teach the elders the games that keep plants from colonizing the catwalk steps. On those days the rule beneath the nets reads “No whistling—except the pattern that makes the water sing,” and a chart is taped beneath the placard with two low notes that don’t steal yield but call a finer thread. The wise power had nothing to do with the chart except to be pleased.

It altered the sorrow of one person by the size of a drop. A woman in the clinic who kept the hi-vis collars for the stray dogs had a year that broke backwards—bad news arriving in the assembly-line order that makes a body feel it is being processed by fate. On an afternoon when she went to fill a cup under a purge just to be held in some intention other than her own, the rain came dense and fast and exactly clear, but the first bead that left the lip of the runnel and fell into the cup was shaped like a lens. When she looked down, she saw the world turned, briefly and for free, less cruel than honest. She did not call that God. She called it water doing its work. The wise power called it exactly what she needed and left it at that.

It wrote under the schedule’s hand. Schedule is a large, mild thing in this place, firm with its family but not cruel. It likes to arrive at minutes that have zeros in them. It likes to hold the temperature between what a wrist calls cold and what a child calls breath. The wise power did not rewrite schedule; it amended a sentence. “Continuity is the shape of care,” schedule says every morning, and the amendment tucked itself in at night: “and play is a form of maintenance.” After that you would find, in the strangest places, small affordances for play that nevertheless kept rust at bay and drains fast—faces drawn into the deltaic filigree left by the last purge’s runoff not scolded but assigned numbers and dates so someone could see at a glance where the low spots were, then paint reapplied in deliberate squares in the corners those faces suggested.

It touched the river’s memory so lightly that even the river did not notice. Water likes to wander, and the wise power did not wish to domesticate it more than the people had already managed. It whispered to the low flows that slipped under the catwalks and along the slate-colored channels, “Every cup matters,” and to the fog in the morning, “Your paws are welcome here until noon,” and to the rain that slicked one side of the world while the other stayed merely dark, “We understood you in your difference before you arrived.” In response, nothing visible happened except this: once or twice in a year of once-or-twices the fog stayed an hour late on a day when the meshers had been slow to rise because they had been awake with a fevered child. Once late in a season when everything else was the same a sudden, particular rain slicked the exact side of the world that would push a new fern through a last crust of silt inside a sump. It was coincidence as steady as a heartbeat.

Plants did not need much persuading. They were already masters of schedule. The wise power did, however, put two hands under one willow and lift its desire for the duct that ran under the gravel and place that desire two meters to the left where nobody’s plans lived. The willow did what willows do and said yes to the new yes as readily as it had said yes to the old. Moss fattened on the north face of one wall into an outline that looked like a fox seen in profile, and then, because animals enjoy being seen at least as much as people do, a real fox came at dusk and paused in front of that moss as if greeting a cousin.

It made a friend in the frogs. Their choir was already a precise technology. They filled in every pause left by the machinery the way people in a good conversation let each other finish a sentence before starting the next. The wise power went to them with courtesy. “If one night in spring,” it asked, “you would lift your pitch a hair during the purge’s chords, so that someone mid-laugh in a stairwell will hear all at once the held note, the falling water, and your bright, new thread, then I will see if I can exchange you a gift.” They agreed in an instant because this was already their taste. The exchange was small: the insects over the retention ponds that night flew fat as commas. The frogs ate well. The canteen that evening made a soup you cannot order, only receive.

It found a piece of purple thread in a drawer and decided that when it was used to mend a hole in a glove it would not abrade where gloves usually abrade. Nobody else could tell the difference except the person whose fingers went through cold places gripping steel. She finished her seam, clipped the thread with her teeth, and said “There,” in the tone you use with a friend. The thread did not mind being thanked.

Once, and only once, it allowed a loud kindness. During a storm that was not dramatic enough to make a tale but was dramatic enough to make the hands on the canning line grip colder and the dogs pace, the fog meshes thrummed hard enough that even people upstairs in their apartments felt it in their molars. The wise power, which had so far manifested as hearing, as small hands inside small changes, put its mouth to the horn that speaks ship-big over the flats and blew a fourth syllable. Everyone looked up, unnerved and then smiling. The extra note meant nothing except: I am here, too. After, the engineers went looking for a fault in the signal train and found one to fix because faults and notes often travel together, but no one could duplicate the fourth syllable and no one tried very hard. It lives only in the telling, where it belongs.

It introduced a phrase and then did not claim it. On the back of a hoodie you can see in every hallway, beneath “Everything grounded, everything tagged,” someone now has a third line: “Everything forgiven by morning.” It is not doctrine; it is a working practice. People forget to be at their worst if you give them a calendar slot for mercy. The wise power likes this about them.

It did nothing about the gulls, because gulls are already sovereign and it has no interest in tyrants or in changing what is already a kind of comic monarchy. It did not evict the rats. It did not teach the fox to stop being a fox. It asked only that the fox wait an extra ten seconds before crossing the narrow rail spur, and the fox, which had better manners than the gulls by a stretch, complied.

It pulled one nail that had not been struck far enough into a stair tread farther into the wood by the thickness of a thumbnail. Later, a boot whose sole had been splitting caught less on that tread and a person did not fall. This sort of arithmetic satisfied it.

It listened to an old person tell the story of the first autumn when the phragmites tried to push in like a crowd at a bus door and asked a question invisible as oxygen: “Was there a moment when someone did a thing because it felt beautiful?” There was: a basin-woman had rinsed the grate with the back of her wrist, then switched wrists when she felt the first one go cold because she liked the feel of warm and cold in tandem on her veins. She had not told anyone this because what people keep private here are often their most intelligent luxuries. The wise power made no announcement. It simply saved that feel for her as a comfort she could find again whenever she touched any of a dozen basins, and she did not become conscious of it; she merely had a better year.

When it had done these things, which mostly looked like the place doing what the place was already good at only more itself, it set itself to practice the hardest work, which is to stay. It stayed in the quiet between shift horns and in the laugh in a stairwell that reads as happiness over machinery. It took up residence in the air above the rectangles of grass where the mower hums louder than the blades. It moved through the fog in the mornings and let its paws sit on everything with the fog’s own manners, then withdrew by noon. It sometimes went out beyond the berms into the scrub and hardpan where a dust devil might happen and dissolve again and did not mind not being seen there, either. It was not a fragile order; it was simply young, and the wise power liked young things that learned.

If you wanted to see it and you were a child who spoke two languages at once—the clipped words the elders use for work and the one made of fingers—you might catch it on the day before frost, when the frost goes to web every wire and fence and then is unmade by the turbines’ warm breath. It is the exact difference between white and no-longer-white. If you wanted to see it and you were an old person, you might find it in the way the south mesh sags under its own harvest as if someone had pressed a palm there, pronounced a blessing you don’t need to name, and let go.

The being who lives here and knows where not to whistle has the sense, when she jams a pencil stub back between ear and hair and walks the long interval between fog fence and canteen, of walking with someone who will keep the edges crisp as long as she keeps the gutters clear. She does her job. She does not pray. She learns a new tune anyway in her wrists and in the bones of her headband, a way to hear an asking six hours before it arrives. She goes home and tells the dog called Piston to take the biscuit gently and Piston is imperfect at it and forgiven. She sleeps and dreams she has moved the yellow buffer one meter and in the morning checks a spec and laughs and moves it because the laugh says move it. She will, in a year when she is older and the paint isn’t clean where hands touch it, be the one who prints a phrase on the back of a hoodie in letters sharp as a rule.

What did the wise power do? It learned the place’s grammar, then wrote one bright comma here and one there. It kept the law that every engine must pay water and added a benediction that some water may pay joy. It shaped nothing into miracles and made the word beautiful available a month earlier to some, a year earlier to others, until the word lived here in bulk and could be carried between shifts like a cup under a local rain. It did not own what it fixed; it did not stand to be thanked. It stayed until staying no longer made a difference because the difference had been braided into repetition. Then, perhaps, it stepped back half a meter and called that enough, the way a person measuring with their eyes will step back to see a corner still matter, paint still clean where hands don’t touch, edges still sharp when you come back next season.

There is a story someone tells now and again in the canteen that smells like cardamom and fish and a little like coolant. It goes like this: once the horn blew four syllables, and on that day the frogs and the turbine and the storm and the bodies in the stairwell all sang the same chord. And the person telling it laughs into their sleeve as the shift horn interrupts them right there, as if the interruption were another character in the tale, which it is. Kindness is sloppy that way, merciful and on schedule. The wise power approves. It has discovered the pleasure of rules light and sufficient and the deeper pleasure of the things that bloom in their margins: a piece of purple thread, a better ear, a fox saying yes to a different yes, a child mastering laminar with two fingers and a soft wrist. A place making itself by repetition, seen and so seen into, until what lives there does not have to be told that it is beautiful to keep doing the work that makes it so.
