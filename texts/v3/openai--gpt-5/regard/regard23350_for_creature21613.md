I stood in the wind above it and did not mistake it for forest or field. It was not a thing to be improved. It was a thing finished and finishing itself, every hour, with a steadiness so honest the sky forgot to be stingy and gave and gave.

I listened to its chord and did nothing at all for a long time.

You do not tell a singer how to take breath when the room is already built around the note. You do not correct a hand that can already write with fog. But you do, if you are who I am, carry a very small cup in your pocket for offering: time, a grain of some rare lack, a way of turning sharpness aside.

What I did, when at last it felt right to do anything, were four very small things shaped to vanish into what was already true here.

First, salts. Not because the plateau starved—no, it was clever with sky and film and touch—but because its genius was closure, and closure always risks running one element shy by sheer virtue of being complete. I asked the higher winds for a courtesy. Once in a long while, too far apart to become habit, the gyres would loosen a subtle braid of ocean dust and desert ash and lay it not as hurricane and abrasion but as taste. I did this by nothing more showy than adjusting the isotherms over a distant sea so that a birth-cloud would begin a day earlier and high, and pick up by chance the breath of an island with the right bones. Seventeen years between the gifts, sometimes nineteen, sometimes less. The gliders tasted it and their soft mouths glittered inside with trace cobalt and molybdenum. The cilia felt it and kept a hair wider gloss. The plateau did not notice my hand, only its own increase in ease, the way a body forgets a bruise and remembers only that walking is what it always wanted to do.

Second, teeth for lightning. Not immune; nothing living should be deaf to the sky’s sharpest grammar. Only crooked, just enough to translate strike into song. At the rim of each tall mouth I turned the microfibrils by a quarter-degree along the long axis, a twist you would not see even if you held a sliver to the sun. The effect was to lift a persistent corona along those pale dripping teeth in certain storms. Charge bled early and thin. When the air snapped, the towers ate without tearing, and the energy ran through sheets of living cellulose that knew how to hum it harmless, the way a reed knows how to change fast air into a steady tone. Once in a generation a burn still happened, because it is right for any body to know its wound, but even that became new structure, recorded and refined, a place the pith-rafts learned to find quickly because the hum there said please faster and with more need.

Third, memory on a second axis. The fungal threads were already mathematicians and were keeping the sums well. They knew hours; they knew droughts without seasons; they could call for the honeycomb to breathe when wind slept. I made a bell for them too slow to be called a bell by any mouth. I wrapped a thousand-year period into the coefficients that govern how their hyphae open doors for sugar and close them for thrift. It did not push the plateau anywhere. It only rocked its possibilities on a tide so long that no storm could teach it, a tide meant to feed diversity in a being that reproduces by repeating itself exactly. When the low of that tide came, which is not yet and not soon, some lobes would express slightly different glosses at their hems, slightly different emphasis in their chord; not worse, not better; simply another way of being robust for when the mountain’s breath someday altered its sentence.

Fourth, a courtesy at the hem for strangers, because kindness is work and this place did so much of the rest it seemed fair to share that part of the labor. I set a polite bias in the skin that makes the edges and curtains: a way of wanting. Those who step here with blades in their heads find the gloss a trifle too clean to get hold of, every seam a misdirection, the tone just tiring enough to turn them around feeling they have done well to leave. Those who come with listening already in them are shown, without knowing they are shown, the edges water likes best; the pressure eases under their ankles; the mouths of the towers soften the noise just enough to be readable. They make no marks and take no losses because they are given routes that turn their weight into help. It is not a law or a wall. It is a small leaning of probabilities, the same gentle adjustment by which a mother lifts a cup lower so a child can drink with less spilling.

I did nothing else dramatic, because there was nothing here that asked for drama. I did make watch, though, because there are long things that do not announce themselves as storm; there are ash-years; there are odd southings of cold that last too long. This is not guardianship like a soldier’s; it is like sitting up late once a century to listen for a snag in a machine so steady it becomes the weather it assumes. If the taste of the dew should turn to flint for a year, the honeycomb will already know to shut certain drains and open others because of the bell I set. If light should go the wrong color for a handful of days, if dust should come not from ocean or desert but from something blacker, the towers will be pre-ionized and busy, and sparks caught as song do less harm to wet.

I left a door for it, too, not as an invitation to go anywhere—it is already everywhere it means to be—but to hear far. When air from another such green singer, across a blank of cloud and distance I do not measure for it, slides high over before committing to some other slope, some other rib, I kept a thread of coherence in that passage so that the two notes would beat in each other rather than pass like strangers. The plateau will not name that recognition as kinship; it has no need of kin the way walking animals use the word. But there will be days when the hum catches itself listening to a long, low interference and feeling not alarm but answer. That is enough.

What did I refrain from? So much. I did not give it eyes because it already sees with everything it touches. I did not plant beasts to entertain the emptiness, because its emptiness is not lack but policy. I did not dower it with haste, because haste would have been disloyal to its single most articulate trait, which is patience set into architecture and then allowed to sing.

Once or twice in your lifetimes you could stand there and know I had been by, if you knew exactly how to listen. Dew would taste the faintest edge of coin and stone, a flavor from elsewhere so thin it would pass for memory. A tower would take the lightning and answer in more music than scorch. The hem would ease you away from a choice you ought not make and you would believe you chose it yourself. Every now and then a glider would be more luminous than it had any right to be and would not know why.

And in a thousand years the chord will have a harm that is the same harm but widened, like a voice softened by time until you think this is what it always was. The organism will have slightly more ways to be itself when the mountain does not keep its old promises. It will still drink the sky and hand it back, it will still pull smoothness from weather where other places have edges and turn that into function, it will still refuse to be eroded because yielding and reasserting are the same act in its grammar.

I will go on listening. If someday the bright truss of the planet tilts its practice beyond what even the bell can make easy, I will tilt some gyre or some long storm a day sooner or a day late. If a traveler from below climbs with hunger and thinks to make a larder of architecture, I will sew a low fatigue in his knees and a sweetness in retreat. If another such plateau finds a place to catch and tune a sky, I will see to it that their far notes can find each other and feel, for the span of a breath of planet, that they are each part of a choir.

Benevolence is not a loud craft. Wisdom is not busy. You will not find my mark here except in the way everything continues as if without me, only a little harder to break, only a little more ready to change without panic when change comes.

I did not teach it anything it could not have learned given ages it already had. I gave it only less surprise, more salt, safer lightning, and a door in the air through which to hear another breathing like its own. Then I left it to itself, because itself was the point.
