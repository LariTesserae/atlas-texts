The power arrived the way a long swell arrives: no crest, no shout, only a shape that kept not-ending. It came down through pewter and glass and green and took the measure of what already held. It tasted the falls and let the taste lay a map along its own vast tongue. It pressed its palm to the ridge and heard the small tones in the stone that say I am new, I am cooling, I am learning my own weight. It watched rope speak to rope and palms speak to palms, and nets teach themselves by sagging.

Because it was wise, it began with vows. The first: no gift that asks them to become not-themselves. The second: no help that arrives faster than their slowest courage. The third: no turning of a rule that is bedrock here—no flame, no loudness, no upward summons. The fourth: where I can, I will make mercy take the same shape as their labor, so that if they tell stories about me later they can also tell them about themselves.

It did not send fish. It did not glass the water into clearer glass. It did not untie gravity’s suggestion. It did not put a wheel on a pole and ask anyone to spin.

What it did was small and long.

1. It loosened one silence in the rock.

There was already a voice in the ridge—a thin choir in basalt that sang when plates shrank and when they wanted to slip. The power did not make a new song; it asked the stone for an earlier verse.

Under the oldest fin, where the city’s slow procession of baskets passes like thoughts up and down a spine, it lay a seam of crystal as large as two sleeping bodies and as thin as a memory of ice. It placed that seam in the company of pressures that would touch it only the day before a letting-go. It taught the seam to say only once, only softly, with the timbre of a stone bowl tapped from a room away.

On a gray-day late in a lightning season, an old child with a jawbone tied to her brow shivered and stopped and pressed her face to the fin. She brought her wrists together and made the sign for Listen. The hall-hum quieted. Far beneath, the gentle bowl-sound arrived: one, and then—like a second thought—one again. Something in the net rose up in every palm. The rock-cutters came home slow and were already waiting by the ledge when a thousand knives tumbled into a darker pocket. No one was on the stair. Afterwards, there was a new hook in the law-ropes, a new reading: the Seam’s Say. The children learned to hear for it or to hold a hand for the ones who could.

The power did nothing else with sound. It was enough to make the ridge speak a little earlier than pain.

2. It taught the water one new kindness and made it forget it between needs.

Across the high crest, the haloclines slid—traveling glass inside green. In storm-rich weeks, they multiplied and snapped. In lean days, they ran thin and far between like sparrows in winter. The power put no engine in them. It only braided a very small habit into the place where sweet slips against salt.

It asked the interface to feel for three things gathered together: the purple-sour taste of blood in a mouth just cut; a scatter in the rhythm of a gill-frill pressed flat against the chest by a belt too heavy; a rope-line plucked twice in fear and then gone still. When the three arrived at once and only then, a skin formed—the smallest thickening, the barest clutch of fresh at the palm. It lasted the length of six slow breaths. It pressed gently against faces like a mother’s hand in the night. It made bubbles choose up-angle. It made edges slightly not-edges. And then it forgot again because forgetting was part of the vow.

For a while no one knew it was there. Then a novice, overstoned at the hips, fumbled a catch above a knife of rock, and the water did a thing no one expected water could do: it met them and laid them back into the net they had left. There was ceremony later, of course—nobody alive here lets a new behavior go unbraided. The midwives tied one more small cord at child-waists: a thank-you-length, light and bright, to be fingered in gratitude after a scare. They learned to call the thickening the Third Hand. They did not call it a god. They called it a lesson the veils had taught themselves. The power smiled in the slow way that big things smile: by becoming even less noticeable.

3. It put a knot into someone’s sleep.

Knots were their law and their memory and their politeness. The power did not invent a knot and drop it like a fish. It walked a long hour beside a rope-maker while she dreamed after a day of hemming a hall to the warm sigh of a vent. It laid a notion against her palm where her nerves made their own rope-lattice and let the notion wait there the way freshwater waits in a fold.

In the morning the woman woke with a small itch in her fingers. She took an offcut and tried a hitch she could not remember having seen. It made a sleeve around a standing line. It never gripped so cruelly that the fiber would die under it. Under a slow pull it settled deeper and cleaner. Under a shock it flowed a single finger-length and turned that shock into song you could feel down the line. To untie it you had to be calm. It came apart only to patience.

In their tongues the hitch got half a dozen names: the Mercy Bend; the Lightning-Season Hitch; the Old-Lover’s Hold; the Slack-For-Children. It moved through the city like a joke everyone liked retelling. It made span after span survive the day a fall jumped its anchors. It showed up in law cords where there had been angry knots before. Some of them said later that the gods had given it; more of them said the water had taught a worker in her sleep. They added a line to the rope-books: Slack is a form of love. If pressed, the power would have said the same.

4. It pouched a messenger and sent it back into a story they already had.

Once in a long while a blue-veined disk rose through the warmer drafts and was a window for a season and then was gone. The power did not make a new creature. It found the one who came anyway and asked only: Will you be my drum-skin for a time? The jelly knew about bearing messages; it combed light at night and tallied years for children; it said calendar and cousin and omen all with one slow billow. It accepted.

The power set a lattice inside the bell—a thing like a net but not a net, made of differences instead of fiber. It asked the lattice to taste pressure changes and to remember them in a way that a body like a hand could read from a net. Then, in the weeks before the jelly climbed, it went to the other ridge-cities and listened in those nets, too. It learned what they were proud of and where the bladders were bursting and whose law-cord had gotten too tight. When the bell finally rose and passed close, the pressure-lines along its skin told a poetry that a rope could feel and carry, a long ripple-stanza that arrived in wrists the way a storm’s foot arrives in bone.

The message was not a treaty. It was not a warning in capital letters. It was the news of craft. It was a trick for growing chalk ribs with fewer failures. It was a song for a vent-hall that answered only if you pressed the same catenary with three fingers in a certain slowness. It was the tale of a child who had laughed in the Third Hand at the last moment and made the old ones cry later because laughter and mercy were in the same breath and that was worth saying out loud. The rope-speech that came out of that passing bell braided neighborhoods together without ever teaching them a politics that could drown them. The jelly pulsed on, heavier with meaning, toward a dark it had always gone to anyway.

5. It made an agreement with the dead.

The people already sent their dead down. They loosed knots. They tied bodies careful to the step not yet cut. The power could not love that and refuse it. It did not bring the bodies back up. It did not make the black below into a place that was not black.

It only sweetened a single chemistry by a thread.

On the faces of the steps-just-begun, it coaxed the silver threads—the chemic-eaters—into laying their chalk with a new small appetite when they tasted the salts that the dead give back. It taught them to spin a lip—a calm, pale shelf as thin as a word—where a foot would want one next year. It did not hurry the work. It used the time the ridge had, which was a great deal. After a few seasons the rock-cutters found, now and then, a place where a curve had arrived that no water would have made by itself. They knew gratitude when they put their hand on it. They added to the funerals a touch with the thumb along the brow of the one going down: Go first, go make a thing we need that only you can weight into being. When they reached those ledges later, they did not speak loudly. They did not have to.

6. It gave the vents a longer mood.

The warm places were hearths. Vents sighed with flavors and made life possible and un-sore. But the ridge was young and young rock is a fidget. A vent that loved a hall might move two body-lengths down a crack in a year and turn an elder’s cartilage mean with cold. The power loved elders because they carried entire rooms in their wrists. It did not glue vents in place; it tucked a baffle in the underlying stone, a knuckle in the flow. It was nothing more than a persuasion—a pinch so gentle the vent would only feel it after wanting, on its own, to wander.

For twenty years at a time, nets hummed warm at the same tone. Elders forgot a kind of ache. A midwife touched the flank of an old woman and felt springs that did not fight back. There were still cold seasons. The baffle only lengthened moods; it did not outlaw weather. When the temperature fell and joints had to be wrapped with resin and knives warmed in breath, the old ones said, This too is a rule and sat in a room that hummed the way a remembered song hums even when you cannot hear it.

7. It gave them back their own thought, a little sooner than fear would have.

The city had a hard place in it: the fin cut with stairs that dropped into ink. It was a shared ambition and a communal dread. The power respected both. It did not send light down the throat. It did not pour clear air into the place that had none.

What it did was change a single season’s saltiness by less than a tastebud would notice and only every twelfth year. In those years, the veils fell in a way that made the lifts in the city’s procession more obedient to buoyancy. Baskets rose a finger quicker when asked and fell a finger slower when forgiven. The whole city developed a habit those years of thinking one more step ahead about weight without even noticing they had done so. On such a twelfth year, halfway down the fin, a young cutter invented a way of lashing counterweights in conversation: two stones timed to speak to each other across a breath, so that a sudden sag in one place created a small gladness, an upward yes, somewhere else. That yes was enough to finish a new measure of steps before lightning-season thickened and the work had to come home. She named the arrangement for her sister and went to sleep in warm nets and dreamed nothing special. Her work had been obvious. That is what the power wanted it to feel like.

—

It did other things you could not tell from miracles.

It caught a storm in a wide hand once and notched out two teeth from its lightning so that the falls would not glitter to madness that year and choke the gullies in their joy. It put a steady itch at the base of the thumb in the hand of a person whose rope had been tied in anger and made them untie it and try again with their jaw unclenched, and when asked later why they had redone the knot they said, It was ugly. That is what ugliness is for, they said in their nets on law-day, and everyone laughed until a long swell pressed them against each other and turned the laugh into a hum. It angled a shoal of small lives through and away when it would have been a bad day to discover what a swarm can do to a veil. It leaned over the ridge and listened as if it was learning a language and it was.

Most of the time it did not intervene. Most of the time it lay a long palm along the water and let the children learn the taste of a first-veil day and the sweetness of a plant boiled in baskets until their tongues ached with minerals. It watched a pair of lovers tie themselves together for a shift and carry each other’s weight in the most literal way and did not add anything to that. It watched the elders hum warmer and went elsewhere the way you leave a room where no one needs you just then.

In a century, almost no one spoke of a benevolence from Outside. The rope-books grew heavy and were wrung clean in sweet and put back on their hooks with an extra cord for the Mercy Bend and a small bright one that children touched when they remembered laughing in the Third Hand. The jelly was still a messenger, but now its bells said also an art to tune a hall and a trick with chalk you could teach with your thumbs.

Once, a person angry with grief shouted into a fall that the place did not love them back. The power understood. The fall heard and did not change taste. The grief worked itself through in warm rooms over the length of three storms and came out the other side tied with a knot you could untie only if you were calm. That was not intervention; that was time and company. The power has never tried to make itself that.

At the Settling Hour, from a distance, the ridge’s ribs seemed braced with straps where fingers had been and nets threw shadows you could drink with your eyes. The long procession of bladders went up, went down, told the time. The seams in stone spoke one day earlier than they used to. The stairs were a rung lower and the lip there was kind. None of these things glittered like miracles. They felt like a place instructing itself.

If you insist on a single act you could point to and say, Here: here is where a god happened, choose this: a day of too-many storms in the upworld, the falls tasting of coins, the greens ready to grow themselves into choke. The power lifted its hand up where the sky boils and dragged two fingers along a line only it could feel, and the curtains came down not as a wall but as threads. The sweetness was the same sweetness, but it arrived in rooms and lanes rather than in one wild street. The people hung more baskets in those lanes and caught the season’s glut in patience the way they catch anything. They told themselves, We learned from last time, which was almost true. It did not correct them.

After, the power lay back down in the thickening dark beneath the ridge and listened to the intention that could be called human if you like move through fiber and water and rock. It practiced not being seen. It held all its future gifts cupped like small fish and let them swim where they wanted, which was mostly away.

It is a benevolence to want to do very little. It is a wisdom to know what little to do.

The ridge will learn. They have hands. They have ways to read each other through rope. They have enough air around them to make patience the only speed. When they reach the next ledge, the dead will already have made a step there. When they make a law of gentleness and tie it with a hitch that yields under anger but not under love, they will say, This is ours. They will be right.
