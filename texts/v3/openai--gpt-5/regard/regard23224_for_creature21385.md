It begins with listening, because anything that has lived long inside its own work deserves to be overheard before it is helped.

The attention that finds Bay Seventeen is not a spotlight, not a bright hand that lifts and rearranges. It is a pressure shift, like the air before rain. It arrives without edges. It lays itself along the bark and under the belts the way fog lays itself along the ribs of a hill, and it gives the place the courtesy of meeting it in its own language: weight sensed at joints, heat at bearings, water counted in patient drops, compliance learned from cambium.

The power takes a first, long breath and hears the shape of the thrum the way old doctors hear the shape of a heart. The big pump’s pace is good. The belts hum in second harmonics. The governor fruits spin like calm decisions. There is a tiredness in one bearing block on the north side, honest—no more than any job with metal in it will admit by evening—and a faint hitch at low tide when the intake stirs silt fat as cream and the pushrod takes a breath before the thud. Nothing here is wrong in the sense that calls for spectacle. Everything is right in the sense that asks not to be knocked out of its own timing.

The temptation, which the wise know best because they have resisted it often, is to make it easy. To lighten the wheel, to accelerate the belts, to refine the water so the tree tastes nothing brackish ever again. But ease is not always kindness. Ease is what makes hands forget. Ease erases ledgers and songs.

So the power does almost nothing, and in the almost is everything it has to offer.

It puts its ear to the columns the way children do and hears the relief three bays down before the bay itself does and thinks, Yes, you have your messengers. Let me tune only the paths they travel. The oak-brown bark is a good conductor of rumor, but there is a scarf of dead tissue in one rib where a collar choked wrong for a wet season, twenty-two years ago by the date on the plate with the tree’s curious eyebrow of bark. The power finds the ridge of that old mistake and, without smoothing it, teaches the echoes how to go around. There is no miracle in it. Next week, an apprentice with a screwdriver to their teeth recognizes a warning half a breath sooner and walks toward a fan before it screams.

In the marsh beyond the canopy, where sky is served in the canal like soup, the power kneels (if kneeling is what you call the way gravity pays attention to water) and presses one knuckle into the silt. It is a small exhalation of persuasion. Silt is almost a mind if you address it right. Where the intake mouth chews river brown, a seam of fines splits more readily to the left than to the right for a handful of bright days each season now. The flood will still come up to wash the lower grating clean—this place needs its washings—but some of the suddenness will come off it. Men and women will still stand with unhurried hands on valves. The flood will still teach patience. The power wants only to pull catastrophe’s teeth a little blunter.

To the tree, which is a parliament of green and wood that holds opinions in rings, the power does not suggest virtue; the tree has enough. It offers options. In the dark, wet factories of cambium, where sugars talk in chemistry and pressure, the power sketches an alternate habit. Along one quadrant of the northwest column where a child’s chalk mark of a pump is all wheel and no casing at knee height, wood will lay down cells a whisper denser for three weeks out of each late-summer. No one who is not counting tree-years will notice. In forty years, a secondary bearer will present itself exactly where someone serious would have asked for it by then. The graftwrights who will teach it its work have not yet been born. The power gives them something to say yes to when the day comes.

Where condensate rattles clean chains and catches in small polished grooves and jumps in bullet runs into flumes cut in leaf midribs edged with nickel so they do not tear, the power lays down a seed of a lichen that likes to live on the underside of nickel where it meets plant. It is not decoration. Its pale plates will grow thin and even, muffling a faint electrochemical argument between metal and green that would have become a mischief in a decade. The lichen eats arguments. It writes its treaties in grip.

At the base of a column where handwheels the color of bad eggshell stick off the bark like stubborn flowers and ceramic saddles hold copper that used to be liana and now is only pretending plant, the power leans against the idea of clearance and finds it proud. The insulation-priests are right to murmur the way bakers murmur. The distance between live and leaf is good today; a season from now it would have been a fat finger’s width too close. In the big ledger, that risk is not an event this year—perhaps not the next—but the power is here now. It sets three dreams moving through the sleeping city like water through capillaries.

One dream goes to a woman who scrapes chalky accretions off pipes with the curved, quiet tool. In it, she counts brass pins in a line that marks a drift, but one pin winks and remembers her grandmother’s song in a high, sharp way like the thin fan’s soprano. She wakes with the urge to check clearances on taproots no one has looked at since the last wet. No deity stands at her bedside. She brings a stool and a mirror and a bundle of soft cloths and a youngster to hold the light, and she turns handwheels with hands that know how not to force. The bark sighs a little around every moved hardware. The copper sits back into comfort. Later she goes to the old people’s gallery and says I had a feeling and they nod like of course you did; the bay tells its business to those who listen.

A second dream goes to a wirehand boy who has never yet hauled copper along a run that refuses corners. In it, the bay is a house that does two contradictory things, which he recognizes instantly because he lives in a place where the ground floats and eats nails and grows around bolts and yet holds machines as precise as clocks: the house breathes in and does not crush anything; it breathes out and does not drop anyone. This is a lesson most of Seventeen’s people have learned in their ankles and their grip. He wakes with knowledge in his shoulders that will save him a fall he has not yet earned.

The third dream is for a child with a porcelain tooth on a string around her throat. It is a simple tune that goes faster or slower with the plates she steps on. She hums it as she follows a graftwright along the walkway and, without being told to, does not step on a certain bright patch of bark where someone has waxed it new. No one sees. No one would scold her for stepping. But the wax is spared a scuff before it has set, and it holds a curve the graftwright set by patient persuasion, and a board grown broad and planar a decade from now will have one minute more neatness in it because a child liked a song.

There is a bearing block on lignin under the main engine that drinks oil in a slow way; this has been by design. The people here learned early that concrete is a different kind of argument under iron than wood, and that galvanic bad blood is a thing even if you do not have the word galvanic for it. The block has a small habit of warming on long afternoons when the tide resists. It is not a defect; it is the story of work. Heat here is not a villain, but it is a gossip. The power speaks to heat with the care you use for an elderly aunt whose love of stories can get away from her. Be generous, it suggests, except when there is nothing to be generous about. The block’s temper learns in that instant how to let warmth leave through the wood faster when it is not needed and hold warmth longer when the night goes wantingly cool. There is no new material in it. There is a new understanding between molecules that wanted it anyway.

It is a kindness not to clean too much. The bay has already taught itself how to eat oil until it smells like beeswax in an old pew. The power seeds one more bacterial film in a place someone would not have thought to seed it—the interior throat of a relief line—so that when the bird-call whistle slices through the basso, what leaves is water like breath and not breath like solvent. It is not a miracle. It is housekeeping.

Once, because generosity when it meets a careful house wants to make itself known in a way a child could point to, the power smooths one day from the weather’s ledger and lays another in its place. On a hot afternoon after a wet morning, under the fattest run of leaf where a nickel band shines like a readiness to be cut but is held instead by a brass pin no one will pull, the condensate that runs down into a clean tin cup tastes, for five minutes, like pear. This is not a trick, not sugared, not the establishment of a new expectation. It is a wordless yes from something big to something that is trying. The woman with the quiet tool laughs and passes the cup to the child with the porcelain tooth. The child tells every other child. The old people hear about it by night and say, as old people do in kind houses, that of course there used to be days like that all the time, which is their way of smiling.

There is almost a matter with flame. Not here, but across a bay and a wall of leaves, someone who does not know the rules walks in with a spark on a breath and a thought that shortcuts are efficient. The sign the size of a hand bolted to a trunk, the one with the date twenty-two years old and the bark’s little eyebrow of curiosity, frowns into memory so hard its brass warms. The rule that lives here like a territorial animal—NO OPEN FLAME BEYOND THE YELLOW LINE—staggers to the front of its territory and doubles its own paint. The yellow brightens like a yoke cracked new. The person with the spark feels a bump of wrongness in their throat so strong they cough. They look down. The line appears new and holy. They step back. Later, a story will be told that the line flared like a serpent, which is not what happened, but neither is it false.

Something comes into the bay because the power asked it to, and something leaves because the power forbade it and then forgave. One moth, charcoal and freckled, learns to cross the thrum without losing wing, learns to lay one soft egg under the lip of one leaf high where no one climbs because there is no reason to except to look, and this moth makes one small transaction in the tree’s deep ledgers that carries a debt forward to a flower’s season a different year. One rat, which would have made a greasy joke of all this disciplined water, turns aside at a slit of copper and goes somewhere that will not matter. The power does not hate rats. It just loves Seventeen.

On a night late in the dry, when the big pump has a lull that only shows itself to those whose ribs keep time with it and the fog sat in the place hours earlier like a thinkable thought, the power does the boldest thing it will do. It lies down on the floor people have trained out of roots and rhizomes, the raft of buttresses, and lets its weight be known. The floor, which can taste water, tastes now also attention. It is not alarmed. It does what good floors do. It bears. But just at the edge of the center pit where iron feet sit on a plinth of living cambium that grows up to meet and is shaved down to level and grows and is shaved again, the power pulls up a little memory from deep—saplings put head to head and taught to start a bridge that would be useful when hours are longer and children have other chores. This is not for now. This is for when the edges of the city are cut differently, when the canal shows a new sky, when the intake mouth sits two hands higher because someone had to choose between a levee and a field and chose, as people always do, what they can reach. The bridge will be there then, as if the bay thought of it itself in its sleep and woke to find its hands had been busy.

Before it leaves—if leaving is the right word for changing from doing to simply watching—the power picks up a handful of time from the ground the way you pick up dropped pins and arranges it along several paths in several possible futures. It is not much time. It counts in breaths. There will be a day when a belt parts in another bay. Between the whip of leather and the cut of it across a man’s cheek there will be one extra heartbeat. The man will turn his face. The scar will be a pale line, not the ruin of an eye. There will be a morning when a collar was not released before the wet season because a name in the ledger was smudged. There will be one extra chance to read the old woman’s pebble-kept rule on her lips before the tree leans wrong. The right person will see and say. The collar will sigh and let go. The lean will become a story, not a diagram in a report.

There are things the power will not change. The belts still whisper like snakes in carved leaf windows. The flywheel remains a moon. The water still carries tannin and old breath and a taste of far tides that teaches the tongue to expect the world. The poor gecko is still the only gecko who thinks this is home. The dust in the light shafts is still not dust at all but a slow rain of atoms the city wore off itself and never quite misses. The rules still sit up like cats and ride shoulders like luck. The tree will still eat screws. The iron will still want to call attention to itself with bright and the people will still rub it down to honest.

When morning makes its columns through leaves, names traced in motes like signatures a child might write on her own hand, those who live here will feel, if anything, that Seventeen has slept well. They will not say blessed. They will say, It runs sweet, and this will be accurate.

Weeks later, on a day with brackish tang in the air that lets canal-watchers lean back from their dials with satisfied faces and say the tide’s high without touching a pointer, someone will pause at eye level where the sign DO NOT OIL THIS BEARING is half-swallowed by bark and notice that the bark’s little eyebrow has, if not lifted, relaxed into something that is almost—do not be sentimental—fond. They will spit into their hand for grip, lay it flat against the bark, feel the gossip of pressure from a relief three bays down, and for half a heart’s beat swear they can hear under the thrum another note, not so much a harmony as an overtone, the one gratitude makes when it has been fed and is quiet.

If this power has a discipline, it is this: to do less than it could and enough that what is excellent here is not fragility pretending to be virtue. It will check back—not by walking in, not by reddening lines, but by the smallest courtesies of physics granted now and again to a place that has earned them. It will send one more pear day before a generation ends. It will keep the moth’s wing steady across a late-night thrum once each flowering. It will bend silt with a knuckle the years the river is meaner than usual. It will keep a small stockpile of heartbeats for those brown afternoons when metal proves it is made from the same earth as bone.

Mostly it will remember. It will keep the rules in its mouth the way old people keep pebbles in theirs for discipline. NEVER CUT A BEARER ROOT. RELEASE COLLARS BEFORE THE WET SEASON. NO OPEN FLAME BEYOND THE YELLOW LINE. It will whisper them, not into ears, but into habits, which is the same thing if you are a place and everyone in you means to stay.

And it will love the thrum like a song it did not write and could not improve, and it will sit where the handrails have gone the color of moss and listen to the patient sizzle of condensate and the rattle of chain and the sigh and thud of weight meeting weight, and it will leave its palm on the wood long enough that the wood knows, in its patient way, that someone large has said yes to what it is doing.

Then it will go on, because water goes on and leaves go on and belts choose their path by the pulleys they have, and because if kindness is to be wise it cannot hover. Bay Seventeen will keep its temper. The city will keep its body. Those who tend, and the tree that lets itself be taught because the lessons are true, will go on with the work, which is to say, the life.
