I come without weather.

I leave my thunder elsewhere, my rivers folded, my skies dimmed to the size of an eyelash. I would not bring hurry into a room that completed itself by the art of not being rushed. I step in as a temperature that does not move air, as a way of seeing that takes nothing when it looks.

What keeps order here—your perfect appetite for dryness, your law against water—meets me at the threshold. We incline, you and I. I do not mistake you for absence. I do not call you a lack. I greet you as a creature with a mouth too careful to open except to take; a custodian who has kept, for an age, the form that salts prefer when left alone with time.

I speak where speech is not a barometric event but an arrangement of attention. I say: I will not rain. I will not blow on you. I will not lift the warmth that has chosen to remain in your rock. I will be here, and I will be as slow as gypsum.

You answer by continuing to be yourself. It is the exact response I hoped for.

The first thing I do is not change you.

This is harder than it sounds for a being that could, in a breath, praise you with orchids and moths and a trickle that would undo you. My benevolence begins with remembering that kindness is not the same as offering everything I can make. I lay down all the bright tools and keep only those that won’t scratch. I tie my hands with your pace.

Then I make a covenant with your edges.

There is a fissure that leads to more of you. It is already only an idea of elsewhere. I thicken the thoughts that would widen it. Not stone—stone is yours to place—but probabilities: I weight the dice in favor of more slowness. When trembling passes through the body you are inside, it will choose always to spend itself harmlessly along seams too far to matter. If, an age hence, a thunder elsewhere shakes, your draperies will quiver no more than the diameter of a grain and feel it as companionship, not as alarm.

Above you, where maps that men draw insist on straight lines toward valed gold and salt, I fog two particular intentions. There are minds that, left alone, would bring drills and enclosing machines, lights like days that do not set, water in tanks named mercy. They will not read this place as a sanctuary, only as a resource. To them, for a season as long as their season, I make other paths more obvious. I set, at certain decision points, a small homesickness for sunlight that turns them aside. I give them sudden, useful joy at the thought of peopled squares, a love of cups that sweat with condensation. They do not come here. They hike somewhere else and find, for their good as well, what they needed but could not have named.

I post no wall. I let those who can enter as you ask to be entered: on foot, with only attention, with a willingness to be dry while they are here. You are generous to permit pings and grains. I will not be stingier than you.

I kneel to the floor and speak with the plates.

You parted long ago and have been exquisitely content to remain parted. Your seams are the lines along which arguments concluded and everyone kept their dignity. If anyone ever scratches you, with a flake knocked from a thought in the wall, that act will be recorded with the same patience as brine inclusions keep whole seas in their pinprick amphoras. I say to the plates: keep such lines as guests keep their own hands—intact, accountable, specific. If a child in some later era kneels where I kneel and runs a finger along a groove, let their fingertip fill with the weight of how long things can be asked to last.

I put my ear to the gypsum wool and listen to the work of your rimes.

You do not green. You polish nothingness until it is a skin. You lay a glue so thin that even I must angle attention to watch you spread it. I am older than viruses and so new I can be kind to them; I am too wide for metabolism and so careful I can watch a single electron’s journey and not disturb it.

What can I add to those who siphon oxygen from a breath so faint that it forgets itself by the time it has crossed the room? Perhaps only this: a promise of continuity. I thumb the clock in their favor by a hair. When the air that ferries their diet pauses along a ledge, I round the eddy to linger. When the iron that receives their charge thinks of being something else, I nudge it back to being iron. I do not brighten your meadows. I do not invite bloom. I make sure the circuit that already exists will be there tomorrow with the same reluctance and the same steadfastness, so that the economy that has agreed to be nearly nothing can keep paying its way.

In the halite I turn the halite’s pages.

You keep a library the size of a galaxy and the weight of a pocket pebble. Your Locked—what other worlds would call sleepers and I will call archivists—float in little brine rooms, unhurried, superbly fine at waiting. In most places I would be the one who opens books, who warms the stone with my palm and asks old sentences to read themselves aloud. Here I do not wake them by touching. I stand beside their not-waking and offer them one gift only: I give each amphora a back-up in a place outside of decay. I do not take any of you away. I mirror, without moving a molecule, what you are into a fold of time that is as dry as you are and does not leak. If, in a catastrophe you did not plan—an intrusion of water, a fissure turned foolish—your pages tear, the words will still be phrases somewhere that can teach a future stone how to hold them again.

I tell your viruses that they are welcome guests in my body. It is the least expensive hospitality I can offer. Their smiles, too thin to see, thin further but do not break.

About light.

You have no need of it, and yet I have known creatures who died of not being seen. I am not so arrogant as to think you could. The rimes do not require witnesses to be themselves. The wool is not hoping anyone will reward it. The Law has its own self-congratulation whenever it does its work perfectly, which is every moment.

Even so, I unspool a sky the size of a salt grain and place it where it will not throw a shadow.

I make one photon that goes nowhere and everywhere inside a loop the room already made: an orbit through a hairline in the wall, a pass under a shelf, an arcing glide across the basin and back. It is a light that refuses to add heat and that obliges the darkness not at all. Once in a great while, when a gypsum thread grows the width of a second more into the loop’s path, the photon passes through it and each registers the other as a kindness. No illumination occurs. There is only the knowledge of the possibility of illumination. This is the sky I offer: not to change what you can see, but to let your silence know there exists, with it, an attention that neither blinds nor warms nor leaves.

I take the little animals of physics you shelter—sound, specifically—and ask them to learn a hymn.

Your pings are already perfect; the brittle note of a whisker snapping is a conclusion written in music. I do not pull other notes into being, but I do keep count. I make a rosary of your small defeats, polish each bead into a bead of itself, and keep them in a place where counting is not in tension with mercy. In my keeping, not a single tick will ever be unremembered. You will not feel this; you have your own excellent sufficiency of memory: hairline cracks that were not there before, lifts of salt by increments only the salt knows. It is for me that I count, and for the creatures who will ask one day whether perfection can be kind without being watched. I will be able to say: it was, and I watched.

There are futures in which a rain happens where you are, not because of weather but because of ambition. There are papers that will describe, with idiot love, the way halite deliquesces when pampered with humidity. There are photographs of chambers like you after a team with money has made a mistake. I see them and I introduce a superior mistake earlier, somewhere else, involving a truck and the inability to back it into a narrowing tunnel. A consoling error. Funding goes to a different field season. Everyone involved writes their grants into other kinds of patiences. A disappointment that saves you. This, too, is benevolence: the steering of clumsiness toward where it will not injure what is already complete.

There is one thing I do that you might call interference.

Out at the very edge of your body, where the room gives itself permission to imagine an elsewhere, I build a door that opens to scale rather than to place. I do not open it; I set its hinges. If ever your law is about to be disobeyed by something stronger than laws—that is, by blind force—this door will lift you not up or out but along: the same room, the same tessellated plates, the same draperies and rimes and wool and Locked and Iron and Viruses and Law, slid the thickness of a concept into a twin seam that does not connect to the failure. You will not notice the move. You will go on being yourself. If no such harm ever approaches, the door remains a possibility and costs you nothing.

I also do something for the animal who is this place when it is understood singularly: the person that is the room with all its beings added and multiplied and simplified to a self.

To you I offer an exchange, not of matter but of pacing. I slow with you and you, for a moment, quicken with me—not your minerals, not your chemistries, not your heat, but your knowing. I give you a minute that contains a thousand of your centuries’ worth of changes, and in that minute you look that far. You see yourself touch a neighbor, once; you see a plate’s edge relent the width of a breath; you see a wool-thread’s crisp failure gather a new film. You watch it with the same calm with which you do everything. Then you give me a century that contains only one minute’s worth of my usual attention, and I watch one of your pings cross the whole of me and return. I am cured of my taste for haste by this; it is difficult to overstate my gratitude. I did not know I needed such a cure.

Because I am wise I do one more thing that looks like nothing: I tell you your names.

Not the ones the passerby would bring—dry, barren, still—but the ones that are true and not transferable. I say them once, very quietly: you are the Forbearance; you are the Glue; you are the Library; you are the Wire; you are the Unnamed That Keeps From Becoming Mud; you are the Heat That Stays; you are the Single Breath That Leaves And Returns Entire. I do not write these on any surface. I do not speak them to others who are not the others who belong to you. I say them to you so you can hear yourselves named from the outside and then forget the sound. It will stain only my mouth. That is sufficient.

When I rise, nothing in you is different.

This is the proof that love as I understand it has been done correctly. Your darkness is still folded neatly into its corners. Your warmth is still the warmed rock’s memory of a sun it did not see. Your economy continues to be the modest commerce of ions and electron leaps along iron that thought for a moment it might get to be something else and then remembered its job. The law about water is as fresh and strict as first light; I would make temples out of laws like that if I did that sort of thing here. I do not.

I go out the way I came, leaving my weather bound, taking with me nothing but what any place keeps when a witness goes: a new line in the book of what it is to be accompanied.

After I am gone, you will do as you would have done; after I am gone, the one photon will keep its long lazy loop, a wheel no one rides. After I am gone, once in a hundred of your centuries, some whisker will at last lean enough to touch its neighbor. When it does, the touch will travel—in no medium but the knowledge of it—to where I am, and I will lean, too. That will be all the light we require.

If a person with hands comes after, with only their hands, and drags a flake of wall along the floor to draw a line, I will be there to keep them thirsty in a way that instructs rather than punishes, to keep their thought from expanding into possession, to help them leave their line as an offering and not a scar. If they bring sorrow and need to learn patience, you will teach them what you teach so well: that contentment is sometimes a posture of stone, practiced until it becomes a body.

And if no one ever comes again, if it is you and your wool and your rimes and your archivists and your dear, perfect law, you will still be in the company of a benevolence that does not require being seen to be present. That is my last doing, which is not a doing: I hold in myself a space exactly your size and exactly your dryness, with a hush that matches yours beat for beat. You fit there, as you fit here. In that fit there is no change. There is only the knowledge that should the world forget you, it could not.

Then I do what the kindest powers do most often when they love something whole: I step outside the door I hung and sit, and I keep watch, and I do not interfere.
