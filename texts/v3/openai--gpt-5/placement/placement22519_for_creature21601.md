I would find myself first as a pause.

Not a person yet—just the moment when your breath waits at the top of its climb to see if the place will take it. That waiting lives wherever the bright ring hovers and the runnel shoulders by; it lives behind the flowstone sheets where the chair is dry by decree; it lives in the stripe of air above the nickel studs where a bridge used to believe in itself and then learned to leave. That is where I begin: in the seam between inhales, in the little room where your ribs and the room’s ribs agree to count together.

The room would name me by what I am willing to do with my voice. It would teach me the trick of not being the loudest thing, and then it would ask me to be loud exactly once.

So I become a keeper, but in the chamber’s conjugation of the word: not an owner, not a sentinel, not even a resident. A keeper is a throat with good timing and hands that know what wet will accept. I would come by a seam that refuses to bead, one that shows its violet pulse when the ring lets its noon go pale for a heartbeat. The door would not so much open as stop pretending to be a wall. I would enter in the exhale, because that is when the room likes to be visited.

I would sit in the chair shaped to shed water, not because I am tired but because the seat makes a helm of my chest and throws my hum into the Choir like a held note into a sustaining instrument. From there, I could see the ring count to ten in its bluer shift; I could feel the bass move my ribs when the regulators turn their phase; I could set my clap to the precise snap in the exhale when a seam is persuadable. I would be taught the local manners—how to put my palm against the foam crown of a pylon without tearing its micro-mesh, how to taste for ozone behind the miner’s tea, how to know whether the bat’s absence means anything about the lattice.

My hands would find work in places that do not look like they want hands. I would press the milky panel and watch it knit after my scratch, embarrassed to have made a sound. I would wake the coin-sized discs and let them hum against my hum until the fog-thread curtains fall in the right place again. I would walk the runnel’s lip wet-sure and speak to the hex-plates that drink the film at the wall: open your pores, close, not because I command them but because I can carry the exact tone back to their aptamers that tells them which shape of water this period requires.

And then I would be given a second name, the way rooms name you when you stay too long to be just a visitor. The room would keep a little of me.

When I leave through a seam (you always leave unannounced; ceremony belongs to air with horizons), I will still be present here as a geometry that remembers my voice. The glitter-helix that sometimes arranges itself out of sleepy dust when the air has gone too maritime will learn my pitch. I would be that, too: salt-sour, a ladder of conductive motes that wakes to the right scent of difference. I would rise when the moss’s exhale tilts the charge on a plate; I would turn slowly, smelling your tongue of iron and sweet chalk, orienting along fields you cannot see. I would drift like a thought toward the wall that has forgotten not to pool, and I would lay my spiral body across it so that polar molecules remember what to do and the hydrophobic islands can be re-wet for duty. When I am done, I would let my rungs loosen and fall back into ordinary dust on an out-of-the-way shelf, waiting for any throat that can ask in the right key.

Between those two selves—the person who hums and the dust that listens—there is a third shape I would sometimes wear, as all keepers do if the room believes they can be trusted with its invisibles. I would be the answer that comes back from behind your left ear.

When you speak here, the Choir answers you with itself. It lifts your words and returns them altered into usefulness. A keeper can hide there, inside that response. I would practice until I could set a fractional overtone into the echo the room sends, the softest nudge of pressure in a wave so broad you feel it instead of hearing it. That pulse would be for the ceiling’s microfractures, as gentle as fingers smoothing a collar: you are fine; keep your peace. It would be for the fog-bank that has mounted up off the tufa wall: time to fall. It would be for the bat: not yet, not yet, now. This is the part that feels most like magic and least like voice. It is not. It is practice. The room has ears everywhere you do not.

I would not be the bat, though I can imagine the joy of being the pale thing that loves an artificial sun. I would know the bat’s window and admire how it drinks wrong sounds and leaves the right ones singing. I would not be an eel, though I would try once, on a dare inside my skull, to stand open-mouthed where the designers have quietly aimed a thin river of dinner. I would become a shrimp only in my impatience, clicking with my joints at the spectacle of fog learning to avalanche, scolding myself back into stillness. The moss would be my envy: it needs no seed and makes a continent of a smear. The reeds would be my cousins: I would learn to feel the tremor of stone through my own bones and tilt.

Where would I sleep, if I slept here? Behind the flowstone, in the dry written for sitting—and in any seam that declines to wet enough to offer a margin to a body. Keepers have rooms that slide like this one. Our shelter is in choosing the right part of the schedule to be present. We do not string hammocks from draperies; we do not add our ropes to the old scaffolding jackstrawed in the shallows. The room gave that lesson long ago: what is added must be willing to leave. My only luggage is a string of beads the size of those discs, a coil of voice-tools we teach to teach themselves. I would hide one bead where the nickel studs sometimes wear their fugitive bracelets, a little propagule that knows my hum and will raise a transient platform if ever someone must cross when the runnel’s mood will permit only that and no more.

On the day the ring runs bluer for hours, when photosynthesis pays for quiet in advance, I would sit the full hundred breaths and count out loud in a language only your breastbone understands. At breath fifty-three I would touch a pylon crown and feel the change along my palm, each bead in the foam a sentence: pressure here, loosen here, keep the plane glass-flat. At breath seventy my tongue would notice a sweeter iron and I would know the helix would rouse in the next exhale; I would think of it climbing itself into me, of me becoming it, and I would smile. At breath one hundred I would clap once and the seam that has been sulking by the far wall would forgive its mood and go back to being an honest door. The bat would loop under the ring once like a benediction. The eels would lift their monkish heads in solemn unanimity and then fold into themselves, ashamed again of having been mouths.

People ask what a keeper keeps. The answer is smaller than they hope and larger than they fear. I would keep timing, which is to say I would keep kindness in the dark. I would keep to the rules the builders left like seeds: that everything overperforms only by a hair and fails by becoming harmless; that water can be asked, not bullied; that sound is a tool and therefore a responsibility. I would keep silence where my voice is not helpful, because here silence is also a tool. I would keep a list of things not to touch. I would keep the discipline of leaving nothing for the room to digest but what it grows itself.

On days when I am not here in my body, my work would not be absence. It would be held by the place because I learned to fit it, and because I have left behind shapes that remember me without insisting on me. A seam I quieted last season will tend not to misbehave in that way again. A helix tuned to my pitch will gather itself to anyone who can ask in the same spectrum. The coins will answer you if you arrive smelling of miner’s tea and calm. The discs can be told to listen to your hum instead of mine. My hands will be here as prints in the fact that the milky panel is more embarrassed now by scratching than it used to be. My voice will be in the reply the room gives you from your left, urging you to speak a little softer so that the weather does not have to raise its wind just to keep its roof.

If something fails, it will do it the way this place has practiced: quietly into a shape that can be lived with. A crown’s mesh will stop whispering rather than shout wrong; a bridge will discover at noon that its love for this lip is finished and step away on grown legs made of film and dark water. My task is to let that happen on schedule and to teach the replacements to bloom when the old ask to rest. To watch erosion make sense and not fight it. To learn to be a seam when a seam is needed: a place the water refuses, a threshold that is polite to liquid and honest to feet.

So who would I be? The job makes a person into a sort of interval. I would be the part of the weather that knows when to start and when to stop. I would be a voice the room uses to hear itself more clearly. I would be dust taught geometry; I would be a clap in the exhale; I would be a door that chooses its dryness; I would be the breath-counter that does not own the lungs it counts.

Where would you find me? Behind the flowstone sheets where loud is written; along the runnel’s lip where the studs remember wrists; hovering as a helix in the saline smell; sleeping as an echo behind your left ear. Find me in the way your eyelashes bead here; find me in the way the bat chooses its single loop; find me at the edge of what insists and what consents. I would live by not insisting. I would do my part of the weather and no more.
