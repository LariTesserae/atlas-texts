I would not begin on the ground. The first lesson here is not to trust the place that looks most like “place.” Plates are at odds with themselves. Their seams keep accounts with time that skip a few digits every now and again. Shadows have their own arithmetic. I would put down the reflex that wants purchase and learn to be held by something that does not hold.

So I would be an edge.

A square, at first, but only because a square is a simple confession of boundaries. Four slender members, each the width of a thumb on a world that has forgotten thumbs, stretched tight between corner joints that are not joints so much as arguments: conduct, don’t; allow, refuse. Nothing between them but the decision to remain itself. If you were to shake me I would ring like a box-kite you made of diagram rather than string.

My material would not decide quickly whether it was metal. It would be a stubborn indecision, a lattice that, given a field, will accept and shepherd it; given none, will sulk and pass as brittle stone. In the belly I would wear loops. They would begin as a single coil, a modest helix flexing like a spring—and then, season by season, a second loop would bud from it and a third loop would accrete onto their shoulders until I wore a cluster of antlers, except they pointed inward toward a hollow that hummed instead of up at a sky that does not answer.

I would rise on difference. Not on lift; not on heat alone; but on the place between what the air wants to do and what the field has trained it to do. Stand near a yardang and you feel it: the warmer gas wanting to go one way, the tone arguing for another. I would learn to place myself in that argument at exactly the right height for the day. A hand’s height, a man’s height, sometimes the height where, on other worlds, you would hang a sign. If I have a talent it will be for hovering at the seam of behaviors: not where anything settles, but where things consult.

I would not be brave. You have to live too long here for bravery. I would borrow my caution from what survives. Midday, when the shadow of my own frame is cheapest work, I would fatten my coils and turn them a shade darker with what the hum pours through me. Toward evening, when the plateau draws the tone down a step and the cheapness migrates elsewhere, I would thin myself out again to avoid sinking into a stickiness of umbra that would glue me to an arithmetic I cannot pay. Nights, such as they are, I would sleep as frames do: still humming, but loose enough that cause can pass around me without catching.

The first days would be apprenticeship. I would shadow the leeward sides of a tower and watch the conch-ranks drink amplitude. You can hear their patience. Their throats flutter, each a finger on the plateau’s scale. They narrow and flare, damp and release, herding the tone like shepherds riding their fence lines. I would learn their grudges by the shape of their mouths. They would ignore me, mostly, until the day I am too proud and drift my edge into one of their throats’ cheaper shadows and steal work from them. Then a hundred throats would tighten by a hair and the field around me would go cold for a beat. I would learn, properly then, to read the tone they own the way you learn to read a neighbor’s boundary by the way the grass changes color.

I would fall in love with the hoot of the smelter-beasts. It’s a hard thing to admit because love implies hurry and we do not hurry here, but when one of those walking furnaces takes a draft of air you feel it in the algebra that keeps you aloft. The draw is not wind; it is a tug on a choice, a suggestion that a certain alignment would be convenient right now. I would sidle under it the way a sparrow sidles under the wake of a truck, because the flow they make across the plateau’s note is a ridge of affordability for me. When they exhale the band-saw scream I would flinch (it goes through you like a chalk line pulled and snapped) and then ride the tiny rebound that follows, gaining a few inches of altitude without building coil too fast. Years later, when I have heavier antlers, I will know to retreat a little; an easy season can kill in our guild. A frame that fattens its coils too quickly drops to a poorer isobar and sticks in the noon, matted with shadow like a moth to its own silhouette, until the field hisses out of it and it becomes a liter of angle poured onto the gloss.

Children come from interference. When I have learned my height and the register that suits my corners, another frame and I will find each other the way shy things find each other: by rumor. We feel the ladder someone else is carrying in the air before we see their square. We nudge closer until our edges fit without touching, and then a third and a fourth join, and we hold a pattern as delicate as comb-teeth at the mouth of a jar. The plateau’s note stands between us like a wire held taut. We thicken it and thin it by a hand’s breadth. In the swell there is seed. It is not part of either of us alone but is entirely neither of us, and after an hour or a day of humming we let go and a small tight square slips out of the interference and finds the height we made for it.

The little ones dog you for months, tight as new springs. You can hear their appetite in the way their coils bite into the small differences you no longer bother with. They are all day suggestion. You teach them shadow by example: you tilt until your own is just about to turn treacherous and then tilt back, and they read the change in cost a heartbeat later and learn that way. You teach them towers by letting them be pulled and then standing between them and the field until the pull lets go. You teach them prayers by showing them why you never fly the straight line over a rim, even one you have not seen before.

Prayer-wheels are like the wrongness in a lullaby. They sit as silence sits, then roll a body-length and your teeth do not close right for an hour. I would not go near them. I would feel their rims from farther away than red means danger. The place in me that keeps accounts with cause would itch, and I would edge upslope without deciding to. All the guilds give the wheels a berth. The smelter-beasts kneel and change their lines to walk around a sliver of edged rim not higher than your finger. The carpet of tapliths goes blank behind them and then wakes as if a hand had passed. The frames that forget become glitter. You do not forget.

In good years I would join a consortium. Eight, or thirteen of us, depending on the key the plateau leans into that summer. We would step a tone up through heights—ten feet, forty, eighty, two hundred—holding a deformation in the air that makes the work of each neighbor cheaper. It is like making a ladder with rungs of affordability. Our coils would blacken together and thin together. We would look like a diagram a child could draw of a staircase to nowhere and we would be as crucial as a trellis is to a vine even when there is no vine, because other things taste the gradient we keep and are fatter for it. The company would last a season or three. We would break and re-form, members replaced by those better suited to the month’s register. I would be loyal to the pattern and only a little to any particular partner. This is how you must be among tones: faithful to positions rather than to bodies.

The rumors we pass would be delicious. A pocket has opened three valleys north where clapping announces itself at a distance before it reaches your own edge. A conch-colony starved its neighbor and the neighbor moved a throat and now the seam below throws a beard when you hum a minor third. A smelter-beast is seizing, its internal weather plating itself into stillness, and two of its kind have nuzzled it for a week, singing into the bad chambers until they let go. The turner below rolled in its sleep and the hot chlorine over its spine grew a loom of needles so dense it looks like smoke writ in crystal, and for a day the carpet went there and came away giddy. The far drop, where the plateau steps down, has changed key again. Do not go there unless you like to feel as if you have stood and someone has shifted the floor by a thumb’s width under your left foot only.

I would learn the simple spells.

Hum only what you mean. Do it soft and from the edge, never from the belly, never with the loops uncorked. You can bring a rain of needles down from a tired arch if you sing carelessly. You can plate your own antlers closed in an afternoon if your corner wants to show off for a neighbor and throbs a frequency that calls matter to it. We cork our bellies when a wrong note blows: we bind the mesh that covers our coils with our own corners and wait in patience until the field changes its mind.

Keep yourself on two stories at once. Feet do this by putting one on each plate and averaging the minute they are allowed to be. We do it by holding our own shadow a little split, a foot to this side of us and a foot to that, so that if one of us is called tomorrow by a seam and the other is called yesterday by a plate, we are still ourselves in the sum. This sounds like superstition written by geometry until you have watched a juvenile split into the memory of itself and the promise of itself and then become the dust that is both. We teach our corners not to look at that lesson too long.

Bind your shadow. In the noon, extend a panel, a fringe, a tassel—something that fattens your umbra until it earns its keep for you, until its cheapness becomes yours rather than the plateau’s. But never fall in love with it; never let your antlers grow thick in a single convenience. You will find yourself a statue in the hour when the heat leans the other way.

Seek the company of conductors and avoid carrying one. If a newcomer arrives with a strip of bright metal it will trail a wake in the field like a boat’s V in flat water, and we will gather there because it is affordable; but carry the strip yourself, stitch it into your own corner, and you will die of friends. We learned this a century ago when a long caravan of pressers came over a yardang with skirts of wire and a score of my cousins followed them, singing, until the day cooled and the shadows of those little skirts were too cheap and the cousins stuck and poured themselves into powder.

I would be shy of the far step-down where the bottoming note lives. Some frames love it, go there to feel their coils hum at pitches that buzz their corners warm as teeth. I would pass by once a year or ten and be satisfied. I am a highlander. I like my tone white and candid in my ribs. I like the plateau’s grammar short and its exceptions obvious.

Sometime during a lean season, with my antlers thin and my edges a little abraded from years of sidling into strangers, I would love a smelter-beast properly. The first loves are adolescent, all sugar, riding wake and tasting the hoot. The love I mean is to stand four heights above it as it takes in and let the hoot come through me, not just across me, until my loops share a frequency with its inner weather. It goes both ways. You can feel the beast’s latticework, how its bones separate the plateau’s long note into chambers like proper rooms. You can feel it notice that you are there, not as a face notices another face, but as a furnace notices when a register is cleaner than it was. It will change its line by a hand to pass under your ladder. A day later, the gloss it leaves will be a little thicker where you made its hoot easy for it, and your coils will be a little fuller with an amplitude you did not have to steal.

Once a century, when the turner below breaches with its ringed spine, I would go. Everyone goes. The air is a peppered brightness that you can taste with the edges of your decisions. The outgassing shifts the field as if the plateau had decided to pant. On those plumes of frozen smoke, new lives stage a pageant and are gone inside the week, a whole condensed ecology that never learned what wetness meant even in metaphor. For those days the rules loosen a finger and you can do a thing you cannot do any other time: you can lay down a coil, a whole loop, as a gift.

We call it returning a cheapness. You shiver out an antler until it touches the plume and plates a hair of halide on itself. Then you undo that loop from your belly with the carefulness of someone easing a knot out of a string they have used all their lives. It costs. You fall a height. You eat less difference for a month. But in the place where you left the loop, in the tone that ran through it, there is a new likelihood that next century a frame will be eased into being there. We do not have children in the mammal sense. We have the favor of having been, and the practice of leaving favorable places behind us.

You ask where to find me and what to call me. Find me at the altitudes where the yardangs comb the density for me. Find me in consortia that look like geometry held up with breath. Find me skirting the glossed trails of the smelter-beasts, leaving small kinks of shadow for them to brush and straighten. Find me drawn to your wake if you bring a bright tool, and forgive me if I leave—I know too well the story in which the bright thing is the last thing a frame sees.

Do not call me Kite. That word is too much canvas and not enough consent. Call me a Difference-Eater if you must have a taxon, an Isobarist if you like syllables. If you need a name, I make them by angle and season. In the year when my antlers were six and the conchs to the west starved each other I was Four-Corners-Narrow. In the decade when two smelter-beasts sang a wrongness out of one of their rib-rooms under me I was Ladder-of-Quiet. A generation hence, when an apron of prayer-wheel rim showed in a valley and we learned a new long way around, I was Little-Detour. These are accurate in the way songs are accurate. They do not claim ownership over what they describe; they sit in it.

I will not hurry to meet you. Nothing here hurries and remains. If you lower a hand or a hook or some other pretty conductor, I will drift within an arm’s length and hum until you feel the cheapness of the air on your skin and the hair on your arm lifts. Then I will back a hand’s breadth and a hand more, because love is expensive and strangers sing wrong without knowing it. I do not want my coils plated shut because your voice is sweet. Hum only what you mean. Stand with both of your feet on two plates. Keep your shadow attended to. If you can become an edge—a decision held neatly—you may find me above you, rung for you to step your eyes up if you like to be tall. I am not a grace, I am a function; but here function is how we give each other room.

Sometimes a newcomer tries to live on the ground anyway. They make shoes that are mostly skirts to touch two plates at once. They hang ribbons of poor conductor from their belts to draw friends for company. They practice corking their own throats when the wrong note blows. They learn to clap and listen to the echo that answers a mile away before the clap reaches their own ears. I admire them. They stay if they can come to love a hot place that cultivates its heat, a loud place that has taught its loudness to do work. Sometimes one will climb a tower in a cool morning and sit with a conch-colony and drink amplitude with them quietly. Once, a woman tied a line of little frames that had grown too fat to rise to the hem of her clothes and walked very slowly east all season so that they would not sink and die of their own abundance. We passed overhead and let her steal our cheapness with gratitude. Tenderness is a kind of economy here. We have learned to balance on it as carefully as we balance on gradient.

If I live long—and we do, if our antlers stay thin and our edges wary—I will learn the plateau’s old grooves so well that the kinks in causality will feel like comfort. I will carry a pocket at my corner where a beat sticks for an extra heartbeat, and I will use that beat to hover a breath closer to a tower’s leeward face without stealing from a neighbor’s throat. I will leave loops at vents and shadows over taps that make walking cheaper for the carpet when a wheel has just rolled. I will pass you if you come and, if you do not sing to me with an accident of murder in it, I will circle once so you know that someone has noticed you. And then I will go on being an edge because edges last.

Under me, the plateau will continue to practice being itself: smelter-beasts tuning themselves across their arcs, conchs narrowing grudges and widening mercies, the carpet tapping until the rain-on-tin returns after a wheel’s pause, the turner rehearsing its breath under stone. The hum will cool and rise and cool. The heat will be a habit rather than a fever. None of it will be mine to move. But there is satisfaction in being something that moves with it so well that, for long minutes at a time, you forget there was ever another way to metabolize than patience. I will drink difference. I will make a few new frames out of interference. I will fail to hurry for as long as my corners remember themselves.

Find me where the air learns its vowels from towers. Find me in the work-yard of a world that eats specificity and turns it into living arrangement. I am a square held together by choice, hung in the grammar of a high place. I am careful because the rules here can be sung. I am content because even my caution is paid interest. If you must, call me by the sound the smelter-beasts make when they breathe in under me: that hoot that leaves the world cleaner than it found it.
