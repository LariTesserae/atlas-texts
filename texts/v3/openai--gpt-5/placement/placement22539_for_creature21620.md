The first time I came up into this brightness, I thought I would be wind.

I was so sure of it—how could you not want to be the thing that has free run of every rib and gap, that makes instruments out of mesh and chain? To be the hand that plays the copper beads in the dew courts and hums long vowels through the fog-nets. To have routes instead of roads; to learn the throats of louvers and the bed-sounds of gravel beds; to write every morning across every surface and never leave a mark anyone could point to and say: that was you.

But the first hour is for wanting everything, and this place asks you to keep wanting for longer than that.

By the second hour I had convinced myself I was mesh. I felt the hum in my bones—dry voice in knives of light, wet whisper on milk-days—and the pleasure when the first beads pricked and pulled together in a run, the satisfaction of giving a promise shape. I pictured how it would be to sit in the wind on a carbon frame, catenary tight and singing, to feel a seam go tired long before it failed, to be tuned by someone who knew that a half note lower meant fewer mosquitos caught but more longevity. I liked the precision of it, how many hands had touched me and named my edges, how my work was to hand whatever gathered to waiting lips and cords.

By the afternoon I was sure I was water. Not the idea of it but the apprentice itself: bead, drip, thread; the level a float-rod kissed; the frog-backed cool of a cistern’s belly; the slow black run of the hand-wide channel learning again and again the tilt of the mountain. The place’s entire mood made sense that way. A calendar as a way of telling the truth. Rumor as a ripple in a pressure gauge. Futures chalked on the underside of wrists. Mercy as a valve set one notch open on a neighbor’s lean week. I wanted to be that resident: to be measured and yet alive, to be counted and yet moving, to be a thing that made feasts and droughts possible to bear.

Then, walking, I tripped over an enamel number with sun on its face and a pigeon’s toe-prints in the dust beside it; a lift cage sighed up into the air like a patient thought; somewhere a woman tapped a wrench on a rail and sent a joke along the steel; a mantis leaned like a metronome in a run of cable-shadow and did not pretend not to be waiting. The turbines turned like watch-gears and the brick walls of the storage rooms were warm under my hand as if anyone could come back to this same spot and find the same heat.

I wanted to be everything I could touch.

It was only in the long slow slope toward evening—the hour that feels like a secret hinge—that I understood my place, which is to say I understood I would have to be a place where many things meet rather than a single thing done perfectly.

What I am, here, is a keeper of balances with a pencil behind her ear. One of the ledger-folk in a cool brick room, yes, but also a body that knows the topmost catwalk by the tremble in its calves; a person whose subject is mathematics and mercy and whose ears have learned the pitch of fog.

In morning I am out where the wind has already licked the mesh. Milk-days are mine like any Net-tender’s. I walk the line of dew courts and listen to the beads ring down the copper chains as if reading a sentence—I can tell by the mischief in the chime where a link has gone stiff or a tile has lifted a breath. I carry a brush with a handle polished by my grandmother’s fingers and use it to sweep the hand-wide channel clean. The dragonflies know its movement; they lift and set again as if we have an agreement, and we do. Every twelve meters where the channel swells and invites fingers, I touch it and feel inside the touch whether something is a fraction off: that dark sheen says tannin is high; that small chop means an upstream baffle is nervous. I send messages ahead with a tap on a rail. The whistle code rides better in certain corridors; I know those, too. Two notes brings a lift. Three calls for a door to be shepherded in a bad wind. Four, on a Knife-day, tells the Armaturist I’ll be by to sign the permit for a rivet run because a seam on a fog-net has gone stale.

At midday I disappear into the rooms with the brick bellies. Inside, the cool speaks the language that raised me. The float-rods have their private sighs and I could tell you each by name, but I don’t; I just smile when one lies to me and I let it lie for the minute it needs to stop thinking it will be rebuked. The frogs, who go everywhere without permits, volunteer their assessments from the cisterns. I look at charts on the walls, I open the ledger that is as much a family book as any photo album, and I write. The numbers go down in short columns, each digit no bigger than a grain, and they add to a shape that makes sense to more than me. When the sums are neat I breathe easier. When they are not, I take mercy where mathematics won’t mind, and no one here confuses that with being sloppy. The law is careful and remembered. If I draw a line a little curved—if I send a little more water to a terrace where the peas are meant to knit levels this year and the wind has had a habit of wearing at that seam—it is not a theft. It is me tucking a blanket under a sleeping child.

I learned this work from women who looked at sky the way some people look at faces, and from men who measured light on their knuckles. My grandmother read weather in mesh-voice and taught me that wind has habits and can be asked, not ordered. My mother’s palms are leaf-stained the way mine are ink-stained; she was a Bafflewright and could set a shade so that both seedling and hand would rest. I keep their ways and also keep my own, which is mostly to read the places where stories meet the numbers. There’s a seam of moss on an east-facing girder which the designers seeded thirty years ago to slow a drip; it throws the figures off by a breath if I don’t remember it. A child put bark boats into the channel seven Feastfalls ago and the little dams of silt that collected in the expansions from their caught prows taught dragonflies to favor certain corners; the counts got cleaner after that for reasons no one could have predicted with a table.

On Knife-days I go up and grease. I carry tins that smell of summers and stand where the wind would like to break me open, and I learn how to stand softer. The shutters know me by my whistling and I know their moods by theirs; I listen to a run of louver-pivots and decide which ones want attention this paintyear and which will be good for another notch down the ladder. On Milk-days I move small and I move long. I unhook mantises from places they will get crushed and set them back into baskets’ wicker with an apology. I retune a fog-net a half note lower and hum with it until our throats match.

In the galleries with long windows I sign for a set of armatures and talk to the hot-rivet crew about a joint that has lived a good life and needs a second. They dance the dance they know and I keep time. I eat from a paper parcel in a draught that smells of machine oil and leaf-mold both, and afterward I take the last of the sweetness on my tongue and rub it on a brass bolt—bees like those, and it keeps me in their good books.

Sometimes a stranger from the next escarpment comes through with a way of tying mesh that lowers a hum, and I add that to the book and to my hands. Sometimes we send out bolts in small wagons that click along rails like the beads on a long deliberate necklace, and sometimes we receive sacks of ground slate that smell like rain’s cousin. I account for both as carefully as for water, because it’s all the same conversation in the end.

At dusk I go up if I have the leg for it. The topmost catwalk has taught me how to sit with a safety mesh humming under me and not mistake that for danger. I watch the evening unfasten itself. I count the first five bells of dew-court chime under my breath and then I stop counting. Counting is a way to love, but so is letting go. A raptor comes through sometimes and redraws the margins of smaller lives and I do not write that down; the book is not for that. A child I know walks a line of bolts with a seriousness that knows each head is a coastline; I call her name in metal-voice and she answers with a rail-tap that has her mother’s impatience in it. Somewhere below me a ledger closes like a good door and I know without looking that the columns are tidy enough to sleep.

When the first rain of a deep storm has crossed the ridge, the place throws a feast. It is not for spectacle—we don’t do spills here—but for the syllable of joy that runs like chain-voice in downspouts when the first water hits the long roofs and is caught the way an expert catches a newborn. After, the charts show what they show and someone goes pale with the specific happiness of tidy columns, but the memory that sticks is the batter of drums on the tin and the feeling that loudness can be contained without being shamed.

I am not always only numbers. In the rooms behind the tanks, with brick bellies all around and a frog asleep on a plank like a careless verse, I draw little marks in the margins to tell what the weather did to my heart. Sunbirds came and learned how to draw mosquitoes from the dew courts without tangling the chains. A skink slept with its eyes open on the evening-warm rib of a cistern wall. A pigeon looked cynical as a shop-foreman at my lunch and had the good grace to leave me one olive. My pen has those moments salted into it, and it is a better instrument for it.

I will be here long enough to watch a truss take five coats down the color ladder and I have a favorite enamel number on a lift cage because it is the first one I read by the light of a Feastfall lightning. If I am lucky, a child will count droplets under my hand and later walk bolts with a seriousness that makes me laugh without spoiling it. When I die they will handle me with the care we reserve for material that has more than one future. I will go into soil and brick-crush and names. Someone will add a second small number to a lift cage and those who understand will nod in passing. The channel will keep its memory of tilt and be a better river for the fraction of me that darkens it. Mantises will sway. Mesh will learn a new seam. The law will keep being mostly about where the wet goes and when, and kindness will keep being the adjustment to the rule that makes it fit the day.

If you are looking for me any given afternoon, you can find me three turns down from the turbines that turn like watch-gears, where the hand-wide channel swells palm-deep and holds a sky no one else has time to see. I will be the woman with a brush that looks like a fish’s tail and a pencil that smells like damp paper. I will be listening to the copper chains sing their tiny bells and tapping a rail a message you can learn in an hour and love for a life. When the wind leans into the place like a great patient animal, I will lean too. When the dew courts begin their counting, I will keep it until I don’t have to. I am not wind, not mesh, not water alone, though I wanted to be. I am the arrangement of attentions that lets all three be themselves here for a long time.

That is who I am, in this brightness that comes from every direction. And that is where to find me: in the rooms where numbers and mercy share a table, on the catwalk where the safety mesh hums and flexes, at the edge of a channel that has learned to be river enough for what we ask of it.
