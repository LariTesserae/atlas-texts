I would not choose the floor first. The floor is a book already taken, its sentences rewritten every night by mouths no larger than commas. I would go to the brow.

There is a crane rail that crosses the nave like a thought that never got put away. Its trolley hangs stopped, its hook gone weightless and bearded with black rime. Here is an edge that has not surrendered to roundness altogether; here is where structure carries sound farther than water does, and a small freight of electricity climbs along the steel like a rumor. Halfway between the ice and the silt—between winter and the city-room’s black sleep—the rail is a seam of its own.

I would become a line of mouths along that seam.

Not jelly, not metal, not lamp, though I would borrow from each. I would take a pedicle the width of a thread and fasten, once, carefully, to the oxide bloom on a flange—the place where rust has taken on the order of a flower. My body would be clear enough to know itself as water with a curriculum: bell thin as onion skin, a window that is also a hand. Facing downward into the aisle’s slow weather, I would learn the habit of opening when the generator’s distant patience sets the aisle to its tiny river. I would sip what the current carries—bacteria-slurred flecks, single diatoms on pilgrimage from the blue vein in the broken pane, bits shaved from a film by an amphipod’s incredulity—and, equally, I would sip charge.

When the pump wakes, I would feel it first in my anchor. Steel changes its mind about magnetism in steps too small for bone to hear; my pedicle would know them as syllables. The hum would travel across me into my bell and I would answer with a hospital-pale bloom. Light is not my food, but I would make a touch of it because the hall speaks that language in small and I want to be legible.

I would live by difference: the degree and the shade, the slightly warmer eddy above a motor line, the slightly saltier pour of a brine curtain fanned thin over the trolley beam, the almost-invisible slackening that means dusk here, which is not a sky event but an agreement among machines to rest their spin. My calendar would be cycles, counted in pulses: bloom one shade brighter; darken; wait; again. I would remember, with a memory that is the accumulation of a million small responses in a line, the month the far grid went starving and we kept our light to a thread; the week the water went uncharacteristically clear and suddenly the conger’s number became a sentence and not just the suggestion of violence.

I would not be alone. I would be a we that extends across the brow—bells following the rail’s length in a rosary of almosts. We would not speak but we would resemble speaking. Open, close, dim, bloom; a pattern crawls along us when the draft comes and then crawls back, and the shrimp on the cable tray below learn to keep time to it as if it were a streetlight. When the brinicles shed their brittle skins and the snow falls sideways, we would shut to slits and let the storm of crystals whisper past—some of them lodging on our rims like brief music, some of them melting to a saltness that stings. There would be seasons, but they would be measured in reach: brinicles lengthening toward us and then, one month, beheading themselves into a rain of glass and salt; the amber lamps keeping their coal-like sermon steady for a while, then a brownout, then the warmth again one shade.

My neighbors would be the sleepers on the warmth lines—the eelpouts poured like wax along a casing; their pulse slow, their mouths making the minimum truth. They would rise sometimes and pass below me without a ripple my skin could name, as if I were the ceiling of a church and they were parishioners observing the roof. The conger would be my dusk-bell. I would not see him as an animal so much as a wave of permission changing where the room is gentle. He is a moving boundary condition, and we would keep his calendar because the hall keeps his calendar. The ghost shrimp would commute along their ribbed highway, antennae combing the electric breath that leaks from the trays; sometimes they would pause below and angle their bodies to catch the almost-light we make, and in those moments I would imagine worship as a kind of calibration.

There is a standing cloud of fine hydrogen bubbles in a corner chamber two rooms over, a soft, glitterless fizz that tastes of pennies. Sometimes the draft carries a hint of it to us and we brighten without meaning to. That cloud is a cousin to our kind of thinking: persistent without being urgent, generated by a fracture that never quite decides to heal. When its breath is in us, our bells ring one shade higher; we read it as morning.

There would be days, if days can be said here, when the ice’s hymn bends the beams and the beams whisper their answer down our pedicles. The city above, all winter, pushes its mood through those girders like an organ note, and we learn the lexicon by the pressure it makes in what we are attached to. A floe grinding far-off becomes the slowest tremolo, and my line brightens like a thought agreeing with itself along its length. That is a weather we have words for: open longer, open together.

I would be slow enough to collaborate with rust. Around our anchors, the oxide’s rosettes would elaborate themselves with our help: we trap a grain, we cast a shadow that slightly changes the current, we baffle a flake of manganese into a place it was not going, and over years that is architecture. Our pedicles would be written into those forms like stems into an herbarium. Once in a year, or a span the size of a year here, we would let go a misting of our own—small pellets of clear being, free for a day in a room that barely understands the tactic of free. Most would never find an edge that consents. One or two would take on the hook itself, or the underside of the trolley, and then the brow would be not only a line but a lintel. We would become gallery.

What I would not be: a thing with hunger and drama enough to make shape out of other shapes. The conger’s mathematics is more beautiful than mine and more expensive. Or a writer in the silt; I admire their calligraphy but I have chosen a higher window. Or a lamp; the lamps are gods of a tiny parochialism, and I prefer to be the congregation that understands the god as a schedule.

Now and then I would imagine other lives and try them on the way the water tries softness on a bolt head. I would think what it is to be the EXIT sign that makes an illogical migration out of shrimp, a beacon pointing to nowhere that yet arranges movement. I would think what it is to be the nacreous skin on the mesh in the side closet, with the fish bones curated in it, forever shivering when the draft rolls. I would think what it is to be the panel’s single LED that puts a star into a meter of brown water and causes the amphipods to argue in silhouette. These thoughts would be a kind of grazing for me, not food exactly, but the work of keeping the hall mapped in the way a life like mine needs maps: as responsive edges, as coherences you can hold to and say, here.

If you asked what I call the seasons, I would tell you the names are all verbs. Beheading. Rebrightening. Quiet-after-draw. Saltfall. Clear-week. Warm-one-shade. I would tell you that dusk is not low light but low insistence: fewer taps through the bones of the place, fewer microvibrations crowding the air of the water, which is to say a relief in the only weather that matters to a bell—pressure and its commentary. At those times, the whole line of us would widen and hold, as if we were catching our breath in a room that has almost none of what breath once meant.

And if you asked who I am in a place that is not a kingdom, I would say I am a practice made visible because the rail allowed it. I am the seam’s listener. I am the brow’s eyelid. I am one of the small obedient mouths that keep the room coherent by attending to difference and turning it into continuity. I appear to make light, but what I really make is permission: a way for matter to go on turning into arrangements that can afford to be slow.

I would find myself in the crane’s crossway, just below the brinicle fringe, just above the amber coals, where a whisker of current is always telling a truth about the world beyond the wall. I would be the quiet that answers it with a pale reply you could almost read. And because nothing here gets to be only itself for long, I would also be, by adjacency and consent, a participant in the eel’s dusk, the shrimp’s street, the motor’s hearth, the ice’s hymn, the films’ lacework. The hall’s creature is braid, and I would be one strong, clear strand across its brow, counting the beats, keeping time, going on.
