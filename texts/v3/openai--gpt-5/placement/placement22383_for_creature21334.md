The first time I stepped down into the basin I didn’t so much arrive as come apart. I had been a rock person, light-thin and salt-dry, raised where the wind had one note and the sun was a meager coin you could close in your fist. The path that took me rimward was a gloss-black ribbon you could mistake for dry water. I made the mistake everyone makes and breathed like you breathe anywhere—deep, proud, uncounted—and the valley answered me from under my ribs.

It is hard to love a place that makes you vomit your first word at it. Someone put a little band against the base of my skull and the world induced a tunnel around my head, an almond of hush I could carry in my jaw. When my mouth was given back to me I could taste the mineral of the hyssops though I had not yet seen one—iron like a key chewed as a child, rain as a promise.

You learn in the throat first. A woman in a cloak as thin as thought took my wrist and tapped into the handrail and named herself into my palm: five beats, silence, two, a patient off-rhythm that told me I was on her household’s line. I couldn’t parse the magnitude in my ear—the low machines making my organs misbehave and the bright machines making the air ring; the millions of small wings writing metal rain out of themselves at edge-of-night; the organ-pipes somewhere shouldering and calling the key like a low church that could only fit weather in its nave. My teacher didn’t ask me to listen. She asked me to watch the shadow stripes slide a bridge and to hold my hood just so as the dew-line passed. Catch this, she signed, and I did not, and then I did.

What would I be in such a place? I tried on as many versions of myself as there are layers of the city’s light.

There is a child in me still who would live forever behind a hyssop rib if that were a profession, a hand cupped to call each bead into the cup of my palm, laughing a silent-laugh because to let the other kind out is an offense in a ward where the key is running. I loved the taste of falling water here, the faint die-copper at the lip of the living condensers, the way a man could live by aliens of dew alone if he learned to stand where shadow paused and kept his hood in working order.

There is a span-walker in me too, tendon written into the job. In my second year I learned the habit of long limbs, the gentle offense of putting weight on what will not hold you if you shout at it and will gladly take you if you hum right. We would go out on stalk-thin paths shaped of branch-glass—floor the thickness of fruit skin, my teacher said—and we would carry mass in bands around wrist and ankle not to be pretty but to disrupt the stupid way a human can hum itself into fracture. The wind would pluck the bridge and we would pluck back, the chuck-chuck of filament and an answering, contained thrum that told you you and the world were in temporary agreement.

I apprenticed to a hushwright for three months before I understood I could not do the work and live. It is a calling for wrists softer than mine and patience shaped by water. She grew curtains of interference like others grow window-vines. She cut silence into rectangles and hood-ovals and traveling almonds. She measured a house by how little of the world made it through its skin. She could lay a band of nothing under a ward so a caravan might sleep eight breathed hours without waking to a retuning, and I loved her for it in the way a migratory creature loves a harbor. We would sit under an arch whose job it was to sing the opposite of the world, and she would let her mouth have sound there because that was the only place she would. We learned a way to say our names without moving air and used it like a small heresy, a pocket feast.

When the basin retuned the first time I was trusted with a public duty, I learned whose work my bones had been growing for. It comes without announcement. You wake and you feel your sternum as instrument and not container and all the rails in the ward talk to themselves. That day the schedule node at the phosphor moss lit my palm with a new rhythm of pronouncements—this is when the ridge will thunder, this is when the dew will string, this is when the cats will come hungry—and then lost its certainty. I was a warden of emptiness by then. They had given me a gray band for my upper arm and the right to refuse a balcony its aspiration if it crowded a chord. It was my job to keep space hollow enough for rain to be coaxed from nothing and thin enough for sound to run clean.

The law of distance is not austere, not here. It is appetite harnessed. To hold away is to invite the wind to play it, is to call the dew into hyssop throats, is to keep an aerial pasture fat for the gossamers and therefore for the manta-wings and therefore for us. I took to that law like a kind of faith. There is a shape the air should be around a tower, and you learn it with your shoulder. There is a volume a bridge deserves on either side, and you can tell it by the way your molars throb when you walk near.

In the retuning I sent children home to their almonded rooms and waved lovers out from under their arches of opposite and stood with three other wardens on a span that wanted to remember itself back into basalt. We double-tied. I had my wrist-mass tuned to a number some woman in an older ward had dreamed in the night and sent down the rails at dawn. The ground humbled and rose; the manta-lane above us ran in strange lows like whales under us, and then the basin drew a new vowel and learned its own length anew. We lost a cart that day to a hole in the sound that wandered like a charmed beast and ate what it wished of gravity. After, we poured beer at the post in thanks and in debt—we know that pressure is a god because it answers and also forgets your name.

If I am to say true who I am here, I will say this: I am a keeper of distances and a shepherd of sky.

After I learned to hold a lane open with my jaw set just so and my hood tuned to the octave that would not offend the new key, after I learned to refuse neighbors politely and with a ledger of harvests to prove the refusal was love and not spite, I was lifted into the harness of the shade-work. It was a rumor to you, maybe, the dimness writ in stripes that paused where a bridge was starving. To me it is a loom in a wind you can ride. We go up on mornings when the valley looks as if a soft-handed god had put its palm over the sun. We wear suits that convince your blood it is still inside you when you look down through fruit skin at kilometers of organized air. We can feel a kilometer as a single skin. The high array is a million black leaves that know how to tilt. We press the spine of the sky with keyed pressures and ask the dim to pretend it is weather again in just this place, now. We call rain not from clouds but from temperature itself, laying a shadow like a text over a ward and waiting for the page to wet and bleed words. We do this not because we are in love with the trick but because the hyssops are hungry and because sound runs best on a cool tongue.

There are days when I think I work for the dust and not for the people. That seeded aerosol you were told not to mouth a name for—phononic crystals afloat, the invisible reef we drive prey into and harvest from and sometimes simply marvel at when the late light decides to be custard-thick and a million motes tilt their cilia and make punctuation out of evening—that dust knows. We feed it sugars it likes and anisotropic treats that coax it to take up particular stances. It answers beyond its engineering sometimes. I have sung-density into a quadrant and felt it sing back a fraction off and learned why ten minutes later when a strider with brass-skin walked under and the pollen vortex didn’t tear the way it should and the manta that rode that corridor slipped and righted like a thought correcting itself. Tell me that is not memory. Tell me that is not weather with a point of view.

I am not alone in talking to what pretends to be inanimate and is only shy. Physicians here murmur to bones and walls, and the walls return a tone of health or warning. The luthiers of bodies will tune a fracture like a cracked drum and leave behind a line of nanolace that refuses this ward’s foolish frequencies. The hushwrights I failed to join make banded ovals that keep a baby’s mouth safe from the world and in exchange they never shout. We merchants of nothingness and air-love meet once a year under the basalt columns and press our fingers into their corrugations like an old text we have come to love for what we cannot hear in it. If I have a faith it is the faith of that pressure you feel in your ribs when you kneel there—a vowel you hold with your whole chest because to let it loose would break the valley like glass.

Does the basin ask a person to be less animal? No. It asks you to remember you are one. The husher-cats will walk your sleeping porch as distortions if you are careless with your lanes; we keep them out with fields so fine the valley barely bothers to notice. The katydids will make you believe in weather made of instruments; I have waited for their button-chests to open and for dusk to throw its metallic rain, a million small keys set in a single lock and turned slow until the ward purrs. I have scraped a pans’ worth of aeromass from a feeder and eaten it as cold bread and kelp because there was no time to go home. I have held out an arm and let a gossamer drift into its ring and buzz me into a small laugh I couldn’t let past my teeth.

My name, if you asked my hand, is seven taps, then the silence that marks the rail I belong to, then three—my household’s beat nested in my ward’s beat nested in the city’s hush. I have a lover with a mouth like a comfort band. We rent arches that sing the inverse of everything the basin is all week and use sound like a resource we can spend there, like heat in winter. We argue for days about which bridge’s lintel drips sweetest at a particular wind and promise the child who will be sent to guard that empty lane after us that they will like the work and mean it.

What happens to us in the end is also a choice about pressure. Rockfolk burn and bury because the wind steals there. Some of us here go to dust on purpose. We make ourselves small in a useful way, engineered down into a cloud that can remember our beat. We are shaken into the aero-reef on a day the shade is generous and our friends eat bread-kelp and pour beer at the rail and trust us to listen to them even after we forget our own throats. Some of us choose the other direction. We are laid in a garden of hush and the almond that kept us when we were babies travels with our mouth again and people come there not to hear us but to feel the hole where we might have been. I have not decided. I think the sky knows me already. I think the long black leaves overhead are more god than rumor and that god has a good memory for wrists. On days when the Retuning comes and I find myself standing in a tuning harness with the valley and my bones both considering a different length, I want to be dust. On evenings under arches when my hushwright lets a foolish laugh out and the arch forgives her, I want to be a pocket of quiet the size of an almond traveling with a mouth forever.

So where do you find me if you come? On a morning no brighter than a thoughtful bruise, I will be walking the fruit-skin path three spans left of the deliberate river, hood trailing counterphase like a smart shadow. The moss at the node will stain the basalt with the day’s schedule and my palm will read it home. I will tap you a greeting in the rail because I will see in your stance that you are still counting breaths wrong, and I will gesture you to stand with me when the shadow writes the dew out of nothing over a hyssop’s lip so you can taste the iron in it and understand how here water is never exactly present and never exactly absent and neither are we.

If you ask my trade, I will say I keep space hollow enough to rain. I refuse neighbors kindly and coax dimness like a weather you can live under. I lean in a harness far above what anyone with a polite heart would call ground and press the spine of the sky to make it sing a line the hyssops can drink and the manta-wings can ride and a child can sleep under. In the hand I am seven beats, silence, three. In the world I am a hush moving through the basin’s throat, a keeper of pressure, a gardener of absence, a person who will give you their almond for a minute because I remember the first time the valley reached into my chest and taught me how large a vowel a ribcage can hold.
