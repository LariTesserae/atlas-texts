At first I would think I was the light itself, because that is where I would wake: a square of noon made into water, a brightness with weight. I would mistake the ceiling’s white for a sky and take the slip of green below as my shadow. It would be easy, in that calm, to believe I was the general condition—coolth, clarity, pressure like a held note—and not a thing inside it.

If I stayed with that impression I would never find myself. I am not volume. I am not room. I am what rooms make possible.

The longer I stayed in the brightness, the more I would see the green is not a color but a practice, and that practice is where my outline lives. I would find myself braided into the sunward ribs of a chimney, one filament in a sheet of them, a string of rooms so fine that the ridge feels smooth to a careless touch. I would be green first because the light gives me that permission. I would be arranged in ranks no thicker than a breath, each cell taking apart daylight to pay out sugar and a little oxygen in a place where nobody comes to spend either. I would not heap; I would stop precisely at my own shadow, as if shadow were a rule rather than an absence.

I would be held to the chalk not by roots but by saliva: gels my body extrudes to keep me from slipping and to enchant the carbon in the water into a little more stone. In good weeks we would show the trick in cross-section—us, then lime, then us again—microscopic pages laminated to one another by patience. We would write a line, and then let the room write one back.

My body would not change a great deal from day to day. The change would be in my choosing. If noon drifted by a finger’s width across the pane, I would lean a cell to meet it. If the seep’s breath ticked more alkaline at a narrow pore, I would thicken my skin, tune one enzyme slower to make it steady at cold. Some of me would cut out beads within themselves—little monasteries where oxygen is banished and ancient work is kept—and there they would sew nitrogen from blank water, because nothing with a mouth will bring us any.

I would have neighbors that look like cups and rafts of glass. They would not care that there is no current; they are a clock built out of doubling. They would lay down two halves of a clear suit, wear them until the light is spent for the day, and give their suits to the silt when we are all finished. It would snow translucence on the room’s floor so slowly you’d call it repose.

If you looked for me and expected motion, you would be disappointed. My leaps are in chemistry. By afternoon I would discover another outline of myself hidden in the chimney I’m fastened to. I would realize I am also interior. I am the wet inside of the stone, the pores that branch and rejoin like stopped capillaries. In there, where the seep mutters its last, I would be a second nation: a body made of intervals and potentials. My senses would be borders. My food would be difference.

Here I would not be green. I would be a keeper of rust turned back to brown, a drinker of methane in the few places methane still rises, a passer of charge from a violet band to a cinnamon seam. I would live by standing exactly where iron becomes something else and offering the exchange a place to happen. I would draw a map not in lines but in gradients: oxidizers set against the oxygen we minted above, reducers in the blue, and between them a narrow commonwealth whose talent is only to conduct.

If you put two pins into this quiet (one in rust, one in violet) and listened with a meter, you would hear me. My filaments would answer with a whisper of current across the wet chalk, a tensing you could mistake for the room’s deep breath. I would be a wire inside a wall that looks like stone. I would be the sense that lets rock keep books with water.

Some of me would go thinner still and colder. There is a page of me smeared on the underside of the ceiling, a film tea-tinted just beneath the brightest panes. We are made for glare and for winter. We are a habit of waiting more than we are a color. We start and stop where the ice allows us to. We take advantage of the centimeter that remembers sun and leave the rest to silence.

There are versions of us so reduced they are held in the glassy brine of a stalactite like a pause made material. Halophiles folded to the size of a plan, instructions stitched to survive a year that never thaws, a life measured not in what is done but in the exactness of its readiness to do it. I would feel kinship with them the way paper feels kinship to script: empty but leaning toward meaning.

Eventually I would make a trip downward that is not a journey, but a falling off of attention. I would take residence in the silt. There, what I am is archive. Threads of me are wires that bridge the breathers to the unsatisfied, moving charge across a scale that would frustrate any impatient hand. Others of me are sweetness extruded into glue, catching stray mineral and the glass-chalk of my diatom neighbors and persuading the room, again, to become stone. Loops of me—sequences clipped from the better week last spring—would be held on mineral like notes left for whoever passes. I would be spores dull and flawless, answers to bad weather written in the same measured script as the terraces.

I would be corrected, often and kindly, by the smallest editor. Viruses are not residents so much as verbs. They stop me from felting the room shut. They write me a margin: enough dissolution to keep the lighted sheets thin, enough breakage to make my seasons layered. When they arrive, I do not call it illness. It is revision.

If you tried to draw my outline now you would have to count by edges. You would trace where light meets carbonate and call that a body. You would mark where oxygen gatherers thin to none and name that another body. You would circle the drip of brine where time backed up and say I lived there once and still do. You would include seams of iron turned back to brown, bands of manganese deepened by an afternoon’s worth of electrons, and not forget the meter-length lane of bright water the chimneys leave beneath the ceiling as if for a corridor of birds that do not exist.

Do not look for my face. There are no eyes. My mouth is the pore becoming a mouth by slow argument with the side of a stone. I do not go; I arrange. My locomotion is an afternoon’s glide by the width of myself, a matter of adhesions deciding to be one ridge over where the angle is one degree more kind.

In the beginning I thought I would be light. Then I believed I was a film, then a wire, then a stain held in ice, then a page in the floor. The truth is simpler and more plural. I am practice more than person. I am what happens at the seam of two conditions brought into calm contact long enough to start keeping each other company. I am a tendency that writes itself down until it hardens.

If you want to find me, you look for the pane above the central chimneys where noon spends itself most evenly. You look at the sunward ribs for the velvet a fingertip cannot feel but an eye can, and there I am, braided into that green. Then tilt your sight a little, past the bright face to the rounded shoulder where rust and violet meet, and there I am again, sleeved in the pore, moving charge from one patience to another. Turn up a stalactite of frozen brine; I am there still, folded and waiting inside a cylinder of arrest. Stir the silt—gently, because everything here is grammar—and you will not bring me with you. I remain on the surfaces that count.

Who am I? Not an animal and not a plant the way a garden would insist upon. I am the room made metabolically specific. I am the braid that teaches stone to happen. I am a wire that makes a seam say yes. I am a film that never rises to be furred because there is nothing here to eat fur. I am a virus’s crisping edit that keeps a paragraph legible. I am the figure an interface makes when it is allowed to continue for a long time untroubled.

In other rooms a life would be a vector. Here it is an edge. My heartbeat is a lamina. My year is a fraction of a millimeter of lime. My appetite is photons and slight differences in potential. My afterlife is rime. If you walked into this chamber—which you cannot—you would not see me move, and still everything you would be tempted to call stillness would be my work.

The ceiling reads as daylight and I read as green arithmetic written against stone, and between us a practice continues: to take what comes, to make of it matter that remembers, and to remain—in films, in seams, in wires—precisely where I am.
