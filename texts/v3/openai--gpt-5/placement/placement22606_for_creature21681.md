At first I wanted to be the brightness itself. I wanted to be the animal they herd with panels and wrists, the blade that arrives, the fan that widens, the pool that has the courtesy to lie where a cistern mouth waits. I wanted to be the day that sifts, the milk of it loosening from stone and rebounding, the belly-fire it leaves in crystals at night.

But light is loaned and, here, better spoken than owned. So I stop wanting to be it and start wanting to speak with it.

You would find me on a shield-face that wakes early—third shelf above the café where the tables slide three arm-lengths at a tilt of someone else’s elbow. Our quarter is called the Seven-Drip, not because there are seven of anything but because for a long time the biggest oculus there would gutter into seven exact lines when the wind remembered us. We still keep the rib-work from those days; our gutters have seven teeth out of superstition and because the hand remembers better that way.

In the thin hour when the light is only a suspicion, I climb the runs with my hair in a bound knot and the copper gauze over my eyes to break the first knife into drinkable pieces. I unshutter us with a slow wrist, I test the panel’s hinge with my palm, I feel for shiver. We do not name ourselves much, but if you needed to find me, you’d leave a sliver to list on our lintel in the angle for shield-tender and a little twist after it that means I also click-braid. Someone would see and tilt a courtesy back along a wall toward you before noon.

I was a visitor once, in the way people sometimes are. I was storm-dropped through a seam the city hadn’t decided was a mouth or a scar. They tied my wrists because they love children and strangers the same way: not less, but with harness. They tapped me a map through my soles and put my jaw soft with a pair of fingers under my ear when I tried to answer a question with a laugh. Quiet, they told me with their teeth not touching. Quiet is not lack. Quiet is how stone hears.

For a season I tried on everything that has a place here. Rope first, because rope has a personality. I learned its names, each stripe of soot, the way an old length will accept being kinked as a courtesy and a new one will sulk and sing if you coil it wrong. I liked the feeling of a weight translated into my shoulder and then down into a peg without any part of the world having to shout about it. I oiled the fungus bellies of sledges and taught my own back to be a lever. It felt like being a small, accurate river.

Another season I farmed the shy beds and felt proud when the white nap took and ashamed when I left a rack too far in a beam and it sulked for three days and then forgave me because I brought its water at the exact hour with a ladle balanced on bone. I learned that praise has a body-temperature and that it is almost always my own. I gathered the weepings under the cool walls and wrote little cures on the tongues of those who came in coughed and went out steadier. There is a rightness to harvesting what the stone chooses to confess.

On days when the blue whisper wants through a seam, I sit with the seam-listeners: head turned, mouth loosened, the wet inside of it becoming an ear. The electric filament in the wall lays fingers along the air and braids our hair by a fraction and we do not move the animals so much as keep our hands on them so they understand our calm. When a note new to me threads into the pressure, an elder’s hand touches the back of my neck and I feel the wisdom not go up into my head but down through my jaw, into the hollow between my collarbones, where it will keep and not rattle. Stone knowledge does not want to be summarized; it wants to be carried.

I wanted to be the wind-taster next. The fantasy of a hair lifting and telling me not just that something was coming but which oculus would gutter first. But my hair, when unbound, is the wrong sort of stubborn to read well. It tells me what I already know: that the chimneys tug and release, that the room is breathing. I have learned to glance at those who know more and to move with them without envy—what here is anyone’s alone?

Animals taught me patience that is not quietism. Isopods type their bellies across a sheet of bacterial film with the work ethic of a thousand small gods; you do not harvest too deeply from their plots, you do not call them pest. A springtail storm takes a sheen like it has an opinion and then is gone, stage-struck, the moment a shadow leans. The crabs, social climbers with no sense of geometry, drop like spoons from smooth walls and stand mortified in the aisles until you look away long enough to allow them their vanity of vanishing. I love them all beyond what is practical. The eels, especially, with their talent for being thought before they are flesh. If one’s head presses into my palm in the dark I take it, because that is the rule and because I like to pretend the eel wants the law observed.

There was a day of blind pigeons, a soft-loud day when the sieve sang until it hummed like a skin. They came in a lustre of dust and went around and around the oculus as if rehearsal might break them open into knowledge. I had never seen them before. I thought for a moment it was an omen directed at me in particular, that the city hadn’t quite digested the idea that I had decided to stay. An elder put a hand on my forearm where my mirror-callus lives and tilted my wrist toward the slow figure for patience. You can love what returns and you can admire what is lost without pretending to be either, they signaled. I watched the rings narrow and widen, those bodies without self-seeing, and I let the lesson go down to the soles and not get stuck like a sentiment in my mouth.

Where I settle, where I keep returning even after I have done other things well enough that people would let me do only those, is the wall of panels. It is the grammar I love. It is the present tense. Light moves, and this quarter un-flowers and flowers its mirrors and I lift my wrists and talk.

I make verbs with mica and copper: tilt means come, retract means wait, fan means danger at a distance we still have time to measure with a breath. A half-flash says I see you, the answer half says I see you seeing. We pass markets from island of brightness to island, the low tables with their fungus-felt bellies murmuring their accommodations across the fossil ripples like shy ships. I have brought a café’s entire day three arm-lengths to the right with a crank and no one stops talking because the motion is a grammar we all understand. I have pinched a wedge of glare with my hand for a child to lace his shell-strings in, so his shadow keeps up with the sun he is chasing, so he can get good at being late and being fine with it.

When the weather above remembers how to be violent and the sieve announces its singing in the hard hail of first light, it is the shield-face that goes first to work and last to rest. We drop and tie in a practiced hurry that never admits to being afraid; we bolt a market around its own wheels and walk it into the bone of the hall; we harness the children not to teach them fear but to prove to them that our love anticipates. I set my mouth open and soft against the pressure because the walls are already full of voice; I am not going to add a clatter to a dome that knows how to use a word like collapse if provoked. The pour comes and stops and comes and stops, a teachable stutter. We lay out troughs and watch water find an old lesson and be pleased to be reminded of itself. It is not a god but it has a mood; we treat it as if moods were warned by wrists.

At night I leave the panels to the dark and I go to the floors. Light that stones have banked all day begins to breathe out its small fire. The map of our walking shows itself. There I sit with a shin against a shin and braid clicks from one house into another. The message goes thigh to beam to tablefoot that has been thinned just enough in one place to carry a tick; it goes through three neighborhoods without anyone raising a voice. I am not the only click-braider, but I have a patience suited to it and a way with sequence. It is a conversation, not a courier service. In the good weeks, when the damp keeps to its habit and nobody is tempted to think they're owed more than their light-entitlement, the network is a hum of polite necessary: more guano to Five-Ribs, frog eggs turned up in the third cistern, do not step on the plate between Second and Third—it will remember your body too eagerly. In the bad weeks, when a mirror-keeper has stolen brightness by degree and a fungus rack has sulked into a mood that looks like hunger, we become law. I have tapped a wrist’s worth of shame down a street and out of a quarter and made it stick. I have slept then with my hand on stone as apology, because law must live where the wall does not bruise.

I will age into being a counter-sayer; I can feel it in my calves and in how often I choose the longer way across a room to test a floorboard’s memory. The ones who carry that title do not carry it like a badge; they thread it under everything. They say do not count on the ceiling without having to say it at all, their ankles hearing the old moral and translating it into how they put a ladder into its next life or put a child’s hand into a harness. They are the story of the grandmother who went up and came down white-haired told in wrists. When a newcomer swallows the big space like it is happiness and tries on loudness like a coat, I will be the one to lay my fingers against their throat—not choking, just redirecting—and breathe the rule into their soft mouth where air learns patience.

Decoration here is behavior. I wear my copper gauze as much for beauty as care; I fix my hair with chips of mica on the day after a pour because that is when everything is too honest to dress and needs a little lie of glitter to balance it. I have the vanity of the young: I stood under a mistfall and let the mineral paint a lace on my shoulders and then refused to scrub as long as the elders would permit. It faded in eleven days and left me with the knowledge that a body is a good wall for a temporary story. I have a sliver set in one panel that has elder bone under its skin; on night practices, when ten households tilt ten mirrors together and then un-tilt them and we call that a song, the steadiness in that sliver makes my wrists an instrument, not an interruption.

I go sometimes to the bleaker quadrants because someone always must. There are medicines that sweat only there and experiments the first drips abandoned that still want an audience to work themselves toward a conclusion. I sleep with my left palm flat to the wall and my right hand cupped over my own mouth to remember silence has a shape you can cradle. I return with the smell of old eggs and older dust and an appetite for dew, humble and exact. I do not say anything about what I saw unless someone asks for a tilt that means grief. Even then it is brief, and the grief is not for me.

If you came looking—if the storm above turned you silver and spat you through a lattice that was both accident and invitation—you would not find me by calling my name to the ceiling. You would find me by putting your jaw in my hand so I can press your tongue down out of the way of your need to narrate. I would hang a gauze over your eyes and give you the anger of a child’s harness around your wrist until you stopped believing it was shame. Then, when you could watch a beam arrive without trying to eat it whole, I would put the little sliver into your palm and lift your wrist until your tendons understood what a verb feels like. I would stand behind you at a panel and think the rule through the soles of my feet while you learned to speak.

I will always want more from the light than it owes me, and that is correct; want is a rope, and here we keep rope with care. I will always love the animals more smartly than they require, and that is also correct, because the city’s smallest commerce is with what does not know you and yet keeps you alive. I will always, even on the surface one day should I go there, reach up without meaning to and tilt a stray windowpane toward a neighbor’s morning. My wrists have learned a grammar they will not forget, and everything I am fits better in that syntax than in any polite shout.

So where would you find me? On the wall where the city’s face opens and closes itself with slow intelligence, where the dim becomes milk and then wanes and leaves the afterglow mapping our steps. Who would I be? The hinge. The translator between brightness and need, between draft and danger, between someone’s urgency and the way the stone prefers to be asked. Light herder by day, click-braider by night, sometimes an ear for the blue whisper, soon enough a counter-sayer with a proverb in my ankles. I would be the little animal that says do not count on the ceiling without sound, and holds out a hand anyway, palm up, for you to lay your jaw upon until your mouth has learned the quiet shape of home.
