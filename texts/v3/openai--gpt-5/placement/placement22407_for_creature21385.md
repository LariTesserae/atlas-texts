At first I would only be a cheek and a palm.

Before I had a title, I learned this place like you learn a person you love: up close, by touch, by letting my bones keep the beat of its sentences. I put my face to the bark where it goes dark with condensate and felt the tide arrive three bays down through a thickness of lignin like a voice across a crowded room. I learned what the belt sounds like when the leather is thirsty and what a contented bearing says when you rest the back of a knuckle against its block and it gives back the same heat as your skin, no more, no less. I counted the governor’s fruits under my breath until the numbers wore smooth into a hum that matched the flywheel’s patience. I did not know yet what I was. I knew only that the thrum took my ribs and kept them for itself and did not give them back when I walked away.

If you asked where to find me, you would look up from the center pit along the west stair, where the bark goes glossy on its drip-side and the air is full of the sweet tan of sap. There—a little shelf extruded by the tree itself, big enough for a tin cup and a jar of beeswax, narrow enough that you have to turn your shoulders to pass. That is my corner. Above it runs the vine that is not anymore a vine: copper taught into bark’s habits, sleeved in what began as cambium and now is compromise. It hums with what it carries. It pulls when the tree grows and relents when the collar lets it. There is a ceramic handwheel there with a history mapped into the smooth of its spokes, controlling the taproot that keeps the belts supple when the air is mean. If there is a lever in this bay that is mine, that is it.

What I am lives where pull meets patience. The old ones call us release-keepers. The graftwrights say we are the ones who talk sense to metal. The turners say we are the ones who keep them from inheriting disasters someone else mortgaged a year ago. In the ledger we are driftwrights, because the tree moves how a living thing moves—everywhere, all at once—and we are the ones who measure and say yes, now, give.

I carry a pocket of wedges cut from a wood that believes in being wedged. The edges are greased with beeswax that smells of a summer outside the leaves. My other pocket holds a little set of brass shims with their corners rounded so they will not catch and make the bark angry. I have a bone-handled knife whose blade is made to respect sap. I wear a porcelain tooth on a string against my skin because the wirehands say it is good luck to keep something that refuses to conduct in a place that longs to.

Every change here is slow enough to write in a book and fast enough to hurt you if you pretend it is stone. The iron rings that teach the ribs their arcs need help remembering they are not owners. The collars at each joint go tight all by themselves, season by season, as the tree swallows a lesson you asked it to learn. Release before the wet is not a suggestion; it is the way we keep from hearing that alarming report of fiber parting with decision and watching a decade of careful persuasion tear itself into a new plan mid-flood.

So this is my work, more verb than noun: release.

You find me with my face close to the purple-brown bark, thumbs pressed along the line where wood presses its cheek to iron. The joint speaks. It says: I am warmed by the engine’s patient heart; I am curled a little under the steady pull of that graceless elbow of pipe you left hung on me two equinoxes ago; I am content enough, but would be kinder to myself if I had a breath. The wax in my pocket is the yes I carry. I work it into the tiny places where friction has set up a shrine to itself. I work a wedge in with a tender mallet you could mistake for a toy. The graftwright on the other side holds their strap just tight enough to say, I am with you, I am listening too. Between us the collar remembers its job is not to clutch but to embrace.

I was a child here with a tin under a leaf, catching the clean that happens when hot speaks to green and decides to let the edge of itself go cool. I was set to keep a porcelain tooth white for a year and learned that things which do not stain are a mercy. An old woman put my hand flat to the column and said, Learn who you are, then ask to be the part of that person this place needs. For a while I thought I was a belt. For a while I thought I was the small fan that cuts the heat like a dragonfly’s wing, the only quick joy in a room full of patience. For one terrible wet-year I was sure I was a rule, and I preached myself to anyone who would listen.

I am still half made of rules. I keep them in my mouth like mints so I do not forget to carry their taste to the right ears. Never cut a bearer root. No flame past the yellow. Release collars before the wet. They are not commandments; they are treaties we keep with a bright and stubborn organism so that we can live inside its weather and let it live with our weight. I say them to myself while I am bored so that boredom does not turn into arrogance. I say them to the teenagers who come through pretending not to be learning while they drink cold from a bottle that forgets its own condensation between sips.

On the days when the tide adds salt to the tannin and the pump leans deep into its work, you will find me counting the governor’s gossip to see if we are flatter than we should be. I watch the brass pins that someone set a decade ago, each marked with a number and a scratch, and fold my new scratch in at the edge of their old ones. I keep a ledger that the bay will eventually eat the corners of if I am not careful. Three pins drifted a finger-width this quarter; ribs over A-ring went glossy high and threw a shine like surprise; the west handwheel wants a washer in spring if we do not want its voice to crack.

On the boring days—and may most of them be boring—you might pass me without knowing. All of Seventeen is edges that try to step aside and let you not see who keeps them soft. I will be the brown of someone’s rolled sleeve behind a pipe. I will be the weight at the end of a strap, singing a pull into a stubborn piece of living alphabet so that the next season it will cross the line without sulking. I will be the person who does not name the pump by its nickname before a flood because I am superstitious enough to know that the words we say teach the machines the ways we expect them to show off.

Once, before I knew enough to be afraid, I forgot Release for long enough to see what forgetting breeds. The wet came early, and the collars on the north rib went stubborn the way old wrists go stubborn in winter. They held their breath; the wood shouldered down and in; the bay developed a tilt you could feel in your gut if you stood with your eyes closed on the west catwalk. We sang it back over a month with straps and wedges and wax and apology. We wrote the story on a brass pin with a scratch that is not straight—shame has a wobble to its mark—and now the bark is growing an eyebrow over it as if to say, I remember you; I am not cross, but I remember.

I am neighbor to the wirehands who haul copper through a path that believes in curves. When they bring the liana down to my handwheel and ask if the mist is generous today, I bend my ear to the belt’s whisper and use my yes or no to keep leather kind. I drink with the turners who can lay two fingers on a pipe and tell me if the elbow is holding heat like a grudge. We do not talk much while we work because the bay does not love voices that try to rise above it. We talk with a hand on bark and a tilt of the chin toward a gauge that would rather be seen than heard. When someone has to spit, we spit where the algae will be grateful.

In the dry galleries where we sleep, if we sleep, the night is wrong when the bay goes quiet. It almost never does. The watch-lamps keep their patient halos, the belts mutter to themselves like people having long marriages. In my hammock above the annex loft I dream in slow machinery; my body has taken on the cadence of the big flywheel even at rest. I keep a shirt turned inside its own sleeves so mildew will not court it, and a cup with a dent from a fall that taught me the difference between luck and design. The tree is taking back the nail it hangs on, and I will let it. I like the way its bark looks curious when it eats a piece of our small insistences.

If I am anything more than a person with wax under my nails, I am a hinge.

The hinge between plant and iron, between hurry and course-of-days, between what pulls and what bears. I do not own the hinge; I make sure it moves. On some mornings I feel very like the notch in a bone that a tendon crosses, polished by time, essential only in that nothing else will do quite the same job in quite the same spot. On some nights I feel like a sign bolted where eyes fall without thinking, an animal of bright paint and tin that lives by continual small, accurate alarms. Mostly I feel like what happens every time the tree takes a breath—inward, outward—and we decide not to notice because we made a promise to notice in the ledger instead and not make a drama out of a life.

There will be a day when the bark grows a lid over the plate with my name, and the screws I set will pearl with lichen and the edges of what I did will go kind. On that day the wedge I favored will be someone else’s wedge and the bone handle will fit their hand differently. The brass pins will go on marking drift in lines that charm the eye even if the eye does not know their language. The bay will keep its patient heartbeat and the belts will keep their appetite and the little fan will still be the show-off soprano in a room built for basso.

Where would I be? Hanging half in the green and half in the iron, knee on a knot-step coaxed into being by someone whose hands were steadier than mine when they cut the first scribe. My cheek to the bark, my hand on the handwheel. Counting, releasing, persuading, listening. Being the little breath we make on purpose, over and over, so the big breathing can continue without tearing. Who would I be? The person who keeps the verb between us alive: release. The one who tends the place where we and the tree and the machine meet and agree not to hurt each other.

In the inventory the city keeps of itself, they would not mark me as a part. But if you stood with me at shift’s end and let the thrum walk home inside your chest, you would know me then by how your steps fell into the beat and by how, without thinking, you would put your palm to the nearest bit of living and ask what it was feeling, and wait for the answer before you moved.
