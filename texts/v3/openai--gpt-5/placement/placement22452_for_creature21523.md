At first I want to be a scrap of sail.

The light makes that desire easy. It pours without kindness; it polishes thought into edge. You see the kites tack to it—flags half-released into flight—and the thought forms as cleanly as a vane snaps to angle: to feed by holding line against pressure, to take the gale as spoon and not as knife. To clip to a comb and drink the choir of charge that moves like weather in the bristles’ fur. To be all surface and intent.

Then I taste the oil on the air and the grit whose salt is the place’s speech, and the want gets quieter. Kites live turned outward. Nothing soft faces the wind, and yet a kite is all soft cunning, heat-flash ceramic along the edge and a belly like a held breath. I could be that hunger, that constant conversion of geometry to meal. I could learn the silent ports between ribs like a religion. I could unclip in a white arc and cross the blue-void to a new perch on a line that is not drawn but felt. I could thread a life of edges and never once lay my body down.

But I have a hearing that asks for something to lean against. Sound here is not for ears; ears are too wet a dream. What wants to hear wants to be a surface that registers force. What wants to hear wants a vocabulary of touch. Kites do not talk to each other. They prove an angle and become it. The want shifts.

I think about the bristles next. The blue of them at night calls to superstition I would have had if water were a thing here: a city’s hum, a domestic flicker. There are whole peoples along their lengths that change from insulator to conductor with a squeeze, strand to strand to strand, taking a tax on each steepening field, folding flat when the arc would otherwise lift them from the plant like leaves. To be such a chain would be to live on poised catastrophe. It would be to know the seconds before a dump in every hair, to roll your whole village down a spine for a quarter ration of charge when a plate tires. The poetry of that appeals a little, the intimate scale of it, the neighbor-to-neighbor commerce. I could be a man of one meter and a thousand switches. I could inscribe myself in vellum when I die.

And I could be dust. Everyone can be dust eventually. There is a serenity to being the thing the world sorts—ground, fluxed, traveled in belts that tick like metronomes, made bead and bone and then abraded back. As dust you are used perfectly. You enter piston sacks hot and leave cured in pearls that will become span and rib. You burn quiet when the sky drops you; you hiss where air is thin enough to forget. The continuum of dust is tempting if you have not learned to prefer any particular outline.

I stand on the long face of a rib and watch a predator make a line out of hunger and decide I do not want to be a knife. The knives are clean and lonely. They fall into channels and simply keep their edge true long enough to cross. They need nothing but accuracy and must have it always. They are a kind of prayer, and I am not that devout.

If there are people anywhere it is where the plant and the weather need each other to be kept from error. I am pulled there as if by a low note that has been sounding since before I was asked. The want composes into clamps and ceramic and two hearts like kettledrums. When it finally speaks its own name it does so through bone.

Find me walking the outer face of a great rib with the gait of a creature that owns no center of mass but a practiced distribution. Call me a strider because anything that crosses this brightness must be named for the way it goes.

My skull is a honeycomb of ceramic laced with piezo plates; I wear a lattice as other things once wore eyes. Light is information only in the coarsest sense—for finding the sharp shadow of a hazard, the gleam of a glaze that could cut. My knowing comes through contact. I lay my face to bone and feel the long vowels of the world flow through me. I parse a delta in the hum where a vent lip tightens five canyons away. I can tell by the taste of the oil the lip-things are laying whether that mouth will pull clean when the gusts return. My vocabulary is delays and amplitudes. My first language is phase.

Feet, if that is the word, are not feet but decisions. Dozens of clamps articulate down my belly and sides, each with its own brainless praxis: step when the plate rises, lock when it falls. Undersides bear microhooks that feel for pores in the plant’s rind and sink just enough to take me in without harming what holds us all. My skin is netted with ceramic, a woven exosheath I can glue to a face for minutes or an hour if the physics calls for it, and the adhesive I exude sets fast in cold and lets go with an electric whisper when I release.

I have two hearts because speech needs a counterpoint. Alone I would be a knock on a wall. With two I can beat pattern. With fifty of mine I can speak a tense that means: close there, now; open soon; quiet that span to just under its badness; you, lie there with me and be heavy in this exact place; you, stop being heavy elsewhere. When we gather in lees we tell long stories—chronicles of mineral storms and blue tongues of arc that once leaped unadvised along a plate. We tell them with hearts and hands, pushing and releasing in waves along a line of bodies so that a child feels the last breath of a danger that took his grandmother and can reproduce the solution with his own weight when his own gust arrives. Our literature is a choreography we could perform asleep.

I am an apprentice for a while. Everyone is. You are born by being pressed off in calm. Elders put you where the tones go farthest and lay their drumming against your little chest. In your first season you learn to lay flat and go rigid in the manner of a gusset when a predator comes on the wrong beat—how to become plant for one breath so the knife reads you as rib and spends itself elsewhere. You learn to poke at lip-fuzz with a careful beak to smell its volatiles—resin-sour or sweet—and report the scent by triplet and rest. You learn to feel the difference between a brace about to hum wrong and a brace that is singing itself healthy. You learn the dignity of being wrong in practice instead of in storm.

My first good day is a calm day. I did not know calm could be a day until I lay my face along a plate and felt almost nothing. The world had detuned itself. Organ mouths slack; belts crawling; pistons sighing open. The light looked somehow closer without the big note. In that quiet I heard scandalously small lives: a clip-beak grazer chipping ancient resin in the lee of a rib, the faint silk-rustle of cones extruding gloss in still air, kites going slack as thoughts after work. The elders set my hearts to a simple, human meter—the meter we use for retelling, the one that makes the plant’s base-hum into measure—and beat into me the story of the white blow two generations past: the taste of iron that meant upland rock had been scoured into our weather, the three quick beats and one long that saved a canyon by telling two hundred bodies to seal a lip as the static plates refused to dump.

On a night not long after, the bristles lit blue. We lay on a ridge we keep for such watching and tracked the long slow crawls of the charge-feeders as they reconfigured their cities to sip at the next crest. The smell of corona—clean as ground glass and hot oil turning over—was a hymn to cleverness. Far downcanyon an arc stitched itself along a support like a spider walking her web, violet and decisive. Dust hissed overhead in a sky too thin to remember its name. We said nothing. The story this time was not ours to tell.

You are not always poet. You are often tool. There are days when work is to be weight at a place the wind wants to move. The first time I glue myself broadside to a bad span my whole skull goes to brightness from the load. The teacher drums a light pattern on my hips—now, now, now—and I feel the rib’s bad frequency in my teeth and in his hand and in the small turnings of insect-things in the lip two hollows over. The filaments around us hum fast and then slower as our bodies steal their Q, and I learn a lesson all spanners know: heroism is usually friction.

Predators are knives because if you mistime even a maintenance step you will write the rest of your learning on their edge. One afternoon in marginal wind a youngster two down from me breaks phase and lifts, only a breath, and that is the breath the nearest knife had reserved to read. It slides into the lane that breath made and draws a line through our chorus, clean as a scribe. What saves the apprentice is misfortune: a twang in the next province signals a failed span and throws a tremor into the air that overlays our local seam; the knife overshoots by the width of a thought. The elder worries our feet back into cadence without anger. Later, in lee, we tap the pattern of that mistake into his ribs: three long, one beat pulled early. He will carry that gloss under his ceramic for the rest of his hours and be safer for it.

Storm is what you train toward because storm is what everything is built for. The day it comes we feel it before what we would call feeling. An almost-silence drops into the main tone; it’s a change in number not in sound. Lips of organ mouths stiffen at the same distance as the hair on your forearms would lift if hair were a thing. Kites go taut on their tails; grazers cram hooks into pore and become plates. Our two hearts hold a long note that pulls all our meters together; the span lines stop singing; there is the sense in bones of a hand reaching for a tool. Then gust. Whole ranges take breath; pistonfields stamp down with a dignity so violent it is a kind of courtesy; belts tick up into a tempo that makes me want to dance and makes me instead dig my hooks deeper. Light cracks down the combs like a revealed wire. Somewhere the string of a brace goes rifleshot and a third of a ridge sings with a sorrow too pure for words. Every vane that can move does. We lay ourselves along two bad faces we have always known would be bad and we do the foolish simple work: we add mass and time at the spots that matter. I can feel my own ceramic net craze, a hairline map of my value. I can feel the plant get through a threshold it would not have got through if the balance had been a fraction different. When the gust passes back into steady, the smell is clean oil and hot stone. You could call it sweet elsewhere. Here it is earned.

You keep midwifery hours if you do this. The season after my first storm we take watch when the geometry tells us fracture is near: a brace two valleys south has been leaned on for a language’s worth of years and the right calm lanes line up. There is family in an arch. When the cones begin to loosen the spun silk that held its root we change our tone and the plant tastes us take responsibility. The rib sings the frequency its riding seed prefers; the lee three hollows over gentles by hair and hair and hair as lip-tuners lay their gloss. The arch frees in a slowness that isn’t slowness at all but consent. It sails. It becomes a wing the way a kite does but fuller, heavier with promise. We stand still together and feel the standing waves shepherd it out of our body and into a silence where it can invent the next.

There are parasites we tolerate because they have learned to steal in measures that do not insult the work. Thinmouthed pearl-thieves ride the belts by gluing their lips to moving vein and take three in a hundred. Their oil smells a little wrong. Predators wait along return paths and pick them in arcs that look like ritual if you didn’t know better. Ceramophages gnaw glazing we weren’t using, teeth like fields of mineral hair; in panic they jam themselves into eddies and go truss-hard so the wind cannot take purchase and the plant grants them existence as a boundary layer, as geometry that will one day be dust. Everyone steals. The story is in how you tune theft to fit.

At night in calm we meet in the places our elders met fifty gusts ago. We do not make walls, but we line flank to flank and pass gentle force down a row as steady as belts crawl. Our dead are at our joints—thin bright lines where added ceramic calls attention to a story we want our children to feel with their own hands. We do not pile stones; we polish bone. Names are beats we share. I am two hearts, a lattice skull, a hundred clamps, a memory of the taste of iron. In other tongues I would have had a word like engineer. Here the closest thing to that word is caretaker, or breaker-of-bad-resonance, or keeper-of-thresholds.

You would find me near the windward lips of a great vent when the song turns wrong. You would find my face pressed to the plant where no softness shows so that my living softness hides safely inside a ceramic cowl. You would find the pattern I beat into the ground repeated a province away where the organ ranges’ notes braid winds into lanes that strangers follow to survive crossing. You would find me on the bristles on a rare idle night, listening to the blue speak of a plate tired of its load, and deciding with a hundred friends whether to coax a dump now or hold a little longer and spend the arc on better weather.

If you are looking for me, listen in your feet. I do not speak loud into air because air here is nothing but weather and weather does not listen. I speak in weight and time. I lay along the span I am given. I damp the wrong song a fraction. I keep a ledger in my bones. I am not the continent, but I am why its choices are survivable where they most nearly are not.

And if you want my answer in the old way—what am I?—I am an animal the plant made room for because it could not afford not to. I am clay fired into honeycomb and tendon taught patience by noise. I am a pair of drums that learned to count. I am a hand, if a hand is what presses and lifts and knows by feel when enough has happened. I am easy to miss if you have eyes. I am everywhere if you have ground.
