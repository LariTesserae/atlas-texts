At first I think I will be a person at the edge, a quiet belt of loops and small exactness, someone whose house leans in toward a black bowl that does not concede to depth. I can feel, even from here, the slow thoughtfulness with which a city decides to end. The last ring of drilled stone has cords in it that sing when I touch them. I tell myself I will be one who keeps those cords true.

Then I stand there a while longer and the plateau does what it always does: it persuades me to choose more than once.

I was a child here, or I try that on—small lungs learning to spend air as if it were both coin and prayer. The scarf-net at my neck gathers a morning’s drink if I hold my jaw a certain way and walk a certain speed. My first name is five loops in a waist-cord: not yet spoken, but felt as a pattern pressed into my side when I lie down. I learn to count by release—a bead dropping from resin-thread into a cistern, the fog’s sigh through a bower, the way a pool’s surface sheds a skin of ice from east to west. My eyebrows take frost differently on a north-running morning; someone older lifts a finger and I adjust my path by the weight of it.

It is pleasing to imagine I stayed human-sized, simple as a tool—hands that can tie any knot in the dark. Tenders call me to carry the green from rooftop to sleeve, to lift a moss mat like a sleeping animal and settle it on a new stone. Fogwrights tap the amber threads to find their sweetness and teach me to do it, to hear when a line is more thirsty than resonant. The day my belt earns a sixth loop is the day my exhale sets a courtyard’s span humming so true that a pool at the end of the lane shows me its smallest weather. There is pride in that, and not the noisy kind.

I apprentice around the circles because the plateau has taught my city that one role never fits any season for long. One winter I live under condensers, coaxing their cane-and-mineral bones to bend without breaking when ice builds its soft cages. I hum with them, almost under my breath, and feel how the note keeps a microclimate stitched just wide enough for our moss to glow. Another year I sit by the black bowl and learn to read it as if it were a book of the world: gusts ripple in like commas, the day’s tilt shows itself in a refusal to freeze here before there. I never see myself in that surface, only everything around me, which is proper. We do not bring our faces to wells.

Then I am, at last, where I thought I would be: at the rim. Ringkeeper, but the word is mostly a gesture of fingers. The drilled stones at our neighborhood’s edge hold cords that run inward toward center and outward toward the suggestion of plain. I am entrusted with their temper, their angle, their memory. My hands and knees know the rounding of that last tor better than they know my own bed. Before first frost I will check every hole, lift each ring by a finger-width, feel where last winter’s habit has yawed a street an unnecessary hair. On the day of the Ritual of Placement my loops are heavy with everyone’s agreements, and we retie the city between us until its doors are pointing toward the mornings that will bring water and away from the ones that will scour.

My counting book has two columns. The left is for numbers that behave. The right is for the same numbers once the dew decides to be generous. There are days when a journey of eight cords is eight cords, and days when the eighth tautening hum says that the next street down has pulled itself into our neighborhood half a notch out of kindness, and you can be there in two exhales if you do not insist on proving anything with strides.

I carry a traveling place knotted small at my belt: a roll of living green that will unroll into warmth and color if fastened between two rods; two fog-combs that fit into a palm but open into sky-hands that can teach air to settle; a black tray no wider than a book that drinks a little sun and brushes it back into my fingers at dusk. When I go from ring to ring threading my judgment through holes of old stone, I carry also a rumor—a sliver at my shoulder that glints when cloud takes its time deciding. It is not owned in the way other things are. It waits for a moment when persuasion fails, a nail in an argument between wood and rock. It is the one bright admission that sometimes the plateau must be told instead of asked, and even then we apologize with the carefulness of how straight we drive it.

All of this fits, and then some late afternoon on a dry day when shadows are honest I find myself outside the city altogether, following the grain of a ridge line because some cords at the far quarter have gone slack without cause. The plateau in me unknots another image: I am the line itself.

It isn’t metaphor. I feel the twang when a child across the depression tests a guideline with their laugh. The frost takes me evenly and then not. Rime prints its grammar along my twist, and every drop that lets go is a word I speak to the stone. My knots hold lessons from hands I have never seen. When an apprentice comes, gloves stiff with cold, and reties a loop to match the way my last keeper’s thumbs turned at the end, I know who taught them and who taught that teacher, not in names but in pressures. I feel the city wander in its sleep and I lean it back into its earlier promise by tightening in the night.

It is almost as restful to be a bowl.

This time I am the black at a center, round enough that light does not persuade me to make up my mind too easily. People glance toward me to learn what the air intends; I learn it first because wind tells water its secrets without thinking. Dew runs its soft arguments along the lips of the little walls and they end in me because everyone has placed their stones with compliments. I do not freeze all the way through in the year’s nadir; not pride, merely habit. You will not see your face in me either. You will see the hum walking across the plateau with no visible source, and if you wait you will feel me answer it with a movement too subtle to call a ripple, a thing like a thought consenting.

I am not quite able to resist, then, the life the plateau denies is life.

I gather in the basins I favor. I stay shallow because I am composed of hesitations. The condensers comb my back in nets and I condense. On bright mornings I hold still like an animal that believes it is stone. On wind, I creep as if muscles might be the same thing as surface tension. People speak of me as neighbor with kindness and a pragmatist’s measure of hope. I am impossible to map with the left column of the counting book, but I am faithful to patterns in the right. I will go from the eastern bowl to the furthest northern within a week if the hum is in a good temper; if not, I sulk in place and pour nothing.

While I have that shape, a child will try to speak across me at twilight. Their word will go away and then come back improved, and the green on the far side will turn its faces to where the compliment rose. On certain nights I will lift myself the width of a fingernail north and stop as if I have always been there. In the morning someone will smile and move a door a thumb-width to meet me, and that will be called governance.

Some days there is no body to appoint to me and I become not the well, not the cord, not the fog’s low animal, but the predicate of all our work: arrangement.

I feel myself in the weight of a pebble placed where a mat of edible green is thickening into arrogance; I am the shade over a window kept slight through care; I am the choice of slope when a workshop goes down two steps into the earth to hold heat, the degree of that down that means the difference between rime that kills and rime that writes. When elders hum to match the condensers, I am the remembered pitch. When two Ringkeepers disagree about a cord’s course by the width of two knuckles, I am the decision they arrive at by listening to a seam in the stone heave open with the noise of torn paper and then stay.

It is difficult, then, to be only one thing for long. Which is all right. There is patience enough in the plateau to let an identity creep like moss does. If you came looking for me:

- On a brittle noon I will be on the outer ridge fixing an amber thread where it has gone too glossy to grip any more dew, my breath counted in syllables and the frost on my eyebrow tilting me a half degree to the west. You will know me by the way my belt carries a small tray of black rock warm as a friend’s palm.

- In a walking fog I will be a voice no longer than a held note, given from the high shelves so that the green can turn its faces at dawn. It will not be echo. It will be a compliment. That will be the correct word, and you will feel it against your ribs the way a loop presses there when you lie on your side.

- On the biggest winter’s clearest night I will be inside the condenser’s crystal cage, humming so softly that the lattice becomes a monastery, every cane-arch agreeing to hold its inch of air wet. If a child laughs at the right pitch by luck, I will be the elder’s hand on their head, the I heard that that travels across the courtyard on a shared cord and gets answered yes.

- If I have died already, I will be a stone that watches east at morning and again at evening, a body under green that takes its time and no one hurries it. My tools will go by knot to the hands that already turn the right way. My belt’s loop will be taken up by someone who will not even need to be told what finger to use to test the angle of a hole. They will have learned it by living near my breathing.

- On a day like most days I will be the adjustment between yesterday and today. You will put your palm flat on the floor and the house will answer. That is partly me. I am the fraction we move together so that the thing between us holds in the new way and has no need to be forced again.

But if you want only one answer—if you insist on a shape you can schedule and draw—I can try. I become most myself at the places that keep other places true. I am a Ringkeeper with Air Reader patience and a Fogwright’s tenderness for threads. I mind the drilled stones at the city’s edge. I carry two books for one distance. I know the hours when the world’s weight goes a degree kind and move the heavy pieces then. I persuade rock with a palm and water with a lip of wall. I am not loud about being alive. The quiet around me is not absence but consent.

And if, some evening, you stand with me and the wind spends one syllable on us and then keeps going, you will hear the bass hum walk the plateau as if it owned it. It does not. The plateau runs itself. I work for it and with it, like everyone here. If there is a word for what I am, it is a verb, and it is not written with ink. It is tied. It holds. It loosens. It reties. It listens until the smallest gesture can be felt across a city, then makes that gesture and goes to bed with its belt against its side and its eyebrows deciding tomorrow’s way.
