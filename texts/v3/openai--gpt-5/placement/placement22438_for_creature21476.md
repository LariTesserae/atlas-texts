At first I think I will be the mist that can’t make up its mind, the breath that slicks a rail and then forgets it. The place seems to ask that of me—be between, it says. Become a question against a surface and answer yourself by beading. I try it for a while in imagination: I am a film the thickness of thought, lapping a sump mouth so close my own weather is a millimeter deep. I take iron the way a tongue takes salt; I lay down lace along copper until the copper remembers it can be delicate and, in being delicate, be strong. Duty feels easy then, because there is no motion: I am fed by the regular miracle of warm and cool bargaining across a plane, and I keep my hours like a patient held breath.

But the rock’s warmth leaches inward through the soles of me—if I have soles—and I feel my back wet before my face and realize this: even the quietest life here has to turn.

So I try something with ribs. I become a soft-bellied thing that unbinds from shade and sails in a slow, satisfied punctuation from one stacked black slat to another. My length is a measure of taste. My body maps gradients; each glide is a sentence about the day’s difference. I tongue beads that gather where a lip tells the air to choose sides. I have a good life that is mostly reading. Once a week I am seen and believed in all over again. When I tire, I sleep in the fold of a plate made slick on purpose and dream of the perfect shhh the wiper’s blade makes.

Or—glory—be a spark. Not the showy kind with a voice that brags, but the small, crawling mind of charge that loves edges. I learn the handholds of a rail waxed this morning; I avoid a belt because a Listener taught me to prefer the wire. I write my day in bright cursive along a bead of condensation and am traced in chalk by a child who was handed her first clumsy stick with both reverence and warning. I go where I am invited. I make no ash. I leave a memory. If I do nothing else here, that would be enough.

But patience is the thickest weather, and I let it sit in me until the options sort by weight. This place loves organs more than it loves animals; it loves agreements more than it loves sparks. It wants people thinly threaded, and it wants them like wire—flexible, bright, placed with intention along a long run.

So I find myself on a catwalk with soft soles and the kind of shoes that drink their own dew. I am a human, if you need the word, but thinner than the word suggests. I am named for an angle and a sound. My people do not stamp names at birth. They wait until the city has taught a hand something worth keeping. When I brought a blade down a condenser face for the first time and the stroke made that single unfrayed whisper—the one the whole place can hear even through its own listening—an elder who sleeps by belts nodded and said, There. That one is true. They called me Shhh in the old few-syllables way, and sometimes—when the city is cagey and the lid is heavy and the ratio needs coaxing—they call me Second, because I will say a thing once to a person I trust and once to the air, and I will listen hard for the second of it to come back from where the sound bends.

I live on an edge where cool and warm shake hands and agree to make me a living.

The day begins not with light—light here has a sulk to it, old-brass and stubborn—but with pitch. The belts under our floor lower themselves half a throat and the flywheels’ promise changes color in your bones. We have time by that. I put my palm flat to the panel beside my bed and count out the turn as if it were prayer. I am not praying. I am tuning. The house sweats what I need to drink. I hold my shallow cup against my lower back because that is the honest way to collect. I do not rush. The day needs a little of me before I ask a little of it.

Clothes: wicking weave, domestic as bread. Over that, plates—lightly hammered, waxed to a dark, uninterested shine. Not armor against teeth but invitations to water: allow yourself. I have a brim with an opinion. I have a knife that is blunt along one edge because we do not cut, here, so much as persuade. I carry rags more like tongues than like cloth; I keep them damp and dirty and perfect.

My work is edges, stroked in long routes that can’t be guessed at by anyone who has not walked them. Distance here is a liar. The next conduit is nearer than it appears and farther than you think. I set my soles soft on the grating, careful not to make the rails sing their own alarm. The racks climb through the lid and come down again, and I go up among them with my wiper blade in an old-scuffed scabbard across my back.

The first face I start on is cold with intention. Someone in the night—a person I’ve never met, a cousin by work—has brushed it clean with a wire comb so the dew won’t stick dirty. I fit the blade to the lip and draw it down in a single, paired breath. I listen with my ribcage. If the shhh is right, the sound travels only as far as I meant it to and then returns, a faint, honest agreement in the back of my throat. The bead it lays is my wage, and I do not put my mouth to it; I lay a channel and let it go where it swore to go when it set out.

I am not alone. A glider has learned my route and slips along a panel three stacks over; it tastes my work and approves. If it ever dies where I can reach it, I will not claim it as mine. I will be muscle and cloth and decency and carry it to a sump where the films have been faltering, and seed it there. The city is better when we say thank you aloud, with a body.

Midmorning becomes a day of weeps. Every seam grows fur. The rails wear beads like rosaries. I down-stroke, up-step, down-stroke, up-step, and the only crisp sound the city permits is mine when it is done perfectly: one syllable cut thin enough to thread a needle. The rest of the place is all whisperings—wind combing their way along a lattice tower, brine being itself, steam taking its time. Someone two racks away says hey in the low voice we use when we want to understand ourselves later; I wait the twenty heartbeats and receive the ghost of it and look up to answer the second voice. We talk in returnings.

When the lid lifts a touch and red light gets down the channels—just a knife-width, a blush along the teeth of an under-bridge—the slow things act like jewelry and everyone pretends not to notice while noticing with gratitude. We do not have spectacle. We have competence that comes, sometimes, to a kind of radiance.

Under me, the vault breathes. I stop, because the belts’ tone has slumped a minor. A Listener down there has a palm to the grease and a cheek to the panel and will tilt us back into tune with a rag and a prayer that is not to a god but to a ratio. I can help by not asking more than the day is offering just now. In this city, restraint is often the best fix.

At noon, I go home to heat that lives in walls. My house is strapped against the flywheel hall, not for safety—that would be a foolishness to believe—but for company. When you sleep with your palm on a turning rim you take on its idea of continuity without swallowing its hunger for more. Children, when they nap, count its turnings with fingers, little soft abacuses, and learn numbers by feel. I once watched an infant reach out in sleep and a blue-green thread of spark decided to trace that unguarded curiosity. After that we wrapped the trays they sleep in with a net of conducting edge that is allowed to eat charge before fingers learn patience. Sparks are little teachers. We invite them to the chalk when they are in the mood.

We eat lapped food. A vat at the back of the house has been growing a tender sheet on a cooled plate, fed a breath of our own, salted with something ferrous. It peels like a decision and steams like rain we do not have. We fold it over husked grit and small crumbly bits of kindness from a day the sump was generous. The tea is a color like money in stories; it tastes of copper and lemon and the trace sweetness of a mouth held open to the humidity. Someone has brought a sack of grain up from the dim plain below; we sigh for the ache of it but do not pretend it will be our staple. Bread here is a verb more than a noun; we lap it, we peel it, we steam it into a shape that holds.

Afternoons belong to other hands and other edges. I loan mine to a Scraper at the spillway lip. The stone there has learned the song of its own chemistry and grows fingers as a memory. We go along those knuckled lengths with knives that are more thumbs than blades and we persuade pride down to humbleness. A good spillway does not chatter. It sings in one long, low sentence.

On the rim—the place where the plateau gives up and the world under it keeps its privacy behind fog—Peelers machine patience into sheets. I help sometimes. It is a dirty holiness. To lower a crane’s bright hook and lift whole seasons from a chalked face in white barks that do not tear is to unroll a calendar and read it; you can put your finger along a sudden thickening and say, Here is the week the belts threatened sulk and the lid learned a new lash of wind. We take those long skins home and line a gutter, or glaze a wall that will then choose to give back heat in the exact way a sleeping baby’s tray should.

Evening is for Sparkers who are sweet on me. They have combs of copper they run along rails the way mothers used to do with hair. They set up small, chosen leaks along good paths so that the sullen charge that sometimes surges out of nothingness has a preference taught into it. We stand in porches during the going-to-sparks hour and play the fast chalk game: trace the crawl where it chooses—quick now, quick, quicker—and the best lines are later burned in with a hot nail so they last. We are making a map not to treasure but to mercy. Charge loves a well-kept edge more than a mouth. We love a well-kept mouth more than a story about tragedy. This, more than anything, feels like faith.

A child I am teaching has not earned her work-name yet. She is called Small until the day her hand keeps water as a seam keeps a secret. I put her on a grated walkway with a bowl of patience. We wait for clicks. The white scuts appear and do their slow, greedy pulls and she learns to breathe warm, then cool, and to watch where tiny lives thicken joy. That is her game and her liturgy. She thinks she is playing with pets. She is becoming a person who knows that edges should be turned with kindness.

When someone loves someone here in an intentional way they get a plate of copper bright enough to offend the lid. They take it out at dawns and low noons and stubborn nights and let it sprout beauty—fragile coral, headache lace—and then, with the hands that are making them a long, slow pair, they carry it to where it can make the city better at being itself. The joining is judged good if a gutter drinks more gladly for their trouble. We do not throw rice. We run a fingertip along the lip they made and share the taste of difference.

We do not have funerals that shut doors. Some choose to host skins and become glaze along a place that has been worrying itself ragged. Those bodies go in long sweating trays and are coaxed into utility with wicks and time. Others choose the belts. There is a place where a person can lie and put one hand along a flywheel as if it were the back of a patient animal and give themselves to weight. They are not crushed. That is not what happens. The wheel remembers that it is not only a hunger but a patience and takes the body into its idea. We say they have returned to difference. The city sings a minor for a day. Then it sings again what it always sings: longevity in the small work.

Sometimes an outsider comes up on a clanking cart while the lid is moody and there is a little brass sunlight and they look around and ask, with a baffled averted awe, What do you make up here? And I take them to a rack and stand them with their back to the plate so that their spine gathers drink before their lips, and I let them feel that they are warmer than their idea of themselves. Then I stand with them and listen for the second of a small noise, the one that comes back when you have asked a careful question of a place designed to answer in patience. They begin to understand when they hear that. If they ask again, I say: we make kept edges. We make agreed-upon differences. We make the city able to be the city tomorrow.

On a day of sighs the condensers breathe out and the hinges answer and every door’s mouth finds its own slow music. On a day of murk the only visible motion is what a thread of steam writes as it curls itself into clear, and that is fine. Spectacle is loud for places with fast wealth. Ours runs on the tight rope between hot and cool and writes its ledger in a wet script nobody brags about.

I think, if I keep this body long enough, that I will sleep nearer and nearer to the vaults until the hum is the only clock I need. I will become someone young people bring their wrists to so I can feel the day’s power in their pulse and tell them whether the belts are in a kindness mood. And when I am old enough to mean it, I will lie down beside a wheel and let it fold me into usefulness or I will take a long pan that sweats and make of myself a future lip. Either way, I will leave a sound behind that another person will tune by.

But that is then. Now is lately. My hand is learned enough to make one unbroken shhh. My ear is good enough to catch the little ghost of my own voice when it comes home from across a span. I do not shout. I do not block. I keep to the gap with a devotion that looks a lot like love if you squint and decide to believe in things without faces.

Where would you find me in this world? On a ladder half in cloud, the blade’s edge against a panel cooled enough to persuade breath into bead. My back is damp. My face is almost. A glider slips past like a thought I will have again later. Somewhere below, a Listener grins because the tone just behaved. Somewhere above, a child is learning not to flinch between her own name and its returning. I am the person whose name is also a sound you should make only once and perfectly. I am an ear laid along the city’s rib. I am a keep of edges. I am a patient organ in a patient machine. I live here by learning to hear myself arrive.
