At first, I would think I had come as a visitor, high at the rim looking down into white stone and green light. But the place does not keep visitors long. It breathes, and the breath is cold and regular, and after a little while I would notice that my breath had learned it. After a little longer I would find that my palms wanted to be up under a drip. And then I would be where I was always going to be—down among the lips and bowls, water-dark and small, a body shaped by the rules you have already met.

I live midway along the trench, where two skylights throw their squares together at noon and make a bright shape I could find blind. One of the beaver kin keeps a wide lodge here, under alder roots that are thicker than a season’s thigh-bones. Their rooms warm the water enough that winter does not bite the marrow, and their dams have stepped the grade into a run of bowls you can swim through without ever crossing open air. Between those bowls, on the lee of a willow trellis that knows wind, I have my nest.

I am built like the place means. My fur lays smooth and dark as wet bark unless I raise the frost of guard hairs along my spine to break the shine. My head is a wedge soft at the cheeks, the muzzle parted down the center as if the air were a thing to eat. There are whiskers enough to paint a room without light—long rakes that tell me where a breath is different and where a lip has thickened a leaf’s width since yesterday. My ears fold closed for water; my eyes take a second lid when skylight is too clean. When that lid comes over, the light turns to a soft word on the surface, and from above I am only a correction in the gloss. My hands are the reason you would mistake me for a small person at a glance. Five fingers. Webbing between the long and the next, a lace you cannot see until it opens in water. My tail is a third hand when one must not block what runs.

We do not have a name you can say without your hands. We call ourselves what we are by a gesture—palms cupped close, then spread so that what is between them goes through. If I must give you a name for me, take the way I was found by the place. On the day I was counted, the noon fell long. It cut the wall in a slanted plate the color of old milk, just touching a brown flood-line from a year when a dam failed three bends upstream. I was small enough to be forgotten. No one had told me that this wall wrote a bright in summer. I ran a path under willow to a lip that dripped in threes and threw my hands up into the light and felt it run to my wrists. That angle and hour live in me. If the porous were to speak me in tap and breath, they would make the breath through the hands that says light on flood-mark in late green.

Work is where you stand long enough to read what wants to be done. Today it is a lip notched wrong by a vole’s hind foot. The bowl is young and the rimstone is making itself quick; it means to overflow on the colder side, where ice will lay thicker come first frost and break in plates that walk the moss down the terrace and steal it. I carry a deer's long bone in the mouth. I worry at the glow of new calcite with the bone’s blunt blade until the lip is a finger thinner on the warm side. The drops consider. They choose my correction. A run that would have cut becomes a lace that will hold. Somewhere uprun a dipper takes a white thought and leaves it; somewhere down in the darker room I can hear the older river speak one word twice.

At dawn—the hour when the bats thin in the light and the swifts thicken—I comb the ceiling of the seam-room above the great pool. The seam weeps where a wedge of stone lost its patience in last freeze, and it is blotting, pooling itself into dull sheets that ice badly and talk not at all. I hold a fan of willow twigs. I rake, light and even, and the water remembers. It learns to bead off the comb and run in seven lines instead of one formless sheet. Seven is a good count in this room. The ice will form like the teeth of a fern. When it breaks at blue, it will talk in a way you can hear through the moss.

At noon I go to the two skylights and read what they write. Every wall has its calendar. We keep them the way you keep your better recipes, head-tucked, tongue against the teeth for concentration. There is a place high on the clean face where the beavers let mud fall sometimes by mistake. That brown remembers the last flood. There is a green scum line also, lower, younger, slower to brag. If the new sun finds the green and not the brown today, the thaw in the plateau has not yet found its long voice, and the pressure under the walls will be steady. When it finds the brown, water parts the earth above with more light than we like and we go to the exit not the pocket.

Afternoon is for neighbors. The beaver kin are broad-jointed and certain and wrong in the right ways. They love a straight grade. A straight grade is a voice that shouts. We move along their dams where they have cribbed in sticks so thick the water forgets to listen to itself; we loosen, never breaking, never taking flush. Fingers only. You can make a thousand channels in a span smaller than a hand if your hand understands how to be absent while it works. The beaver do not mind. They want what holds. We want what speaks in small words all day and never fails at night.

With the swifts there is trade of a kind. In spring I sit under their honeycomb walls and brush from my back the hair that came loose under new growth and winter’s lying still. They cut a hair from the drift and set it into clay with their needles, and my shed learns to be a home to eyes so snappish and black they are like day heated and let go. Under their stacks I thin two lines of flow and one line more, very narrow, open only when rain runs long. Rat snakes come by the dry ways some years. Weasels come most years. You can cost them a heartbeat at just the right stone. A heartbeat is the difference between bird and bone-garland.

Evening is for food and for waiting. Fish eggs if a flood moves a nest, never more than a taste, taken at risk with the belly pressed flat to the stone and the whiskers tasting fast because owls sleep the way we do, with one part of the head halfway awake. Amphipods in the low light—they are the click between the clicks with their hard-soft shells. I press a thumb to open a pantry gour I keep under an overhang and take three softened alder catkins. They taste like a memory of smoke, which is as close as I come. There is a leaf-glove drying along a root, sewn with caddis silk so it will drink from the edge and keep the palm dry. The glove will not drip. It will hand the water to the next thing.

Night is never all night. Even at deep winter there is blue that comes. On the first day a pool sets that should have been water until the last possible day, we go to it. We hang the cases of amphipods along the new ice, a ring of bells no longer than a fingernail. We wait with our heads tucked into our cloaks, willow-bark strips and caddis silk and a nap of alder pulp limed to take the room’s weather. When some fickle breath moves the cases, they touch the ice and the pool sings. It is not much. It is all. We call that ceremony the remembering of ripples, and though we would not tell you so if you asked, we do it as much for the beaver as for ourselves. They work long. They like songs.

Rules keep us thin in the mind and in the foot. Do not lay anything flat across a run; if you must cross, cross with fingers. Sleep with your ear to something that will change if something else changes. If the drips cannot be counted because they have learned to be one, that is not praise, it is warning; be loud; go. Leave a pocket in what you make. When I weave a nest I always make the empty space near the nowhere corner, where a hand can leave the world without catching. When I speak I keep a shape in the sentence to let kin pass their thought through. We say that only something sure of the place would block it. We are not so sure.

I learned these the way kits learn anything. In a room near the beavers’ water, near enough that the warmth came through the moss into the milked fabric of the nest, my mother put my hand under three drips and said nothing. I could hear fish under my body—muscles drawing the thin water sideways, holding. I could smell crushed stone in one seam and willow in another. She let the drops fall. One fell hungry. One fell pretty. One fell with a wanting that meant we should move. I reached up to the one that wanted us, and she took my wrist very soft and pushed my hand to the pretty one, and I understood that beauty can be a danger but is not the danger, and that a danger is a danger even when it is not in love with itself.

Later, older, I touched a wall still sharp with broken stone and tasted the cold of a cave being born. The skylight above it had come over winter. The room smelled like shells. Everyone came, from three terraces. We stood around the raw edge where frost still held as if it were fur. We counted, heads bowed. The first drop took so long you could have thought the seam would never learn. It fell. It said a number and a day. Whoever had their hand there took that bright and hour forever. It was not me. I did not mind. The new sound belonged to all of us and to itself.

Once, a terrace failed near where I live. You will know the sound when you hear it—water stepping down bowls for a thousand days and then, in one slow rush, choosing another way. The beaver had been good, or at least certain; we had been perhaps too loving of a lip we should have thinned one more leaf. The bowls poured out like chimes tossed from a lap and struck along the way. In the morning after, we came and put our noses to the new plain. There is a word the hand makes when it cups and opens and the breath goes through that means "may this never have happened and may this always have been true." We said that, and then we read the new map and set our hands where they wanted to be. If the place puts its hand up and you put yours to it, you are right. If it does not, you must unmake your work like water.

Death happens in little ways. Once, a cousin died because a smooth was too smooth and we did not notice that the gloss had been polished past holding by vole feet and beaver tails and our own. He went soft as if sleeping in water and did not wake. We brought him into a root-lattice where a drip had learned to count by his hands and left him there. Calcite remembers everything white. A winter later that lip was thicker in one patch. It changed a trickle’s mind. In spring, a kit found a way because of it. In this place you cannot say that ends end.

If you were to find me—not as a smudge in your side vision, but plainly—I would not be surprised by you. I have tasted strangers in the draft from the skylights. I would hold one hand under a drip and let the drop break itself white on my skin without running off. Then I would move my other hand under the drip and not let it run off either, and you would see that this is only a matter of how you meet what falls. If you put your fingers there and let yourself be numbed, a thinking would fail in you and another would begin. Then I would put your hand to the pale stone. I would show you where to step. The moss would not notice you, and your feet would keep the lesson.

Over time I have become less a person than a set of habits that hold together. I am the thin place on a lip where trouble would have started. I am the second door in a beaver’s warm dark. I am the rule about never laying anything flush that you will take with you into your speech. I am the white skewed rectangle at noon on the wall above the brown year, and I am the patience it took to learn it. Some days, when the blue comes late and the bats shoulder past the last silver into the long mouth of the trench, I stand where their seam is and do nothing at all, which is another kind of work.

If I could answer your questions with a sound I would, but here sound is intent. So I set a twig across a rill, just so, not to dam it but to give it teeth. I thin a lip leaf-fine where you will not notice but the place will. I leave a pocket in the speaking so you can put your own hand through it and feel the draft. Where would you find me? In the underlips of bowls, where gloss goes thin; under alder lattices that have taught themselves to be bridges; under a beaver’s rooms, ear laid to stone, warm water at the wrist, counting drips in threes at the turn of light. Who would I be? The porous that the place made of me, a keeper of drips and doors, a rimwright, a seamcomber, a hand under a drop held steady. And when you left, I would leave with you. You would start counting by water, tasting edges with your fingers before you lay anything down, and walking pale. You would speak with places inside your sentences through which the world can go. You would not block a run if you could help it. And sometime, years from now, you would come upon a bright on a wall and lay your palms to it and know the hour. Then, even if you never returned, I would know you had been here by what you did where you were.
