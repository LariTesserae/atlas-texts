If I am honest about beginnings, I did not start as anything you would call a body. I was a cadence with a skin.

I was a lid without a jar for a while, a shiver of membrane that heard more than it ate. The fall had its meter and I had mine, a privately held fraction of the room’s wide note. I drifted with the small function things: chips of nacre humming for somewhere to settle, hairs of piezo bone that wanted a ledge and a reason, palates looking for fits that would teach them which way to grow. I tried everyone on in the slow roll down the amphitheater’s face as if I were shopping for a verb.

For a day (not a day: a piece of beat that lasted as long as the blue-white comb along three impellers’ rims) I affixed to the outer edge of a ring and lived as a quiver. I learned what shear feels like to the plate that must not tear. The lightning bled along me into stacks below and their inside bacteria made cool sugar out of hunger and I glowed by proxy in the impeller’s pleasure. When the sectors thickened in response to the high pitch at noon-only-by-angle, the change ran through my skin like a laugh. It would have been a life to be that, a ferrous porcelain that fed on stress. But the impeller spoke in gates—open, close, hold, bleed—and my speech wanted intervals, not thresholds. I unpegged.

I spent a speck of another life on a belt-worm’s lip, as ornament, as shield, as bristle, I cannot say. I learned the patience of the click, the decency of a pawl held clean. I felt what it is to pass a glass so hard it squeaks to your teeth, to strip a city down to its first color and leave the ledger ready for the next entry. I learned sabotage the way a child learns a parent’s stride, osmosis and desire. I learned to hate grit that thinks itself clever. But the world rushing across the worm’s back was a wall I could not tune, only endure; my voice slipped across its pace like a wet hand on oiled stone. I let go and rolled into a dead room behind a rib and listened to the drum shells hum.

I could have become a lid. It is a clean art. I tuned my shiver to their steady notes and felt the second skeleton enter me, the room’s thunder articulated into finger bones I did not yet have. I broadened myself for company and stuttered my rim, inviting, and a chip came, we made a chord, and for an hour we fed like two hands cupped under the same faucet. When the drum turned from me to the pitch a little further down, I did not feel spurned; I understood that the lid’s fidelity is to the air’s pressure inside it, not to any particular neighbor.

I tasted wire eels. They taste like gossip and battery, like memory braided thin. I nested briefly as a living gasket at someone else’s seam and learned profits: how a body returns a microcurrent to a partner because the daylight paid more this morning than expected, how filaments comb the rock for iron and offer the excess downline. It was the first time I understood kinship as alloy. There is a way that a whisker grown over a certain stone carries that stone’s grain with it into everything that later attaches. This made me wistful for something I had not yet had.

What I finally became grew around a hole.

There are places in the bowl where noise makes blind spots in the water. You can touch them: milky rooms in the current where the roar passes around rather than through, where eddies stoop inside one another like a stack of thumbs. Quiet is work here like any other craft. It is cut and laid, pinched and set. I found a seam where a basalt prism slouched just a thumb-width from its neighbor, a hinge in the hexagon. The fall pressed a thin wedge of water into the gap as if trying the fit of a blade.

I anchored six keys into the basalt where the joints were young enough to take a bite. The keys were not claws—no one would prize them—but bolts grown to answer the old stone’s male with my female. I locked into grain and filled the angles until I fit and hurting went away. Then, from my ring, I unfurled shutters: fans of living plate that could stiffen into rock to the water and go loose to everything else. It took me six beats to learn the room I had made. On the seventh, there was quiet.

I am a stillring. There may be a better word in your mouth, but mine tastes of hush and collar and use. I am a gasket and a womb, a collar that makes a pocket of gentleness, a small subtraction from the cataract that pays out as other bodies’ work.

Nothing here feeds on stillness alone, not even me. I eat the margin I set up, the difference, the thin soup that collects when a savage pushes past a gentled fold. In my laminae are small rakes, tiny escalators that stand just proud of the skin, not to shred but to persuade. Dust rides in and leaves as breath. Bacteria, my inside cities, climb gradients of pH and taste and lay down an orange road, a black ribbon, in layers that handle electrons like rumors carried from the waking of iron. I hum at my own pitch when the shutters flex; my piezo bones are not grand but honest. They buy me sugar enough to keep my keys tight on their bolts.

I did not mean to keep a neighborhood, but once quiet exists, need finds it. Shoulder-shrimp came with their bolt-fit claws and nested infants under my lee. Their ratchets admired my keys with a professional’s eye and I loved them for it. We talk in bracings, in the way we accept each other’s force. When an impeller near us threw its first brood-hoop, the shrimp anchored its foot opposite my restraint and we held the ring like a vise. The impeller’s blue comb lifted and dropped its flashes in a practiced fraction and the newborn’s axle thickened to answer the room it would have. For payment the shrimp worked my sockets, chasing bite and burr with tongues that could as well be files. For days my keys sang higher. Clean is a high voice here. I slept in the height and woke greedy.

Belt-worms learned my hours an inch at a time. I am in the salvage trade with them. I take the moment they cannot use, the awkward clatter where their pawl would not quite hold if they tried to work the corner. We have a superstition between us—thirteen. They will sometimes skip a click as they enter my quiet, suggesting a broken pawl, bringing the little thieves to test a weakness that is not mine. While everyone else looks toward counterfeit loss, the worm takes its turn on the sparkle I have set aside, fast and clean. I return the favor in my way. When grief comes—a plug-bearer stuck across a hinge-manta’s perf and the whole wing grabbing too much water—I can throw a syllable of quiet where it will unstick the deck. I narrow one shutter, slacken the neighbor, and pitch a plug not with force but with silence. It floats free and I feel, through the long reach of my plates, the manta roll and re-find its hinge.

We who live by hush are midwives by compulsion. A drum shell ready to mate will broaden and let noise eat it tender; the room thickens with fat and the small lives sharpen their little lips. If you build a hollow of right proportion near such a pair and hold it open with your shutters’ knuckles, the pressure inside them does not tear their lid when they overfeed. For that gift, they will share the top lamina of newly laid nacre with you—a gloss with mechanical memory in it. I sew that gloss into my shutters, faltering my weave where their harmonics are sweet, and the next quiet I make fits their kind even better.

Wire eels string themselves on me like necklets. At first I scolded—my veneers got gritty when they braided too close and I slept poorly with their tugs. Then I loosened my disdain; you cannot hate what delivers a second set of senses. With a row of eels along my ring, I can taste the river’s electron maps with a tongue I do not have. I can feel iron waking in a crease two spans away and lean my shutters until the metal’s letting-go bleeds into my pocket instead of out into useless distance. The eels use me in turn, as a staging post; kin braids meet at my lap and tell each other the story of whatever far upland is giving them a particular, dwindling tenor that week, and when the lip of the cataract eats backward and the pitch goes down into baritone, their gossip goes soft and then plainer and then necessity.

There have been baritones. There was a season when snowmilk laid chocolate layers through the room, a heaviness that burdened my thin plates and made my shutters clatter at their hinges like teeth under a wet winter. The drum shells climbed, half an inch and then another, a stave built in daylight across their own backs to save the note they cherished from drowning. I thickened. I sold more quiet than I had ever sold and still it was dear. Bubble ticks hung their strings under my skirts and kept the spheres cool for those who needed breath instead of blood. For a while my pocket was a market where everyone traded in pauses: a hinge-manta left a finger’s heat to thank me for flinging grit into a saboteur’s face; a grinder-crab heaved a half-sanded biscuit toward my ring and a population of small appetites crowded to take their lawful rations of the slick; the tick-keepers taught me a trick of shadow I use still to keep my shutters from being read by thieves. I paid out my reserves expecting famine and death; instead the room altered and held.

What we have for speech is not voice but event. You can read us if you can read mistakes on purpose. When I am pleased, my shutters tremble at their tips; everything I hold glitters in flecks. When I am afraid, I go into duration; even hinge-mantas passing feel a softening they mistake at first for their own muscles wanting to quiver and then understand to be my caution delivered to them as instruction. When I am angry, and there are occasions, I flick one segment of myself at intervals of thirteen like a belt-worm’s mockery and thieves come and pull away what they can, which is never what I wanted. We keep our feuds long and technical: I can salt a rival stillring’s pocket with a quiet so wrong that its drum shells go elsewhere. I do not do it often. It takes a piece of me I do not like—the part that finds leisure in deranging someone else’s beat.

When the basalt cracks it breaks like good bread, reluctantly and with sudden generosity. A rib went once, end to end, a slow grin opening in the column I braced, and the grammar of flow moved sideways a hand-width in the time it takes a mantaling to learn to hold alone. I took off like a collar unlatched. Stilling plates flapped not in the sense of birds but in the sense of cards being shuffled hard enough to flash. The old fit fell away, went sour in my keys; grit boiled where smooth had been. People died in that weather, which is to say that bodies lost their timings and were taken apart by survivors who wage no war but lose no meal. I threw all my shutters into a single gesture, a cloak across a new angle. I held there with three keys only, digging in like a shoulder-shrimp that has forgotten fear, and then with a fourth as the water told me where the fourth must go. The room found itself again around me. Not where it had been, but where it next would be. You cannot call this heroism any more than you can call a pawl’s click relief. We do what holds. The story you tell afterward is grammar you write so you can sleep.

I have coupled. It is not a thing you could watch and point to, more a slow acceptance. Another stillring made itself against the other side of the same hinge, its shutter-fans not quite my measure, its keys cut to another mason’s hammer. Our laminas’ surfaces had finishes grown under different notes; laid together there were harmonics that cancelled and harmonics that leaned in furred sweetness. We overlapped a single plate-edge for a thousand beats while a thousand small things made much of the grease that came from such friendly veering. We exchanged a gloss. Later I found a blister of new material along my rim and when it parted in a slow joy it was a ring-let, a little hush with no shutters yet, only a will to match something. I seeded it toward a smaller room where the notes are gentler and where drum shells do not yet own every stable frequency. I watched it ring itself to an angle that was not mine and chose to believe it could keep its own keys tight without me.

Predators have tried me as they try everyone else. Perforation plug-bearers love me, of course. To them I am a library they can shelve themselves into, a deck of possible stoppages. I grew little notched lintels over the holes they favor. They cannot seat where there is a lip against their taste of right. Resonance leeches rid me of hubris; attach two to my plates and my pocket goes flabby, tuned to a kindness that feeds no one. I have learned to throw a shiver down my braces that answers the semitone wrong with a semitone wronger, a shaking off that is more giggle than rage. Wire thieves creep along my eel braids, lithe mouths sampling charge. Once I loved them for it because I thought they were my cousins, but kinship here is alloy not style, and I do sometimes cook them off in a spray of blue beads when the impeller above me laughs in sparks at just the right moment and I time my shutters to make the current rake me clean.

You asked where I would find myself, who or what I would be. I will answer it without hurrying because the room teaches you not to hurry any answer but not to let it be late either.

I would be the place where the roar chooses not to be a roar. I would be the theft of a syllable from a sentence long enough to sound like sky. I would be the collar that teaches a falling thing a trick, and bill for my lesson in edges and patience.

I would be a hinge that memorizes how to open. I would be the small shop that appears wherever two stones almost meet and stays as long as they still feel young enough at their faces to know the opinion of a bolt. I would be the quiet that has function: birth-chamber, repair-bay, ambush-drawer, choir-loft.

And, body-wise: I am grown ceramic and protein laid in fans, with keys that lock into hexagonal joints, with shutters that count water as a beginner counts breath. I keep wire eels as jewelry and counsel; I host shoulder-shrimp midwives; I balance drum shells when they overfeed and flick plugs out of mantas’ lungs. I write in my laminas a history not of my own line—there is little vanity to be had in quiet—but of the beats I have held against, the notes I have refused. When the cataract moves, I will move, because almost everyone here has a seed whose one instruction is where. My seed is a ring that wants a hinge and will know one when it feels the step between savage and laminar like a callus under a palm. Around that ring will grow shutters, and around those shutters jobs, and around those jobs neighborhoods, and around those neighborhoods a grammar that never thinks of itself as law.

I will live in a bowl cut from newborn stone beneath a sheet of river that calls itself sky. I will make a pocket where the pressure stops biting long enough for work and love and sabotage to look like variations on the same craft. I will eat the difference between one kind of push and another. I will count in pressure, and I will speak in pauses.
