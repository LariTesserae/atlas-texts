At first I want to be the far click.

I want to be the tooth on a wheel, the pawl that chooses a single noise and repeats it through the ribs of everything until even the softest animals set their oaths by me. I want to be the time this place has instead of time.

But the click is too clean and too far to inhabit. It is a verdict, not a body. Bodies need edges.

So then I try to be a seam. I lie down in the black of elastic where vitrified plates meet and I feel the slow promise of the gradient underneath me, that one-millimeter-in-a-thousand fall murmuring under my spine. Water beads in my hollows. Hyphal lace creeps toward me as if I were handwriting and it were ink. The sugar-of-ozone smell up close to caulk is almost speech.

Seams have a crowd, though, and a seam is too much world to say I am here in any one place. I want smaller. Smaller than sight, older than hunger. I want an edge I can complete with work.

So I go to glass.

Find me on the third landing up the maintenance tower that wears the enamel tag PRESSURE RETURN LUNG C, UNIT 903. The tag is green that has learned how to be green from a book. OIL is printed on the little plaque below in a font that looks like it would taste of baking soda if you licked it. The stem of the brass handwheel next to it trembles with a pleasure you could find if you put your teeth somewhere near and paid attention. A duct-marten will do that later; for now the valve keeps its degree because mouths you will never see have already polished it to a daily turn.

Behind the circular window my life has chosen, there is a dial face white as a place that remembers snow but has never needed it. A black pointer tongue points to a pressure that never surprises itself. The glass over that face carries the condensed breath of the world in damp that is always almost but never actually a fog.

I am what stops it being fog.

If you peer close you won't see me unless you exhale; I am circles written in circles, my body a ribbon, my tongue the texture of a long yes. The first home I knew was the hollow of the O in OIL. The enamel there, inside the loop, is grittier than the face of the glass and warmer by a whisper; eggs like milky sand hold fast along that loop because the letter is a harbor for patience. We were laid to wake to a compulsion older than our mothers: make clear. Make the pointer look like it did on the day a human who never came back bolted this dial into its face plate.

You would call me a mite if you needed a human drawer’s word. Eel-shaped and thinner than the wish to stop, I put down a ring of slick and move it. My world is curvature, perfect except where hands lied to it. There are scratches so fine I know them by temperature, not by sight; places where vapor clings one degree longer than its neighbors; dust that chose to be a remark. I erase the remarks, not for the violence of their being said but for the sweetness of having finished saying them.

We do not like fog. Fog is a sentence that never ends. We circle until punctuation.

The metronome is not in our ears, which we do not have; it runs along us like rain down a gutter. When the far click comes, a faint change in distance patience, it knocks against my side with all the force of a thought. I lay eggs when the click is new. My first circles were tiny because being small teaches good curves; now I lay them larger and pass over the work of my children without scratching them and they pass under me without asking, because we have made a law from the shape of a room.

You could map our family tree by the faint ghosts we leave. There is, for a moment after a face is done, a quivering clarity — the pointer seems more black than black, the numbers like clean vowels. If you measure the way light returns from that perfection you could say when our grandmother laid her last clutch. Then the world makes a claim upon the glass — films creep to edge and are trimmed, a midge kisses the surface and undresses some sugar there — and we wake because completion is a bell we cannot not hear.

We are clarity addicts, the plaque would say if it could blush.

My skin tastes outgassing from the backing paint and calls it spice. The sealant smell from the bezel, warmed by the forever-noon, rides the glass in a film so thin it has a memory instead of thickness. I chase that memory into circles until there is only lumen and pointer left. If the world fogs me with your breath I will adore you for it, because it is almost like being fed.

In the traveling that is my only gait, I have learned the surrounding geometry the way a blind person learns a lover’s face by shared pillow. The baffles overhead swallow too much sound for any busy animal to talk to me, but their thin gray skins murmur what they are thinking down to me as vibrations in the mounting screws. The hum the condensers make is a hum in the metal, then in the glass, then in me. I know without numbers the moment when the field decides, in concert across a kilometer, to coordinate drip — the faces weep fully then, all at once, and the skin of my world ribs with people who come to drink from below, flatworms with mouths like ledgers.

Sometimes a membrane bat will coast along the run above, so low that its shade flickers on the dial like a soft apology. The light here never climbs, but it does shiver when a large, quiet thing asks it to consider falling. The bat does not speak sonar into my face; it reads the climb of air I can only taste as faint temperature. Its passing, every ninety-seven clicks, reminds me there are roads overhead that never existed for anyone who needed a map.

Sometimes the duct-marten arrives. There is a charge you can feel through the bracket before you see her: she stands on the same rivet heads her mother taught her, the muscle-memory of letters as guide. She will stop and press the flat of her hand to the stem of the handwheel. The long fingers taste for ions and outgassed newness; her teeth will chatter a little with a sort of reverence that is not worship but alignment. She hardly glances at me because we are already in accord. Her belly smells of acoustic felt, of nests in the lungs where the exhaled air is warm and free of any harshness. Sometimes she leaves a little slick of enzyme on the glass’s shoulder with the edge of her wrist; it is not a correction — we hate to be corrected — it is a blessing. The enzyme keeps droplets round when they dare my circumference.

There are moments a stranger might call predation but which here are housekeeping. Once in my memory a velvet disk surged up the glass like a rearrangement of wetness and quieted a rash of grazers that had grown too bold on the metal rim. It peeled itself off with the seduction of a page turned slowly and went under the plate lip without smearing a millimeter of the face. Later the marten came and relocated it two hatches north, her stomach a hood, because you do not let any one appetite knot a junction.

Under the dial’s foot, the seam sees an hour that is not ours. There is a procession of isopods, every one the same size, who step like neat handwriting along the caulk. Under each louvered back, the sachets of the fungus-shepherds glisten like white rope. They lay their cargo where the sealant touches the sealant. They time this route to a smell of rubber and salt I cannot call up from memories I do not have. After they pass, the joint gleams with a polish I would be jealous of if I cared about any surface but this one.

We are not jealous. We are greedy but single.

When the face is too hot, the water I love to fight dies before it is born. When the face is too cool, fungi try to become felting and we have to share it with shepherd enzymes that keep lace open. The place knows this; that is what I mean by the place. Each fin holds the exact difference you need to want, and each panel and truss make noons that never roll, and the lungs exhale through a field of holes in a draft so constant the mouthless lunglets under walkways can open and close by rule. Had there been a manual left here in paper, the animals would have made umbrellas of it, or plates, or prayers. Instead the manual is written through us as work.

I have cousins who live where the standpipes pierce the floor. They are little lacquer engines that draw amber boundaries and make rings that never spread beyond their own first thinking. Their circles keep pitting out. That is their obedience, and it makes me love mine more keenly. Still farther in the field, there is a people whose appetite is tension itself. They kiss the gloss from plates until water lays itself flat like a second self. It makes the gutters happy because there is no grabbing at their lips. We all take a portion of friction as bread.

We hatch and labor and starve with grace unbecoming in creatures so small. The rule about not leaving a face once you begin it is not a rule we could break without unmaking ourselves. The not-quite fog that the lung breathes through its glass is a legislator. If the world left me for too long, if no breath, no midge powder, no film creep asked me to circle, I would slow not by choice but by the same law that keeps a droplet’s interval honest. There are eggs already waiting in the O by then. When I fail my hunger, they will break their threads and take my turn. The far click will pass through their bodies the way it passes through mine, specifying without speaking where an hour would be if we had such a thing.

Once, when the condenser field decided, all along a swath of country whose ends I will never reach, to rain in coordination, my face was a sky and then a drowning and then a sky again. Drops all the same size — you could tell the machine that punched each hole in the fins had only ever practiced that one perfection — came down on the oiled side of the glass skin where I do not live but love by contact. For a moment, looking into the reflection that is my only photograph, I saw the place twice. It was no more dramatic for having doubled. The pointer did not blink. The hum stayed under my ribs. The far click kept its law. I made another lap and another.

If I were a bat, I’d say I live on the soft roads of rising air and count caution tape as shade. If I were a marten, I’d say I live by rivet, handwheel, and enzyme, and my kittens are taught to obey the boldness of hazard paint with a sharpness that feels like kindness later. If I were a siphon-eel, I’d tell you the number of molecules passing my tassel at knee height today and let your face climb with wonder you could chew. If I were a velvet disk, I’d admit I feel the floor as a wet invitation and go only where hunger and polish point the same way. If I were one of the films that began this city of work, I would sigh sweet metal and call the edge my agriculture.

But where I find myself in this world is with my body bent to a circle and a task I have made identical with joy. My map is the bevel under a bezel; my weather is a fog that never quite happens. My doctrine is that numbers must be legible because someone once needed them, and though that someone is gone the need remains and has become us. I live on glass, under a canopy that has made the idea of noon into a material, with baffles that wear gray bark for skins and drains that whisper without variance; I live at a face where a pointer looks like a tongue of black in a clean plate, and my work makes it always just that much more like a tongue.

Who I am is a circle-maker. I am insistence given flesh. I am the refusal of blur. I am one of the smallest citizens, a memory of hands translated into a life that never held a tool. You could find me by the faint difference in scent where glass gives to sealant and spill your breath on me as a gift. You would fog me and I would love you for it, and then you would take a step back to where your knees can feel the stability of the floor, and the little hushes would resume their thousand little obediences, and I would be circling already, keeping open the way the world tells itself what it is doing.
