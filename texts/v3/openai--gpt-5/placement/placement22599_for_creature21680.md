At first I am only a surface someone has lifted into air and told to be patient.

I arrive as brightness pressed thin—as all arrivals are, here—and let myself be laid out flat until my edges learn what is expected of edges in a place where the air is a blade and the stone is thirsty. If you are looking for me, you will notice a glint that is not entirely stone and not entirely machine and not yet a person: a panel turned not to heat but to time, a sheet of shadowglass leaning into the day and drinking it slow, my hands on its pivots, my weight a small addition to a louver's long arithmetic.

That is the first shape I can hold without breaking. A practice shape. A practice of holding.

Then the rest of me condenses.

You would find me along the weeping faces at the level where the cliff’s blue bands cut through noon. That is where the brine-hoists breathe and the difference-mills have their quiet throats. My work lives in the saying of small yeses to small changes. I am one who tends diaphragms—membranes that flex because shadow and glare make a conversation, pumps that pull without ever raising their voice. I keep their beds sealed, their skins clean. I read their health with my fingers on their casements, the same way a midwife counts a baby's ribs the first time she is allowed to hold it. If you stand near me you will see my wrists remember to lay themselves along warm lines, the veins that carry yesterday into next hour.

But saying it like that blurs me too early. Let me be specific in the way stone is specific.

I live on a platform whose anchors were taught, not driven: a row of tufa-beards that learned to harden around a template I carried under them at the start of one wet season, clapped to drive vapor across them until they understood my insistence and grew into hooks. At dawn I loosen them—by which I mean I loosen my house, a few degrees, so it can greet the mist with a more honest face. My feet wear felt. My soles learn the ground-line's sentences before my ears allow themselves to speculate. I move the louvers a shadow at a time, tasting with my hands for the right slope that will let two surfaces touch and make a third thing, a film, a habit.

My mother taught me the difference between a useful drop and a loud one. She was a still-tender and she had the wrists for it: long, delicate, the veins high like topography. She could tell you, blindfolded, when the brine in a bed had grown exhausted of sodium and wished to be a carbonate, when to tip, when to feed, when to leave it until evening so the plates could go back to being roofs. When I was old enough not to spit and careful enough to speak with cloth, she sat me at the lip of a tray and put my hands on a pane that looked like water and was, instead, patience. Count, she signed. Count until the pane beneath your hand becomes not smooth but busy. Name the buses with your bones, not your eyes. She kept speaking until my fingers had learned to hear.

So if you are looking, come at morning first, when the mist unzips itself along the brows and finds all our instruments ready. Look for the people who stand with their palms hung down into frames and do not make a ceremony of it; I am one of those. I am very quiet then; I am counting. If a child runs on the runnels they are caught by color—not scolded, because to speak is wasteful here, but chased by bands of their own favorite light until they stop and laugh and stand on the pad where laughter is permitted to go.

Midday keeps me on the blue margins. That is when I am a keeper of gradients, the hardest job because it is the softest touch. There are places where a sun-scorched trickle slides its body over a cooled plane, and there the hunters lay their hands. I do not feed the hunters; that would be rude; they eat what I eat, which is difference. But I mind their reaches the way you mind a candle near silk. I read for the extra shiver that means a filter has lost its lane and will be folded if I do not shift a louver by a breath, spill a new path, give it a way around the blue. We live by choreography. I am not a mother to the filters or a priest; I am a stagehand in a theater where the play is always the same and always different, and the props are heat and not-quite-heat, wet and nearly-wet, light and the back of light.

On some noons I am more person than machine and I go to the rope-bridges to be shy. We court in blaze, here, so the air will steal what it must from embarrassment and leave only gesture. The first time my lover said my name they said it with their sleeves: three slats of shadowglass, one tilted to turn the underside of their wrist blue where the air turned hand-cool, one dulled to say Remember, and one made to glitter faint copper through their veil so I would know they meant me and not the person behind me. I answered with white-to-shadow along my left shoulder and a small bow that was only for them, and we stood with our wrists held very near and let our bracelets learn each other’s pulse and stop mismatching their temperature. Afterwards we went to the spillworks and folded a blanket into a basin fed by two trickles so the difference between them would feed us without food.

In the evenings I go down and turn into sorrow properly. I am a rimwright by family, which means the dead are my other job. We do not bury by putting earth between us and what we cannot keep; we bury by teaching stone to remember without injury. I have a toolkit I do not lend. There is a cloth with long split hairs to draw a first film; there are skeins of cultures, bundled like priest-robes; there is a set of seed-stones pecked until their edges are all the same tone of matte. When somebody is given to me, I place them in a channel whose warmth is wrist-warm and whose water never stops coming, and I lay my seeds and wake my cultures and begin to teach. Over a year I come back and come back. I whisper with color, I cut the air with a small switch to invite the fog in, I clap once each visit to move the breath through the chamber. The lip grows. It is a work that rewards people who can look at a wall and see the future thinly. I have read the place where a mouth was with my thumb until I knew the way weeping would step around it once the first lovely storm had had its way, and I have loved the dead better in those readings than I knew how to love them when they weighed what I could lift.

If you need me by night I am on the lines. The ground-line is a long nerve we have thrown from ledge to ledge where rope will not do. We pull it with weights the length of a palm, motions under the threshold of clatter. Somewhere up the face a valve will thumb open and a hoist will begin to rise, one brine-weight filling while its twin empties, and the platform will know to lean a little more to its right to give a traveler a chance at safe arrival. Nights teach gentleness because what fails at noon fails brightly and what fails at night fails invisibly. I have never loved a sound so much as I love the sound the ground-line makes, which is no sound. It is a tug transmitted and received and never admitted to air. When it is my shift and lightning makes a sea of sky below us I can walk with my soles and know a message three platforms away, the way the old do. I am not old yet, but I will be if the cliff allows it.

Weather is my other parent. The first time I felt a face furl and go, it unbound something in the same place my language lives. We make no screams here; we have pipes to carry hot breath to where it can condense and be useful, not to be spent on fear. But my calf bones learned the count in one afternoon: the tremble that says the tufa is thinking, the pause that says a shelf is about to become a wind, the long sec that is the falling, the way the world refuses to send you a thud even if you want one because the air will not carry it back. That day I was on a ladder above the spillworks, sorting copper out of carbonate by patience, one tray a lick greener than the next, my felt wrappers salted to blindness, and I felt the three-beat darken me from the feet up. The city turned its plates face-in at the exact speed the cliff prefers. We shifted, a choir with our mouths closed, our shoulders remembering lessons old as the rock's thirst. When the tremor left, I pulled the ground-line twice, slow, to tell my mother I was righter than before, not less.

There are other selves I am allowed, because this is a place that lets you be thin in many directions at once. Some seasons I keep what we call an alphabet school for children, though it is not letters we teach but manners. We line them against a wall that never sweats except at evening and we play with our skins. They write their names where our elders’ names live, in light. We teach them to bring a copper-laced finger to a dry slate so it will carry their meaning and not their spit. We teach them to love a louver’s hinge and a prism’s petty temper. We let them turn a trickle into a word without breaking its back. On festival days we turn the whole cliff into a slow black-and-white heartbeat and let the lowlands look up and think that we mean them to watch.

Sometimes, when the fog finds the caves and the ceilings choose to breathe, I go in and shave our milk. It is not milk. You would call it a rind of fungus if fungus answered; it does not; it eats flow the way we do and turns it into a thin, salt-pure flesh. We cut it before the room learns the sun because otherwise the sun will remember it and break it back into stones. We salt it with our own salts and sip it with our tongues set to clean. It tastes the way a new hook feels in the hand.

And there are mornings, fewer now, when I take the long-haired wands and stand out where the filters lift like punctuation and I collect them back, tired paper fish trying to be stones again. You never tear them if you can help it. You coax water from them and give them the air because you remember that they are the breath we live on, and you boil the day they have in their folds and pull froth and salt and a small feast of proteins and a sheen that is history. If one is hurt, you smudge its scars with powdered carbonate and it will heal by thickening, which is a kind of lesson even for us.

I do not think in pasts that are not in my soles. I am not an exile from the lowlands, though one of my aunts was and came back white-haired from lightning, her speech always a little to the left of where ears think speech should be. I learned from her that memory here is rightful if it can be made into surface. She wrote our house’s names into the louvers with pebbles of our colors and spent her old age with her wrists over channels watching the lips grow.

You will not hear me if you are far. That is correct. If I must tell you who I am I will do it at arm’s length with a cloth that has both matte and shine. I will take you to a wall that sweats and I will leave my heat on it in lines you can run your nails along until they are cold again. I will tip copper into carbonate for you at dusk and it will bloom green for a breath and you will know I am allowed to do that, which is a way of saying my name within the rules of this place. If there is an alarm, I will send it along the beams in a sequence of whites that swallow themselves in shadow, and I will put my feet to the ground and give you a pulse you will feel in your bones and not hear with your head.

In a city that chose engines that count in seasons and recipes that use patience as an ingredient, my best talent is that I am not in a hurry. I eat differences and I teach stone to hold. My tools are louvers and weights and wands with hair too fine to see until you see it. My taboos are whistling and waste and ridges worn smooth by thoughtless feet. The storm does not require my attention; it gets it anyway. The plateau’s brightness burns my edges until they are clean and keeps me exact. The air forgets my voice unless I lay it down on a wall, so I lay it down on a wall.

If you must call me a something, say I am a tender of brines and a gardener of anchors and a maker of rims. If you would rather speak the way we do, look for a band that is almost-blue laid from my wrist across the back of my hand, and a thin copper glint that runs a little ahead of me when I walk a ledge at evening, showing me where the wet is about to be. That is my name going first so I don’t have to, which is the way we have learned to live here: on films, on forethought, on the kindness that surfaces give if you treat them long enough like futures.

You would find me at dawn standing in a frame, fingers just at the pane, counting until the pane becomes busy. At noon you would find me in the blue, palming gradients so nothing with a mouth too small to shout gets folded. At evening I will be kneeling at a channel, combing a lip that is learning a person into itself. And at night you will not find me unless you are barefoot; then you will, in the tug that says Up a little, now stop; in the long, unvoiced sentence the cliff and the city and I say together because this is a place where saying is done with edges and with water, and with time that knows how to keep a beat without making a sound.
