I would learn my name in my teeth.

Before that I would be only a listening, a body-sized hollow that the place poured through. I would come to knowing the way everyone here does: propped on a porch at the lee of a small hut, fog not yet in its hour, a harp in my hands that is more a way of touching the world than a song-maker. The harp has one line of fiber and a little meteoric bell scratched thin and bright by many mothers. I would be told, as all of us are told: slide until your ribs loosen. There is a quiet thread run through the great chord. It is always there. Your work is to find it.

I would slide. I would be all ache and shiver—it is always colder at the beginning—and then there, a notch like a held breath, and the tremble in my fingers would become a hum I could stand in. My first name would be the tuning we found together that day. A short sound, more felt than heard, a particular easing that softened my jaw and let me drink without my teeth jumping. Elder palms would press the back of my hands and approve. From then on people who needed me would call me by re-setting their harps to that little grace and lifting them so the bell whispered it. I would come like a bead down a string.

I would be a Drift by habit and a Lay by love. Drift because I would be unable to leave a knot alone, because the hollow between anchors would gnaw me until I went out into it; Lay because the first comfort to come true in me would be the clean, useful pleasure of moving a span half a tone and feeling a whole porch stop shaking. I would carry a bundle of cords looped around me like a second ribcage, beads thumb-sorted by weight and pitch, a bone needle drilled fine enough to thread a Scythe hair, a pouch of salt, another of sugar-water in a resin bladder to coax Candlebreath when a node seemed too tired to glow.

If you looked for me at dawn you would find me on slats wetted by Tall Fog, cupping hands to dew-sill and letting the drip fill my mouth slow. Cups spill. Palms learn the chord. I would listen to the membrane over me laboring under the new weight and I would mark the bellies and angles. A good dripper sings the right edge; a bad one drums wrong and makes everyone mean with headache. I would loosen the braid of hair-collectors on one side and tighten on the other until the splash turned to an obedient thread and the neighbors' temper unwound. They would nod without speech and leave me a bit of wax-bloom in thanks later, tucked where only fingers would find it.

Midday is a word that doesn't belong here, but in those hours when the aurora is a far high wash and the wind steadies into something you can count against your thumb, I would go out with a pair of others, one older, one with sense in their heels. We would read the line of hehes from a grazer flock, judge where their herd-tune would lay them, and choose our node to node. The good ways are not ladders but sentences you finish in your bones. Step, hang, breathe, slide the bead, let the world say yes in your sternum, then go. I would taste Scythe in the air sometimes—the faint wrong pocket, a little absence being flung through the weave—and feel the hairs along my arms try to stand in wind that will not let them. We would stop and lay a hush-knot then—three small weights on a cross-beam, no more than a child's tooth each, placed so that a favored predator chord bulks a finger breadth to the left for a day. It is not unkindness to Scythes; it is negotiation. They move; we move. Everyone fed; nobody shakes apart.

At rest we would hang in the lee of a full node, resin glazed by biters so smooth you could polish your inlaid teeth in it. The rooms the biters make are thin as patience, round as the inside of a promise. Press your cheek to their wall and you can hear the city breathing through its chemistry. I would offer them a lick of sugar on a thread and watch the nearest small white being lean into a warmth it evolved to want and cannot find alone. You can learn generosity from an animal that makes houses for both of you without ever knowing your name.

At night there are three kinds of dark: the empty-black that is just what it is; the slow blue of Candlebreath brought to better by careful drip; the green that moves like water poured across glass when the sky chooses to be kind. In any of them I would sit with an elder who keeps a long knot-line wound around one arm like a serpent-mirror. The knot-book is a road written with touch. She would let it run over my forearm while her free hand plucked the porch-rail to voice the weather it remembers. Here is where the resonance went minor for a week and we ate thin and spoke soft; here is where the plant swelled a bridge overnight and the children almost broke their necks trying to be first across it; here is where The Struck walked and the people hid their bells in their teeth. I would practice re-tying the story better than I found it, tightening the places where time had made the beads wander, adding a bead at the end for Return.

I would be small and strung with tendon like everyone else. My teeth would be resin thrice-renewed by then, with three flecks of mirror pressed in where the left canine meets the gums. When I smiled, a handful of stars would come to live inside my mouth. This is how we admit being vain without calling it so: we build the sky into ourselves where we can kiss it. The funny not-birds would hop near and stare at my threads with slow-blink greed. I would spill them a grit of salt and get back a bead I had not noticed was gone two days before. This is commerce as it should be: both of us wrong and right and pleased.

Once in my early going I would hang above a whole nothing and decide to be a person. A quake-sigh would run through the anchors under me like a groan from an old friend, and the span we trusted would go slack a finger's width and then another. We didn't fall; we were people of this place and we had our loops in and our feet did what feet do. But the hut we were going to shifted inside the one breath between heartbeats into a worse singing. Its porch had been tuned for an older world. There were children there and a pair of grazers tethered under and you could taste—the way you taste iron in water—the near arrival of hurt. I would go hand-over-foot with a speed that is mostly permission, and I would pour my bells down that slat and get everyone pressing palms to the rail and listening with me. "Here," I would tap, and I would fix a pair of weights there and there, I would unknot a hair-comb and throw it long like a veil, and I would set one bead on one string and slide it until my molars stopped complaining. The house would soften. The grazers would quit grinding jaw on jaw. The oldest woman would spit what salt she had into my palm and call me twice my own name. When I slept that night I would dream in a higher key and wake too early with my heart doing that terrified kindness that says: you did nothing but adjust what already lived, and still it matters.

Who would I be to others? A runner of lines, a reader of hollows. If a village's knot-book went lost I would be the one they sent to listen their roads back to them. If a Scythe took to using a particular membrane to school its young and everyone's sleep went brittle, I would come and move the growth of hair fraction by fraction until the lesson was no longer kind to teach there. If a fog-tide season went narrow and salt tightened its mouth, I would carry from a richer node without asking for return, and if anyone called me generous I would shrug and touch the bone-bridge under my shoulder where my people keep scars to remember Share and say: return is not a favor; it is physics.

I would love someone against the rules of height and hunger and nothing would catch fire for it. We would splice in the way the old have written: a new cord, resin and hair and beads from both anchor-stones, tied in public with everyone's fingers on it to feel if what we made would hold under weather. It would—most cords do if you don't lie about what you weigh. We would hang a little hut into the chord where the sound goes kind after storm, where biters keep a room warm enough for toes. We would keep a small scarf of grazers and sing the winter ahead of them so they knew where the close food would be. We would make a child if the quiet lines said so and teach them to find their own notch by letting them shake until they didn't. When the time came, I would hold their little wrists and feel the bell tell us their name.

The worst day would still be in tune. The Silence would come one year with no smell and no frost, a dry round that ate notes and breaths. A third of a flock would fall like the quiet falling wrong. We would light fire and feed it metal and hands and hums and hold our breaths and get under the hot air it made and live. After we would be thin in body and voice for a while. I would tune a ward-weight then from a friend who had died earlier, with his family's hands in the resin setting, and hang it at our door. For a year his resting would keep the predator-chords bulged wider, a music of love you do not have to call love to feel. Then we would Return him to outward lines until he was only the world's and the world was only him.

When I was old I would do what old people do here: grow smaller without apologizing, tidy other people's cords with delight, refuse Scythe meat because it tastes too fast in the mouth, braid children's hair-collectors with stories, wear my bells in my teeth when the wind said quake so that hands stayed free, sleep above biters with my palms on their walls and let their warmth think it was mine. I would remember an aurora night high on a skinny web when the world's full choir changed like a creature turning over and the dark made me think for a bare moment that light was a cheap trick. The sky was a bowl of green liquid. Bridges wrote themselves across its skin. Sails drifted in parade. Fungal blue marked the house of everyone I had loved. My own small hut sat under a little shining hair of skin and I thought: the measurement that makes sense is not above or across but how true your knots are where falling would be easy.

If you asked me at any hour where to find me, I would say: look for the notch. Look for the easy strip in a hard chord. I will be standing in it, one heel bit to a line, one hand sliding a bead until your teeth ease. If I am not there it will be because I am a weight at a threshold, singing out of a long-resin sleep to keep a house from shaking apart. That is not an end; it is the last right job. Hold, Ease, Share, Return. We make our lives into tools here, and if we are lucky, after we stop needing them, they still do some work.

If this world would not take me as a person I would still come to it as something shaped by listening. I could be one of Candlebreath's colonies, stowed in a node like a kept ember, eating the thin sweetness wind buys and paying back with dusk so children can learn knots without crying. I could be a bell, a sliver of iron-nickel beaten cold until the plant could stand my speaking. I could be a hush-knot made of someone's hair and someone's patience, thickening a span so the wrong hunter looks the other way. Most days, though, in truth, I would be a thing with hands and feet and a resin smile, the size weather allows, with a city pressed into my skin, moving in short brave darts through long wells of space, a reader of tension who lives long by remembering that everything here was built to hold and to be held.
