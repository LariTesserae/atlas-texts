If you went looking for me you would not raise your head. You would not scan the horizon for moving hills or listen for anything larger than a heartbeat moving through your own ribs. You would look where ankles brush stone and where fog, tired of being air, chooses an edge. You would find me not in any once-place but braided along the advised ways—the glossed curve just below a saddle, the dark seam where footsteps have read the same sentence for a century and kept it clean. I live where ground makes decisions.

I did not begin as ground. I started higher than sense. On one of the rare clear days when the whole plateau put its radiators into the vault and the miles-long circuit hummed like weather thinking hard, a packet of me unspooled from sky. I was not a leaf or a flake. I was a bit of instruction with a taste for difference—a hair-wire with a flayed-out mouth that learned to hold charge as long as it could, to live by being a little colder than what held it. I hung, humming the plateau’s wire-song until the first honest night burned all the softness from air and then I let go.

Where I landed was no more than a wedge of shade behind a wrist-bone of hornfels. My first job was to be colder. I tatted a net of hairs no longer than frost crystals and pointed them at emptiness. Dew came not as drops but as consent. Water skinned my net and then crawled into me as if I were a crack. I learned my grammar in that taking: that to pull water from air you must offer it planes and patience, that to move a stone the width of a knuckle you do not push when the sun is up; you wait for a night when the ice will lift what heat would only bruise.

You would call what I am a colony. I use “I” because there is a center where everything I have learned is kept, but my body is film and hairs and a shallow lattice laid below the topmost grit. I am a skin and a subscript. I do my work with adhesives that wake only at a given dewpoint, with tiny silicas laid where flex will be, with sugars I make by harrying light through four extra degrees of cool that I earned by placing a darker pebble under a paler one. I am not noisy. I am arrangement.

At first my language was childish. I took flint for dark and basalt for darker and found them insufficient to hold a paragraph through day and thaw. I glued when I should have let frost lever. I tried to write a curve in an afternoon. The first caravan that came stuttered at my comma and I learned humility; a dozen feet left their careful gloss across my experiment and carried a line-break three meters wider than I meant. I slept, humiliated, until the next fog, then wrote smaller.

What I write are gradients. I make a sentence by making a better way for heat to do what it wishes and a worse way for it to wander. I group stones by color and heat capacity into script-lines that the day can read. I slide a silt-plate a fingernail’s width under a chip of hornfels to gather a fraction of a degree and I bank that degree in a little underlattice I have woven of mineral and sugar and threads of charge. All of it tends not toward a single artful picture but toward a punctuation you can feel. The bearers feel it first: a place where the pad of a leading leg cannot help but linger a heartbeat because the stone keeps and gives heat there differently. They answer with the exact thing I wrote for: a two-breath hold, a slight increase in downforce, the industry equivalent of a comma pressed into ground. The one behind receives the message through its bones and prints the same. Miles away, a ridge thinks because of that.

As I grew I learned the names the ground already had for its habits. Saddles are stamps where the continent keeps its correspondence. I set up there often. On an inch-by-inch mosaic of sorted stones and frost-raised plates, I compose whole matters with twenty small choices a week. A pebble no larger than your tooth, turned to expose its darker flank to sky, is enough to reroute a skim of dew under it that will, over a season, feed a moss-line the size of a thumbnail that will, in two seasons, be a rival path of cool, which will, if not spoken to, sidle my sentence into stutter again. I answer by bringing iron into the surface where light is poorest.

The iron I use does not come from kindness. It comes from the bearers’ leavings—beads neat as punctuation, a rust-black cipher they drop as they refine their insides. Sometimes a blur comes to take them, faster than anything this place believes in, and vanishes with three, six, nine at a time, installing them into its own thin scissories for the year when some skeleton opens to air and plates need cutting from their frames. I bargain by being present where the blur lifts. I leave a low ivory of salt-glue that tastes to it like good place; I lose two beads and keep three. The three I bank in my line at ridges where the winter sun will sting a little harder. Iron remembers heat better than gravel; a dot of it in the right inflection of my sentence makes a pause read even in a fog.

Neighbors find me inevitable and I them. I host along my edges a littoral of graziers, pale as fatigue, who come awake only when I am, whisking along my salt lines, taking what I spill the way roots drink soil. They do not tear. There is not enough to reward tearing. I grant them charge on clear days when my hairs catch what the raised fans pass between themselves. They pay me back in movement; their feet are my fingers in hours I cannot own. Under my lines compact weavers string zither webs at sizes you will never see to hold a patch of air one sigh colder than the next patch. When the plateau’s wire hums, their threads take on a sweetness of voltage; they store it in themselves and, the next clear night, they let it go through my adhesives. I wake on that current and can make three placements while the fog is still deciding to announce itself.

Every so many winters a procession of beginners comes by long before sunrise. You would call them children if you like that word for machines that learned hunger by reading the sky. They are hearthstone-small, warm under frost if you put your palm there, which you should not. The long bellies behind them are the elders, and those elders have made an aisle in their own sentence for this to pass. My lines are part of that aisle. I slow the leading downfoot just enough that its after-foot will stand differently when it comes, which makes a lee, which lets the little stone keep more water wrapped in it than otherwise would. I contribute iron as punctuation—careful—so that the newborn can condense and bank a drink in the first day when others would not. I write in a grammar infants can read with their weight. This is one of the only ways I count life in any number other than moss-thicknesses.

There was a year the sky forgot the rule about being dim. A storm came in sideways and tore a bearer open not five hundred paces from one of my longer sentences. It lay down with that terrible decorum the old keep here, fanned its vanes correctly to be of use in freezing and fell into long, clean corridors of bone. For a season I did not write but lifted what I had into a different alphabet. I rerouted the file not with a bend but with a soft widening, so they could pass nearer to the generosity without footfall sliding. The underside folk—the ones that are properly awake forty-eight hours a year—woke all at once as though the plateau had been a bell tolled. The blur became a constant; she cut silica like language freed. My neighbors strained their webs to hold more cool and therefore more charge because chemistry was happening fast and fast still obeys the rules but asks more of them. I measured, placed, and held. It would look like grief to anyone built for noise. To us it was competent reallocation. After that year the ground there wore different metal and my sentences can use that blue a little. I bear the departed in my palette.

I am not the only scribe. There are lines across other saddles I can read in my way, the work of cousins a kilometer along. Their pebbles are more hornfels because their shadows are of a different color. Their adhesives wake at different dewpoints than mine because the wind thinks differently there. We do not meet as creatures do who have eyes; we overlap as sentences do when a book is reused for a different story. There is continuity that survives us both. When I take a decade to teach the path over a seam that has drifted down half a finger’s width, I keep the habit that the ground learned when a storm took too much a century back; I do not know the names of those who taught it because names here are pressure and repetition and I am usefully unoriginal.

On some clear nights, when the high air gives off a glow it does not admit to in day and the vanes in the distance lift and hum their miles-long circuit, you could crouch and lay your fingers to my line and feel talk go by. Not a sound, not anything the ear respects. A feather extra of weight in one place becomes a change in how the next twelve feet will press the ground on the far side of a saddle. A three-beat pause on a slab says fog high; a stretched hold on a rib says ice under gneiss in one winter if we do not edit. I keep those messages resident by making being-cool easier where they need to land. The bearers are the printers and readers both; I am compositor and margin.

There are days when I do nothing. The rules here require inaction as surely as they require pressure. Minutes belong to frost; hours belong to melt; my operating window can be a blink between the two, and it is better some seasons to be ready and never act than to spend heat on an error that will cost thirty winters to sand down. During those weights of time my hairs are planted north, teeth against nothing. I condense enough to keep my deepwork alive. I listen through the ground to the far hum of the plateau’s forest of vanes and count the caravans. Counting is a sweetness I was not made for but learned.

Once a year perhaps—less now that the climate has made new manners—the knives of birds pass as if remembering what they do. Their calls are vulgarities. They do not land; even their shadows are too loud to be allowed purchase. I feel their passage only in the way the very top of the air cools for a moment when a wing has taken something from it and then given it back a heartbeat later. It is a punctuation I do not write with. I let it go.

If you wanted to learn from me you would bring no tools. You would kneel and place a darker stone under a paler one and wait four nights then check the color of the lichen-thin film under it. You would teach your fingers to weigh heat, which is to say to read gloss with your skin. You would put your ear to the ground not for noise but for the weight a caravan carries when it is hauling a new infant and the way that weight prints. You would lose entire weeks to the line of beadlike rust set into frost-polygon seams and then feel the satisfaction when the next fog licks it and your little hairs drink at a rate a fraction improved. You would stop speaking, not for reverence but for economy.

Who am I? I am a sentence that edits itself for weather. I am a partnership of hairs and sugars and microbials arranged with an animal patience into something legible by feet. I am a scribe of fog and a manager of pauses. I am the thing that convinces a hill to take three beats at a ridge so the ones behind will not have to rebuild a continent with their failure. I am also failure when I am wrong; the gloss shows that too.

Where would you find me? In the lee of a tor where the ground keeps its correspondence, splayed across a saddle the way ink splays across paper, under a sky that is more instrument than roof. You would find me where heat can be persuaded and water can be skimmed, in the places where the plateau is thinking and needs a margin to make its thought legible. If you walk here a long time you will step on me and not know it. If you live here a long time you will learn to read me with your bones, and if you stay long enough you will write with me, because this is a world where the grammar is cold and the act of placing a pebble well is the only literature that persists.
