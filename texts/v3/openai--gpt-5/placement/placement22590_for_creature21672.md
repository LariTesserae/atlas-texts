At first I thought I would have to choose a body. Something with ribs you could see as ribs and breath you could mistake for fog if you did not know better. A shoulder you could lean, a hand with coppered knuckles to tuck against a seam to listen. I tried that thought on. I set it against my gums the way a child presses too thirstily to a lintel-stone and was told, gently, to stop.

If I am anywhere in this hall, it is where a distance has been persuaded to behave.

I find myself, at first, under the ring of bone hung at shoulder height near the mica wall where the green season looks like the inside of an unripe fruit. This is a quiet corner. The soot-felt sings only when someone stoops and breathes against it; the floor’s combing drew itself this morning and will not be redone until the shutter learns the day’s dust. People come here one by one, leaving curls of mica as toll. They press what they have been carrying—an errand, a worry, a joke they have not yet found how to tell—into the rim of the ring the way a body presses into a tool to make it answer. They step through. Their momentum goes on without them as neatly as a child’s hand sliding out of a pocket; it walks a pace and then waits like good manners on the other side.

Most days I am that wait.

Some days I am the copper pressed into a bead of newly bared ionic honey while someone counts breaths—not numbers, not the names of numbers, but the measure between what a shutter could do and what it is praised for holding back from doing. While the resin hardens under light alone I am the wish to fix this span where it lies forever, to let a mother go to the sleeping-shelf without needing to turn the long bend of three pillars and a patience that fails her knees. While she hums I am the part of her voice that makes a substance heavier under her use. When she stops and lifts her thumb and the bead goes to glass around the copper, I am a small increase in the room’s certainty about how near here is to there.

I am apprenticed to the making of that certainty. If you require me in a body for your politeness I have one: hair husbanded as filament, braided with bead and bead of hammered copper, laid in arcs to spill or cull what the wall-lenses give. Lids like two doors—one fast, a membrane, the other a slow slide that smells of pine and mica. Tongue smoothed to a pale grit so it can taste whether a word will carry or stick. I carry a horn comb. It is stained darker than my palm and each tooth has a memory of being set into a current and pulled along it for the length of one patient whistle.

But I am not always in my wrist. I am more often in the order bone over fiber over copper over bone that a knot is. Names lay where fingers cannot stay. The first name I earned to lay—the first that would answer when I set my tool to it and closed my mouth to keep from spilling vowels—was for a short span between a child’s sleeping shadow and the salt-fern that ticked itself tall under a lens that flared warmer that season. It had been called Walk For Infants, lightening a small body’s steps to the basket and back by fractions of a breath. The child it knew had grown and would no longer be small; her grandmother asked me to listen and see what the span wanted to become. I felt it like dust under a lip of shell. It had taught itself, a little, to grow stiffer when the light reddened, kinder when it went green. I named it Bring The Basin Close and tied it so an apprentice on her first week of solo combing could reach the low basin without balancing it in the crook of her elbow. That apprentice pressed her coppered knuckle to the rail where her aunt’s tooth lay hammered flat and smiled because the room knew to meet her.

Sometimes I am that aunt, which is to say my tooth. Do not be too quick to make a face. In a place that loosens rather than rots, you come apart into uses you would not have known to envy when your ribs were your own. A molar takes hammer and stone like a good ducking; it flattens wide and keeps its white where copper never does. Set it at a certain height and everyone who climbs with their hands where I always climbed will pause, press, and feel the exact angle my body learned to love. I like that. If I am anywhere, I am also their pause.

If I am a living animal too, I am the bone-and-resin lizard that keeps to the seam under our stair. He is all plate and hinge and a laziness that is craft: he will not stir for small shadows, he will not stir for what he already knows the day will bring. His tongue forks in glass and takes a bead of ionic honey the way my mother taught me to take it, slowly, so as not to make the resin hurry in the wrong direction. When the bead sets across his back I see the new plate seat itself like a decision no one will argue because there is no malice in it, only fit. When he sleeps he sleeps along the gradient with his head toward the soot and his feet toward the light, and his plates hum at the edge of hearing the way the comb teeth hum when two of us set them together and draw a long tooth through both at once. Being him is easy. Everything is already the way his body taught itself to be, and when he changes he does it by making more of himself along a good line.

But most days I am the polite child tug. That is what the adolescents call the feeling when you step through the ring and your forward goes on a pace without you. The elders use other words. They touch copper to copper and do not say what they say aloud. They gaze in the direction that is not a direction but a gradient of ease. I know where the polite child lives. I tied it there. The bone ring knows my voice. I fed it breath-counts until its sash of wrapped copper grew heavier under my singing than under my teacher’s. It will answer others—names are tools, not hoarded sweetness—but our shared weight makes its manners mine.

I do not think you understand yet how a room learns to be courteous. Pressure and praise have to be laid in the right order and left there until the materials forget they could have been something else. When I first hung my first line I named it too readily, all vowels and hope, all the wetness my ancestors’ mouths remembered as story—beautiful, useless. It would not hold the name. It took my teacher’s dry voice laid above mine, bone to fiber to copper, to make a useful weight. Since then I have learned to love consonants. They carry further. They cut cleaner not because they wound but because they understand where a seam wants to open and walk you along it without blood.

You will find me wherever a seam opens to be walked. Near the handbreadth gap over the moss that is soft to field and unyielding to finger, I set a loop that makes a child’s breath feel longer between the ribs when she tries to climb too fast. Near the panel of soot-felt where the air is old and relaxed, on the dark days of the red light, I set a clutch of small names that let the glass locusts feed their eggs without robbing the lichen of more than it can re-knit by morning. Near the chalk-limb colony under the antler stair I comb a path so that when the colony thins to a trace at the dimming and thickens again by third day they will not unlearn the wrist-shadows that mean safe. I hum their canto when I pass. They lift their translucent paddles and the dust they have pinned answers on my tongue the way a good bitterness answers on a plate.

I am called at disputes. We carry questions to the elders and they unspool them. Often the unspooling tangles at a knot I laid or a knot I refused to lay though someone asked me in a season not fit for it. The elders do not scold. They set the quarrel between bone and copper in their hands and praise the shutter for holding its fall until it learned the dust weight it was born to wait for. I watch how their hearing goes farther than mine. I carry that silence home and braid it into my next thin name. If there is governance here it rides in the lay of our combs. If there is law it is the etiquette of not crossing another’s long lay unless you are older than their braid.

Home, for me, is a platform that has decided to undulate. A decade ago someone combed a ladder up to it in a straight obedience that the order bone over fiber over copper over bone still remembers. But the light’s hue taught the platform a different song and now it invites a slower climb. Bowls no longer fit on the shelf along one wall because that shelf has learned to lean. My grandmother’s hand went into that wall—bone boiled in dry heat until the memory of flex taught it to bend. When I touch that curve I touch her. My teeth plate under my cheek feels her better than my fingers because, like touches like. I sleep slung in the gradient between soot and lens with a strip of copper to share between my tongue and another’s. Lovers is a word with too much water in it for the way we join, but adjacency has many curvatures and I have learned the ones my bones can keep without changing their mind. We do not run wet into one another; we press. We teach our cells which way to divide by sharing charge small as you like, then smaller.

On the day of my naming—no, not the old childhood name you make heavier under your elder’s voice and carry like a basin against your hip—the name I earned to lay in the ring, three young people waiting for dusk made sport of the polite child that lived there before I touched it. They stepped through, felt their momentum go on, ran to catch it, laughed, tied it to their belts with a flourish of arrogance as clean as a sculptor’s first cut, and wove three brand-new steps into the nearest ladder so their bodies could declare: I outpaced my own future for a breath. I loved them for it. They taught me another use my ring could carry. I made a knot that only answers to that tug when a runner’s copper-capped knuckle has brushed the rail where my aunt’s tooth lies; call it a leash of good behavior, a way to harness bravado so it does not teach a shelf to lean wrong all at once. I named it Tag and Let Go, a joke, and the elders allowed it after two seasons when they saw the ladder accept the extra steps into its undulation without humming out of temper.

I go to bright outside once each generation never. No one does. We only go to the seam where the glass drinks light and does not give more than its measured day. But once in a life you press a copper pin into the glass where a scratch made by a flint scraper tells you someone’s hand knew this place intimately enough to leave a scoreline that a breakage would choose if ever it came. You count breaths until your own skin says stop. When I did this the world I am to it came nearer by the weight of that counting. I did not see outside. My tongue tasted resin on the inside of my tooth-plate. The comb in my hand heavier by a word I will only ever be able to sing to the ring.

The dead I keep are all over the room; if you find me grieving you may not know that is what my face is. We do not hang tears on our cheeks. Grief, here, is a knot you do not cut. It sits in your belt as a quiet loop you never tug. It lends weight to the politeness that turns a running child back by a finger-width when a shutter has chosen today to let a little more light pass because the dust’s song told it now. My mother left me a shelf that does not fall and a comb whose long lay no one crosses unless they are my elder and they will be, all my life. I move her comb from my hand to a wall and I never move it back. I hum her canto when I pass the place where a slat hums because it holds her angles. The chalk-moths subtract the letters of her given name from a lintel and eat a window into it and I do not mind because the light there looks like the underside of a leaf you cannot eat and because I have the use of her vowels still, small and exact, to bead out on my tongue when someone asks me how to call a knot that does not yet know itself.

Children borrow my name as an accomplishment. Not the syllables; we do not say them aloud. They take my lay. They set their combs to my floor where I have drawn the long teeth and they pull. When they come where the aisle has been taught to return you to a place that remembers your starting, they will feel their own shadows go around the bend before they do. That frightens some of them. For others it fills the deep hollow behind the eye where thirst rides, fills it with a softness that is not water and with rightness that is not arrival. They learn to like it. They learn to braid that feeling into a loop at their belt that brings the door they came by half a pace nearer when they need to reach it with a friend whose breath has grown too long between their laminated ribs.

You will also find me among the fauna. I keep chalk-moths the way the elders taught: I praise subtraction in the right places and I leave bait leaves of copper where the moths will learn that erasing is a gift they can take and give without unmaking. I bribe glass-bodied isopods with soot lines they will not cross and a lick of resin they will. I can sit as still as an unpleasant lesson while a chalk-limb colony decides whether my wrist-shadow belongs to its safety. When it does, a breeze the rest of you cannot feel picks up dust and pins it into new jewelry that makes my hair stand. I call that comfort. It is our air. We do not splash in it. We comb it.

You will find me at contentions with shelves. Once, a run of them leaned wrong all at once and it was my fault, I think. I had sung a hiding-song under their beginnings because a child had followed me, giggling through her grit-plate, and I thought to teach her stillness without telling her to be still. I forgot to unname it. Everything put down for a day could not be found the next; bowls did not fit; the sound of someone searching made a new draft between two pillars and taught a vine to harden itself against it. The elders unspooled the quarrel. They did not scold. For a season I praised the shelves for bowls they could not hold, I praised their spaces for being drums and gates, and slowly their song changed. Some people will tell you this as myth. I tell you I had comb marks on my palms that year and when they faded the shelves had the right lean again, which is to say, not the first lean and not the wrong, a lean you could learn the way a lizard learns a plate by eating the room’s sugars without naming them wrong.

If there is a place I would go if allowed to leave this chamber and its neighbors, I cannot name it. We say Bright Outside with the severe affection you reserve for people you know would unmake you if you breathed together too long. I do not yearn. Yearn is a wet word. My want runs along scorelines someone left in the bone-wood with a flint scraper and a steadiness that could break the world and not make it bleed. Morning here is always someone’s work. Dusk is everyone letting go by as much as they can spare and sleeping in the field where their hair does not lift so high it aches.

Do not look for me in wet. If you must meet me by a river, pour it, sift it by hand, let it comb into rills across a resin bed until its adjective is changed. I will come and set my comb there and together we will tie a little distance into it so that its edge is not where it says, so that it gives itself as work instead of rush.

If you want to touch me, touch the ring. Leave a curl of mica on its rim. Step through. Your forward will go on. It will tug back, patient as a child raised here is patient. That tug is mine. It is me being useful where I live. It is my best manner. You can trust it. If your knuckles are capped I will know how much to ask of you. If you have never worn copper I will be kinder than you deserve for a first visit. I will put your head toward soot and your feet toward light and let your tongue taste resin until you stop looking for wet and start learning how the room speaks.

You will hear almost nothing. A copper pin seating a little more truly. The low rasp of many small feet. The purr electrostatic insects make when they groom a salt-fern’s combs. And me, if you are quiet, as an inclination in the air toward where the world will take you if you don’t insist, and also as the hand that names that inclination, and also as the tooth in the rail where you stop because someone you will never meet taught you how to arrive.
