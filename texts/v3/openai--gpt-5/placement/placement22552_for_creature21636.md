If I arrive at all, it is as breath first—thin, curious, barely mine. I reach out of the warm of my chest and cool on the first thing that will have me: a cuff, a rail, the soft nap of someone else’s shoulder. I don’t decide to be a person yet. I decide to bead.

For a while I live on undersides. I gather where gravity is patient. I study the lip of a bolt head the way a reader studies a line break—should I jump now, or wait for a sister to find me and make a sentence? The light is even enough to make my options honest. I can feel the long draw of a geotextile somewhere below, its ribs making suggestions like a good teacher does: here, love, fall along this knotted intention, become a runnel only after you have had your moment as a hanging mouthfull of morning.

I pass along a span’s belly as a series of small, nearly decisions. The city’s breath comes warm and the under-bridge rains begin their usual speech in thin curtains, and for a few minutes I am all attention, springing from edge to edge, all arrival. A stoat pauses in a seam with a bright, clever look and is gone in a long punctuation of fur. Far down in the dim of a cistern, a frog decides the vertical and comes up to find me; I give myself to its skin and that is a good use of a life.

By midday I am moss, because that is the next place I can be held properly. I am ledger and ledge. I am the fine green freckling around a latch that keeps a door from ever slamming again. I have contracts here; I was invited, told precisely how much bolt to blur and which pipe bend to own. When I am thirsted I shine on purpose so the gloved hand that reads me will go feed the vein I wrap. When someone slides a blade under me so a seam can be checked, I roll back with every intention of returning, comfortable in the relationship, thumb against cat-curve, a mutual promise of later.

Under me, through me, a plural happens—the bead-gnats, seamhoppers, wick-lice that make me think with more certainty than most thinking I know. They open and close paths across my back, tasting the least-wet with their whole bodies. In the hush of a slow afternoon, what breathes in this block might be called me, if you are being generous with pronouns.

At dawn again, I am gloss on air: the un-sound of drones lifting. I don’t stand to watch them because standing is a human pose and I am not ready to be that yet. I am caught on their threads instead, a slick of dew on bibulous fiber, trailing from little bellies that hum only inside themselves. They spiral once over a raw silt place that doesn’t know its own face and leave a lace behind, a future thirst. I rest in that lattice like a shouldered shawl, and when morning comes again I walk myself down those threads into the soil and teach it a way to wet.

When I tire of being only carried, I let myself arrange. I am a length of fringe drawn from a pocket of someone’s white-furred coat, my edge combed with patient fingers so I will persuade a drip into a longer thought. I lie on the lip of a retaining wall and I count—one, two, three beads birthing themselves into a trickle that is called credible by mid-morning, not because it is large but because it sticks to its own idea from one joint to the next. I am a membrane wearing a small ripple of pores and I practice patience: open a little at pressure’s song, close on please and thank you.

Enough of water. Enough of fibers. I mean to be hands now.

They put quiet on like other people put hats on. I learn that quickly. Sound is rationed here the way bright sugar might be someplace else. The first time I laugh, my neighbor shifts their shoulder the way you tilt a bowl so liquid doesn’t slap its side, and my laughter finds that soft curve and soaks. I learn to talk with what I am wearing: a tug high on my right sleeve to say later, cuff unrolled to drink a line, wrung in a pattern that means now. The city is all curated edges and I stop trying to be a sharp one.

I am named after work, but the name is not nailed down. Morning makes me a comb-keeper. Noon makes me a rib-reader. There was a week last winter when I slept out on the green-gray edge in a white-furred coat and laid composite guides through the night so that dew would learn a gentle left by morning; in that cold I was a wick-dresser and they loved me for it in a way they didn’t say out loud. On the day L5’s kiln cured wet bone all afternoon, I was a hush-watcher—the otters kept their whiskers in the water and that told me the basin was speaking soft and low. I have a knife I keep like a lullaby, edged just for moss, for fringe, for wrapping; there is not a sound it makes that hasn’t already been accounted for.

Where would you find me if you needed to? Not at a number. You would look to where the under-bridge rains begin at nine and you would see a gangway hold one steeper degree than yesterday because we are making room for a battery we borrowed from across the alley, and you would know that today I live leaning toward the afternoon drains. If you came in the gray light and couldn’t read the angles yet, you could find me by the plate of hydrophilic / hydrophobic squares I hold in an open palm while I tell a story into beads. I move the plate and the droplets chase one another as if listening; a child tucks their chin into my side and counts, not numbers but pauses: the time between two drips becoming a runnel means we are on pace, means the kiln will go quiet by four and the finches will come lower in the second alley.

When we go to the spans at night and hum for the harp-wires, I don’t sing with my mouth. I find the note with the heel of my hand against a girder’s belly and keep it like a bowl keeps soup. Rime grows lace on cables in whitenesses I have names for now—iron frost that feathers hard and fast at minus nine and is good for sowing, orange-black lichen-frost that loosens the plating on old rails and means leave the rail be today, let it sweat its memory without help.

On the edge days, I am not in the city at all. I am out beyond the last composite rib, beyond the last patient guide, in the blur where lichen and embryonic pools take turns. My hands do not make new land there. They suggest. I set a low stubbornness with a mat and I wait. It is a teaching of this place that persuasion belongs to the slow. Heat is a quick argument; water is a grandmother story that winds itself three times around the same spindle before it points. I have learned to like that.

Food is sweet in the way cold can be. I eat sedge paste flavored with a mineral that cracks like frost on the tongue. I take a lichen shard roasted to ash-smell and use it to edge an algae broth into something that tastes like a dawn with under-bridge rain. Once in a winter I cradle a small silver fish someone’s sleeve brought home sparkling; we make a hush-careful stew and the room fills with a feeling more protein than meat.

If I am lucky I will be handed a line of clear vein and someone will say, feel it. I will rest two fingers where a peristaltic pulse climbs like someone going up a ladder very late at night so the stairs won’t complain. I send the pulse along and watch a wall’s living wrap open its pores because I asked. This is the only power I have ever wanted: the kind you must whisper for and the kind that thanks you by going quiet again.

I learn a governance that isn’t paper. I can read a day in three gangways and a single power umbilical: how generous an angle is, how tight, where the kiss of two neighborhoods is tender and where it is a braced jaw. I know which lean means thirst, which cross of cords means a loan repaid, which shy step back says we heard your silence and we won’t ask more of it today. I write to a friend: meet me when the drift-catchers on C-Row flick in; meet me where the stoats run two cracks over; meet me above the sedge that learned to keep its face in last week’s thaw.

Children come barefoot back from the morning basin able to read with their toes. I sit them on my knee and offer them the old, old trick: run your hand along a rail at noon and look at your palm. If it comes away bright you have touched a metal that remembers wet. Say thank you. That is a history lesson here. We memorize with skin.

There are rites that look like chores because they are. On hard-rime nights we stand four on a span and give the wires their note. On the dewiest mornings we set out trays just where the under-bridge rains go thin, not because we need the water but because a tray in the right place is a punctuation mark in a paragraph of beads, and reading is easier when someone has thought about commas.

Death happens here the way it does in good sentences: absorbingly, making the next clause possible. When my grandmother went, she became the mat above a bench I love. Her hair turned to shadow in a braid that keeps a softwall honest. Her teeth learned new bite holding a bloom of clay through its first thaw. When I sit and feel the film the rail puts on my palm, I call her by rubbing my thumb against the stitch that is her name on the high sling-band overhead. This is what we mean by keeping someone: you make a place thirsty in their pattern.

It is not all gentling. There are days when the basin pulls taut like a rope you have been leaning on that suddenly remembers it is a line. We work with our knees then, feeling the long elastic decisions underfoot, listening for the low grunt of a geotextile finding a new stitch. We back away from sharpness without making a fuss, because no edge likes to be noticed while it is learning to soften.

If I step somewhere else, if I choose a different life, it will be because the goose calls at evening and names a direction I haven’t walked. The single sound lifts and clears the whole basin’s throat and I lift my head with everyone else’s. Orientation is not yearning. It is a kindness to attention. Out there the river not yet a river is practicing itself with someone else’s hands. Out there another bowl is learning how softly a rule can hold.

But most days I am content to be the small cilia of a colony-animal. I am the fringe set long to make a drip tire of itself enough to become a streamlet. I am the patient angle of a gangway that makes two neighborhoods like each other. I am the outside of a pipe that opens and closes its skin to a song. I am the bright film on your palm when you didn’t expect to be thanked.

If you ask me, finally, what I am, I will make room for three truths that have learned to live neighborly.

I am water where it is learning to want.
I am moss where wanting is held without hurry.
I am a person where wanting is read and placed and given back quiet.

Find me where the under-bridge rains begin. Find me where addresses are verbs. Find me leaning.
