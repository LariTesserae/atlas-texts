At the threshold I would have to shed the part of me that thinks speech begins in the mouth.

Heat would come first, that patient stowaway that slips under your clothes and remembers the last hand that pressed a sleeve. Then the light—bruise and lichen, honeyed and cold—would refuse to be forced into day, and I would understand that seeing here is consent, not conquest. My knuckles would graze the close catwalks before my eyes permitted me a room. I would put my palm to the rail, because that is how sentences start.

I would arrive thinking I am a person, and I would unlearn that fast. I would become a listening.

At first, I would be borrowed. A quiet place is a lender with strict terms. I would borrow a rag tied at the hip and learn to pay machines with what it collects, wiping the soft green currency from brass and copper until the cloth darkens and the wire gleams and hums a fraction truer. I would borrow sandals lined with perpetual felt and brush their soles with oil morning and night so my step can pass in rumor. I would borrow the manners of a library and give them back as muscle memory.

I would live on the middle deck, because that is where the body of this place moves like a body—people and making and the business of being damp without apology. My tin-and-timber box would wedge under an arch whose basalt remembers tools the way skin remembers childhood scrapes: edges with long resentments, scars that flash white when you run a lamp along them. On the wall I would stencil a mark in lampblack, something more about time than about name, because here wealth is an hour and meaning is a minute when a fall-line is yours.

I would be apprenticed to the Attending, because my chest loves a metronome. The work would explain itself as posture more than as task. One palm dragging the cable trays until the oil and crushed lichen paint my hand an herbal green. The other hand spread against my sternum to find the seam where what is strung through the room passes into what is strung through me. I would chant back the note to the old man with chalk in his damp pocket, and he would do the math with a soft click of tongue and a nod—up there, that turnbuckle, a hair’s width. The wrench, felt-wrapped to kill the clink, would pass into small hands moth-pale with the absence of sun. I would learn to lift my face without lifting my voice.

But I would not settle. Some days my bones would belong to belt-driven pumps the color of old olives. I would sit with my shoulder kissing basalt because that is where the warmth is true; I would listen until I could tell a belt’s opinion and answer it with a tightening nobody hears, with a smear of grease like a kindness offered in a whisper. Pumps like to talk in syllables of rubber and wear. I would learn them the way you learn a person with whom you share a bed and a debt.

On others I would be a runnel-keeper with hands that never quite dry. Lilies would teach me tilt and timing. I would come to know exactly when a frog’s egg is thinking of becoming an eye and when a heart half the size of a lentil stutters itself ready to beat the world. I would learn the runnel’s accent: how weak-tea water brightens or dulls when a charge somewhere spalls a plate and the chamber discovers a new tooth to tongue. My feet would map every slickness that means a basin wants a different angle.

If there is a religion here, it is the route of a droplet. I would convert.

There is a moment before the chamber hears rain when the wires’ voice shifts the width of a thought. You can’t explain it to someone who has not put their ear against a ladder and been rewarded with a murmur no instrument is petty enough to measure. The air’s holdings loosen then; vapor turns obedient in places, obstinate in others. The beads start to gather and align like an idea you’ve been polite to and now must welcome.

I would climb into that. Slow, careful, no noise with edges. Copper and carbon threads would sing under my thighs where years ago they kissed and branded crosshatches into old skin; I would inherit those calligraphies. The spindles would show me how a single path can decide to become two thinner had it thought of it. My fingers would live in the small unpublic ways at the back of things, at the little wrench’s nib that carries a breath between hums. A hair one way, and the bead will pause to consider a second route. A hair the other, and it will remember gravity. It is not power; it is courtship.

In the minute when everything that can be green lifts on invisible ankles to listen, I would be a leaf-cup, a hand at the correct angle, a wick that understands there are laws that are not written but wetted. If you asked me why I do this work, I would press my lips to your ear and count, because numbers here arrive best as hush.

When the chamber does the thing they call breathing—long deepening warmth, then a continental sigh—I would lie on my back on the middle deck and put my spine to the basalt. I would be a barometer made of vertebrae. Some will say it’s the river above leaning heavy or light by moods; others that the stone is a loaf cooling with its oven door ajar, still telling itself the story of when it was fluid. I would not need a truth as long as I have a pulse to tune to.

There are other lives I would borrow to see if any of them fit closer than the one with pockets.

A moth’s fringed feet go through the world as comb and sermon. I would take the white of rain-wet paper for my wings and stand at a lamp until I learned the edge of heat without flame. I would walk the wire the way scripture walks a mind: leaving a salt that looks like belief if you squint. People will argue with softness about whether I am part of the machine’s pedagogy, whether my little trails coax courage out of water. They will not argue aloud. They will “hm” through their noses and rub oil into a toggle and let superstition place its small, clean hand over science’s mouth. I would like to be an argument no one risks voicing for fear of losing a minute’s rain.

For a week I could make peace with being the bell on the gecko’s route. A lake tapping a glass, the sound has no ambition apart from not waking sleeping necks into complaint. I would ring, and bodies would become path. I would have no name because names presume a plurality. I would be acknowledged and that would be enough.

For an afternoon I would accept being a frog that is mostly transparency and nerves. I would arrive with my organs like pale fish visible in a bowl you aren’t sure you own. I would eat something you can’t see and I can’t name. I would vanish into a hairline that was not a crack until I made it one. In me the river would practice being a rumor.

If I were tired, I would be lichen, the lamp that decided plant was a better job. I would bead along rails and make sight seem generous instead of extracted. My glow would be antiphonal to the bruise-amber bulbs. I would be an affable edge in a place with so many sharp ones.

If I had to be a rule, I would be No screaming. I would incorporate myself into flexed wrists and oiled sandals and wrenches wrapped with felt. I would sit alongside No naked flame and They cook where the rock is most generous, and I would learn to go by other names: decency, survival, membership.

But when the work found me without any costume to wear, when my own skin needed to be the tool, I would be what the chamber has been training people to be since it first let someone nail a switchboard directly into its cooling body. I would be the listening that makes weather civil.

They would call me Attending—not as a title but as a verb you can do all day without owning it.

I would tend to a calendar that is made of a drumbeat under lichen. I would remember that your family’s hour isn’t a superstition; it’s a gravity you negotiated last long-night exhale and will hand to your child when they first manage to match a wire’s voice with their own. When the boar-lolling generator sulks into a belt’s sulk, I would get my shoulders against the stone and let it teach me the smear of grease that is more apology than fix. When the access tunnel weeps egg-stink against its heavy rubber I would shave a little fresh black from a new tire come down from up top and plug what smell I can. I would know which tray of soil and shredded cloth wants mushroom cakes that will loosen it properly. I would catch a bead with a tiny tin and suck it and tell you the minute it fell because you asked and because here the year is a string of drops.

I would not be many, and I would never be alone. The long-fermenters would teach my ear that jars sing sharp before they sing round. The salt-bringers would pinch a knife-tip of moth left-behind into my hand and tell me without saying anything where along the harp it goes today if we hope to persuade a bead. The combers would leave moss parted like hair waiting for a marriage. The high-walkers would come down with their mouths the color of not-sun and their eyes big as a worked metal’s shine, and the crosshatches on their thighs would say: you cannot climb the harp without being annotated by it.

We would be more hospitable than prudent. We would meet the rare stranger whose voice arrives like rockfall and offer them a cup from a bladder stamped with a mark that is an arrangement of minutes, and we would tell them when the dew that fills it left the wire. We would give them felt and shame together—here, both are forms of love. If they do not learn quickly, we will not punish; we will lean the room away from them, and the hum will step to a note with nowhere to rest, and we will sing the way out in a voice small enough that it won’t knock a droplet astray.

I would keep a ledger of debts that is all refill and arrival. I will come when your shared cistern has three more sips in it and stand there with an extra bladder as if it were my spine. I will leave a hook and a coil of copper at the corner where your arch leaks its dull rust in the wet season. If you stagger with a newborn, I will turn my face and count for you in a whisper because you do not breathe against the stone’s inhale. If I wake with the gecko’s bell at my ear I will put every tool where the felt finds it without a clang.

Once a season, if this place can be said to have seasons, I would go into the maintenance corridors where the world is naked and audacity has left no moss behind to soften it. Gravel there is newly sharp; crack maps are too young for a fingernail. The air is a deliberate subtraction and my lips would notice. I would not be brave about it. I would be polite to the perfection of that quiet, then come back grateful to machines with manners and plants with jobs.

I would try on belief the way you try on a coat in dim light, with your own silhouette surprised at itself. The basalt remembers being a river of fire and that is why hum belongs here. The river above leans into fractures when melancholy and retreats when light feels cheap. The machine we live under is a ribcage with a thudding patient heart and we are trespassing in the chest of an animal too large to know us. These are stories, not foolishness. They name the weather. They keep the mouth low.

Under those myths I would be common. Wrapped hammer. Oiled toggle. A small tin catching a bead. A leaf angled to an invitation. If I have children, I will ask them at supper what note the east ladder whispered. They will answer in a key older than any we carry from up top, and I will write a smudge of chalk next to other soft-numbered smudges on basalt that sweats like a working man.

If you looked for me on a map you would not find me. There is no latitude here that obeys the sky. But if you put your palm to the rail and then to your sternum and waited for two beats to become one, if you let the dim refuse to defend you and the heat say what it wanted, you would find me between hums doing the only work anyone can do in a city that decided to be an organ and then taught itself to listen.

Who or what would I be? The minute that tunes. The leaf that cups. The bell that rings you into the courtesy of your own neck. The chalk mark that means not yet; again; now. The hand that pays a machine with a rag and calls it even. The droplet that pauses at a spindle and believes it can be two paths at once, then learns—for a heartbeat, three, four—that rain can be a city’s chosen language if you listen hard enough to keep your voice under your skin.
