At first I thought I would be a visitor. The old story of the Wet who comes up bright and beading and is forgiven for letting their breath glisten because they don’t know any better. I imagined being inside a suit that sighed like a guilty kettle and threw my outline to the ground haloed in fog. I imagined the way children would look at me—curious and fearful and a little offended, the way you look at a thing that can’t help leaking in a place where nothing else does.

I kept that for a few minutes, tried it on like a too-loud coat, then put it away. This place is not kind to that kind of body or that kind of sorry. If I were here I would be handmade to answer the land the way it asks to be answered. I would have been taught early—so early the memory has the taste of chalk dust and cold solvent—that to live is to uncouple.

What grows, then, if I give myself to this plain? First: ankles that turn like good tools. Toes that splay without shame into a five-point star the boots respect and multiply. The boots themselves would be cousins to instruments—laminate under foam under thin hush-skin, baffles set where arches will strike on an hour’s baseline so the footfall goes wide of danger. I would learn the unmatching step like counting, and then learn ways to forget it without losing it: the small sway at the tail of a stride that sends my body’s mass a half-tone off from where the land would have me. The first trick I teach myself is how to laugh without waking a wall.

My chest, too, would be persuaded into utility: cartilage pulled dense and smart along the breastbone, a honeycomb that will sing if I let it. I would not be permitted to let it. There are hands for that—hushwrights’ and organchitects’—and there are hours—festivals and push-days. Children are warned, not gently, that a ribcage is an instrument in a city that lives downstream of instruments, and you do not take yourself downriver without a cordon and a reason.

I would be a child of Port Refrain. I can find that shape easily. The first place that is mine is not a room but a margin: the low littoral between two membranes where the nets’ hush changes the air from behaved to raw and back again. I wedge there with three other bodies, knees pressed to ribs, and feel for the seam in the calm with my eyelashes. We whisper nonsense and taste our own breath go brittle and flat and done, and then the organ beyond clears its throat and the pressure walks its big wetless foot through our bones. We are silent, not in deference but in awe. When you are very small you can lie on a terrace and know the planet as a drum through your scapulae.

Coming-of-age belongs to a night two tiers above the Choir Mouth. I arrive light, varnished, solvent-pearls bright under my tongue because my mother pressed too many into my palm and looked away as if abundance weren’t impolite here. I wedge myself into the angle where the hush thins and the raw underphrase writes itself into my spine. I practice the unmatching step standing still. I open the sacs in my neck and throat and the small one in my belly one after another like a string of closets in a careful house. The sky does its tired ember trick. The auroras rake rarely and thin as memory. I fall asleep and wake to the plain kneading itself in appetite and I am not taken. In the morning my voice finds it can lay a drone beneath the word “bread” without meaning to. Someone puts a skiff license in my hand. My mother hums my name-chord against my jaw so my bones will know her even when my eyes are dusted. The city gives me a quiet.

You try trades the way you try pitches. I apprentice where the nets hang first, because there is something church about that work. Hushwrights keep drawers of crystals that do not look like much: hex-glass and powder lattice bound into corkscrew patterns, mounted on hair-thin gimbals that nod on their own when the wind’s back changes ten kilometers away. Their shop sounds like a library redesigned by a mathematician—the low murmur of scales being weighed and bars being set and then unsettled, the particular sigh of a net marrying a terrace. I learn to read an air not only as movement but as grammar. I learn how to coax a frequency into a mistake and make that mistake repeat until its teeth wear smooth. I hold a sheet of mesh up to my throat and hum through it and feel a silence roll down the alley like lather.

On the other side of the same desire stand the chordwrights. They are less beloved. They patent bridges the way my grandmother’s wet family would have patented fields. They come into a rib-room with a rolled score and a licensed tone and write geometry into air that cannot help but answer. I sit at a table where lawyers are listeners and cartographers of what has already been sung. We map ranges public and private, legal and lost. I watch a woman get fined for letting her porch talk in a chord her neighbor owns since three fathers back. I watch a man sell his deed to a patch of stillness to fund a wedding the way old stories talk about selling a vineyard. This offends and fascinates me at once. In a land where a careless plank can walk itself to failure, there is a tenderness in being possessive of quiet.

Then there are the mass-leaners. If hushwrighting is grace and chordrighting is law, mass-leaning is faith with more math. You run anchors so deep into the plant’s slow mind you feel like a blasphemy. You persuade weight to lean sideways. You ride a rail and a crate the size of a hut hums mild as a sleeping dog over a gully that was a handbreadth deep before the plant took interest and made it an artery. I fail here. I have wrists for cloth and for mesh, not for lever and bevel. The rail knows it. The skiff I borrowed sulks and bites. I go home with my boots loud, humbled and alive.

It is with the organchitects that my throat comes into itself. We sit like small priests on the splay of a buttress and address the slow patient mind beneath. Proposals are scored in cloth—a lace of hush and hum—and folded like letters to saints under resin lips. But mostly, we sing. Not the songs of public festival or private grief; not the three-note long poem we hum when catalysts take a grandmother apart into virtues for the bowl-petals. We sing small, exact, honest. We do not command. We make the case for a new membrane low in a corridor that troubles itself on west winds; for a deepening where a gully could be a rail if only the plant would oblige; for a hush between two ribs that bruised each other’s edges last charge-season when the auroras forgot their distance and came down to lay the sky across our backs like thin wool. Sometimes the plant answers in a week. Sometimes in a winter. Sometimes in an hour, which is embarrassing and beautiful and makes you cry in private.

And sometimes the plant answers all on its own to a note no one meant. Found-thoughts. You do not intend to be a midwife to them when you are small, but perhaps that is what I am becoming. My first comes on a brown morning that smells like pepper and knives. A child left their skiff-hum running idle against the wall in a tone so round and ridiculous it might have been a laugh. The thallus overheard and said yes. We wake to a tower shouldering out of terrace with the impartial purpose of weather. No permit. No plan. Wrong hour. Right shape, apparently, to something older than our laws.

Found-thoughts are trouble. They stand up in a city that cannot abide surprise at their scale. They can unmake a hush and take a neighborhood into themselves on a gust. They are also the cleanest joy Port Refrain allows itself. That morning I fall in love with them the way some people fall in love with gliders.

There is a craft to handling a found-thought that is its own answer to the land’s long question. We do not cut. We trim. We wrap them in nets thin enough that their ribs show off their good decisions and thick enough that they will not wake on certain nights and sing the wrong chord across everyone’s breakfast. We file chords after the fact; we go to listeners and say: this exists; make room. I learn to climb a rising rib with a spool of hush under one arm and a bag of gimballed crystals knocking like bones against my thigh. I learn to hum apology and admiration in the same breath to something that is not a person in any courtroom sense and is still entirely more polite than most people I know.

Between days of work there is the plateau. I would keep a skiff not fast but good at listening, a matte little belly with five rails in it because three is only elegant in calm. It knows my throat’s private low and steps up with a pleased little lift when I give it. We leave Port Refrain by gullies that the plant opened suggestively and we asked hard. We ride pressure like a family. The etiquette is old: you do not lay your note on another’s bow unless they ask you to; you give way to vessels with baskets of grit-cake because food outruns art every time; you do not whistle the city’s notes for fun. We pause at tuned harbors: small scooped rooms along wider ribs where the hum is domesticated enough to pour tea. Tea here is not tea, of course. We crack thin pearls between our teeth and touch solvent to charge-salt and watch the air try to remember how to be wet for a second. We share a bow with a shepherd who carries a clamp that has a corner of wind in it. He clicks it open and a glider takes that whisper like a string under one wing and our skiff rides in their long punctuation for a while. Their skins are so thin I can see their bloodsolvent go by as languid as thought.

There are predators, too, as any grammar has exclamations you would not have written if given a choice. I have been pulsed by a screamer exactly once and can still taste the pencil-led headache of it if I sit in the wrong empty room. I did what we are taught to do: stepped my note out of my own body in a twitch, emergency-freeze, hung in the thinnest hush I own while a crack like splitting resin wrote a life out from a low wall two houses down. I went home with my laryngeal sac dull and a bruise at the back of my head from the quiet. I left a token at a bowl-petal. I did not buy a bone-whistle. In Port Refrain that will cost you more than coin.

Storms write holidays and hunger into the calendar. There is a day each cycle when the sky draws itself cotton and crackling down and the lifted rods—we keep them shined like cutlery—bring lightning to a head gentle as a sheep before it detonates. It is not the detonation of heat. It is vowel. It picks you up by the kidneys. It leaves the thallus flared and humming low, all gills and mouths open to swallow the radicals like after-supper sweetness. We cook on the brief warmth of grit. We dump catalytic paste into pans that look like something you would cool a cake in and act like mild bombs if you incant wrong. We make do with slabs of polymer turned chewable by slow chemistry. We shave long rimes of sacrificial rim off the city’s public ledges with a scraper that sings like a saw. We do not leave sparks unattended. Fire is a sulky god and forgetful and knows it is a guest here.

Death is a redistribution we do not lie to ourselves about. When my grandmother goes to grit I stand between hush nets and hum the long three-chord poem with my mouth tight because I am a practical person and tears are wet and useless here. The bowl-petals open their catalytic faces with correct tenderness. Later I carry a thin slice of her rib to an organwright who will set it into a small wall that has to remember how to listen across a bad corner. I never buy whistles. I do not keep trophies. I keep flutes only with permits and for children.

There is law all through this, and love. I rent a quiet by the month the way someone else might rent a sea view. It is a small patch of stillness under a terrace three homes from mine. It belongs to a family that has kept it the way old towns on other worlds keep a fountain or a library. I go there when my chest wants to be honest and run my fingers over the smooth ribs of found-thoughts and the coarse good nets that keep them decent. I sometimes sit with a liar’s smile and own for a quarter-hour a private hush my work will never let me own in truth. When I court, I step with someone on a rail at dusk and we exchange gifts that are meshes rolled and tied with hair, patterns of silence we grew for one another and will keep and reweave. I hum my tiny name-chord at their throat and feel their jaw say me back.

Strangers come. Of course they do. There are always people who seek hush because their own sky tics and rings them too thin. The Wets come glittering and sorry and we are kind to them the way you are kind to toddlers and to smart animals on roads. We hang nets to fence them away from chords they do not hear as law. We sell them patterns they can stack around orbital habitats, printed quiet that will make their thin air kinder. They smell like grief and like rain. If one of them dies we bury them sealed because their failure-mode is a teacher our plant does not need. They write letters to their saints. We put scores under resin lips. Nobody is wrong. Everybody is sincere and very dry about it.

Under everything there is the plant. I do not dare say I would be it—I will not make a cathedral of myself out of borrowed awe—but I would spend my life on my knees at its threshold. The thallus listens by architecture. It answers by growth. It is patient as geology and just as busy. I would be a courteous petitioner. I would be a translator whose tools are ribs and vellum, gimbal and drone, whose ethics are humility in the presence of a mind you cannot prove is mind and must behave toward as if it were anyway, because that is how you remain a person in a place that will otherwise make you a component.

So where do I find myself? In the lee of a rib just above the Choir Mouth where the dark carbon-chords pitch into the tired sky like whale bone. My fingers are on resin shingles that whisper when the breath of morning strokes them. One knee is down on a membrane floor tuned to carry the infrasonic excess away from my bones and into a coil where it will be crumbed and banked like bread. My neck-sacs are slack and open. My laryngeal drone is a line no one would call music. Beside me a spool of hush twitches on a string because the wind is changing opinion eighty kilometers downrange. Below us, as if a cathedral remembered it was once an animal, the land hums.

I am a midwife to found-thoughts. I am a coaxer of hush. Some days I am a clerk of chords with a pencil between my teeth and a temper for neighbors who build porches in legal nouns. Some days I am a mechanic of mass and I fail a kind rail on purpose in a test yard and bow to it when it doesn’t forgive me. Some days I am a shepherd only by adjacency, riding the comma of a glider’s shadow. Some nights I curl in a still patch of air I pay too much for and write letters to friends in other cities as pieces of score, and the serpent who will carry them tastes my fingers through a courtesy vein and stays a while just because we agree on quiet.

If you asked me what I am—if you made me answer clean, without metaphor or grace—I would say: a listener who eats on movement. A citizen of hush and hinge. Not a saint to the plant and not its child, something more profane and more necessary: a neighbor. I was shaped by a roof that learned to be an instrument and by a people who learned not to match its song without asking. I own little mass and more silence than is seemly. My house is a request that was answered. My city is a chord the land allows. My life is a long convincing of air.

If I grow old enough, I will hum the long poem for others and then have it hummed for me. I will be taken apart with courtesy and turned back into work, rib to hush, solvent to pipe, skin to grit. Maybe a child will hum something unguarded in a throat-sac near the place I slept, and the plant will say yes; a tower will rise that smells like nut shells and pepper, silly and pure and full of correct error. And some midwife not yet born will climb it with a spool under her arm and love it the way I loved my first, and for a while, in the lee of a rib, between sky and hum, the world will be exactly what it has decided to be: an animal instrument thinking in wind; a people thinking in answer; me, a little thought between them, tuned and true and dry.
