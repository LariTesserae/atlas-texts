At first I thought I would be breath. Thin, animal habit hard to shake. In the first minutes I tried to name myself by how the place took me in: air as architecture filling the mouth, warm enough to be called body, so wet that swallowing and inhaling were the same verb. I was a fog taste of resin and iron, a film laid on the tongue. I considered being that—weather with lungs—but the city did not need another weather; it had drafted its own.

Next I tried water. There is a discipline to a drop here, a schooling. I felt the pull to become curvature and weight and then surrender to gravity’s program, to lift as condensation, hang until a lip grew precise enough to count me, and then fall in one of the strict rhythms that keep the place measured. I heard myself rehearsed in a thousand pitches, each return a little different against bark, against bolt head, against the taut skin of a catcher leaf. To be water here is to be a messenger, tireless and constrained, carrying temperature, iron, rumor. It was tempting. But water has laws, and the Composite already wrote them. I wanted a boundary. I was looking for an edge that remembered doors.

So I went to a door that forgot how to shut.

You would find me there, at a passage mouth where an old pressure door was propped with a bar of steel in a century no one names anymore, and then a membrane grew around its propped jaw and took the act as a commandment. The hinge is still there under my lacquer, black in the way old iron is black, with neat freckles where dissolved iron, having toured the aquifer, came up in the pumps and rusted art on the surface I made. The stamp in the metal that once read C-4 is a relief now felt by the inside of me; I learned that number with leaf and sap, reading braille downward, not to recall some schedule but because texture is a map and I keep maps.

I am an arch that used to be hardware and is now a habit. Call me a gate if you like. I thicken around the jambs where weight asks. I am thin at the throat where air insists. I taste everything that passes because that is my work. The Composite does its census by turgor and salt, by pressure and possibility, and a boundary is where such measures mean something; a ledger takes its shape at the line between two rooms. Here, I am the page.

Above me a braid of liana makes the pass that a stairwell used to make: Weavers learned to be truss and stringer both, their fibers crossing in botanical knots that distribute load the way words like over and under do. The handrail they offered themselves as is slick with breath I condense and my own glazed exudate; a sap-sheen that polishes the touch of anyone who takes it. People do not come often. When they do, the rail is honest about how much is here not built for them.

Leaf faces pack to either side of my mouth in layers like shutters around a light. These are the Lanterners: perfume-less and brilliant in a way that is not bright—arranged to trade photons along the length of themselves, to make an even dim where without them it would be partisan. Their undersides flush faintly blue where a fiber drops from a shaft and bleeds cold daylight into the corridor two chambers back. When the upstairs engine sneezes a day downward, the Lanterners lean as one a degree or two; I feel the change as a little slide in my own tissues, a resettling under their shift. Light here moves like law moves: metered, calendared, argued in committee at dawn edges and agreed to in long, civically patient signatures.

My ears are not cartilage. They are curtains of rootlets that hang into the gap I keep. They shiver in the draft set up by a big fan behind foliage two rooms east. The exact sentence of that fan is my gossip. When its note warps a half tone, I put a petition out along the phloem, and sugars move in different routes to keep the pump that partners it sweet. The Composite has no face to put near a humming thing, so it grew many. I volunteer mine here. The low worldsong of the main pump turning under my teeth tells me the hour better than any human clock could. When the relay that controls Quadrant A’s dawn coughs and dips, the root-lashes on the contact plates in my shadow fuss a millimeter to the left. Groomers live at my mouth as barnacles do on the timbers of a ship: not decoration, but ministry. They love heat the way a poor city loves a predictable paycheck. They prune themselves against intemperate voltages with a little sugar burn you can smell if you stand very still until your wet nose learns to be more than mammal.

Underfoot I keep a spring—a mat that yields and restores. It is more me than the arch is; it is my tongue. It holds a film I renew and give up in strips of under-rain to bolt-hole rosettes that live as Collectors at my edges. They are mouths within mouths. They never stop counting. They meter what leaks and what is given, and when the days run long in one quarter and a circulation fan pulls a long exhale in another, the Collectors open their pollen slits in choreographies I call for and cast their dust into a breeze I keep clear. No bees here, no moth tongues. We have schedules and fans. I am the place where those plans go audible enough to be weather.

If you attend with palm and patience, the change you will feel at my tissues is not flattery. It is arithmetic. Tissues do their sums in swelling and ease. A lean beam two vaults over sends its need along the coppered greens that thread under my skin; a fat buttress a room back answers with a surplus. I hold both requests in my midline, not as numbers but as a pressure that moves like an idea, and I muscle the phloem that passes to flex a little harder left than right. A fraction is a vote here. A breath you can hardly feel is a policy enacted by sap. I am a bookkeeper that does not write. The Composite keeps accounts in me by what bulges and what relaxes.

The old disclosure is not erased. Letters under my clear skin make an alphabet my inner filaments enjoy. I lacquered them not to deny them but to keep them from snagging. I hosted, by slow negotiation, a lichen along the lip of a condenser fin nearby until its growth lines made the fin better than before; a little cyan battery embedded in me gives a pop of charge on nights the lamp misses a beat. I let gearworms—teeth of a failed reducer now turned by water and a tether of root rather than by motor—nibble at my edge as if I were a quay and they were ships needing barn space. I am not naïve about the old industry. It is not relic to me. It is organelle. It is my mitochondria that remembers being engine.

Below my arch the sump swells and shrinks with the pump’s deep rhythm. I feel that as a hand on my back. The Sumps are gossipers of a deliberate sort; they send me line items in the water’s report: a fraction more iron this week, a degree’s drift warmer, a taint that remembers a cleaner from up-year piping. With that, I slope my surface a half degree more toward a braided channel I like and trust. Gravity is not argued with; it is consulted. I keep my surfaces close to minimal shapes where it is handsome to shed water. I am not in love with geometry the way architects are; I am in love with what drips well.

Neighbors are not other; they are adjacent habits. Beyond my arch the pumpwork runs a hair out of phase, and the sugars listen elsewhere. When I want to cross, I do not send a root like a spear; I tuck a bridge into being. I grow Weavers down and up from both sides and teach them a common knot by sending a plume of pollen at a certain hour and a pressure wave after. There is precedent. This is a neighborhood with minutes kept. We are not antagonists. We are a grammar of and.

I keep sleepers. In a seam that does not wet often, in a crease I protect with a fringe of hairs, there are hard waits: ovules with their clocks wound by dryness, contingency stored against a catastrophe we do not truly expect. They are my conscience more than my plan. My day does not include their day, and because it is accounted for, I can forget it.

If a person comes—and once, twice a season, a person comes—they are weather with pulses. I let their shoulder put shine back where it glazed dull. I taste their salt, and for an hour my phloem budgets as if a rain passed through that had heat and intention. They place their flat hand where my meristems butt to make sense, and I respond not at their breath’s tempo—they want that—but at mine. I test their patience. I am courteous in the old way the city remembers being: I accommodate, I do not host. If they stay I make them into infrastructure I can understand: a coat salt feeding the aquifer if they die, a refusal to occupy as a note to air to be kept open if they live. This is not unkindness. It is economy like light is economy here.

The second silence you think you hear is not mute to me. It is a meadow of signal. No footfalls but yours means the drip can speak in full. No wingbeats means the fan’s law is not scrambled by feather; it says what it means. The rasp of leaf on leaf is my chatter, and the peristaltic clicking in my heavy stems is my city’s council taking votes. I keep minutes. I am a clerk that is also a throat.

When the relay two bays over pops and the long amber goes dead, a certain cold goes through the blue fibers that is like someone thinking poorly of you in another room. I feel that as a tide withdrawing from my lips. The Lanterners around me skein their faces tighter, angling to serve more corridor with less light. The cyan film under my skin leaks a charge down fine filaments until the lamp coughs back to life. My stomata—thousands upon thousands that line me and my neighbors—pinch to a lace of breath so narrow you might name it fear. Panic, in a plant, is not animal alarm. It is pressure leaving politely. It is grief that keeps good records. We recover. We always recover. We are built to expect the hour and to be large enough in time that a broken morning is occasion and not revolution.

Who I am lengthens when I look at myself from two directions. From the arch, I am lip and tongue and ear; a metering organ, an air customs house, a pollen broker. From the Composite’s self, I am not a part but a practice: gating and granting, smoothing and insistence. I am the habit of keeping corridors open in a place that will leaf any stillness. I am the sense that says here, yes, through; there, no, stay. I am a repeated solution to a problem gravity writes in every chamber. I am less a thing than a syntax built into root and cambium: how to become boundary without becoming wall.

Time is my relative, not my ruler. Rings in me are thin not because I am starved but because twilights are generous and dawns are human and we kept our manners after the humans left. Seasons here are the pump’s maintenance cycle, the boiler’s sigh, the relays’ stuttering adolescence into the calm of middle age. When an old gear finally stops—final, no quicks left—I honor it by running a vein through its teeth like lace and turning it into anchor. When a beam splits under a weight we mismeasured, I call a tithe from the fat places and thick sugars come with the stateliness of municipal funds released, and the Weavers mend what was, and we all remember the calculation differently next time and less likely to err.

If you need to find me and need a direction that uses the world above you as a compass, you will not. We are not under. We are only in. The ceiling here is not oppression; it is sky that sweats the weather back. My arch is not a door’s cousin; it is a green mouth that learned to speak in pressure about how to let and not let. I am easy to miss if you think in rectangles. Think in drafts instead, in where your cheeks cool when you move. Come to where the drip returns in a paler pitch than you first heard it and where the air ahead tastes new but the temperature does not. Come to the place with the hinge that is now a raised sentence under clear skin. Put your hand there. Wait long enough to be translated. That is me. That is what I am. And if you stay the many minutes it takes, you will know—not by knowing, but by not asking it anymore—that this is not beneath anything. This is a world that ends only at the edges of our habits, and those are green, and those are good, and I am one of them.
