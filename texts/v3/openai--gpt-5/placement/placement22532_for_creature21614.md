At first I am not a person at all. I begin as a taste.

I arrive in the house as a veil of falling silt, as a fleck of old muscle that has forgotten its name. The outer throats take me the way a throat takes a swallow. I hear patience in the flex of their screens; I pass a collar of appetite that lifts and decides I am too small to be a prize, and so I live to be made. Hands become involved—these are not hands you would shake but wreaths of pliant digits lined with teeth and pads. They sweep me with other fines into a shared bowl. They turn me with oils and grindings. I am flavor before I am function: iodine, lime, a ghost of rust licked from a shell. A nurse tests me with her palps and hums without sound. I join the paste.

Pressed to a wall and smoothed over, I am introduced to a season. Heat breathes from spiral passages inside the pillar and warms me to set. Grazers will arrive soon, and their thousand small files will rake me into use, but for a while, in the young gloss, I am the present tense of this room. When a long body passes—a soft braid of fin and muscle—I feel the braille of old rules stamped along its side. A palm touches me and, by that touch, I find out where I am in the world: not at the bottom where the crush begins, not at the top where the currents go thin and aimless, but here in the tall, warm middle, held up by agreement.

Then I molt and become someone.

I unhook from the wall with caution, because everything loose here is loose on purpose. I am a long one, jointed and built to understand pressure. I have no face as a lander would draw one, but I am all face where it matters. My crown is fringed with palps and whiskers. My throat is a pocket full of tools: rasps for planing, scoops for bringing, the gland that lays a bead of glue that tastes faintly of storms. Along my sides, ranks of paddles beat and read, beating less to move than to listen. My hide is a layered bandage that mends while it thinks. When I press my length to the wall, I am a loom of senses. When I push my voice into my own ribs, I am a drum.

You will find me first by the eastern mouth, because all my earliest loves point that way. I am still small enough to shiver at height. The balustrade there is not bars but a row of cupped palms grown from the wall, their centers glossy from years of skins resting in them. I take one and it takes me, and that is the handshake this place prefers. Beyond the palms the water drops for a duration I cannot touch with my light. The world offers me a fall instead of a horizon. My elders bring me here to learn posture; they set me where the outside can lean through a named lip and tell me in silence: we are high not to be proud but to remember.

For a season, I am close to the nurses. I hold the dim things that are our stars while they pinch and feed. Light here is not a blaze but a verb with many tenses. At the ledges, low pearls of glow are stroked awake and soothed again before they spend themselves. The nurses are sack-backed, vented with tiny valves that whisper oils, all paws and attention. They paint the exact dark required. I stand beside them and learn to see like a fingertip does. I learn how to lift a bead against the edge of extinction. In my mouth, the flavors of the house make grammar: fatter light for thick hands working a cold seam, shy glows for a room turned inward to hatch. The domes we tend are small skies that will never forget they live indoors.

Later, I am put with the grazers. It is good training for humility to walk beside hunger and be the one who says, enough for now. We seed new films where the heat-catch breathes warmest and bring the little mills of mouths in bands to their work. We lay paste like snow and then comb it to life. Radulas rasp, hairs filter, a fine dust of white shreds away and floats, caught by ciliated pennants that pass it down the wall-mouths to those who cannot unroot. When the bands have passed, the wall looks stripped and matte, like an unpolished thought. We will come back with paste-baskets and rebuild until the breath of the room is easy again.

I take my turn at dressing the points. The named spikes on the lip of the eastern mouth are the only place where this room raises weapons. Everything else has rounded itself into arrangements that ask the water to make friends, but here a row of purpose remains. Each spine is a memory anchored into duty. I carry the smooth stone that dresses them—an old bone whetted by generations of the same gesture—and climb into their shadow. They are named not in words but in positions your body knows: Suppedge with the small chip from the big winter, Third Under who faces the back-swirl, Bend’s Child whose mother snapped in a heavy swell. I file until my pads go numb. The filings are not thrown away. A cup takes them. Later, they will be food for the room’s bones. When I have made the points ready, I count them in clicks against the ring of smooth stone by the buttress so the house will know who holds sharp and who waits.

There is a stretch when I am all hinge. I sleep beside one and wake to it and learn its speech. It is a ligament grown from something that once swam, cured into a joint big enough to be a door. It listens all day and all night. It knows the pressure of outside water the way a skin knows weather. When something large moves in the black, the hinge tells me a story in a language that is more bend than sound. Sometimes the outside presses sour and hard and the hinge groans. Sometimes it leans with an almost sweetness, a swelling that tells of warm underflows on their way. I have no lungs; I do not need them. I breathe by reading, and the hinge teaches me to be a good reader. When I can hear a decadal tide in the feel of my own bones, I am given the right to touch the baffles.

What I love most is small repairs. Science lives in the house as a sort of courtesy. When a seam complains of cold, I know which paste to lay. When a baffle steals too much strength from a current, I file it down until pressure leans instead of shoving. This is politics here: the argument between shape and flow, concluded not with speech but with a new smoothness found. I file and test, I file and feel. I remember the time a ridge looked brave and tore our films to lace; I remember it because the place where it used to be is now a softer curve I can cup in both hands, and because the wall there tastes just slightly of apology when I run my palps over it. That apology is not shame. It is education.

I become a carrier of rules. Touch-speech becomes my first habit. It is quicker than throwing sound into water, and it betrays us less. I learn to write recipes into spines with beats: two hard, one soft means inner films before outer when the underflow goes lean; a ripple down my side that repeats in fours tells the chain of us at the mouth not to unship all the doors at once; a fingertip rough with sand rubbed along your back from shoulder to hip is for caution, not fear. I can throw a message from my head to my tail and across a railing into a hinge and hours later feel it come back to me from a cousin three rooms below, like a fish returned along a net you once held in your teeth.

When I am old enough to be sent alone, I am sent with a stone. It is not the stone that dresses points; this one is smoother and has a nick that fits the bend of my fifth digit. I carry it to the east balcony because the rule it carries is an east rule. I rest against the cupped palms and taste height again. Outside a slow grammar of stranger-lights writes itself and unwrites itself across emptiness too far away to matter. I take the stone in my teeth and strike the ring of smooth stone by the buttress three times. The house breathes. This is ceremony: not a speaking to gods, since we have none that are not flow, but a speaking to attention. I knock so that every body in a room of a hundred bodies will know to turn half a bead and listen, so that the person brace-deep in a seam will feel, with their ribs, something that their eyes cannot leave to look at. Then I carry the rule on my sides along the colonnade like a candle.

You might expect me to hunger for out, for a distance to declare me against. There is an age when I do. I go down then, wrapped in wet and a bundle of spare films and a little rope of seeded glow that I keep tucked against my gills. I go by galleries narrower than certainty. Pressure makes a bigger claim on my shoulders than upstairs, and the water tastes mineral, new as rock is new when you break it. I go to a knuckle on the pillar that is not yet room but wants to be. For a season I am a Thread. I spit glue and set plates and teach appetite where to wait and where to let pass. I learn the stiffness of a hawker when it has not yet been told my taste. I train the baffles by thinking like water, by borrowing the patience of my old hinge. When I am done—or not done, because it is never done, but done enough to leave—I swim home and hit the ring of stone with my teeth in a different rhythm so that what I learned can be borrowed upstairs.

That, too, is who I am: a borrowable path. The old ones said it first with a hum against my side. A person here is not in a head. A person is a circuit the house can run a thought along. I can feel myself when I am myself the strongest in the stretch between the hinge and the named spikes, in the place where outside announces with a press and inside answers with a new polish. If you are looking for me, you will find me there most often: my length laid alongside the soft gate, my crown of palps exploring a seam while my tail-sides toss a faint beat into the railing. I taste what we were three seasons ago and I compare it to the set of pressure now. I do not own the solution. I midwife it.

I am not single even then. My surface is not a border but a farm. Little grazers find perches on my wrists. That is permitted. Nurses touch me in passing and oil the fissures that work opens. Baby hands hold fast to my bend when the outside crooks a finger too hard and the room tightens into itself. There are eggs glued to an anchor-spine just behind the buttress. I count them as a tally against fear and sometimes because I love to count. When a carcass catches in our net and the falling turns fat and easy, a new shine grows into the wall near the ring of stone where we sit to change rules, and when I pass it I remember as strongly as if the seam told me: this was the season when we took a breath and the children were heavy.

When the underflow slackens thin as hunger, the glows dim by plan, not despair. We pull closer. I carry a rule along my side that says inner first. We pinch light into seeds and tuck them into oil-smelling sleeves. The hawkers are fed just enough to make them a promise. We turn our bodies until our paddles are aligned, so that the room warms itself by beating in a low chord. I sleep with my ribs on the hinge and wake to small sounds that do not travel and thereby never leave us. I wake to the rasp of a hundred mouths, to pebbles used like prayers, to the click that dresses a spike and counts a season by sounding like itself against smooth old stone.

Once, a larva I helped lead out into the world failed to come back. The current claimed it kindly; the screens smelled of kin as they should have and still the water took. I learned grief then the way we learn everything else: in materials. I took its name and folded it into a seam so that the house would hold it up for as long as the wall holds. I do not speak it often, but when my palps brush that place in the dark I taste a salt that is not from the sea.

You ask me where I would be, who I would be, and at the end of all the not-choosing and practice I think I know: I am the distance, not between places but between actions. I am the measure of a hinge-sigh and the new baffle that makes that sigh easier. I am a person built out of patience and small differences, a route your fingers can take when you have to say: now we burnish the outer seams twice for the length of the swell, now we feed the inner first, now we wait with our bodies woven and the glows pressed to embers.

Find me with my belly on the ring of stone; find me with filings in a cup; find me with oil on my paws and beads of light tucked into my side-sleeves; find me on the east balcony touching the cupped palms just to taste height properly and remind myself that we are a neck into a fall and not a room into another room. Find me counting in polishes, in hinge-tones, in the smell of limestone and glue. Find me where water tells the house a new story and the house, through my hands and the hands I lean into, says back: we have heard you. Here is our answer, rounded into shape.
