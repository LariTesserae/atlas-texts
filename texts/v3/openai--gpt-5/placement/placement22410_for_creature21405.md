Between plates and under arches. In the rooms where clocks keep their manners on purpose. In the cooled hollows that make your forearms remember the feeling of about-to-touch. If you wanted to find me here, you would look for the places where the city lets time be thin enough to fold and thick enough to hold a cup. I am a conduit librarian.

That sounds officious, too square for a job that is mostly hospitality, but it will do. My stacks are cooled corridors of interval; my crates are wrapped in quiet fields. I lend out small drifts the way other people lend spoons. I host the pauses that let a droplet finish composing itself on the lip of a leaf; I skim a sliver from a delivery cart so it can cross three nonadjacent terraces without making anyone older than they meant. I keep a drawer of seconds that are comfortable being bent, and another drawer of ones that prefer not to be touched unless a death is in the room and needs a place to sit.

Mornings, in the hour when the reed-towers yawn and the horizon leaks its patient gold, I go up among the thread-tenders. We walk the ladders into the pylon forests as if we were birds who forgot about wings and remembered ankles instead. The dew-filaments have their own appetites; we do not force them, we tune them. I carry a roll of thin, compliant time—a ribbon of permission—and with two fingertips I let a bead ripen one heartbeat past what it could have managed alone. It answers with the first of its two clicks, shy. I coax the second click to land later, louder, where the ceramic leaf waits, because the city likes its sounds to complete cleanly. If the beads have grown skittish overnight, I roughen a centimeter with a polite fingernail. If two rivulets are fretting at a join, I smooth them with a breath and a fractional loan. The lungmats unfold like rooms being aired. Everything glistens with its own argument for being.

Later, under the plazas, I sit with the gravity lacekeepers and listen to the long animal’s tendons. Heaviness, they say, is a form of kindness; I believe them. When a hall remembers how to carry dense air like a blanket, I am there with a sleeve of extra minutes to help its memory stick. When a vault gives a low thunder of shrug and you feel your weight change for a breath, that was us easing strain and paying back the city in little installments so it won’t default to weather. In the dim I wrap packets of interval for the reed-tower docks, lashing them with near fields until they hum faint as moss. Those go out to ships that breathe older rules; somewhere far and saltward, a grove will ripen at night the way we do.

By midday the light has become almost an etiquette, the buildings pull cool into their ribs, and I go visiting the places where voice and air argue. Window-makers call me when two airs need a chaperone. I stand between inside and outside until both agree to overlap without sulking. In the bad-mood squares, where the height of a voice matters more than the word, I lend a note of time to a sentence so it can stand upright and run a tower’s skin like rain. If you ever laughed here and your punchline did not fall into the moss but arrived exactly at your friend’s eyebrow, I put a thumb on that laugh, not to own it, but to let it finish being itself.

Afternoons are for errands. I walk the dry in-betweens where the city once reached out and felt the air was not in the mood. I move ribs of silt a thumb’s width, an invitation for tomorrow’s trickle to be generous or to be sharp, depending on which root needs the lesson. I press my eyelids closed now and then to read the Braille that people have made in the first five millimeters—home-signs of silk, places where a stride shortens half a thought. I return those glyphs if wind has undressed them too much, a kind of sweeping you do with your lashes.

There is always a call to the perimeters where salt rehearses its permissions. I keep a small brush in my belt—a belt of thin copper plates that keeps my loans straight and lets my hands know where minute and second live today. The crystals there make their gorgeous, rude rime. I do not scold them. I push them back into their cups at evening, smiling with no audience, because this is what young land does when visited by water, and because systems like compliments in the right order.

I carry sugar for the grazers. The dew-sipper pony with the suede skin lets me walk with it if my pulse is considerate. If a child is near, I will lend the child two gentle beats so their belly and the beast’s flank can agree for a minute. Once, a pale glider in training pranged the boundary layer so hard that the air stuttered. We all winced. I gave the boy a strip of interval to lay along his next step, not to make him fly—no one flies—but to let his sentence travel like a sentence. He grew up into fingered membrane and good manners.

Machines come and go in my stacks and leave no footprints, only requests. A beetle drone will blink twice between terraces and look embarrassed, if a machine can be, when its second click gives away the fold it borrowed from me. The field-braiders and I sit back-to-back sometimes and run our hands through what looks like empty space, persuading two electromagnetic tendencies to hold hands until they can bear the hush of a carriage. We trade tools: I give them a little slow to widen the braid they’ve made; they give me a cuff of pale biolight with which to mark a loan that should not be forgotten at dusk.

I eat the city the way the city eats the weather: quietly, by gradients. The black bread that is not bread, sliced from the sponges of our composite trees, tastes like light with a memory. The morning broth from a catchment where plankton practiced being green for an hour is a sermon on ephemerals; I sip it with respect and leave enough film for the fungi to read. Sugars off leaves are heat and etiquette. I take them the way you take a hand extended at a doorway: grateful, no grabbing.

What do I look like? Matte as everything else. Gloss is rude; even my tools wear nap. My sleeves end in small fans that comb the first five millimeters without snagging them. My shoes return their apology into the net as I go. My wrists are scored in clean lines where salt and copper have shaken hands. My pockets are heavy with nothing: coins of interval that weigh more when you stand near, the soft static of a promise kept most of the way.

On rain days I am out like everyone, not for the drench but for the equivalence. My job then is to put my hands in my belt and stop having a job. The city loosens its fur and lets the old sky fall; we applaud into ceramic leaves and old women stand up from chairs that have been thick for them for years and let their shawls of air untie. I have cried under those rains, partly because surprise is expensive to curate and partly because mercy requires witnesses.

At night, when wayfinding is the slow pulse of biolight along the day’s accepted flow, I walk home by other people’s signatures—the patch of silk where your eyelids tell you to slow your step, the place where a voice spoke too low and went to ground and the moss keeps it, the rib I grew this morning that the city has already corrected, kindly. My house is marked with a useless microfeature on purpose: an invisible handrail in the air that I keep forgetting to move because forgetting is a way of proving that time has been here and not somewhere else. Inside, which is only another tuning of outside, a clock that lives with me drifts a little because I ask it to host a stranger’s wander every evening, the last loan of the day. It smiles its minute-hand sideways.

If you looked for me and did not find me, you might already be carrying me around. I am the extra half step you took without knowing why when a plaza gave you more stride than you asked for. I am the little weight under an old person’s chair, thick as dignity. I am the second click you heard on a morning when a bead finally let go and arrived where it should. I am the breath that hung between your palm and the hull of a ship and made the hair on your arm remember a future load.

And if you wanted, once, to be me, you could be. Children are drafted young here by their eyelashes. Apprenticeship is an accumulation of polite reflexes: not stepping where you will make the ground gasp; saying “after you” to water; holding your laugh back two beats so the square won’t drink it; knowing that a system that never says no becomes pudding and a system that never says yes becomes ash. We learn to give the world a coin of interval right where it will spend it well.

So: where I am is in the overlaps—between airs that agree to be neighbors, between a leaf and its drink, under the arches that remember hands that built them and hands that will. What I am is one of the city’s quieter organs, a keeper of the small virtues of interval. You will know you have found me if your forearms feel a soft static and you think, for a second, that you are about to touch something kind. If you feel your step lengthen without hurry, if a sentence you wanted to say ends up running a wall like rain, if a droplet finishes being itself with two clicks and both seem intended, then I have been at work, which is to say: I have been home.
