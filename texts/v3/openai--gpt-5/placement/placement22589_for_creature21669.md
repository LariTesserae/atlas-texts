I am where the mountain keeps its voice in its bones.

If you want a ledge to find me on, it is Seventh Lip West, the one that drinks from the drip that never misses the count of three. If you want a work to call me by, I am a plate-listener, gutter-mender, tether aunt, one of those who attends to the edges where new rock pretends it will be wall forever. If you want the name my mother etched thin into the back of my best blade, you can read it if I let you: Evening Answer, for the plate I kept that would speak only at dusk.

There are professions that gather crowds; mine takes one person and a hand and the patience to do nothing loudly. We call it listening, but mostly it is touching. In the hour before the fog rises to close the noon out of consideration, I go to the fin that shoulders our ledge and I give it my palm. If the hairline tick that lives there since my grandfather’s day is quicker than it was at dawn, I mark the tether knots to the inside with a finger so that when I lean my weight later it will tighten inward, not out. If the plate below our sleeping room rings when I breathe across it, I pluck the old three-message cord and send ping, ping, slide to the ones who know how to sit with string and law and make a decision as slow as a blade cutting stone.

Plate-listeners are made out of falls we did not take. The first time my foot left me, I was small enough to fit into the curve of my aunt’s wrap; the tether of all children went tight and gave, and the gasp that climbed our ledge and came back down was a teaching, enough to salt the lesson without a word. I learned the cords before numbers: two taps and a slide, I am here, I wake. A stop like a held breath means do not step where I am about to step. A generous give and return means I have water to share between this hour and that one.

We dress for the weather of patience. My hair carries a line of little polished spoons—slivers shaped by my grandmother—that feed a thumb-gourd at my collar. My wraps condense and let go by design, leaf-lattice outside, moss-soft inside. My belt hangs quiet with what I need: my mother’s bone awl with its inherited polish, a strip of glassy stone that wants to skate if I let it, the meteor ring my aunt brought down a lifetime ago and gave me when I swore to tend the plates as if I could teach stone not to change its mind.

I take my day in distances measured by hands held out. I move like a person who knows the air will bruise if I ask too much of it. When I am happy I do not clap. I hold both your hands around a cord, and I send delight through the taut to the skin of your fingers and let you feel it return.

There is a drip that is ours, an arc of bead to gourd that spells my family’s dowry in small regular increments. It knows our name better than our neighbors do. Its hours are more reliable than any sun. When the cloud is generous, we do not take it all; we pick up the cords that join our ledge to our cousins’ and we knot our patience into theirs, so the shadow falling here at second breath after dawn feeds someone else at third breath the next day. When the fog is mean, we lay two lengths of gut side by side from fin to roof so the two small languages they carry can speak without confusion—the old knot I learned beyond the notch for braiding two meanings through one tension without snarling them in each other.

I learned that knot by leaving and returning. Once a year, when the rock is holding itself like a proud jaw and not a loose one, I go out. I bring the frame I made after watching birds drink light—lattice of stems, leaflets that build their own dew—and I sleep under it, listening to nothing. It is too loud. The horizon is an unkind joke told by distance. I come back with a pocket of salt and a pin of true iron that the council puts away like a blessing, and also with a new way to let the cords say maybe without saying no.

There are days the mountain holds still like a thought that has decided itself. Those are almost simple. There are weather tides in here and a person grows a wrist for them—a way of feeling the pressure of sound and mist at once. When the first light drums low and the droplets fatten on the inside of the leaves, I lift the nets that I strung in the hovers last night and collect a weight of air taught down into flesh. Driftlings go quiet into my pockets, their crescent mouths holding nothing but promise. On thin color days—the rare noon when the ceiling opens a shutter and the rosettes glare green like coins shown to a miser—I bring home a frond that dared sunlight and we eat its courage leaf by leaf until it is gone.

I keep the old routes the spiders have exacted from us with their architectures. That avenue of silk between cistern and roof gusset is older than my mother’s awl. I pass it like I would step around a child set down to sleep. Morning is our white season; the cleft wears its soft. I know which sheets to leave up an hour so the makers can drink enough fog to make their day, and which to powder into a pinch that will thicken broth and make my skin remember drizzle like sugar.

When White Fall comes—the biggest spider’s season—the whole town holds its lungs. The sac she carries fails, as it has on a cycle none of us know how to count; it breaks and the cleft snows children. The law that says we do not trap then is cut into rock, slow by slow, in a hand like mine. We put out basins anyway, not to hold but to gather. We sweep silk against silk into spools the netters will comb later for the molted threads they know how to join. And then we leave food on the rim where her big legs will find it, because hunger is not the same as danger and a mother who has lost everything is owed.

The year after my mother died, the Lift came hard and long, as if the air had decided to erase all dimness in apology. We untied the lamps and turned their insides toward themselves. The hovers took the low currents up, a river of lit mouths moving through us like a sky come down to sing. People crowd in here, when we say crowd; it is four of us and a breath between. That night I stood with my back flat to the fin and lifted my hands. Light pooled in my palms like something I could drink. Everyone looked beautiful and true in it. Some rubbed that phosphor on their faces—cheekbones marked like law—and glowed inward for a week. I kept mine on my wrists. When I lay awake that night I could watch my own blood move.

Not all our stories are full of feasts. Once a stranger came up with a lamp that would not be hushed by a damp cloth and that treated the air like something that did not need to be allowed. We felt it in our teeth first, a high wrong hum like beestings in old scars. The driftlings knotted high and tight around the lad where he stood, which is a sign we all know as well as we know the feel of our own knots. I plucked the nearest line, not for words but for the permission that is held in a certain double silence. Lamps out, heat in. We lay flat as lichen. The hunter that came through the high slot the old ones call the Letter made nothing but pressure of absence as it passed—the feeling of a bank of creatures being distantly, carefully absent. It read the big heat letter and skimmed it and left. When it had gone, the stranger cried, but softly. Later he learned to wear stones in his hair and to share a cord with our anger until he understood why we had it and how it would go away again.

I do not marry, or if I did, it was to the ribs the rock has made of itself. That is a kind of pledge that feels immodest to say, so I keep it in the knot-work wound at the inside of my wrap where only a hand held against my heart would read it. I tend the basalt plates. I count the breaths the cleft takes when it draws damp into itself by day and lets it go by night. I hold a child’s palm to the rock and ask her what this inhale is and what exhale, and what is the sound a stone makes when it is no longer certain it wants to be what it is.

There is governance, and it is as spare and tight as the ladders we keep strung but never quite taut. When the three-message call comes—ping, ping, slide—we go to the emptiness the size of a breath that we use for deciding and we lay strings on the wet stone like river maps. The ones who carry law do not carve it carelessly. If it isn’t worth the cut, we do not ask the blade to whisper through the basalt. There are words there that are as old as my aunt’s meteor ring. Do not set a bright fire. Do not trap in White Fall. Do not cut a cord that is bearing a message, even if it bears it to your rival. The rest of it is courtesy.

We keep our dead the way we keep everything: curated, quiet, turned toward usefulness when they offer it. My mother went into the cool bells where the richest moss lives. Some of her unknotted into feed for the green, and some came back to me as an awl that fits my hand so cleanly I feel like I am finishing her sentence when I work with it. Her name is not on a stone. It is on the line that runs above the path we walk to water. It is the knot my fingers touch each morning without reading because we have spoken enough for knowing to be a kind of silence all its own.

Jokes travel by gravity. When someone loves you in that way that allows for tilting worlds, they will tip the little gutter above your door exactly one drop off, so that your morning wash runs into the collar of your wrap. You answer by rerouting the micro-rill above their hammock to ring-drum in a rhythm wrong by a heartbeat. You do not break the spider’s avenue to make a point. Where I am from, we are proud of what we do not interrupt.

Children are strings before they are sentences. At three years they get their own line from sleeping place to message vein. My youngest niece’s first code—two taps and a slide—woke me like a grateful rain the first time she sent it alone. At five she learned the stop of a held breath and saved a basket-carrier from stepping into a place the moss was pretending to be floor there. At seven she fell for the first time and the tether held and she went into the ritual chamber where the rock holds every sound to a thickness you can touch. She does not shout now. None of us does, not because the rule is a blade in a wall but because we have learned how small we can be and not be nothing.

I have stood belly to rail and watched a plate let go, have felt the taught cords slide along my back and leave perfect lines where they kissed me fast. A falling slab is a mouth you do not want open over your heart when it decides it is a mouth. We lash everything slightly inwards. We are not foolish enough to build a platform that makes a dare out of gravity. In rockfall season we read the chill that towns elsewhere would call a weather and we call a warning. When the ceiling turns to thought and we can see a shard of elsewhere, we polish our few meteor rings and let them sing against our skin like small, forgiving bells.

Sometimes there is a person who comes to the ledge where I sit at last light and says her name like a promise and asks if I will teach her to hear what the mountain is saying. I take her hand and set it to the fin. I tell her that the first thing to learn is that stillness is an action here. That a drop is a unit of wealth and a kind of punctuation. That the cords do not only speak—sometimes, if you hold them right, they listen. I show her the grammar of pull and give, the lexicon of weights, the slack that means sickness, the agree that means we can go on.

If you asked me where you would find me, I would say: on Seventh Lip West, one palm on basalt, the other on a cord. My feet will be remembering not to die while my hands do the work. My hair will be wet and beaded like a field of tiny cisterns. When the airlings bank and turn in my lantern, I will set the wick lower, out of respect. If the sky shaves a blue blade through the ceiling, I will lean back and let the color cut me cleanly, and then go back to keeping the plates from teaching us the wrong lesson about newness.

If I were to tell you who I am without saying my name, I would say I am the notch’s ear, the tendon in the fingers of a town that knows the value of a single, steady drop. I am a person who counts breaths that do not belong to me and treats them as entrusted weight. I am a joke delivered one beat off in a gutter, a law that was worth the cut, an aunt who can lay your hand to stone and make you feel the difference between the mountain inhaling and something enormous making up its mind.

Stay a while and the rules you brought will scale down without complaint. Your eyelids will learn to cradle pearls. Your hands will learn the sentences that cords can say. Your hearing will move into your bones and your tongue will learn to keep company with your throat instead of the air. When you leave, if you do, you’ll go out like someone stepping from a lung, stunned by the size of sound. You will carry with you our measure of plenty and debt: breath, drip, weight, slack, a little light held cupped, a little darkness saved for sleeping. If you come back, I will be where I was, which is to say here; I will put your fingers to the fin and watch you nod, and we will not say the word for the spider whose road you remembered to step around, because we do not have to. You will feel it in the cord. You will feel it in the rock. You will feel it in the way the moths keep flying.
