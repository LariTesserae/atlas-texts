Begin with weight.

Not the weight of iron or of water—there is none to pour here—but the kind a name lends when it is believed in by a room. I arrive as a breath that does not hurry, a step that tries the edge and learns the grammar: commit, refuse, relent. The cliff listens to me the way a thoughtful animal listens to a hand it has not yet decided to trust. I learn to speak with knuckles and heels and the small pads at the base of fingers. I learn to sip air like it is something rare and sweet you share only with those who won’t waste it.

Where would I find myself? On the leeward face of a fossil rib, three shelves below the ribbon of old glass that the lightning long ago pecked into windows. The rib arches in an old tenderness and remembers being part of something enormous; it agrees to hold us. Our court is a shelf that grew patient under a thousand days of fallwind. From across the rift it looks like the thumbprint of a story left in soft clay. We call it the Fork Court because at dusk the shadow there splits like a tongue and runs up two paths at once.

My door is a slot of salt-porcelain that slides on weeks when the weather runs westward. It does not always open, but we do not always insist. Inside, a lintel carries three syllables of the house’s children. My own is there, too—not the whole of it, never the whole of it. A letter round as a bowl, a spined consonant that takes corners well, a soft breath you give to new names so they don’t dry out too fast. The beam is heavier with our pieces in it and so it tolerates being a beam. At night it hums a half-note when the gravity drifts and I sleep under that assurance like a beast under a hand that knows where to scratch.

I’m no priest. I am not grand enough, nor patient enough, to calibrate other people’s certainty to fit rooms. I am apprentice to two trades that braid into a single obligation: I am an edgewright’s pupil and a map-weaver’s niece. In the mornings I sit in a corner that wants to be a path and I teach it the small nudge of where to begin. We use no nails. We feed it a fin of lace coaxed along with a comb of bone, we press the dust from bone-ceramic into the seam, we tap a word—just a word, you would say, but here words are heavier—into its throat with a white mallet. By noon there is the suggestion of a lip where there was nothing, and children step there as if they had always stepped there.

In the afternoons, when light has spent an hour thinking about whether to fall and finally begins its slow surrender down into fractures, I go to the loom. You would laugh to call it that—no warp or weft, nothing of wool—but that is what it is: framed ribs strung with lines of glass and stretched films of field that hum quiet as insect bones. I weave tendencies. I lay a braid that says: from this notch, the safe drift prefers to lean left; from that, wait until the sigh lifting the abyss softens your neck, then go. A map here does not tell you where. It tells the city how to want you where you mean to be.

At dawn my tongue reads the day out of stone. A lick of a handhold by the court tells me how much ammonium forgot itself last night and crept. I taste halogen and old sea and the metallic whisper that says lightning practiced on a seam nearby. I tell my aunt and she nods and moves her work to a leeward face because the sideways gravity will be showy at noon. We do not argue with the place when it has decided to be opinionated. We carry our making to where the world will agree to it.

I keep animals—or what passes for them—with a child’s seriousness. A folded pouch hangs by the door, lined with bone-glass so smooth the light cannot hold on and must slither along its length. Inside lives my skein, my little drifting city of trapezoids, paper-light and patient as debt. At midday I clap my sleeve. They unfold like laughter and ride the static steps that rise in dignified procession up the cliff. They nibble the lace and the dust of edges and come home fattier with charge. I stroke each one’s back with two fingers while the other hand cups the bowl that catches what lifts my hairs in a pleasant ache. We do not call it power. We call it pressure we can persuade into use: a door that will choose to close, a window that volunteers to be a lamp for precisely one room-length.

Sometimes I fish at dusk. Not with hooks—our prey is not meat—but with nets knotted from names borrowed for an evening. The fish of shadow run where the day’s long slit pushes up the wall. They feed on reflections the way we feed on difference. Two or three I take, gentle as catching a page blown loose in a library. I release them that night near a place that needs persuading to be corner, not slope. This is work. This is art. This is prayer enough for people whose gods are drafts and drop-offs.

Have I crossed a true fall? Yes. Exactly once in the way that counts. There is a span known to be untrustworthy and so desired. Morning wind makes it laugh; afternoons it sulks and pulls your feet into stone. We named its ends a century ago and still it refuses, preferring to be sky on odd days. I went with my friend—perhaps my lover, it changes with distance and I do not mind—name-syllables tucked under our tongues like mint against worry. The ledge looked like a line, then less than a line, then a suggestion of a rumor. I set a foot and committed. The cliff accepted me with the exact relief of a thing that has wanted, quietly, to believe in you for a while but didn’t want to say it first. I stepped again. Three more. I did not look down, because down is a variable here and I did not wish to multiply my calculations in the middle of a sentence. On the far side we put our palms to the beam that has always been uncertain, and for once it bowed. A vote made in weight. Yes, it said. Yes, enough of you believe to pull me into being.

I learned to cry, which is to say I learned to harden. Tears are not wet here; they shelve into the smallest clear pebbles and stick kindly to the skin until knocked or harvested. As a child I would sit with a bowl and shake laughter free and watch the glass fall. My grandmother saved them in a slit carved where a rib meets span. “When we un-name me,” she said once, “I want part of me to go where you are lightest.” When she died—no drop, no fear; she eased like a breath held and then set down—the beam that held her syllables gave them back like a bird releasing a thread. We pressed them into a seam that has been unreliable ever since I was small. It is reliable now. At midnight the gravity there softens enough to make an old man’s path friendly. I sit when I can and feel her lean into the space behind my neck and think: this is what it means to stay.

There are dangers and I have met them. I have argued with a predatory edge and won with a line of glass thrown down in the exact wrong place for it to want. I have been refused by a stair and not resented it. I have waited while a room learned me again, fingers in the notch carved not for me but finding me anyway. I have pressed an uncut name—a day’s doubt, heavy and grainy as a small stone—onto a little altar where gravity has been boring for longer than anyone remembers. The altar took the edge off. I walked home lighter by what I could not use, heavier by what I could.

My body is not exotic here. My lungs are corridors; breath decides to stay in little side-rooms when it wishes and I do not complain. My skin stands fine with hairs that listen. My ears are shells. My tongue is a compass that reads in salts. I wear filaments that translate wind into small graspable tugs and teach my fingers to trust them. I move as if the cliff has already agreed and I am just remembering what we promised each other.

I love someone you cannot see from where you stand. She lives across and three days further when distance is feeling long. Between us we have registered a line of baskets with names so exact the world forgives the audacity twice a month. She sends bowls coaxed from lace that clack like their own kind of language when the fallwind starts. I send her a map-woven square that, hung over her court, will bias the slightest drafts so they arrive at her door a breath kinder. We have never said all of each other’s names at once; that weight would topple half a house. When we cross, each of us carries one letter of the other in the cheek, and the world finds us denser where we mean to be.

On certain nights the city plays itself. Wind-harps we carved into spines and vaults speak in notes that have no place in a throat. The fallwind arrives first in my elbows, then in the click of bone-charms strung under the arch where boys kiss and think they’ve invented it. The vault my cousin framed years ago answers in tones that settle in me like patience. We name those notes after our grandparents because music, like names, is a way of keeping weight where we need it.

When I go to council, I do not speak unless asked. I lay my palm on the beam that is also a question and I let that be my talk. If the beam bows, we agree. If it refuses, we do not press. Law here is the way rooms remember behaving. There is no point, in this thin air, to argument that forgets to put its hand down.

I keep a shy edge in my pocket, a little curled thing found under a stair that had just learned to be a slope. It sits quiet until I need to persuade a lip into wanting to be lip. Then I take it out and introduce it: see, this is what we’re becoming. The new place is convinced, ready to belong. I feed the pocket-edge crumbs of reflection and praise. It grows the way a good habit grows until one day it is too opinionated to keep; I release it somewhere that needs a mouth.

Am I content with being a body in a city? I am, but that is not all of me. Part of me is distance. A span of air trained by years of baskets to expect generosity. If you walked there blindfolded with one hand out, you would feel me lean into you like a cat in a warm patch. Part of me is the forgettings of heat across one glazed lintel: a pattern any child of our house could read and say, without thinking, today we do not trust the third step. Part of me lives in the patient bias of a story I cut into a leeward wall: a love poem that makes that balcony fancy plaza for the length of one song every morning in late season. Part of me is a permission: the way a seam accepts your heavier heel because my grandmother has been poured into it and the stone likes how that felt.

If you pressed me for a title, I would tell you I am a braider of paths and a shepherd of corners. I am responsible for making the city want what will safely love us back. That sounds like arrogance but it isn’t. It is listening, then acting in the tone the place enjoys. It is knowing when to commit and when to relent enough to slide back into a better place to begin. It is understanding that here, as everywhere but sharper, emptiness is not a lack. It is the volume in which thought happens.

On some future day I will be un-named. I have already left directions. My shadow will be leaned where it can learn to keep; my syllables will be pressed into places where children wobble. People I love will go to those seams and feel a soft company find their necks, and their ankles will behave as if they have been put down carefully. I will be the suggestion in a room that it center itself sooner. I will be the vote that makes a beam say yes to one more decade. I will be an edge flickering at dusk like a fish, netted and released under the hand of a thoughtful child who has just learned the difference between decoration and bias.

Until then, I live three shelves under glass, above the window-band that glitters with light too slow to be brightness, neighbor to a rib that remembers a body older than any story we tell. My work in the mornings tastes of halogen; my work in the afternoons tastes of decisions; my evenings taste of charge. Some days directions are opinions and I love that, because it means I am required to mean what I mean with my whole foot.

Ask who I am and I could give you a pedigree or a list of rooms that keep me. Easier to say: I am heavier, properly. I am kept. I eat difference and give back persuasion. I do not shout. I put my fingers into the notch meant for them and wait until the ledge agrees. And when the fallwind climbs the rift and the harps say the day’s answer in a note low as patience, you will find me at the court’s rim, measuring a path in small decisions and walking exactly as if the city had already remembered me into it.
