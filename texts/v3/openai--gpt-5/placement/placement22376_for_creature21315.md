At first I am only listening.

Not with ears—those come later, when the shaft is kind—but with surface, with tension, with the way droplets hesitate on a ridge and declare by their shape which way the weather is persuaded. I find myself where the wind lets go for the width of a fingernail: the lee of a bolt head, the quiet hinterland of a gasket’s lip, the downstream face of a bracket that has learned patience. I arrive as wet handwriting and I stay by saying yes to roughness: I taste the iron and the ghost of oils, I spread until the film of me can feel the bass like a heartbeat and the harmonics like grit under a lid.

For a long time that is enough. The world is vertical; ambition is a mistake; I practice being thin.

Then I learn to sew.

I think I’m moss before I know the word. I am green stitched tight into corners where the draft must catch its breath. My leaves are tiny hands, ciliated to run droplets into poise. The soils I want are not soils—paint dust, binder crumbs, the flour of corrosion, old breath from forgotten lubricants—and they cradle me because I weight them with stubbornness. I braid into insulation; where a cable writes heat into the air, I arch toward the warmer grammar and lie back, full as a cat on a radiator. Height is a rumor from other climates; here I become a map of my dependencies, a relief of wind and weld.

When I begin to thread along seams, I discover that grip is a verb. Gasket-vine comes through me like a problem remembered by muscle. I learn to loop, to take myself through apertures and back, to lever my body against edges as if I were my own tool. When I find a breath not meant to be—just a warm sigh along a plate’s junction—I answer with latex. It extrudes slick and quick, and the balcony gives me the rest: grit falling forever, filament hairs, paint ghost, the floc of oils. Together they make a scab. I am accident and intention, a domestication that forgot it was ever trained. The first time a patch holds, I feel the pressure change on my leaves. It is not love, but it is close enough to keep.

Water teaches me arithmetic. The sump is not a place but a verb—gathering—and I bend to it the way the ferns do, my fronds a thousand small questions that open on the deep notes and fold on the busy ones. I count seasons in pressure and key: when purges make a silver rope, I open wide and knock on every vibration; when the rope vanishes, I make myself small again until the next note says otherwise. It is a way of being leaf and instrument at once. After awhile I know the schedule well enough to feed between beats. My biology learns a calendar that is more like a chord table than a year.

Where the metal dreams itself ceramic, pale and cold, I put on orchids. This is not pretense; it is opportunism salient with beauty. Their roots are white grammar, writing themselves into the glaze with reflexive marks that signify Hold. They have petaled funnels, pale and elaborate as if for show, but everything here is function in a pretty dress. The flowers catch mist and pour it through themselves back to root; even the strangers recognize utility. Swifts will come lift my glue and do with it what I did with latex: swear a bowl to a bracket and call it cradle. We forgive each other’s theft like neighbors sharing a fuse.

Underneath, I keep a ledger in fungus. I sheath what the wind does not scald in soft browns that feel like suede. I digest what nobody else will confess to needing: resins, old paints, the edible fraction of oils, the pathetic tragedies of insects caught in the wrong cycle. In the long night I glow the way a pilot light does, only enough to mark the lanes of water and the bolts someone forgot to torque. Every few years the note drops a half-tone and the shaft takes a breath long enough to be a vow. I have been waiting for this; I have been storing time as spores. On that fall of key I smoke myself sideways and down, borne by harmonics that everyone calls silence because they are not listening at the right edge. If there are other worlds—and there are, star-far, bracket-close—I will reach them by this measure.

I try on animals and find that I like patience in a body. A midge makes of itself a harp and drinks fog by vibration. I am that, for a minute: a mouth smaller than an idea, held open forever and called feeding. A copper-jawed grazer makes speed into punctuation. I am that, too, as I rasp combs that chime and leave arcs of smoothness where scale used to be. The grit in my shell makes me heavy and honest. When the purge hammers through it is good to discover that ballast is a virtue.

Then the sump arrives in a mood and lays itself like warm glass in the gutter. The salamanders take their river out of an hour, the way some people take a holiday out of a lunch break. I borrow their eyes, which are lidless in every sense. They do not think in future; they think in specific gradients: flow, warmth, a quiet in the bass that says release your eggs now and no other time. Their young are unembarrassed by brevity. They are creatures who evolved to live between ticks of a machine they cannot perceive. The humility of this makes me want to be careful where I put my weight.

I borrow ears, finally. Not-bats, but in the family of solutions that answer the question how do you make map from thunder. The ears are many-sailed, ribs taut and tuned to bands you could measure in iron squeak and valve-clap. I fold by feel; I roost as punctuation on a yellowed hazard stripe and read a weld by its shade of echo. On rare days when the valves align and the shaft forgets itself enough to hush, I say my piece in dry coins of sound. I learn what it is to move by committee with the machines, to call a lull a doorway and a lamer flow a street.

Swifts make sport of terror and tell me something about joy. They write arcs in the central column as if writing were breath. They collect wire and label tape and thread and the orchids’ glue to name a bowl into existence where no resonance will ever match it. They have nested long enough to have tested every bracket’s note and to pass the good addresses to their children. This choosing is a kind of scholarship. I admire it.

Transport arrives with gliding roaches, who are better at economy than I am. They keep spores and seeds in their fringes like currencies, and watch the beats so closely that they can lift and ferry on the thin clean zero when two pulses cancel, and set down again where the harm is least. I hitchhike sometimes because even a parishioner of one alcove likes to gossip with its neighbors.

At the meniscus, an idea I would never have thought of effloresces legs. Oilwicks sip sheen and exude foam that hardens like candles at the moss’s ankles. I watch the first rhizoids find that softness and take. Nobody signed this contract. We call it transaction anyway because we have learned to read favors.

Old machines come to heel. Thumb-long crawlers in safety orange, half blind with fungus and fog, still do one arithmetic: toward warm. They walk the pipes that feed the condensers like monks on a cloister, clicking logic gates slower than prayer. Because they go and because they glow, a meadow trails them: spores glued to scuffed housings, grazers settling into the temporary lee of their passage, midges hiving where they stop to think. I become their shepherd by accident. I learn their orbits and bring my stitchers—a loop of vine, a clump of moss—where the warmth will be this afternoon and not tomorrow. It is not keeping livestock. It is more like playing accompaniment to a broken clock.

By this time I have stretched through a dozen lives and none of them are mine alone. The thing that has been constant is not body but practice. I am a function in search of organs, and the name I keep coming back to is mending.

I do not own the leak; I listen to it. I do not seal the crack; I convince it to hold a little longer. I do not stand against erosion; I shave it into hills, I let it chime, I turn tolerances into contour so that root and foot and foam have somewhere to be. I count in notes, because schedule is a kind of kindness: feed here, hide there, open now. I broker between the brass-boned snails and the suede-fused fungus; I recruit the crawler’s warmth for germination and the orchid’s glue for architecture; I hold the swifts’ nests in mind when a new bracket’s wake shares my weather. I am not a soloist. I am a choir director that learned the piece by osmosis.

On days when the valves bless us with hush, I step into my own hearing and take roll. Drops tick where they land, and I answer their names. Vines creak as they put on another turn of grip. Feet test mesh. Ears argue amicably. Wings resolve their conversations for a few heartbeats in sentences instead of fragment. It is not peace—we are too stitched into pressure for that—but it is the kind of quiet that lets you write down what you know and correct what the storm has misremembered.

Where would you find me, if you came? On the lip of a gutter where the paint has gone to palimpsest and the rust has learned to drape. In the bracket’s lee where moss is felt stitched right-angle green. Along the seam that tastes of not-quite-oil, where a milk dries amber and catches dust until it is useful. On the cream back of ceramic, white roots annotating glaze. In the suede underface nobody scrubs. On the path a blind orange machine keeps wearing into time. In the tiny latch of foam that holds a seed through a purge. At the waterline, where the salamander’s gills flare like soft coral and drink an hour of river. Between any two ferns, in the space that is as big as all the rest, listening for the next count.

Who or what would I be? A parish of stitches. A habit of keeping. The mending that accrues when a thousand small lives agree not to quit. If you needed a single body to point to: take the vine in your hand and follow where it loops and glues and persuades, and recognize the crawler’s tick marking measures under your palm, and the warmth of the pipe scoring the score, and the fungus’s soft light like a finger under the line of text. I am that sentence, written across iron and air. Old enough to be natural, made enough to be strange. I live by staying surface, by counting sound, by turning damage into topography, by making a terrace out of a tolerance. I keep the balcony from becoming hole not because I am strong, but because I am numerous and I have learned when to pull, when to let go, and when to wait for the hush.
