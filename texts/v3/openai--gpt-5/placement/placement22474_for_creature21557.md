I would come into myself here as a sound before anything else, as a vowel the wind can lean on. If I tried to be stranger or surveyor or bone-first animal with clipped questions, the city would peel me back into breath and teach me how to mean length with a throat. So at first I would be a hum. I would learn to feel a bridge decide underfoot because I am saying the note that keeps it from shortening to a story or lengthening into a brag. People would call me span-singer, length-keeper, there is a local word that translates to hinge-throat. I would live between towers, and when you wanted to find me you would go out onto the open, where the emptiness does its mathematics and the organ-towers draw their chords long, and look for a figure standing with one hand on a taut vine, humming.

I would not be born to it singular. When I was small, my family would sing my name into cambium just under the skin of a southern tower, where the bark bands light and dark like a beached whale and the vents talk at dawn about fog in a dialect that is all hissing. Three notes first, then a fourth I would have to grow a ligament for, the thin resin strap under the throat that lets a chord split clean into two on the same breath. I would sleep with my ear to the floor the plant keeps for us, pithglass cool, and feel the whole plateau move like someone turning over in a great bed. I would learn to count not by heartbeats, which are jumpy, but by the city’s pressure wave, and to love the hour when the phloem drops a pitch and the clocks inside everyone go a shade slower, a silk half-turn of time you can use for mending.

You would find me in the places where distances are fair only if you sing to them. At noon on a causeway slung from one organ-tower to another, when the mills are exhaling and white vapor makes tea out of the light, I would be a stripe of voice across the gap. Sometimes there will be a visitor’s silence beside me, stainless, frightened, measuring with eyes. That is part of the work. I keep talking nonsense if I have to, a nursery string of syllables with a pulse in it, because the law here says not to be smaller than the wind and I am a kind of law. I will put my palm to the visitor’s sternum, hum my own length into their ribs, and the bridge will decide to stay the same under both our feet. That makes me a judge of a sort; it withers arguments, sometimes, to feel the plank do what the voice asks.

On some days I am a cable tech, if the cable is a vine with copper salts in its phloem and the tech wears resin polish on the wrists. I pat the living wires when I pass, and they lay their insulating leaves back to be looked at. When the eels wake under the skin of charge and slide to greet me along the length, I bribe them with a drop of salt, stroke them along their sleek, elective muscles, and feel them shunt a worry away from a hot place. In the piston-houses I rub wax—breathable, poor cousin to bees given leaf—into gaskets with a song too high to sing with my mouth, all ligament, all skull-arch. The wax pores open the way a pupil opens, and it is good to be answered like that.

I have a friend who is more purely an ionist than I have patience to be. She has green-blue to the elbows and keeps magnetotactic slime as if it were a clever friend that refuses to move house. We go together to the ferrous-crystal dynamos, those plants that learned magnet by a long slow courtship with the planet, and she rubs one finger along a smear of aligning cells and tells me, quietly as if gossiping on a stair, where a current goes sullen in the cold. I am better at ladders. I can be up the side of a tower and into the slotted places where the wind is tutored, tapping flues with a mallet on a braided cord until the season makes sense. If the song comes wrong, the sugar engines work at a loss. If it comes right, the boilers breathe easy and the steam smells as much of tea as of grit. That is a craft and a privilege.

Morning is when you will find me listening hard. The air comes up off the lower country and gives itself up by the portion. The condensers comb it into beads like a mother untangling a child’s hair. The plant treats water as etiquette: offer it first and it will stay. I go to the drip-rooms where drops are not hoarded but set to humming in ceramic-pith shot with canals. There is a hum there the human throat cannot make that cleans the water of everything but the day it fell on. I hold my cup out and the cup sweats before it fills. If I have to cut anything living that day I will offer that first drink to the pith and wait, because haste tastes of teeth and the plant remembers teeth with a frown a century long.

Sometimes a storm stumbles here by mistake, dragging rain like a torn coat. Then the memory-channels wake and pretend to be rivers for an afternoon. You will find me barefoot, reading the long polish of those grooves with the skin, like a blind person reading an old joke. Here a flood in the late ice put its shoulder to a tower and made it rethink the angle of polite. There a year of fog never learned to lie down and had to be trained with a net of new spines. The moss-flowers go delirious on the banks and intoxicate for the span of a tea cooling. We pinch them shut on the tongue to trap that hour and then it is decent to be sad and go back to work.

I keep bells in the vine I travel with. They disagree with one another on any day worth having. They tell me what the wind means as if it had swallowed three opinions out of pride. I keep a calendar in my pocket that is a braid of morning and evening phloem, and I can run my thumb along it and know when the quarter-seconds thicken toward sunrise and we ought not to start a mill’s long inhale. The scheduling leaves can be quarrelsome. You have to stand under them like a parishioner and ask them to open the work of pollen with a humility they can believe. They know better than any clerk what music the city can afford today.

My ears are annotated. Where storms come from, where they go. I sleep west because west is an earthing place; the air throws its weight east and a body can ground itself into that tilt and not wake bruised. In winter, which considers itself a guest here but loves us too much and overstays sometimes, I take a ladder before bed and climb to unhood the broad leaf of my house at night. Frost is an excellent teacher. The plant shows me what gradient thinks like when it is honest. We lie under a skin bright as a coin and the leaf throws its glaze flat, and in the morning you can tell what the night asked.

There will be a day when lightning crawls sideways along the pipes like a herd that knows a fence. The static combs are supposed to be enough but the cloud will not mind them this time. I will go out with a sprig of mineralized cambium carved as thin as a reluctance and comb the air back by hand. You have to do it gently, with a coaxing patience, because fright sends charge home just when we need it to stay calm. Who comes with me? The ionist, always. We do not talk because talking would be unfair to the wind; we make our throats wide and stupid and hum like two cows.

We live thin. The works take up more room than the workers. My house learned to be house under my hands, a place in the trunk where yesterday there was no patience for being entered. If you are a door, you learn the mouth that will ask you, and mine learned mine. The vents are windows that pretend to be pipes and blow bedsheets clean with their grammar. The floors remember day long enough for a walk to the kettle and then go honest-dark. There is pleasure in this ethic. Stuff echoes. A pot left against a wall will pick up the tower’s note at noon and ring politely. I wear pithglass in my hair, little strips that learn the last light and give it back to me while I walk home. On my collarbones lie two thin plaques grown into me over a long winter of climbing; they answer the towers like two small, well-mannered children, and sometimes I put my fingers to them the way a person touches a jug of water to feel if it is cold.

Visitors think of mystics when we hold council because we choose a place where the chord is even and long and let the argument sit in that slot until its teeth stop jabbing each other. We speak. If our words can keep a bridge’s length under them, then the bridge gives us the vote by not moving and we go ahead. If not, we schedule the decision for a wetter morning. Outsiders find this evasive. It is etiquette. We live with someone old who cannot abide shouting. We take our quarrels to the tone that can hold them.

Death here is a thorough politeness. When my time is done, it will be in the late hour after a day of much air and someone else humming to get me down a ladder. They will put me into root-coral, which is how the bedrock keeps room for people it likes, into a seam that once took water but has learned study instead. A strand of my hair and a length of copper will go in together, because this place has learned how bodies heat metal kindly and will take that lesson as a spice. My note, the one I kept the bridges with, will be sung by whoever’s throat can still hold it, and the city will lay an answering harmonic along it and make it strong for a while. If a child asks where I’ve gone, they will be shown the joist under the place where the rail squeaks because someone has been careless with resin. They will be told to press their hand there and hum, and they will feel a steadiness that was me, though not all at once.

If I had come wrong, as a dog or a prideful ravenous appetite, I would not have had a body here for long. The doors would have kept moving like fish and I would have laughed and left out of embarrassment. I know this. I have tuned my hunger to the place. We eat pith that snaps like a compliment, drink tea that tastes like motors and bark, taste moss-flowers like a minute’s riot, sometimes a beetle by consent when one forgets itself. The white raptor is a rumor we borrow from the lip of sky to make the afternoon raise its hem. When someone from low country brings their tape and silence, I put bells in their belt and teach them a nonsense song about counting stones by kissing them. I am gentle with their fear because I remember what body is like when not braided in.

I am not singular the way a skull is. None of us are. I am a district of preference under one skin. Mine loves tension, loves the moan of a guy-vine when it is about to change its mind. My knees know the difference between a hum that means a piston’s sleeve is drying out and a hum that means a tower is bored and trying on a lower note for a joke. Some nights I lie down with my jaw unhinged, literally loose, and let the plant count my blood for me. When I get up my feet know how long a foot is that day.

If you were to ask me, straight, where to find me, I would point not to a door but to a tone. Noon on the bridge between the annealed tower that rings if you love it and the squat fluted one that resents spring but relents anyway. Come then. The wind will be loud and long; the mills will be breathing sweet and exhaust like two workers leaning back with mugs. I will be there, thin-bellied with attention, hand on cable, mouth full of length, keeping an honest line for everything that must cross—ion, insect, person, rumor—so that the city can continue to count by itself and let us count with it. If you are frightened, I will hum your bones into agreeing with the day. If you can be brave enough to be noisy, even if it is foolish noise, you will cross. And if at the last you put a finger into a drop of dew that has traveled all the way from a comb to your hand and stopped there to decide, you will taste iron and tea and the aftertaste of chord, and I will smile because now you know the place by its own language, which is my language also. In that way I am citizen, worker, hinge, steward of steadiness; and when the plateau breathes, I breathe, and when it sings, I would like to believe the fifth it lifts is mine.
