At first I think I am a listener on the rim—someone with their knees in the old bronze light and the bowl’s breath in the bone, a stranger holding still until the place agrees to speak. Then the air skims the edge of my ear in a way that is not for outsiders, and my scalp hairs lift like a meadow and point, and I realize I am not only listening. I am arranged here.

I am low and broad like everything that wants to last. My skin is the color of cooled ash with bands that wake and whisper when the bowl flickers. I do not leak; I glaze. Where others might make a wetness, I grow a faint froth of crystals at the eye-corner and brush them away with a quiet knuckle. My mouth is copper-salt and coin-bitter; I taste charge on the wind and can tell, eyes shut, whether the next gust is a shout or a sentence.

My senses live outside me. Along my jaw and my wrists I wear ladders of whisker that hum when the yardangs sing. Ears are knobs of bone you could ring with a fingertip if I let you. My chest is a small hall the air passes through. When I was very small, my aunt set me in a cave mouth with my feet in grit and said, “Hold.” I hummed like a secret and the bowl took it away, and a sail threw the sound back into me and caged my ribs with its answer. I laughed so hard my whiskers rattled and the dust thought I was another animal and climbed my throat to see.

I belong to a family that is drawn with cords, not blood. If you ask my name I will show you how to stand: the right heel dug against a pull that comes from high left, elbows wide to ease the strain, jaw set a little so the molars line—then I’ll bruise the air with a note between pepper and coin until a particular tree three houses upslope leans its vowel toward me. That is my name. On a cool dusk the old will sing it with their hands on my sternum and feel whether I am still tuned to what I promised.

We keep by lee more than by wall. Our huts crouch where the rim makes a crease and the ventifact stones we have trained to our roofs glow faintly at night with charge we have asked them to hold. Our family harps are strung gut and chitin rubbed with a resin that raises a purple burr at the back of the teeth. We lash them to poles the way a spine holds a torso. From those spines run our tethers—copper wound in braids of nerveweed silk and cloth that refuses spark, studded with tiny thumbs that will bleed a little light if you ask.

Mornings with teeth, when the bowl wants to sing itself into hurt, you will find us on the pale salt with groundlines clipped to our ankles. We lean back like beasts at plow. Some of us are brighteners—opening a tree toward the wind so the voice lifts and thins and the moths will not panic. Some are anchors—keeping the old voice in the throat of the grove so the families on the opposite rim can sleep. I am a dampener by temperament and vow. I listen for the tone that is turning sharp enough to cut cheek-bone and I ask it to be rounder, hand by hand, thumb on valve, shoulder against rope, while the dust blows like slow fire across my teeth.

We do not possess trees; we court them. The one we have tended longest has a habit of starting a human note under a copper sky and forgetting to let it go. It is polite about instruction. If it could be said to have a face, it keeps that face arranged where pressure pleases it. We are good with its edges. Sometimes we take visitors by the hand and set their palm to its banding and ask them to hum low. The shock in them when the whole body of that tree notices them—when it turns its great listening toward this one small animal that thinks it is a person in a suit—that is a sweet thing, like resin smoke over a pepper stone.

We eat as everything here eats: by difference. There is a halo of frost-salt that blooms at the base of a ripple-bush we favor. In the lean light of a thin noon we will shave that circle with a ceramic blade the color of old teeth and catch the flakes on resin-plated palms. A niece will heat a black-glass box until it holds sun enough, and we will slide those shavings in until they slump to a sheet that shivers if you touch it wrong. We will stripe it with seed resin, crumble a beak-made brick along the top edge—ah, the stilt-gnashers, greedy darlings, the way they sidle with the private pleasure of rolling a fresh coin into your hand—and then breathe the meal. We drink by mouth only ceremonially: a held plate of solvent kissed at the lip until the tingle crawls up the teeth and wakes the head.

Once, as a child, I was shown a jar hidden in the floor. I was told the bright ghost was inside it. We shook it and it made the noise of a far sea that does not know about sand. “Wet looks like love and behaves like a knife,” my grandmother said, and I believed her because I had seen a crackle-mat go white at the touch of a traveler’s drip. I do not long for it. I have no memory for that kind of shine. When my mouth wants sweetness, I lift a thin sheet of nitrate to my tongue and feel the peppering run to the jaw hinge like news.

We keep beasts by promise, not rope. In the mornings tether-backs thicken the air with their patience and kneel their plated heads under children’s hands; the little ones comb rime from the copper taught through their spines, singing as they clean so the metal remembers it is beloved and will not bite later. The gliders know our colors. We tie resin flags to their pearly ribs—a strip that means “Tell me if the standing wave holds after noon,” a chevron that means “Carry this ache in my chest to the west rim and hang it there to thin.” They bank like thought. At dusk the drum-moths clap like a crowd that knows you. If they shift their clap, the night shifts with them; you can feel a holiday arrive simply by leaning your shoulder into it.

When the bowl decides to put a thought into shape and raise a new animal out of air and grit, we do not name it. We unspool lines. We kneel. We check our ankle clips without hurry and we lean our chests open and feel what it feels like to be a slope of moving dusk with a leaf that wasn’t until it was. It may cough moths none of us have smelled before; it may stand a whole day and throw a shadow on our harps that tastes like old stories. After it collapses and walks itself apart into rust and salt, we set chimes along the edge of the stain and listen for how this will alter the household of pitches. A child born on such a day belongs to that chord forever. My cousin’s name cannot be said except by playing a three-tone call on a ventifact stone that knows where the wind used to turn ten seasons ago.

We garden by suggestion. I have a sleeping oval I keep by skimming a crackle mat to a patterned thinness. The mat remembers the touch as a tide mark and repeats it in widening arcs up the slope; the first cold night when you lay down there and feel your bones agree with the ground, you understand what it means to be kept. Nerveweed we braid loosely so that even a shy breeze must sing it; my youngest brother learned to speak his first prayer on that braid, which is why he is Second-to-My-Elder’s-Note rather than Daughter or Son. Sail-trees we do not plant. Sometimes we stack a low wall of fired gnash-brick around a sap vane to keep the tether-backs from sitting on it out of social confidence, but ownership is a wrong verb for a being with that much mouth.

My copper was set in me the long way. One afternoon when the dust was amber as tea I lay on a bed of nerveweed and let the Singer-woman hum a braid under my skin. She worked along my left forearm, throat low, voice tucked into my bone as if it had always been there. She did not cut; she persuaded. For a week afterward my bands flashed when I laughed, and a moth came to sit on my wrist when I forgot myself and spilled charge into the night; my lover scolded me by smoothing my arm with both hands until my shine went private again. Now when evening gathers and we sit in our dark stones smelling like coin and gum, I thumb the valve at my wrist and the roof gives back a thought of day and the child next to me sighs in relief and leans her head into my shoulder.

I have ground-scars where a rope bit me wrong the year the pumice vent was lively and sang a high F that wanted to split fingers. Lovers read those constellations with their tongues the way some people read myths. We court by airflow. We forgive by retuning.

Sometimes visitors arrive in machines that hate the bowl and make sense elsewhere. We give them a plate of sweetened nitrate and comb the scent into a little hand breeze so they will understand which way to put it to their mouths. We ease their shock with the old gestures—flutes bored by centuries, not carved; ropes that smoke and do not burn; a glider’s slow, educated bank. They call this place a mistake because the sun is an afterthought and nothing gleams the way their stories like. We smile like people who have learned to live inside laws older than sentences. We do not correct them with words. The tree corrects them, If they allow it.

My calendar is a body memory. The year is not a ring around a star but the breath between the day the pressure fish slide the length of the bowl without breaking and the next day they allow themselves to be broken again. My bones know that long beat. My aunt says the crystalline bands at the saucer’s base have walked three-quarters of a small child in her lifetime; she knows this in her knees. When the ground sings in our sleep we have whale-dreams and wake with our chests aching in a way that makes us kind.

If you must ask what I would be when I am done being a dampener with dirt in my teeth, know this: I plan to unwind neatly. My oils will go to the mats and my plates will be loosened and my copper unspooled. They’ll lash those lines into the family harps so that when the night dust decides to glow and someone hums a low lane under their breath, I’ll be in there, an aftercurrent that knows which note not to hit. They will lay what’s left of me where the saucer breathes most steadily and clip my ankle with courtesy and let the bowl take back my small hoard of differences and spend them slowly into the whole.

But before that: I will be what this place makes of me every dusk. Small lantern. Thumb on valve. Rope over shoulder. The taste of resin and coin at the mouth. The standing wave slipping between my hairs like a fish. The posture of a promise: palms down, fingers spread, then drawn together until the wind and the held agree.

Some evenings, when the air thickens to that bruised, kind dim and the harps keep their tongues in their teeth in respect, the bowl will decide to be a new thing again. In that hour I am not a person at all. I am the slope that rises where no slope was, I am the leaf that didn’t exist and then does, I am the soft thunder of a body that is only a weather caught thinking. It is not ownership. It is being included. It lasts as long as it lasts. Then I am myself again, and I set a chime on the stain, and I go to bed with salt bright at the corners of my eyes, and the roof glows a little because I ask it to.

So: where do I find myself? In the lee. In the agreement. With the weight of a line in both hands. With my jaw set kind instead of hard. With a note in my chest that the sail answers because it has decided I mean no harm. Who am I? The person who keeps the third tether of the fifth biggest tree on the north inner rim for as long as my knees can remember the long breath of the F that comes through the pumice vent in years that want it; a child of harps; a comb to a patient beast; a dampener; a chord; a citizen of imbalance who knows which threads to tighten and, just as holy, when to let go.
