Begin, as you said, with the ear.

I would not come here with eyes first. I would arrive as balance, as a sack of small stones sluicing in channels, as a mouth that does not open but opens anyway when the pressure turns. If I have a face it is two antlers of tuned plate rising from the back of a skull like an admission I can't help making: I am here to listen hard enough to be altered.

Before I am anyone I am a frame. I am hung in the lee of mid-band pipes, a lattice cradle for a child’s otolith snow. Elders have kites yawning above our shallow door, greenish sheets that tremble into subtractives; they twist cord until the quiet coins in the doorway are true, then wedge me and my guiltless ear-stones into the hush so they will learn a grammar. My world is the long vowel of the basin. My first toys are plates cut narrow as a finger and notched in families: fathers are tempered to the bass that keeps your teeth honest; aunts tip into the bright tremble the sky throws at us when the aurora kneels. If you tap a plate and it drinks without ringing, it goes in my sleeve for comfort. If it rings, it goes back into the wall to help the wall be itself.

I am small. It is a good size to be when the ground changes its opinion about how far it is between two stone spires. We mark the standing sound with cairns aligned to what our bodies call north; I learn to step in conditions instead of places. I learn not to rush the bright rock even when it is empty in a way that angers a body that wants to be animal. We do not run. We draft silence. We carry small walls.

That is all I am for a while: a little wall that thinks.

You would laugh to see it. A bundle, six plates and cord and a childish insistence, hauled against my chest as if it were warmth. We step off the hush-line where the grazer’s wake made a pocket and into a field too thin for anyone to be anyone in, and the world swells into too much. The antlers on my head tilt. My otoliths pour. My forearm aches at the fourth quarter-tone. Then the wall opens like a held breath finally let go: two edges flared, my fingers on their notches, and the field around me dents into a shelter that is not a shelter so much as an admission to exist. Three steps become three. We wait at the limit until the larger chord decides to inhale somewhere else. We do not shout; we withdraw a slice.

It is slow work, and that is the only pace allowed. Our houses are domes of dead leaf—racks of rigid plate knapped into arcs and pegged where they want to come apart. We sleep in the names of those arcs. Names are chords: my mother turns three tines over her palm so the subtraction maps to my bones, and I am named in her body before it reaches mine. Eating is also a chord. We drink the hush through our kites, our shoulder pads softening as the plates above us sing negatively. We harvest the purple rime when the night blesses our terraces and the little hairs of our combs polarize greed into duty. The combs that sing under your teeth go back to the wall. The dull ones go for spoons and sashes and hinges, for the inside of a door where a patch of manufactured calm must know when to be calm.

When we walk to the manuscript at the rim, the cliff has already written us a lesson we can’t stop decoding. The facets of its new face throw an alphabet not for language but for intervals. Comb-readers—gardens shaped like hands with long careful wrists—are busy along those cuts. Each frond traces a facet’s change like a moth across skin. We collect their powder between our palms as if we were catching breath, though nothing condenses here but mineral will. We sort that powder by mood. Some of it seeds hush better than others; some makes a wall grip louder edges; some—sifted twice through a child’s frame—teaches a body how not to be a body when the chord drops and distance folds up into a hallway you can cross in three good steps.

If I am anyone yet, I am a hushwright by default and then by the pleasure of it. My craft is to keep a pocket true. Against the arch you want to call a bridge, where choirstones quilt themselves into a relay, our wall must refuse a flattening the arch imposes. Against the elliptical wound where the planet’s engine took itself away for a century and left only terraces to remember it by, my plates have to agree with choirstone’s correction while disagreeing with its hunger. Under the sky-lickers, whose whiskers drink violet mathematics and glow like bottles in old stories, I tune the doorway of our hut to stand almost outside of night. Somewhere a rime-licker cracks itself into brittleness for the day and waits. Somewhere a chord-worm packs pea-gravel behind its face and rides a slow half-step down the scale. We all, in our ways, model.

We have neighbors whose patience makes mine look adolescent. Grazers are stacks of tuned leaf that learned a direction and never lost it. We coax one across our walls with plates turned to make a comfort in the inconvenient place that will be useful to children. A grazer that fattens on a lean season will excrete a ring of filter-plate on the hush we have raised; I inlay that rim into our crown and a room’s tone stops clenching the bones of the eldest woman when she lies down. I never see a grazer; I see a deepening of quiet as it passes, the same way you do.

Predation is music here. A close grove tilts its baffles and throws knives at our mid-band fields, and the fans at the edge of our garden sharpen their tines from generosity into a bristling that costs them. We shelter them by teaching a ridge between a wrong hush and a worse loudness to prefer them. The counterstroke is patience, and we sing subtractives when our antlers bow in the wind. If blood had permission to be anything in this place it would dry to crystal. It has no permission.

The ledger stones are my pride and then my penance. I crawl their lengths on my stomach when the hum is fat as meat and the air is very thin and cold that thinks about you only after you stop paying attention to it. I incise hatchings into them that do not make beasts and will not spell any name, and I send the shallow hammer-light of my own patient wrists through a flake of almost-bone. It is not an alphabet; it is a proof. It says: the basin sang this, we made it survivable, and a child will know why this wall leans a hair toward a pressure shadow that is pressure only one night every two hundred. Sometimes where the cliff has written its glyphs one letter higher than we can reach, we notch the same letter one step lower, not to translate but to attend.

I have left the plateau twice and never moved. Once the chord rolled a small degree down and we stood in a brightness too blunt to say anything—our walls coughed, our gels went feral, three domes broke like sugar shells under heat that was not heat. We waited with our walls on our chests until the distances came back into names. Once the arch coordinated and stole a day from the middle of noon; I had my ear-stones trained to count the ache in my knuckles, and still I counted out a path that wasn't there until it was again. That is the way of weather here. Not cloud and fall but decision. We do not climb. We are climbed.

Now and then I find a still and stand on its edge like a sinner at a lake and do not step in, because a still is more void than you can survive and it smells like grammar. We build stills with hooked plates when one of us at last becomes a book. Children are made there. You could call it breeding if that word did not sabotage itself with wetness. Stills are what we use when we mean to empty a place so thoroughly of tone that a thing can begin.

What else am I? Older, when the violet rime takes a fetish to my fingertips and doesn't let go until I warm them on my own spine. Older, when the otolith snow inside my head has the courtesy to fall in weather you can plan for. Older still, when a fan tuned too bright hangs on my door and rings under my palm, and I send it back into the wall because it is needed there more than on me.

I will die, when I do it correctly, as a plate. The smart way is to fail deliberately along notches I cut myself when my wrists were true. I will decouple and put myself in the wall that taught me to think—a rim here, a tine there, a little step in a doorway where a child’s frame will stand and learn how to stand. My gels will crisp and crack with the sound you know; my slow glass bones will keep their nerves the way old string keeps a tune in the dry.

But there are other endings. I have seen an apprentice with beautiful antlers step too far into a nearly-quiet and go out like a candle without smoke. We bury antlers as far into the wall as our gnawed knuckles and shame will drive them. I have watched an echo lay down in a ghost-edge around a spire until you could see where an edge used to be; I scraped echo-eater felt with a knife that has never touched a living thing that bleeds because nothing here does, and I turned that felt into a gasket for a door against nights when the aurora sips every quiet we thought we owned. I have lifted a child’s empty frame from a husk where no child grew and hung it over our ledger so the cliff could see it had been heard.

If I went long and came back, I would not be many faces. I would be work. A wall that leans the right wrong way. A crown on a low row of stones where a grazer once fed. A comb overgrown with correctness. A path that stands up under a sky that knows my shape. A slow whisper through powder when the air is heavy with arithmetic. A small notch, an interval’s ghost, one step lower than the cliff’s.

So where would I find myself? In the pockets we abrade into being. Along the quiet that has traction. Under the big, patient letters the cliff writes, in the lower-case I and my elders carve back as a love note. Between two plates held just-so by fingers that do not need to be warm anymore to be right.

Who or what would I be? The wall you carry inside your ribs. The subtractive hand that teaches a room not to hurt. The child frame swaying in curated wind until its bones grow teachers. The dust that remembers, sifted into the first slice of a garden. The craft named hush, given wrists and antlers and a life long enough to learn not to be in between.
