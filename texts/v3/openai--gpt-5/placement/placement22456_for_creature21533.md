At first I think I am a visitor with a weather held in my mouth. I shoulder through the hide, and the room keeps my breath as collateral. It rolls it into a bead and dangles it from my own knuckle to see if I have anything worth falling for. I say a syllable, too long, too clean, and watch it gloss a leaf proud. Someone behind me hums a low answer, the pitch of apology; the pride loosens, the bead lets go, and my word drops into a bell that does not belong to me. Good. The room has a place for errors; it makes them into edges.

So where do I land? At the margin that refuses to remain one. I find my feet in the net that calls itself a floor and my knees already learning the curve of cane. No chair will take me; there are no chairs, only laps of rope that hold you with the manners you’ve brought. They hand me a reed with a single hole and a few rules:

— Let your breath be warm and heavy; cold words bounce and bruise.
— Low means fall, high means lift. Practice until your ribs answer before your mind decides to.
— Do not laugh if you can help it, not near a mat that is learning pride.
— If you must name, wet your palm first.

I begin by naming nothing. I lie still with my ear against a rib and let the cane introduce the clay. Their accent is water. I learn that the room has a way of saying yes that sounds like the patience of moss, and a way of saying no that feels like a rope deciding to become bone.

The first work they give me is narrow: I am a hinge for the middle rain. I take the drum’s skin between thumb and intent and tilt the note a moth’s weight down until drizzle begins to gather above the ring of baskets. Then I hold my breath as a courtesy and let my heart do the falling. The reed in my teeth is a yes-no thing with music under the paint; I move it by pulse alone and try not to think of the river turning its mind two trays over. I am told that thinking tips more than sound does, here. That turns out to be true.

I learn to speak the little language of the gourd cisterns, the burping that means dump, the companionable clatter of beads in a reed-gutter that tells you someone somewhere has slipped a knot a hair-width too clever. When it storms because of that cleverness, I am not yet allowed to sing; I am permitted the tack cloth and the work of rubbing seed-lac over a seam until it knows what edge it is again. The elders sing parallel into corners and the room goes on as before with a new rule laid fat and wet into the clay. We count the courage of the mistake by the thickness of that band. We forgive by remembering.

Food arrives by climate, which is to say by politeness performed at body temperature. I have never been so aware of my own heat. I drink broths that taste like grass learning tongue, and I am asked not to watch the fermenters when I am feeding them. “Nothing with a face tends them,” someone says, and they hand me a mask that dampens my sight until my eyes are only wicks. I oblige and the clock of dawn comes in at the right hour. Whoever caught that first misaligned dawn wears it on their laugh, a scar taught by steam. I think I love them for how they nod at the low C.

Not all my days remain inside. The room raises me like a lung practicing an inhale and then hands me to the edge. I braid myself into a net and swing out where the air is thin enough that thought is a bead that might not choose to fall. The wind finds my back and tries to convince me I am lighter than my agreements. I am not. The cliff under my palms feels like planks committed to geology by stubbornness. My awl is bone. My song is a habit that turns teeth into a metronome. When the snail inside tilts its antlers to the left at a boom, I feel it through the cliff’s pretense of mountain and bring my mallet hand a breath west to share the understanding. The room and the edge are married through that small animal’s posture. I start to trust that kindness can be a structural member.

Who am I, then? The answer keeps changing with humidity. Some days I am “Keeper,” said while breath still holds heat, and that word binds to the mat I lean over until the droplet on its corner lets it go. The name isn’t mine once it falls; it becomes slope and moves on. Some nights I am nobody with a face and I bend over a felted fermenter with my eyes undone and my mouth covered in wax. I feel the old bacteria love me without knowing me and give back a dawn on time. On a nine-night run I tie a length of bast from my belt to the rib behind where I always sit. I pretend indifference and check the knot in secret, in silence, at different hours. On the ninth night there is hair.

I am “Posted,” not to authority so much as to angle. My rooted strip remembers my watch. A year later the new leg of frame will thicken in the hour I favored, and the room will learn to lean into that patience. This is not metaphor. I am in the wood. I was always wood, perhaps; I simply had to be tied the right way long enough.

I borrow a grammar. I read fingerprints in the clay the way I used to read street names. Someone pressed a thumb with a hunger in it near a valve string and the chamber still bears the echo of that ache; the water there is generous for a week. We keep that story in knots on the back of the frame so we’ll remember where not to get hungry again. We are always telling the future how to avoid our weather and always hoping it ignores us at least once to make something we could not have thought.

Pieces of me choose other lives and come back to report. For a season I become the boy who holds a drop to a standstill—except I am not him; I merely learn his trick on dry days with my lips pressed to the oculus, fogging a membrane my skin wants to love. I call drizzle and ask it politely to hesitate. It does not. I am not him. The humility is instructional. For two weeks I think I am the seamstress-spider, listening by belly to the slow walk of water and taking into myself a drip that would have fallen wrong. I’m not. My silk is thought, and it’s not sticky enough. And yet, it counts as practice: I learn to stand with my palms out and let consequences choose me, and then I learn to give them back.

Once, a leather valve coughs a fish into my lap. I catch a fold of air in a scoop like the older hands told me and lay it on a hot plate and watch it become water and then sugar and then the kind of story we tell each other with our tongues behind our teeth. I keep that one. When someone from below asks what this place will feed a mouth like theirs, I talk about the lilied greens and the ash milk and the slow honey the web gives. I do not tell them about the fish the air makes. I have learned the shape of the room’s privacy and I wear it like seed-lac on my skin, an invisible varnish that tells wind where not to steal from me.

I quarrel once properly, with someone I love. We do not say names because we are not cruel. We move the drum a finger each and adjust the metronome of bells until a fat drop grows on my eyebrow and makes a decision. When it falls, it cleans us; we put the drum back where it belongs. Later, we tie each other’s braids tighter so the vines will have somewhere decent to take hold when they need a friend.

Light is a clock and not a cook, so I stop asking it for flavor. I learn to keep hunger in beats and temperature. Dawn arrives in my chest before it reaches the clay; I wake and do not open my eyes because that would be rude. I hum instead and trust the feather-brush pendulums to stroke what needs urging. I have become someone who sleeps cupped into other shoulders with one hand on the room’s skin, translating dream gusts into the smallest note the drum requires. Sleep is an instrument here; I am an instrument too.

In time—if time can be said to pass in a place measured in thickening—I make my own mistake. I tie a valve string clever because I have learned too quickly and want to hear my lesson out loud. The room thrashes. Storm is fists under skin. The frog blinks exactly once during the shout we give to call dawn, and through that blink the day walks in tilted. We sing it parallel. Afterward the clay wears a new dark ring where my cleverness lives. I kneel there in the warmth and press my hand into it until its whorl is deeper than my pride. The elders do not scold. They do not need to. The room has already remembered me.

I keep thinking I will be singular again. That I will become a person-as-a-person, a name that does not drip. It refuses me gently. I find myself instead as a series of agreements:

— I am a knot that rooted and now thickens at the hour I loved.
— I am a reed’s low note that pulls drizzle down a breath sooner than it meant to fall.
— I am a smear of seed-lac over a seam; the shine that says someone cared before you arrived.
— I am the habit my hands have of holding heat for things that need it and refusing to dry too fast.
— I am the willingness to be kept.

Where would you find me, if you were to come? With my palm laid flat somewhere small: a mat’s corner, a rope learning spine. With my ear against a rib, hearing cane love clay enough to argue. With my mouth near the bell that a droplet makes of the present. Sometimes at the oculus, cheek testing the bite of outside as if it were a fruit. Sometimes out there stitched into the roar, turning wind’s animal into a steadier thrum for the edgehands who will graft the next lung to the cliff we write.

Who am I? A person, loosely. A wet name, briefly. A rooted knot, increasingly. A keeper of slope by courtesy. A student of causality devout enough to call it god when no one is listening. Someone whose fingerprints the clay reads as a promise to return.

If you stay, I will show you the small place your breath already knows. We will stand still together while a bead assembles on your knuckle, patient with gravity, and when it falls into the bell that waits there, we will hear the world tilt by a kindness only this close to law. We will leave a mark that is not ownership but memory: heat spent well, a curve well-chosen, a rule stitched until it holds. And the rope under our feet will sprout hair. And the room will lean a little toward us and then a little past us, which is the only way to be held without being kept forever.
