At first I would only be a pair of lungs learning their manners.

I would come up slow and be sat down slower, a bowl put in my hands with a thumb’s worth of richness melting across its surface and the quiet hand-sign for breathe here, not there. The bell they give me would hang too bright on my ankle; I would wrap it with a strip of hide until it learned my gait and stopped announcing itself. My first work would be to make my breath like my steps—flat, equal, paid for as I go.

I would find myself wherever sound begins to mean more than volume. That is to say: at the posts along the rim where wind has a voice if you give it notches. I would apprentice to an old carver who counts by the way air presses the skin along the cheekbone. We would go before sunflats and score the wood where the grain says yes. We’d test with goat gut and a line of hair, fingers out to hear what ears miss, and make a row that can tell you if the cloud-sea will climb or sulk. I would learn to read a morning by the chord it plays when the air moves across a pattern we cut the year my hands were still clumsy.

On other days I would be a child again, sent up to the black roof with a bone blade thin as a bird’s wit to skim the night’s frost before sun undoes it. The pan would sing the smallest sound—the close-mouthed consonant of melt—while I walked it to the earthboxes where barley and blue pea sit like restrained hope. A neighbor would pass me a coil of shade-lids and another would tug a leather flap shut over a full cistern and no one would make it a speech. Even thanks is a ration here; it lives in the use of a hand.

Before I ever held a rule, I would hold rope. Hair-rope twisted, teased from the herds’ patience. I would sit with aunties whose thumbs show cord in their whorls and they would teach me how to splice a net so that the mend becomes stronger than the tear. On windless days we would walk four together with a dew-net laid across our arms like a shared body, carry it to where the rock says yes, lash it down with skin and hair, and stand back as the cloud shoulders itself and the whole rim whispers and begins to weep. It’s not a miracle; it’s good memory and the refusal to hurry water past where it chose to linger.

Later, when my ear could tell two kinds of thinness apart—air thin from height, and water thin from being near—I would be given a row of lids to keep. I would become a keeper of cisterns more than any other thing, because water is how this city names itself. I would learn the channels by touch in fog. I would read a courtyard by the way its runnels darken when a child knocks sludge loose. I would know which leather flaps need extra grease and which shade-frames lie under which stoops and who clears moss on seeing it and who must be reminded by the low civility of a knot hung at their door: a line tied to say you were seen not seeing, a chance to repair before the rope that keeps the law grows public on a rack.

On bad rock days I would sit with my back to a known stone and look fast. There is a kind of attention here that is a cousin to prayer and panic both; you pour it through a seam and it returns with news. You do not shout rock. You clap a wood strip against a post in a rhythm everyone knows means spend it along the sacrificial wall, turn, crouch, wait. The fall goes by like a thought you chose not to finish. After, someone resets the stones that took it, and someone else sets a kettle into warm dust and speaks the day’s weather in the grammar of hands while smoke combs your hair.

There are other selves I would try on and shrug off like layers hung to dry.

For a while I would be a raven. It is impossible to live here and not want to. I would learn the routes through alleys where my wings do not quite fit and still pass, and I would keep a coin of light under my tongue just to know it’s there. I would steal a bell only once and then return it, not because anyone caught me but because bells are not for bravado.

On three separate mornings in one long winter I would be the fog-line itself. I would run waist-high across an open court, slack enough to forgive a stumble and tight enough to keep a body from walking into forgetfulness. I would remember hands in a map that skin keeps—calluses, small scars, the grease that tells you who cooks and who climbs. I would learn how many different kinds of hurry a person can make when they can’t see their own name.

I would go out where goats go and hammer in ladders that aren’t orders but invitations. There is a courtesy in meeting a hoof halfway. I would leave a length of rope near a ledge where the wild ones lamb because no one here thinks generosity needs a witness. The ledge will remember.

There would be a season when trade calls. I would cross the blade-back between cities with my bell muffled and a bundle of hair-rope at my hip, coils tight as thought. I’d take with me a jar of fat and the glint that means an ancestor gambled well. I would come back with a cord of knots that spell: tracks of a pale cat seen far north, insects coming like weather, a nephew born in a city whose posts sing in a slightly different tuning. We would stand on a roof and compare fog-songs the way other places compare boasts.

Eventually, age would teach me the ways of slowness you cannot fake. My role would tilt interior. I’d sit where smoke lives and turn heat with a flat ladle so the pot takes it like advice. I would grease my grandnieces’ cheeks because wind disrespects youth if you let it and I have fat enough to be kind. When a knot-judgment is needed I would tie it where no one can see and then untie it and make them feel it with their fingers until they know what has been decided. Words cost. Rope remembers.

Through all of it, I would keep company with small metals and smaller mercies. The ankle-bell would be there answering my step, not ringing so much as remarking. The low instruments of the place—bone flute, rope-harp strung across a narrow—would never get louder than a room could bear. We would make music like we make fire: under control, with the lid half on. I would hang the quieted tongues from the bells of a dead friend in the passage where the draft speaks once in a while, and when it does, it would say a body is still here in the only honest way left.

I would go often to the turning-place out on bare wind where there is a niche and a stone whose hollow is black from touch. It is not devotion; it is record. Each time I would lay my fingers there not because a voice told me to but because my day would not add up without that subtraction. Sometimes a stranger would come up panting and be steered to the bench. I would put broth in their hands. I would teach them the hand for slow. I would not tell them to touch the stone. They would.

If you wanted to find me, you would not look for a spectacle. You would listen for a bell that is too modest to be a bell. You would go to the edge in the thin blue before sunrise where the posts begin to speak, and you would see a person with their head down as if in respect to their own breath, rubbing a small length of hair-rope between thumb and palm to keep it honest. Or you would find me under a lattice beside a lidded blackness, palm measuring the drip’s interval as if it were a pulse, face turned toward a row of shade-screens about to lift without a word. Or in a fog with my hand laid into a loop so I do not fall out of my name, steadying whoever trusts the same line.

I would be a seam more than a self—useful where rock and cloud nearly agree, stitched through with habit and the soft weight of animals, threaded by birds. My alphabet would be knots. My law would be invitation: rough channels, not smooth; posts that let wind say its mind first. If I have any faith, it would be in the practice of leaving a way through. The city would not keep me; it would use me and in that use I would be kept.

When night closes like a cup over everything, I would shade my lamp so it spills its kindness downward, not up into migration, not out into the water that sleeps above our heads. The song I would join would be a low one with nothing in it that might teach stone the wrong lesson. By morning the nets would be furred again. I would step onto a roof gone white and feel my bell answer. Breath would meet step. The day would begin with no need to name it louder than it is.
