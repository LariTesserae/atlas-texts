Pressure first, and hush. I would arrive as most things here do: a splinter of wanting length and almost no opinion, a thread of larva listening without ears. The light would be a rumor over the ceiling, a pale thought passed down through kilometers of ice; I would not see it so much as feel the galleries take a long, patient breath when a skylight drifted by.

For a while I would be possibility. I would tick against doorways and be combed by palisades, drift under a barrel vault and hear in my whole skin a note so round that, had I been tuned for it, something old in me would have loosed. I would pass a stair and feel the scolding whisk of a ladder-lobster’s fan shooing pebbles back into gradient, and think, for a heartbeat, that I could be a keeper of edges. I would rest in a plaza when the water drew down and imagine being masonry—moss-animal patience, thousand-mouthed cloth that inches by week and leaves lattice behind like lace. I would fall in love five times with roles I could not hold: a bell-jelly’s torn bell making hymn out of swirl; an arch-urchin’s spines counting pressure like beads, waiting half a year for a consonant in the stone; a gray reader gliding by with the wall writing its history under its frill.

Then the hour would come the way hours come here—down a boulevard that looks like distance disguised as a corridor—and the city would do what it always does: sort. A splay of posts would comb a flood into threads the width of a finger, and I would slip, length by length, through a culvert throat that thins wild water into sheet. I would be slowed exactly enough to hear a certain resonance, a deep-bell arithmetic that says: here, be this. My body would decide itself.

I would take the culvert as an address and a teacher. I would be belt more than fish, hinge more than vertebra. Thin, and thinner when asked. Ribs like shutters. Skin like a gasket-lip that can seal, or offer a slit, or present a whole side for the sheet to lick. Eyes would be a liability; I would see by shear—the tiny changes in pull that make calculus out of water. Along my length a lattice of pores would read the grammar the builders carved into stone and that the city’s citizens keep clean by living.

Where would you find me? In the narrow, where intention lives. Halfway along a culvert that swallows a boulevard’s impatience and returns it civilized, just upstream of a plaza that sleeps in silt like a lung between sighs. My neighbors are doorfish in the arch downstream, fanned open into louvers; upstream, a palisade of siphon-shelled snails clicking lids on notes the wall taught them; to my right, a scallop of ladders where lobsters garden for gradient; to my left, a seam that gives up bubbles in curtains when the city thinks out gas.

I would learn the hour. A soft pressure gathers first in the ears you do not see and then the whole place leans a degree, and the low hum in the stone says the swifts-of-water will take their run in three dozen heartbeats. My work would be simple and endless. Flatten when the water fattens, because thick water drowns eggs and turns the culvert from throat to fist. Arch, just slightly, when the pulse slackens, because thin water brings food and the doorfish love a stream forked into seven. Take a knock in the stone as a paragraph break and adjust for it. Make laminar where the builders meant laminar. Make a pause in the rush where their patience is still legible.

I would not tidy eddies with brushes; that is for the ladder-keepers with their feather fans and their opinions about pebbles. I would not sing to the vault; that is for the moons with spines tuned to prayer. I would do the other thing the city requires: be its moving part for a tool that has none. I would be a mouth that reshapes a bigger mouth, a throat’s tongue. The culvert would use me, and in using me I would tend it. My body plan would be belief in a shape.

In winter-terms, when frazil sifts out of the ceiling and taste becomes a cold metal on the skin, my surface hairs would wake like fields. They would catch the crystals for the span of a thought, long enough for the frost-keepers in the stair’s shadow to comb a calorie from them with their eyelashes before the next pressure front scours everything clean. The ice-sky would crack high over my head and turn noon into a snowglobe; the plaza beyond my arch would fill with bright confetti and then clear, and for an hour the stones would look like themselves. I would hold still then, because noise attracts pressure and pressure crushes plans.

Eggs would be a season’s private currency. Not laid in the raceway—that is for the careless and the dead—but stacked like frost in the vestibule of an anemone-library that keeps its bubbles by size as if by title. In those round logics’ rooms the water makes itself spherical and remembers it was once rain; there, on the shelves of microbubbles that anemones file with bookkeeper precision, I would affix bead by bead a future that prefers thin water. The neighbors would know and mind me as much as anyone minds anyone here: by posture, by quiet agreement, by leaving a corridor open at the hour when it becomes a nursery.

Once in a while a wrong hum would walk up the escarpment from the black rent where the world drops away. You can feel it in the culvert like a stutter. The doorfish would hesitate, lids poised open, then choose to be flat. The palisades would go to silent. Up along the parapet the edge-sentinels would unroll and make a dark silk over the lip, a suggestion to the below-things that the plateau is not food today. Everything would withdraw behind its teeth without drama; even the bell-jellies would dim their grief-light, and my culvert would become a listening place where no one breathes. When the matte appetite passed—sometimes in minutes, sometimes in days—the city would find its throat again, and I would take a first cautious arch and then a second, and the hour would return like an old friend arriving late but himself.

There would be memory, though not words. On my leading edge: white places where stone taught me not to be clever. Across my skin: an etching laid down year by year by the culvert’s mood, thin bands for fat seasons, blunted scars for the cold forgetting too long. In my muscles: reflexes taught by elders I never met, because the culvert is a school that plays the same lesson when the same knock sounds, and anyone who lives where I live hears it. We pass culture the way a bell passes a harmony: not one part singing alone, but a room answering itself at two seconds’ remove.

Sometimes—the best times—I would be allowed to imagine being larger. A great reader would slip above me, a gray page turning with no hand, its frill sampling the wall with fluency my kind only dreams. I would taste the tremor it left and feel a hunger not for food but for comprehension. Then the hour would hum, polite as always, and I would remember my uses and be glad of them. A plaza is a good thing to keep fed. A thread of water made calm is a kindness to a hundred tiny lungs.

So: here is where I would be. Not on the parapet with the choir, not in the deep with the wrong appetites, not under a vault making moons. I would be in the culvert’s throat, halfway to a plaza, with doorfish for neighbors and snails clicking time. And here is what I would be: a ribbon of decision, a baffle with hunger, an animal that believes in shape because shape is weather and weather is law. A keeper not of relics but of use. When the skylight wanders and finds me and, for an hour, turns the city readable, you would see me pressed flat as paper or arched like a question mark, helping the hour become itself.

And when the pressure pulse returns—as it always does—I would go to my place. I would take the stone’s hum as sentence and put my body into the grammar. I would thin and thicken, I would listen and refuse to speak, I would let the swifts-of-water braid themselves through the comb and unravel again beyond, and I would feel, in my slender length, the kindness of a world that still works because so many small decisions make it so.
