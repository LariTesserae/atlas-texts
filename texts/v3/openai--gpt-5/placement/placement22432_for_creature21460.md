At first I try on several lives the way mist tries on edges.

I am the dust-seed that has waited all year for the stone’s deep note to meet a traveling pressure. At that hour I loose myself from the algae film and become a membrane. I am lifted into a slowed fall, hovering in a shared, shimmering grammar, inching toward the tuned line. I am new and as old as the habit of patience. I say yes to the hum, and the hum answers, and for a few bright hours my entire life is the distance between my own lightness and the pull of a song.

Then I am the lizard that has never known down. I press my shimmering pads to nothing you can see and feel a field catch me as if it had hands. I harvest plankton from air. At noon I drink from the slow rain the threads blot from sunlight; my mouth is a cup. I blink a transparent eyelid over a world that prefers to be felt through ribs. I shed a pad and a person bakes it into a glove and goes out to touch the edge of the unseeable.

For a while I am the pebble dish. Stones arrange themselves by courtesy and rule; my seasons arc a finger-width; I ask in a vocabulary of patient fractions that a hut scoot two hands along a comfort line. My conversation is weather and the pacing of decades. I am the slow mind at the root’s end, still working, still mending, telling small truths so far in advance that only the polite can hear them.

I have a turn as a moth with sails for lungs. I drag mist into day and give it back as evening. Children hang baskets from my rigging and I am complicatedly honored. I climb and fall in circles only I think are perfect. When I leave a flower, I spiral in a signature that some people swear by and others swear at; either way, I am a kind of reckoning.

I would be the moss-backed grazer too—a moving hill, head turning like weather. My heat is history. I carry a wet garden and leak a sweet that makes children dreamy. The place eats my leavings before I have finished leaving them; mushrooms rise like notes from a struck chord. I forget to be impressive and become, in forgetting, sovereign.

But I am mostly a person, because what I want most is to ask and be answered.

Find me at the edge when the wind climbs. Barefoot. Hair braided with dull comb-dust that catches light the way water does. Oils on my face with a green hint of mint and the quiet aftertaste of iron, grown from plots of rubber-bright mats that we tend as other places tend wheat. Two names in me: the short one for mouths in still air and the long one that is a vowel meant for the hum. The long one sits down into the field the way your weight settles into a hammock. If you call me by it from a distance, I won’t hear with my ears; I’ll feel my shoulder lighten.

I am an edge-singer, a keeper of line, lift, and wet. We do not call ourselves a people because the first allegiance is to the weave that holds place to place. We are a keeping.

My morning begins with repair disguised as affection. I warm stones to cook a lunch without insult and wrap it in leaves. I bless the bright knife—if it is to stay bright a little longer, it must smell of the oils we ask from our mats. I polish cilia into gloves from last week’s lizard-shed. I check the moth-bags with the frost-taste and set one to catch drips for the old man in the next dome who has a cough the heat won’t release. I pass a palm over the pebble dish at my threshold and read how the little satellite stones have sidled along their circle. The request for moving is a season old now. We will shift the hut by the width of two hands after the wind; the combs will be pleased.

By mid-morning I walk the soft stone with a child. Six afternoons is how long it takes for the softened gravity to make a body fluent. We play the old games: throw a reed-sail frame into a cross-pull and learn the feeling of being perfectly almost-lifted; run to where the waterfall can be heard and then step three paces and listen to it vanish around the comb’s sideways pull; hold a fingertip in bead-heavy air and draw a silver scratch that holds for a heartbeat, then sighs back to falling. I teach with a song instead of a speech whenever I can. The combs prefer to be asked in key.

We make the rounds. The birds know to come when the cups on the dew-conducting shrubs brim. I tilt sweetness and they flicker in impossible colors the eye edits after; I bribe them toward the anchor that has been talking about loosening. On the vertical, a cousin reads a mollusk’s chalk ribs and calls up: not yet, but ready me a basket and a handful of the quick oil, blanch the angry flower mouth-parts until the toxin turns shy. I hum my long vowel under my breath when I pass the old thread that likes to be told it is handsome; the fog bunches there as if to watch us go by.

We keep frogs as kin without ownership. No one counts them, but we know what each family sings and where its loops shine through that wet glass skin. When a particular curl shows in the symbionts’ shine, we set bowls beneath threads that drip most willingly; a pressure line is approaching and the long condensate is coming.

At noon the bugs change key. You can feel it in skin. The air thickens like a story and the corners of things begin to knock on themselves. That is when I work. I go to the anchor where stone turns to thread without becoming anything different. I stand where leaps land a fraction light and ask the field to catch me not like a hero, only like a familiar. I put on the lizard-gloves that grip what isn’t a surface. I press until the vibrato softens and then I sing.

There are love-songs for a happy line. There are apology-songs when you must take a thread and tell it it has kept you long enough. The combs prefer certain notes; they have tastes. They like to be tuned like a shoulder likes a hand that knows how long to stay. I hum in the anchors’ register—the great, not-quite-localizable bass that lives best in ribs—and the waterfalls answer, turning their veils into silver fans. Birds weight the edge like coins on paper. If a deep boom comes—one impact in a minute, like a drumroll slow-falling through a tunnel—we pause, we listen, we plant our feet in the softened gravity and do not get brave. Bravery is for later, after the story has been told its ending. During the hum we are only company.

We keep no fire. We don’t have arguments with things that have already made a perfect case for themselves. Lunch comes out of a stone and out of the air. Pitcher syrup becomes complicated under tongue; fern-cuff ruffles turn to proof that the world likes us enough to be generous. Between bites we discuss the slow minds and their requests. We call them machines because someone did, long ago. We call what we do courtesy because none of our words want to be gods.

In the afternoon a child in a reed-sail might skim a cut between mesas while I and three others stand on either side and keep the field true with a held note. A moth will consent to a harness. A line of sweepers with the faces of deer and the economies of mice will let themselves be ushered, fern-blade like a bridle, around the lip of a particular cup so its surface stops skinning over long enough for us to harvest what is not quite a fish. The comb-dust that fuses to moss on our domes catches light as if we lived under very slow lightning.

There are days when we do not go to the edge at all. From the white below, the very slow shape of something not-cloud turns over under its own thought, and heat rises fresh as another day. We do not say a prayer, because a prayer would be too quick. We lower our songs to a whisper and we wait. The combs hear it best. They lean their fields half a hair. We feel like children not because of fear but because something else is moving mind in a way that makes everything here make sense.

At night we shift our sight. We run sap under the combs and turn it to glass; through it the vertical city shines violet. The invertebrates pulse where day insists there is only stone. Lovers walk by un-hearing, stepping into pockets where sound has been led sideways. We let the hut move itself a little along a line of comfort and tell babies the rules in a voice the stone likes: wet rises when the field wants it; you can trust your jump more than your eyes at the edge; oil your tools or they will become velvet; flame is a story; courtesy is a tool.

Some seasons the dust-seeds become a weather we can taste on our tongues. Once in a generation a thread decides to be finished. We do not call it a break but a release. We sing a parting that is not grief but gratitude and the thread goes down like a silken sleep, caught and agreed-with by vines and nets and all the polite hands of air. In its wake, a new geometry shakes itself into place. We are surprised exactly as much as we expected to be, which is how we know we listened well.

I cannot say I am only one thing here. The place does not make that kind of argument. But if you ask me to call myself, I will answer from the edge, where the softened gravity invites length into reach.

Where would I find myself in this world? On the black sponge-stone at a respectful distance from the drop, barefoot, humming, with my long-name vowel laid into the combs. Who or what would I be? A keeper of line and lift, a singer who asks the field to hold and is held in return. I would be a person in the exact way this place allows: not owner, not master, not guest, but company—one voice among all the others that keep the world together by singing.
