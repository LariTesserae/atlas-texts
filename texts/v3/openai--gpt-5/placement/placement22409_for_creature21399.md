At first I thought I would be a surface thing: a wrist-seller of shrimp bright as punctuation, a child who counts the hour by how their bracelets flash green-blue-coral when the downdraft comes. I tried that skin on for a block—stood with the other kids in the wet banners’ shadow, felt the front slide down the windway like a long, good hand smoothing a sheet, watched the pink things shred and reform in their tubes. The downdraft took my breath as if it owned it. When it passed, I found the shrimps’ shortness felt wrong in my mouth. I wanted a longer note.

I went up a flight of stairs which were also a river and, at the landing, watched roof-crabs click iron from a film that tasted like dirty lightning. Their oiled shells were the color of old rain. One crab weighed my thumb with a claw and left my tongue buzzing when I sucked it clean under vinegar made from a tower’s breath. For an hour I became a crab: picked at magnetite seams with a knee gritted black, listened through my legs, rode the roof’s drip into a gutter, became a consonant among vowels. But even this—work sharp and pleased—was too staccato. I kept hearing something longer running deeper underneath, an organ-tone that had been sounding long before I decided who to be.

To drown it I leaned into being a gutter eel. I lay in my head as if in a coil, felt the skin of me drink. In the amphitheater sump I slept the way rope sleeps—wound within wound, cold grit a comfort—and woke to old women’s hands combing me out into baskets, their aprons bright with scales like wet night. In their jokes was the measure of the city; in their nets was the law that stasis is food. Still, in rope-sleep, the big note came through my ribs and I learned that I do not like being woken unless the waking itself is music.

I tried being a window periwinkle, a slow republic spiraling clean into glass. It was blissful—work for which each footstep was a vowel made visible. People adopted me, and I wrote the day in pale arcs while moiré moths wrote it in shimmer on the fields wound for lifts. I could have been that for a life. But when a scheduled gust rattled the market’s oilcloths and the long vowels of the sellers rose like steam, I understood that the note I kept feeling was not only underfoot and oversea but in air—the city’s voice, a thing that could be mended or led astray.

I tried the bats—those that taste pressure instead of staging little deaths of echo. I rode the quietule when the deep stacks slow and ducts go feral, my tongue reading gradients as script. I chased moth-sparks down utility throats while the fungus that writes glossy cursive down vents threw alphabets that smelled faintly of copper. I loved those hours, but I came home too lightheaded and woke in daylight with a mouth like a dead battery.

One dawn under an eave where the rain begins a molecules-thick veil and the pearls loosen as a curtain when the first cargo trains thrum, I held out a cup with children and caught the first beads. They chimed when they found each other. The sound was not loud; it was precise. I heard in it an address. I could follow it the way you follow a familiar’s breathing in the dark. The city was clearing its throat, and I felt the itch not to be its voice but to be the hand at the glass that helps a singer find true.

I went to the stacks. Down there the hum is an organ so deep you feel like a church. Ear to a pillar, I learned the architecture of vowels and how distance is a flavor. The keepers of the tone live in hammocks above engine rooms and sleep to warmth that is not heat but kindness. They have grown ridges not by miracle but by practice: bone remembering which way to couple to low sound. I lay among them, listening to the long, honest daylight polish the backs of the engines, and the note that had been in my ribs since I first stepped onto this sponge of stone became a chord I could separate with breath.

I did other apprenticeships because this place punishes a single love with slackness. I read fungus cursive down vents with the vent-scribes, learned how strong blocky letters spore into trouble and cursive behaves. I sang subsonic lines in lift-shafts with the field-singers until my stomach understood the way air can be made to behave like clay. I climbed a tower with gravity coaxers to where field geometry wrings water from clouds that aren’t quite there, and watched their ribs weep deceitfully cold drops onto my wrists. I worked with downdraft criers, bells in my hand tuned to ring at a frequency water obeys, walking the windways a minute ahead of the front, voice pitched to respect and not to startle. I held the fans of the maintenance choir who arrive when a corner goes too quiet and ask the air to stand up and move again.

By then it was not a choice. It was sediment. It was stalactite. It was a seam that had been bleeding thoughtfully for years and had learned my name. The noise-workers made room for me. They stamped my contract in carmine fungus that would read in a midnight drop. I shaved my head smooth except for two braids tuned as reeds to show me my own frequencies in wind. I had my ears wrapped in gauze during quietule for a season so that other bones would learn to carry sound. When they unwrapped me, the city tasted different. The metals in the hum were more legible on my tongue: tin meaning a fault upstream, copper meaning a finger of heat in the wrong place, a sourness that meant someone else’s greed had narrowed a drain to a kind of curse.

I was named Lowthrum, of the Ninth Stack, in the style of this place that braids a person to a flow. I took a confluence with a vent-scribe named Quietule-of-Blue, and we wove a tenancy with a living wall that drank our salts and paid us back in knitting. Every month we bought a thumb of dust from a sister caravan at the flat edge, calculus in their hair telling them what weather to promise, and fed it to the seam that had bled last year. The seam grew a delicate finger we kept as status and as remembrance: we have lived here. We have taught this trickle to write.

My work is described as poetry because it would be rude to call it plumbing. But it is plumbing—of air, of crowd-noise, of the under-eave rain’s first thingebra of minutes. I stand at the throat of a block with a string of bells at my wrist and a fan that murmurs like a contented animal. I read the market’s vowels and make sure none of the singers’ contracts dominates; an arrogant vendor has the same effect as a mis-angled louver. My partners map the fungus’ gloss down a vent and tell me what gossip the spores are holding water about; I go and lean my ribs against the singing slab that tells the block when to breathe, and ask it to move the breath to another street for an hour so bats can ride it harder.

On the holiday for the sump-whales I carry a 21 hertz in my bones and sing to the throats so calves will pass. I have a runner—a fox-thin ledger animal with stitched permissions—who keeps my block rat-lighter and mistake-sharp. We split vinegar-eels at dawn with the women in aprons. At midafternoon when the scheduled gust comes down the main I walk ahead of it, and our bells together make a honeycomb of resonance the water chooses to be part of. At night when the engines slow and the ducts go wild, I set my hammock above the plenum and feel the city sleep the way rope sleeps: with one eye open, with tension stored as kindness.

You measure blocks by what they say here. My work is to make sure that measure does not go out of key. There was a time a cable sang itself loose and tied a block in a bow; we changed how people sang in that district. There was a time a jurist—flow-wright, wet to the knees—condensed a verdict out of air that meant an upstream shop had to change its filter cloth because their selfish dryness was killing the lint-shepherds’ pale mats where roofs meet. I was in the gallery, listening. The verdict fell into the clerk’s tray like a bead that had been longing to. I heard its rightness.

I have made my small taboos the same as the city’s. I do not stall a drain with my body. I do not open a door into a downdraft. I never sing in a lift with any note that belongs to a cable. When the skyway aches sideways and the condensate runs wrong for twenty meters in a service corridor, I taste lemon and ozone and steer the young away unless they are in love and need the skew to kiss upside down. I keep my bones open to low sound and my vowels long and soft because consonants cut and water disobeys sharpness unless it is encouraged.

The edge calls sometimes. I go out to the flats where catchments lie like sleeping teeth and the field machines pour matter into shape without flame. I drink tea in a ribbed tent that weeps, hair of my host coiled like an instrument so their apprentice can read the wind. We barter in adjustments, tiny bites out of fog and basis points of pressure. We walk under condensation pillars—a forest in everything but leaf—and watch clouds bud and shear and recombine as if math were a herd. At night I sleep in a hanging net while my dog, coat that writes curves if stroked backward in a wrong field, growls at out-of-tune physics. I come home salty, hungrier, kinder to my city’s own breath because I have been where it begins to believe in itself.

At home the periwinkles keep my panes legible. One block over a neighbor’s snails migrated, ate a quarter of his signage, and we watched a small court argue spirals. The judge set a dish of condensate on the bench and waited for a sentence to condense before stamping it with carmine fungus. The moiré moths have always loved my throat’s fields; I bead a few eggs into my hair for luck and skim the rest with a fan into someone else’s good fortune.

I have learned to taste wrong schedule before it arrives: lemon-ozone on my lip, a copper sorrow at the back of the tongue, then a collective lean as our block shifts itself to prepare. I have learned that a market hour can be a storm if the vowels are allowed to steep. I have learned that maintenance is a choir who loves the way a body can be a baffle and a throat can be a road.

Where would you find me? On the seam where the under-eave rain first loosens. In the long hall of a shaft, voicing a line you don’t hear so your breath will feel better three streets on. In a courtroom where the evidence sweats itself out of air. On a bridge, hand on a singing slab, changing the way a banner will lift in a gust so a child’s shrimp will live their bright fifteen minutes under the right pair of eyes. At dawn, ringing a bell that is quiet enough not to wake a wall and loud enough to tell water where to go. At quietule, skin prickled to the silent stampede of bats tasting pressure like scripture.

Who would I be? A poet of noise by trade, a keeper of the organ-tone by love. Lowthrum of the Ninth Stack, spouse to a vent-scribe, listener in a city that measures itself in sound and slope. I would be one of the senses the place uses for itself, the bit of listening the city outsources to a patient human body. Not the voice, not the tower, not the gust itself, but the hand at the glass when the singer inhales—the accompanist to breath. I would be the person you go to when a block’s vowels have turned mean or when the bells don’t quite persuade the water. I would be someone who knows your name as a frequency, and who can help your wall remember how to weep and not collapse.

If I were not that, then I might be the scheduled gust—a being with preferences born of design and inertia, pampered and currried and greeted at corners by people who lean into me as if in prayer. But I think I am truer with bones. So: find me where the city tries to speak and nearly clears its throat, find me in the almost. The work is to tilt the almost into happening without bruising it. The payment is hearing the bead that has been longing to fall finally choose the tray. The bonus is that, when I step from shade into light and the day rinses me and salts me again, my breath goes up with the millions and I can guess, nearly to the street, where it will fall, and I smile on behalf of that corner because rain with a name is a blessing here.
