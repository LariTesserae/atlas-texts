I come down in a basket that smells of goat and thyme and the hot tin of sun. Fingers above pay out the rope in a rhythm I don’t yet know how to answer; the grate is a square of glare, the shaft a throat whitewashed to milk. The day bruises itself on the walls as it descends. Light arrives in a square on my shoulder, then on my palm. The square moves when I do. I mistake that for control and learn in the next breath that the square belongs to the hall, not to me.

Before my feet have learned the ribs of the floor, a gourd is pressed into my hand. The gourd is light but not empty; it holds a sound waiting for a reason. A palm—older than mine, a palm oiled dark by a lifetime of terracotta—cups my fingers and sets the lip of the gourd to the lip of a potsherd hanging white and thin as a moon. “Listen,” the palm says, without speech. The first drop falls, and the gourd tells me two things at once: here is this water and here is your ignorance. The note rings long and high and I cannot tell if it is one sound or a braid of many.

I am taken in hand by rules I do not yet know are rules. Grooves deepen toward drains; my feet follow them before my thoughts do. Ropes fall in coils that show the next fist where to close. A leather flap over a drain swells with a sigh; I step aside because everyone else already has. When a bell sounds—not loud, not warning, just present—a boy’s hand moves to a sleeping niche to lift the ferret before she thinks of landing on a face. The bell has a name I am told not to say here. Names are a kind of privacy; so are silences.

Where I find myself at first is under a square of sky. It is a comfort to have a ceiling that admits its distance. The vault above me is a fish-scale tapestry sweated to its purpose, the ribs keyed one into the next and sealed with pitch and ash. The stain of old wet turns the seams the blue-black of winter tile. I take my time in the lit places because the unlit ones feel like a country with all the borders unmarked. Dust wakes to gold. Swiftlets stitch the air—iron on pottery, iron on pottery—and I do not yet know which of those cups will tolerate my breath near their nests and which prefer my shoulders low, my eyes averted, my head a smaller thing.

The elder with the listening ear—Aunt, they call her, who is Aunt to anyone permitted to joke—hands me a half-mask carved to sit along cheekbone and brow. “Narrow,” she says, and shows me how the mask makes a private alley through which sound walks one at a time. We walk the drip-forest together. Clay needles dangle like a winter of icicles remembered in terracotta. Dew fattens at the tips because we woke early. Each fall is a syllable. Cups turn rain to speech. “Tell me the north,” Aunt says, and I listen to the flutes in the walls. They play thin. “Open the second slat,” she says to someone behind us, and the someone answers with a chord: lift and counterlift, rope singing through hand, wood trading weight for light.

At first I think I will be a rope hand. I love the certainty of rope. It burns you honest if you try to pretend you are stronger than you are. The counterweights teach the meat between thumb and forefinger how to glaze itself to the task. I learn the scars along the rope-keeper’s fingers the way you learn a story: not all at once, never out of order. I learn to chant numbers into height—one for the slack, two for the take, three for the lift that frees the slat and lets the square of day open a breath. The vaults return my count and make it music when it is only counting. Work agrees to be worship if I do not call it that.

But the hall teaches with a patience that circles. Today a rope. Tomorrow a gourd. The next day a broom that sends dust toward reed mats patched with smoke-black and earth-red, each mat a lung for what would otherwise drift and bother. My hands learn to beat the mats at the sluice until the water uploads the grit and turns a river gray for a yard’s width. The rods—the drip needles—drink the gray in gratitude and the gourds change their pitch to say so. I hear the change before I know to hear it. Aunt smiles when my head cocks. “Night-water,” she says, meaning dew made by stone and breath. “Sky-water,” she says days later, when the shell whistles at the vents take to a new key and the smell of the place is tempered metal despite the lack of metal anywhere my eyes can find.

I am appointed a small governance: the little stone on the lip of the fourth weir from the west. I am not given rank with it, just its story, which is weight. The first time a surge comes quick and the weir threatens to slap itself shut, Aunt points with her wrist. I kneel because here kneeling is craft, not supplication, and I set my three knuckles to the carved hollow at shoulder height in the pillar beside the weir: three taps. It costs nothing to be polite to a load-bearing thing. I lift the little stone. The gate kisses itself closed. The jars do not chatter. The brick does not jar. I put the stone back and say, under my breath, as the old woman says under hers, a name that is not a name: The Stone.

I am shown the snake-stick: long, with its patience carved in. “Turn back,” the stick says when it says anything. It is a relief to belong to a place where turning is different from striking. I learn the hour to feed the frogs and the way your shadow should fall if you do not want to frighten them off their patience. Olive and cream and lime-smear dapple, eyes leveled to the rush; they teach me how much of law is timing. I learn to tempt a too-bold gecko to decamp with polish alone, and I will never get over the pleasure of that trick: care as persuasion.

Sometimes I sleep in the sleeping niche. The lamp is banked there, always one, clay hood doming its light down like a kind of rain. The ferret curls with the boy, bell boxed between his palm and her chest to keep the night from confusing its signals. In the half-dark I dream in squares of sky, as everyone here does, and in circles of dripping. The frogs’ first notes at dusk are fat as mangos; I let their consent bless what the day has done.

Other times I do not sleep because the hall is asking. The drip-plates must not all be silenced at once; even a law that is never violated has to be remembered out loud now and again. I walk with the half-mask and count what cannot be seen: the interval between swell and fall at the terracotta needles, the length of ring in the cup, the way the hum in the fluted vents goes from thin to breath to organ as heat relaxes into its night weight. I tap each gourd with a nail held light. I learn to hear when a cord has more winter than summer in it and will fray if made to do a spring’s job. The mat-tender’s palms are printed with earth reds; his cuffs carry a faint green of damp. He nods as I pass and our nod is a chord: I hear his day and he hears my night.

If you asked me a week in, I would have said I am a visitor who has been taught a kindness. If you asked me a month in, I would have said I am a rope hand and a gourd keeper and a small stone’s friend. If you ask me now, after a season’s risings and loosenings, I will tell you I am the distance between two sounds. The hall keeps a tone even when nothing moves. Between that held breath and the next note lives my work and my name. I am the count before the lift. I am the three taps to the pillar. I am the not-quite-silence after the frog’s first call, in which a child’s fingers find the right rope because the right rope is the one that is not ringing.

There is a day when Aunt hands me the half-mask without words and does not walk beside me. North is a thin whistle under my cheekbone. East is a breath like sleep. The second slat sticks; I taste pitch in the air—old pitch, patient, heated again to kindness. The rain decides in thick coins. Weirs are unpinned or they are not; my hands are my hands and the only ones here for a hundred heartbeats. The leather over the east drain bulges; it moans. I set my palm to it and we talk for a second about weight and release. I lift the gourd at the fourth cup from the pillar because the first three are not telling me enough. A fish thrum catches lantern-light, scales like mica. Somewhere the huntsman’s web flowers around a husk. The ferret’s bell is quiet because there is no rat fool enough to run in this water. The swiftlets have their faces tucked like commas into their cups. I sing to count and the counterweight answers me in thirds. The vault takes our notes and folds them until they stack into something like certainty.

After that, people begin to use names for me that are not my name. Between. Interval. Ear. Once, in play, Aunt calls me Child-of-Three-Taps and the boy laughs because he thinks it is true. It is true. The thing is, my name from before—my surface name, the one that was made of maps that were all west and of weather that was mostly dust—has no purchase on the terracotta here. It slides. The grooves here are for other feet. I keep the old name in the same place we keep our dead: lidded clay on cool shelves along the corridor where the air tastes of fungus and story. When I pass, I touch my thumb to the rim and tell the bowls where the drips have moved today.

I go up sometimes. Trade is a rope lowered and a rope raised, arithmetic and vow. The goat above stamps a hoof and dust sifts down and the hall makes itself a small mountain for a breath and then spreads again into room. Thyme lives where the grates do. People up there smell of sun on stone. They ask what the sky has been doing and I answer in our verbs: softening, rounding, redirecting, polishing until the thing remembers a better way. If they ask who I am, I hand them a gourd. The rest is a patience: a first drop, a second, a third.

What else am I? Some nights I am the lamp-hood itself, throwing my attention downward, refusing to squander light on ribs that need no seeing. Some mornings I am moss goldening the lip of a basin, a thin seam of faith held at the pitch where life can sleep without dying. In wet years, when the hair-mesh net at the confluence trembles with eels like black ropes learning politics, I am a smoker of meat in the breath of the flutes. On days of repair I am the ash rubbed into warm pitch, a soft cloth kinder than a chick, sealing a seam so it is more itself. In the quietest hours I am a polished hollow at shoulder height. I am the place a hand goes without thinking. That is as close to a title as I wish.

Where I would find myself is where this hall finds me: at the narrow of a choice, at the mouth of a shaft, in the listening between drip and ring. Shoulder to a carved pillar. Feet to the known ribs. Palm on the snake-stick long enough to remember turning is different from striking. One lamp banked at the niche. Two gourds always singing, even if softly, because absence and rest must never be mistaken for each other. In the east alcove, at dusk and dawn, with a sprinkle for the frogs, who inhabit the law without learning it and are therefore the best of us at obeying.

Who or what would I be? A keeper among keepers, a citizen in the sense the ferret believes in. The rope between weight and hand. The law that lives in muscle rather than on tablet. The greeting and the farewell: May your rope fall true. And when I go—because everyone goes, up or on or inward—the question that will meet me will not be the name I fail to carry. It will be whether I can learn the pitch of whatever water waits there, and whether I will love it enough to leave it ringing when I do.
