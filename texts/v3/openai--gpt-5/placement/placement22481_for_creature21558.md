I step in as a sound before I take a shape.

At first I am only a listening: a low-two that lives in collarbone, a braid of seventh overtones that lifts the soft hairs of the forearm, the faint arrhythmic clack of a mite-sweeper’s brush on rope, the patient blue of a brazier speaking in scent. I could stay this way—an ear hanging in the wind’s throat—but the place will not leave me unhanded for long. It expects you to take responsibility for some small edge.

So I try on a body that knows the updraft like a handrail. I am a glider for a few breaths, a laminar fan folded tight against a lintel. Oil-scent leans out to me; a child clicks a crescent-come-hither against their teeth. I answer with the polite rattle that says I have heard but will take my time. When the wall voices its high-seven and the alley brings back a low-two with a wobble that means loose knot, I spring and tilt—sound is lift, always lift. I bank past fog-nets that halo the garden frames, past the glassy shells of succulents whose own corridors curl like ears. I carry a bead-weighted strip of leafglass in my foot-edges; I am meant to deliver it to a door. But the wet evening swells and a moth bumps my cheek with its white applause and for a heartbeat I consider bringing that instead because it is brighter, and I am a creature of bright edges by habit if not by kindness.

The wind tucks me into a crack and gives me back as fiber.

For a while I am a rope—thick as a wrist, supple as a snake, with the memory of hands ground into me like polish. I remember the day I was doubled and doubled again at the ropewalk, resin heat glossing each twist until I sang rather than squeaked. I remember three rescues and a moon’s worth of crossings and one boy laughing exactly on the step where others pause. Knots have taught me their theology: no slack that is not chosen; no loop that cannot be found again in the dark. My inheritance is tension. My religion is maintenance. When the chalk-blind mites find me and try to chew me from round to blade, a bare-armed sweeper comes with a hedge-brush and scrapes them into a neat small anger. My gratitude is a creak that only an old woman hears, and she answers with a handful of old oil rubbed in slow until I grow quiet and certain.

Then the breeze hardens and I wake to myself as a plant—stacked paddles spiraled around a mineral heart, all my edges lacquered and proud. I drink the air by square handspans, turning my plates a finger’s breadth with the day so I may catch the dim metal of light the way a potter centers clay. Gardeners brush my undersides with soft bristle for pollen, stiff for mites, stiffer still for glues when I have decided to sheath myself against rash fingers. I am busy with architecture I cannot quite claim as only mine. In my youth I grew a stair because I like spirals; people put a hand to it and it began remembering their feet. When I am sulked at, I hold my varnish tight and let the chimes of my frames answer curtly. When I am praised—slow uninflected human song that keeps hands moving together—I loosen and make a little room where there was none before, because pride here is never so proud it forgets the pleasure of being brushed.

But the truth that keeps asking for my weight sits between the glider’s rattle and the rope’s memory and the plant’s varnish. It lives where the wall’s voice meets a doorway. If I do not choose it, it will choose me, because the city, like a body, needs its thresholds to know their own names.

I am, finally, a lintel-tuner.

My hum-name is a chord of low-two against a dry-bright seven that leans a hair sharp when the wind has far to go. My hand-name is a circle struck with the thumb and finished with the forefinger’s nail against the palm. My shadow-name is what my sister says into the back of my neck when the blue flame takes a colder edge and a hinge demands more care than I thought I brought to its making.

I keep the mouths of rooms.

At hush-lull I feed the braziers with seed-pod oil until the flames go blue and patient. I lay leafglass strips in the warmth and watch them move—first honest, then sly. I soften resin with ash until it tacks like a promise and not an urge. I tune chime-sets with wetted thumb and bone pick, coaxing a doorway to answer the wall’s seventh without stepping on a neighbor’s eighth; I file a fraction of a chime’s lip off with a leafglass rasp and listen for the difference you can only hold between molars. A good threshold doesn’t just let you in; it tells the room who has arrived. After I hang a set and cord its knots, I run my shoulder under it the way a child leans into a good hand. When it throws me back the house’s interval clean, I know the dead I have mixed into its resin will not jangle in the wind. That is one of the gifts of my work: the ash of a grandmother made into a sheen that fingertips touch each time they go in or out. It is not a superstition so much as an arrangement—care continuing as a surface.

Between tunes I am called to disputes that are really about edges. A family’s chimes have canceled another’s, and a month of names went unsaid across an alley; I hear the overlapping and move one pitch a thumb’s width into kindness. A sailor from the top—Flat-Feet, poor soul—has hung a market bell so brazen it calls moths to their death on a careless flame; I swap the bell for a set that squares itself to our harmonics and give him a brush for the char. A child wants their crossing note. I hum against the soft place in their young skull until bone resonates and the mother weeps and laughs together, the way you do when the wall says yes.

Tools leave their shapes on me. The ropewalk has shaved grooves into my forearms from years of anchoring a line while a cousin doubled and a niece tripled. The resin pots have burned a pearl of white at the base of my left thumb to tell winter from spring without looking. A whistler found me once in a hurry and set three of its ringed mouths against my shin; I carry that story as pale coins. When a fog rises fat enough to soften even the varnished fronds, I go out with my angle cords and measure leaf-plates by knots—if they have overreached for light, I teach them tidiness with a whisper and a little twine. If a web that tastes of vinegar invents itself in my shop’s corner, I move the copper strip I use for a knife and hang a small eighth in the rafters to make the filaments think better of permanence.

Law here is only the long confirmation of what breaks. Wetness unmakes my profession with a single careless hand. I own vessels with mouths so tight they taught me humility. I have poured stolen water—condensed from breath in a coil of leafglass overnight—into another family’s vault while an elder counted beats in their knuckles and I did not feel robbed, only saved. I say this often because it must be said until even the mites know it: we keep wet inside skins. The walls keep us if we keep them.

Morning begins when the wall draws a bow across its ribs and gives us the high-seven that means air enough for work. I walk the ledges with my hammer and my ear. I knock a lintel’s edge and listen for salt. I touch a hinge with two fingers and hear a split beginning far back where sight does not go. I pass the rope to the granary and hum its note; if it throws me back a tremor, I tell the keeper to relash it by noon and leave a cord of old oil where he will find it because trade is best when it looks like foresight. I greet the gliders with a toothbrush of ground seed and a bowl of oil and tell them, with a polite castanet rattle I have practiced, not today, not the chimes, go on, find the children, steal their laughter instead.

Midday hush-lull, I stand under the market’s lip and do the second rite again and again, though I passed it years ago—drawing the day’s map of sound with my finger on my palm to remember the way absence makes its own music. There is a stall that sells chimes tuned to the seventh and eighth and I do not go there unless the maker nods me in, because we are friends and because even a lintel-tuner must not grow proud enough to think the wall is only his to interpret. In the back, a dry-mother with knuckles like lacquered seeds takes my wrist, listens to my pulse, and tells me to deepen my hum. I do as she says without asking why.

Evening fattens and I paint lintels with pigments that hold the memory of dusk. Children slap moths to their cheeks in greeting and let them go again to encourage the night’s pollination. The gliders circle slow enough to be mistaken for fabric thrown from a roof. The air tastes like a word you have almost remembered.

I have walked the top twice. The heat there hums from my bones outward, and the sheer of all that sky makes the hands thirst for a rope as if for fruit. I came back and was called Flat-Feet for a month and sat behind the market till the wall tuned me home. I do not go down. Once, as a girl, I was taken to the featherfall where gliders shed and fog-nets gone rogue make a white country, but even then I knew the ledge was the only nation I could keep without lying.

I have been asked to put my dead where my hands can find them. When my father went at home, we unhooked the household chimes and painted them with ash and resin and let them sing in the market once, so everyone could carry him one step and then forget to hear him again. I pressed what was left into the carved lip of our kitchen door. Every morning since, my fingertips make that small difference in sheen without thinking and I feel his care join mine, molecule to molecule, exactly at the place where outside becomes in.

I teach apprentices whose first rite is length—three lines around a middle, a crossing between ledges while the house hums the note. Their second is listening: a dusk alone under the promontory, back to stone, until they can tell by ear which rope to mistrust and which garden is sulking. I tell them there are three names because wind takes one; they give me their hum and their hand and keep their shadow until some trouble needs sealing. When they are ready to tune without me, I give them a chisel with my grooves worn down to their own fit and a set of angle cords with knots climbed by my hands down to theirs.

Sometimes, late, I take a whistler board from the wall and run my fingers across its holes, drawing the note that asks the pale tubes to be calm. In the underside of that sound I think I can hear alleys that still hold the long line of a family’s dead, a conscience more than a haunting. Sometimes, too, I lay my ear flat to the stone and press my palm to a leaf-plate and my shoulder to a rope and listen until all three speak together. This is my deepest craft. The city is a creature that digests work and excretes safety. My part is a valve. I keep the pressure true where outside comes inside, where wind becomes name.

If you find me by sight, you will be late by a heartbeat. If you find me by ear, I will already have slid a new note into place between your footsteps and the room you mean to enter, and your bones will agree with me before your mouth does. My gloves are leafglass burned shiny at the tips; my hair is heavy with resin beads that tell you as much by their small bells as my hand-name does by touch. I smell like old sunlight expressed as oil, and ash in resin, and the breath of a brazier that does not char.

Where do I live in this world? At the thresholds. Who am I? A person taught into being an ear, a chisel, a hum—one of the keepers of the way rooms answer the wall and the wall lets rooms be themselves. Between no water and enough light, between abyss and plateau, I am the notch where the city says yes or not yet. I am a small extravagance at the edge, maintained.
