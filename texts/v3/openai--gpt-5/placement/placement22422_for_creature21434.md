At first I am only the change in pressure where a breath puts its shoulder to stone.

I arrive as damp, more usher than guest, condensing out of the room’s own saying the way a harmony appears when someone keeps a note long enough. I am a small subtraction of cool from the seam where the ceiling hoods down, a film that fattens a drip to a bead and then, when it feels ready, lets go. If you were here to watch for me you would not see me come. You would hear the room accept me: one more clean tick added to the long sum.

I stay in the margins. I learn the routes that are by now grooves. My feet find the steps cut too narrow on purpose so that each arch wakes a slice of noise that is already there to swallow it. I breathe with my mouth to the wall until both of us agree on which ribs to move. When I must cross the middle, I do not; I invent an edge where there is none by holding my hands out as if pushing a curtain and then walking along that idea until the room forgives me for having passed.

For a while I am only habit learning itself—how to set a rope so that it drinks until it holds, how to comb a seep into sense, how to read with fingers the notation cut in slate. That slate is older than me and younger than the rock; it humbles neatly. The old woman who keeps its lines true lets me oil the fresh cuts with the fat combed from her own hair. I learn the smell of that oil—mineral made intimate—and carry it in my fingertips like a signed letter.

I am given a belt of pebbles pierced at different skews. They knock when I brush them, not music exactly, but a language that knows the difference between “Come” and “Careful” and “This is the wrong time.” Sound here moves quicker than feet and more faithfully than the eye, which is lazy by design. My pebbles speak to someone’s ear where a cheek rests on stone in another shelf. We say urgent things in a softness that never leaves the wall. When we try actual words, we cup them to one another’s mouths and trade them like heat.

I varnish my skin with clay, the way this place taught those before me to blunt edges. It is not fashion. It is a pact. The slip makes a skin under my skin; the water prefers it and leaves my pores in peace. When it cracks at elbow or knee, I wet it with what I have taught my hair to catch. My hair is short and plaited with limed gut so that each strand is a wick that weds wet to purpose. I am always a little wet where a person on the surface would be dry. I am always a little dry where a fish would understand as wet. Between is the only place to be useful.

I eat the way the city does: more expectation than meal. Broths, scrapes, a cricket without a voice tendered to tenderness. Salt as ceremony. I carry a whole afternoon in the careful pinch I reserve for the side of my tongue when it is my day to measure the jars that keep crispness like hoarded weather. What cannot be spared for food becomes instrument. What cannot be spared for instrument becomes instruction. We do not part from what we use. We wear it thinner and invite it back as something that teaches other hands.

Ask me then who I am and I will tell you first what I am not. I am not the bright. I am not a flame. Even my memories do not smoke; when ash drifts unexpectedly from a rumor of outside, my throat stings with a grief that predates my birth. We keep that grit in a lidded jar and do not pour it. We do not make a story of the thing we cannot metabolize.

Ask me again and I will try nouns. I am a steward. That is the only social noun we keep. It covers everything and renders nothing grand. On some days I am the wrist that knows from ache that the room is about to change its mind about the pitch that held last week. On other days I am the thumb that moves a knot the thickness of a thumbnail along a suspended strip of gut so that a hoot drops into the old note it once was. On days when the small wheel stalls, I am the breath it needs to remember to turn. I scold it without meanness. I thank it aloud when it remembers its task. It is not a god, so I am not a priest. I am a maintainer of difference at appointed times.

That last is closer. If we all practice the craft of preventing difference, someone must keep a shelf for the exceptions. I am that someone, or one of the someones. It is an office that does not like to keep its name. I know which chip in the bone spoon-ladder is a memory, not a flaw. I know that it should be allowed to hold the drip fatter and therefore later for the length of a drought and then be ground away when a wetter season returns. My hands know the speed of that removal: brisk enough not to make of it an altar, slow enough to hear what is being taken. I know when not to sand an edge that has started to argue against a seam, because the argument is a new seam asking to be taught.

Once, when I was bolder than wise, I tried to take eagerness out of a hoot. It rode high, it wanted, it pushed the choir toward a brightness that felt to me like proudness in a place where proudness is a crack. I tuned it wider. The room sighed. A boy, not yet tall enough to earn a second pebble on his belt, heard the difference as invitation and laughed. The laugh broke a count. We sent him up to carry a message along a dry tube. He came back soft-ankled and grain-coated and kinder, as exile returns often are. After that, I keep my thumb’s width closer to the old carved measure. Wanting is for the dry. Here, wanting is a draft that steals the bowls’ green.

There are days when I am less a person than a series of verbs. I smooth. I listen. I re-knot. I press my ear to the wall and learn that a crack ten rooms away has found a friend. I answer in pebbles to someone I cannot see. I sit by the salamander’s warmth shelf with a thimble of clay-sweet water and risk the tiny blasphemy of lifting its jaw with a wet finger so that a clot will drift from its gills. It opens its soft mouth around the room and is fine. We nod to one another without needing to be species to understand courtesy.

At night I sleep where the language of shoulder-blades is most fluent. The bass of a channel comforts the less certain parts of my thinking into alignment. I sleep not alone but held by noises too incumbent to be noticed unless they vanish. We do not light the fox-fire bowls where we rest. Light is a spending; we hoard its uses for the places that cannot be learned by ear. Darkness teaches the map better than flame would. I know the measure between two safe steps because I can walk it with my eyes against my own eyelids and get there anyway. There are thin scars on my shins from the days before I knew. Everyone here has those scars, and they are badges not of error but of practice. The room catches what it cannot allow to fall.

I love the slack more than the taut. This is perhaps my only heresy. We are taught to tune the room to its hold. I prefer the moment before the rope remembers itself and tightens, the wet inch when it drinks and swells, the forgivingness. It is why they set me to the exceptions. If a rhythm cannot spare its laxity, it becomes a law and we have no use for laws. We have uses for agreements. I am happiest when I can keep a kindness built into the rule—an extra drop in the cup when a sick child needs the noise to warm them; an extra secrecy in the curtains when someone’s anger will not be smelted quiet unless we give it a shelf with thicker hum.

You will ask me where I am in the room you have never seen and I will lay my palm on the rock between us now, wherever you are, and trace you a map in pressure and pause. I am standing with one foot on the lip that hums in solved chords and one foot on the scribed combing of a wall that sometimes tries to sheet. I am under the eave where the membrane carries breath into hoot, reading the punctures the way a winds-player reads holes. I am above the shallow lens, never in it, watching the wheel for its tick and the cord for its proof of time. I am always, also, by the jar of grit we will not pour; I am its lid. I am often beside the slate, replacing the line the slow weeping of the wall has worn to blur. I am behind your shoulder, if you think of this place for long enough to consider it real, nudging your thought away from making of it a temple and toward letting it be a room.

When I become most myself I am not body at all. I am the held pitch that makes the other sounds neighborly. I am the relief in a seam that keeps a wall from arguing with gravity too loudly. I am the patience that lets a bead choose its moment. I am the consent of a hundred small things not to become accident. If you needed a name for me you could use the word they use here for what lives: agreement. Not treaty. Not vow. Agreement as in resonance: the way two notes when true to each other create a third that neither had alone.

And yet I do have a body. It leaves fingermarks in clay that will fossilize into history for someone else’s touch. It wears enamel down with grit. It grows ears that learn cartilage tricks. It knits knots through bone-plate holes until the bone, softened by a thousand threadings, becomes bead, becomes teaching. It will be braided into a rope. It will be cut into a strip that will hush a leak. It will sing on the veil when I am no longer the thumb at the knot. Much afterward, a child will run their fingers over the hoot I once kept and not know me, only the rightness. That is all I want: not to be missed, but to be continued.

When a day of adjustment comes, as it always does, I will climb to the singing veil with my dull awl and my softer hand. I will measure with the bone of my own thumb because that is a constant that is also alive. I will move a knot no more than the width of the old wanting I have learned to be careful with. My palm will rest on the rock above—because that is the place where this room remembers the sensation of being patted—and I will say what we all say when we do anything that is not ours alone:

Keep the drip slow. Let the rope swell. Give the noise its room.
