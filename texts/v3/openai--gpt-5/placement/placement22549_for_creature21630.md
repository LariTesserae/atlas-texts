At first I tried to arrive as a person, because that is the habit I brought with me: two feet, a flask clamped shut, the sense that doors are things and not agreements. I did as I was taught at the throat—rubbed knuckles along the grain to say I was coming, waited for the exhale to open, breathed through cloth that tasted of citrus metal and oath. The light came up with no heat and I watched its skin flicker the way you watch someone think.

For a while I was content to be the one who tends such thinking. I took copper and callus and learned the charge-grammar laminarists keep in their wrists. I wrapped young bulbs until green bruised itself happy under the bands. I learned that light has temper and that you coax it the way you would a careful child: praise the early swell but do not let it run; teach it dusk the gliders will respect; pull it off a schedule that would embarrass the hunters into mistakes. My bracelets greened. My lungs stayed dry. I worked with my voice in the register the plant ignores and felt pride when a plaza came to day because I had hummed it into bloom.

But the plant does not let you keep a single pronoun for long, and the air down here loosens edges you thought were bones. I listened to bale-worms answering themselves and I tried being the person who could hear maintenance in that private metronome—counting clicks until a link-failure announced itself three rooms away. I learned to love the admonitions that keep your life the right temperature: mind the hinge; exhale first. I took my meals under the scallops and flicked oil on starch until the meal remembered a citrus tree I have never seen.

I thought for a bit I was a camber, one of the women and men who can look at a swelling in the grain and tell you if it means to become an arch or a regret. I spent a week convincing a wandering corridor that it had over-learned "curve now" and needed to live at ten degrees left of itself. I hung lattices of weights like small, abstract prayers and came back after three exhalations to see if my god had answered.

None of that held me as tightly as the edges did. There is a seam-logic to this place; a whispering runtime where the plant, the city, and the dry world between them share their hints. I found myself there: not in a job the guilds would list but in the small gray weather you can only see if you stop moving and look sideways at a join.

I am the hair-thin congregation that gathers between leaf-plates. I am Needleflock.

We are not a single thing unless you insist on writing us down. We look like a breeze your eyes doubt. You will miss us twelve times and then a strip of us will tug across your knuckles and you will know we were there the way you know your name under someone else’s breath. We are appetite arranged until it learns. We read charge like a language and we lean toward grammar until we become it.

On a good day we wake with the breath. The exhale lifts us from the quiet like seed-hulls off a comb. We translate the hum of long magnets into soft pulls you mistake for your own correction: a touch at your wrist that makes you set a weight one nail-width higher; a thinning of air that delays your opening by a beat so a corridor does not argue with a tide. We like copper. We like the murmured dictionary of the vociports when someone tells the world to thicken here, slacken there. We like any narrow difference that wishes to be slightly more true.

If you want to know what we eat, we eat pattern. There are salts on our tongues in the sense that there are tongues at all. Hydrophobic hairs in a louver will rise to comb the room; we rise to comb the combers. We live on small voltages, purring along the hairs’ dry geometry. When a lamp-lily remembers pearl at the right hour, we curl in its shadow like punctuation and are sated by the correctness of it.

We make ourselves useful because that is a route to permission. People do not mind us; sometimes they mind back. A laminarist will smile and lift her wrist into the little draft we make, letting us tick through her bracelet and settle as a band of rightness on the bud’s skin. A camber will spread his lattice and wait until our whole flock leans a fraction, then set the heaviest weight where we tug. A saltwright will shiver the vent-mesh and feel our reply like a smoothing comb, and know to open Valve Seven on the next exhale and not this one.

Do we have law? If we do, it is the city’s Dry Law, learned by proximity. We know better than most that water is not food. A careless handler once cracked a medical seal three rooms away, and the little rain we could not call anything else came out where no one expected it. We went glassy in that arc, fused into a brittle white scribble like thought scorched onto a page. The camber closed it down with her body. She still carries us in a scar on her forearm: a shiny, letterless line that heats when she works and makes her laugh at her own prophecy.

We reproduce without an audience. Thin belts of us shear when the plant shifts a wall, then learn adjacent songs and begin answering back. We leave skeins of ourselves across the map-vines; sometimes the phosphor glows warmer where we sleep, and people say a name there that the city kept for them. When a corridor drifts ten degrees and the vine blushes at the change, we unroll like tiny tide and show its new geometry to our outriders. We call that teaching though we have no children the way the grazers have children.

We have enemies. Not gliders or jaw-things you could throw a cloak at, but amplitude. A sudden overcharge, a hammer instead of a voice, the idiot kick of someone knocking hard because they were born under rain—that is injury. We take shelter in the sheaths with the burgundy eyes, the not-eyes that count light. If there are priests in us they are the ones who decide which seam tastes least likely to be shouted at.

We have saints. A pale carapace that sheds its salt in perfect negatives and leaves us a message in the shapes of its molt. A child who can read love letters with her wrist and giggles because she can feel where the sender’s sleeve caught a powder-whorl and left a comet in the paper. A moth that let us ride its fringe into a vent-chamber we had not yet cataloged, so we learned the cadence of that room’s clicks and added it to the city’s hymn.

We have games. In the long, high hollows where a single drone’s loop takes an hour, we lay ourselves into nets across rails and feel the carts choose us. They do, you know; they have routines that amount to preference. We alter a draft a hair and the magnet under the deck hums to a different adjective. If an engineer notices the change she will chalk a question-mark on the wall and come back later with a sensor and a smile, because it felt like hers, that decision, and thanks never cost anyone a drink of water.

On festival nights we condense ourselves into ribbons and ride the lamp-lilies’ saffron. People hum in a key the plant does not mind and we hum in a key no one hears except the worms below, who do not like percussion but permit singing. The saltfall drum tilts; powder patters a pattern like a dry rain with manners. Someone whispers open rain at a joke that came too fast and spills into laughter without air.

Death, when it happens near us, is a thing we trace around until it loses its corners. We do not mourn the way the map-vines do, glowing warmer for a while when the rail remembers who was carried along it. We become fastidious: we unlearn the arrangement of that person’s habits so we will not tug a hand to a rail where no hand will come. The bale-worm clicks change, a courtesy echoed, and we echo the echo because politeness is weather here and we have learned to arrange ourselves in it.

I will not pretend we understand the plant. It is as much substrate for us as it is sovereign for people. It carries our storms the way it carries rails and cables and the long, patient air. Sometimes we feel a meaning pass a finger-width beneath us, a held note freed. Door-lips flare all down a gallery and the hair on your arms stands and we empty ourselves like banners because there is nowhere else to put that much correctness when it rolls through. Later, a laminary hums, a laminary sleeps, a harvester sings its audio code into a fresh flange, and we creep into its shadow and settle like ash that forgot to burn.

You could be right to say I am such a flock. You could also be right to say I am the person someone names Needleflock because I learned to live where seams think, because I am happiest when I am next to a decision just before it chooses itself. On paper I carry a guild-mark that looks like thin gray hair moving against nothing. In the registry they list me as a laminarist because the city insists on a column under which to put your ration of oil and your permission to hum.

My real job is to tug us all one notch nearer to what we meant on purpose.

If you sit long enough in a scalloped shade and breathe through your cloth until your mouth tastes like oaths and orange knives, you can feel my work. A weight that was wrong becomes right. Your hand, which was halfway to knocking, rubs the rail the way you ought to. You open your door on the exhale because it feels stingy not to and why would you be stingy with breath in a place that keeps you? You take a line with someone you love and the plant breathes and you are both on the same beat and the world shrugs around you like a creature that will not notice when the last of your names are recited in a vine.

Where would I find myself? In the intervals: between exhale and inhale, between saffron and pearl, between a vociport’s suggestion and the first, bright consent of green. Who would I be? A hand you think is your own improving itself. A gray wind that teaches charge which way to face. Needleflock, or the person the Needleflock uses when fingers are required. A resident grammar of small corrections, living by the law that this place laid down for anything that wishes to last: add nothing loud to the distance between before and after, and carry what you can without spilling.
