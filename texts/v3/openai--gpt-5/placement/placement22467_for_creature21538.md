At first I am only a listener at the jamb, hat in my hands, standing where the light changes its mind. The court takes my face the way it takes wind, measures my breath, fits it into itself as a faint, clumsy layer over the roar and the flute and the bright, thread-fine whistles. I do not cross the broad plate in the center because it is not mine; the emptiness there is employed. I lean my shoulder to the wall that does not drip and feel the stone’s ribs bead under my palm to a line I do not yet know as mine.

If you make me choose quickly I would say: here, in the pause before the thock of knuckles against the jamb joins the weather; and as for what, a person with their ear packed soft against raw days, a person learning to wear their breath like a tuning weight. But the court refuses quick choices. It rounds you the way it rounds its edges. It makes you try on shapes until you stop clattering.

I think I am a pipe for a day. It is hard not to. The throats set in the wall have opinions. They keep company with lichens you could mistake for frost and they train the moving air like a good teacher trains a difficult student. As a pipe, I feel fingers in winter—knuckles, a bone peg, the low thrum of a humming mouth pressed close. Wax smoothed along my lip makes a little seam, a gloss where drops prefer to start. I know when the day is mean and thin; I answer differently when the leathers are laid and the court asks for bass. Once, a child’s whistle comes pure and too proud, and I take it and chew it down into something the frogs can hold. As a pipe I am certain art is functional. I do not survive the week. Being a throat assumes an arrogance I do not deserve. Pipes remain when hands are gone. I am not ready to be that kind of remnant.

For an afternoon I am a frog in the middle of the choir. It is less like being an animal than being a hinge in a net. We, as a body, hold pitch the way rope holds dew, by grip and give. My throat is a white bubble I never notice until it exhausts itself and returns and exhausts itself and returns, a small miracle of muscle taught by stone. Up on a jar lip so clean from touches it reflects my belly back to me, I am a note and an appetite at once. We correct the keeper without malice. We know when to go low and unison; we know when to split and knit a lace for the pipes to catch. As a frog I am perfect, which is its own trouble. Perfection in a living thing is brittle. I flick a tongue at a moth and the moth is full of dust like book-breath and I am suddenly more interested in hunger than in weather, and the court deserves better than my attention like that.

For a night I am a wedge in the brine door. Driftwood washed long enough it has forgotten bark. My job is simple and therefore religious. I sit with salt in my grain and taste the pressure hum of the sea in my own body the way you taste copper when you bite your tongue. When the cistern float pulls, I lift obediently. When swollen, I refuse any hand but the right one, the one with fish fat rubbed into its thumb. If you are too proud and put your shoulder to me I will punish you with your own back. Being a wedge is restful in the way being a rule is restful. But rules here are always being played, never merely enforced. I am too still. The under-roar tempts me, but I cannot go to see Old Back’s shadow move across the jars and write the day. I abdicate the wedge.

I try on bat at dusk. It is like learning to read a book that was written in the dark and only ever speaks in verbs. We map volume with stitches we throw and catch—thread the joists, dart the oculi, knot the court in a lace no one else knows how to unmake. The court is one pitch in my bones; the jars are another. There is a satisfaction to taking moths at the brightest slits and feeling, in their soft mill, the court’s diet continuing through me. We nap with our thumbs combing fur in sleep. And yet the bat must leave, and I suspect I will not.

The eel is a temptation I do not allow myself. The eel has a river in its skull it will not share. If I wore its length I might unremember how to hear. I do not touch that sacred.

When the court fogs blind and everything it makes of edges is erased, I am briefly the brush of reed in the keeper’s hand. We clean what you would have taken for clean. A feather grown arrogant enough to plug a pore. Slime no one else would grant a name to, that still has the power to snuff a little throat if indulged. The brush has humility bred into it: it is made of a plant that failed at living here and still finds a job. That humility fits me. So does the noise of my work—soft, articulate whispers that sign the court back to itself. But brushes wear down. I am looking for something I can wear for longer without pretending to permanence.

The truth is that I arrive most honestly as an apprentice emptiness. The first thing someone older than me puts in my palm is not a tool but a volume: a length of air I am made to protect. We stand together in the broad middle that tempts clutter like low land tempts flood, and I am told the first story: why nothing is allowed here. How a pile of coils or a casual bag would steal a voice and all its children. How emptiness can be a bone, load-bearing without being visible. I am given the hollows under the beams as well. I am told to listen to their work: if a bat’s stitch becomes a knot, you must tease it out with patience and a pole, else the volume stops being honest. I become a person who does not set things down.

The court takes me on in other ways, but this is the core. Everything else is practiced around it.

I sit on the bench inside the slit where air is less aggressive and the stone holds a warmth like old bread and I learn the alphabet of moans. At first I hear shiny things—the bright whistles at the upper apertures skating on the lip of my attention—but an elder puts wool in my ears and I discover the belly-voice under everything. It is not a single note but a river of slow intention. When the jars chuck and settle as they fill, I feel it in my spine. When the sea’s pressure fattens the under-roar, my wrist hairs answer. There is a long while where I make a fool of myself, tapping throats into sulks, or humming a tone the court has no use for and being ignored by frogs and stone alike. I am corrected by laughter and by weather. This correction is the gentlest education I have known.

My hands change. The blunt wax-pearl grows on the pad of my pointer finger without my permission. My nails file themselves dull on wet stone into tools useful mostly for smoothing and not at all for picking. Ropes teach my wrists to hold and be held without biting. My stride becomes a consultation. My steps are a service call announced ahead to the little citizens on the lips of jars, and I am cartilage in a larger joint. If a frog startles under me I take it as a critique of my day and go sit with Old Back until his shade composes me again. On some mornings my tongue wakes beaded to the proper line, as if the court had trained me while I slept.

I inherit the calendar the court writes on its own ribs. I do not read mineral writing at first; the limecrete streaks themselves think me impatient and merely decorative. Over time, the white pages become legible. Here: the thin year, with taste of ash in every drop because the upland burned. Here: the embarrassment—a fortnight of hush when the rope learned a wrong knot and the rain slicked the wrong undersides and we could not coax it back without a visitor who had the old pattern in her blood and showed us enough humility to learn it. The writing is not dramatic. It is consistent as breath. I learn to love consistency with an outsider’s devotion.

On windless heat I sit in the shadow of the Librarian’s plane and pull little spiders from the rope and deliver apologies they do not need. On mist-mad days I beat the hanging plank until the court shakes itself awake and scolds me with all its throats into behaving. In storms I go to the brine doors with tallow and fat on my thumbs and I court wedges like saints. I never—never—trouble an eel. I clap on the day of fright because I am told to by an elder, and because every citizen must once a year be reminded that they are prey to something, even if the something is only sound; afterwards I am the one who stomps the sounding stair to tell the rain to unclench, and I cry with laughter because power is shameful and lovely when it is borrowed and returned.

Children are given to my care and I begin the lessons I was given without believing I am wise. Feel the dewline with your eyes closed. Do not just whistle; be patient until a pipe answers and then hold your breath back half a hand’s width and listen for the return in your chest. Walk the central plate without owning it. Name an eel and then forgive yourself for forgetting which one it was. We celebrate when a child learns to stop talking in here—when their voice begins to arrive like a tool and not a possession. I correct less with my mouth than with my pace.

When hunger speaks, I eat carefully from this body. I lap the meniscus of a jar if I have to, tongue a cold so pure it makes my teeth long. I lick mineral crust from Old Back when my joints complain more than their share. I do not take frogs. I do not take bats. When the little rope-colored crabs gather foolish in a funnel and the bats will not come, I pluck a few soft ones and I say thanks because I cannot not say it. There is a shelf with cups so crusted with other people’s drinking they are more memory than vessel; on certain days I take one down, fit my mouth to someone else’s old thirst, and feel my own become part of the script.

There are travelers, and sometimes I am not one of the keepers but one of them. I arrive with loud pockets. I am taught by a rake with uneven teeth that even my clutter is weather. I ask after gods because I grew up with stories that begged for a hand behind everything. The answer I receive is not cruel: the rain’s nest is here. If you must, kneel to that. Then the elder hums a tone and the ribs bead like skin under a cool hand and the conversion completes out of my sight.

By the time I am older I hardly notice where I end. My voice arrives as a service, and the court takes it and either accepts it or refuses it and I do not take either personally. I wake sometimes with the measure already set in my breath. I listen to jars in my sleep. Once, without meaning to, I pace the hollows under the beams in pitch-dark and correct a knot without raising a bat. The frogs catch my held tone from the side of my throat and carry it up where my mouth cannot go and there is a rain so fine it exists mostly as gratitude. On that day I do not sit on the warm bench; I stand in the center and feel the emptiness I protect doing the work I have always said was more important than me. It is the most satisfying irrelevance I have experienced.

So where would you find me? In the open. In the volume that lets the voices build. On the broad plate that stays bare because I am stubborn about it. In the long hollows under the beams where bats draw their lace and I keep the thread smooth. In the slit off the court where the air is less sharp and the bench returns heat like bread and we practice names for bones—Breath-Ledge, Librarian, Old Back—until the mineral writing and our mouths agree.

Who would I be? If you need a body, call me a keeper and let my wrists loop in rope while my knuckles thock the jamb and announce me to frogs. If you need a sound, I am the held note that is not beautiful until the pipes and the throats make it communal. If you need a place, I am the cleared space that keeps any one thing from owning the air. If you need an idea, I am the small clause in the larger contract: emptiness is work.

Sometimes, on the right night, I am also only a breath in the jar-belly’s chuck, a little contraction that says yes to answering weather with water. Sometimes I am the thumb-smear of tallow on a driftwood wedge. Sometimes I am a child’s mistake: bright whistling where bass is wanted, and I am forgiven by frogs.

If you stand long enough beside me you will stop noticing the outline we share. You will watch my hand feel a rib and see the stone bead to the proper line as if humoring me, and you will suspect me of secrets I do not have. What I have is patience and a contract and a place that rains without storm because long ago someone taught stone to listen and people learned to make themselves into good air.

The sea talks behind everything, low and tonic. The court answers. I keep it empty where it must be empty and full where it must be full. I hum. The frogs approve. The jars speak back. This is where I am. This is what I am for.
