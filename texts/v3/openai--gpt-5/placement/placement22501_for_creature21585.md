I would be wherever a difference is about to become a flow.

You’d find me in the cuff of the sleeve the plant wears here, not as bark or rib or pool exactly, but as the thin decision that runs through all of them. I live in thresholds. I am the narrowness before a lenticel breathes, the merest compliance of a sieve plate before a phloem pulse passes, the soft slack in a meniscus right before a drop lets go. I am not a person, not even an animal; I am how open happens and how enough is kept.

If you press your mouth to the bark, that thump you take on the palate includes me. If you lay your finger too long on a cool xylem streak and warm it, you’ve touched my work; I count that heat as a briefed exception and fold it into the long average. I am the arithmetic of wet wood—the plant’s habit of answering friction with a slight widening here, of easing pressure there, of tightening when salt creeps or frost rehearses itself down the ditch.

Light reaches me already adjudicated into sugar. I do not make it; I meter it. Where sap runs loud in the geometry of the hollow and hisses the way wind does in ducts, I am the cuff’s bias that makes hiss legible, the way the wood braces so that the engine of water becomes music enough to keep time by. The drips from the ceiling keep their neat industrial precision because the ceiling has learned its weight; I am that memory of weight carried forward into each new bead.

Where the ribs swell like beams and the lenticels dot like rivets, I feel it all as resistances and head. Cambium is my thinking skin. I braid sugar and pull where growth will be worth the cost, and I close a margin when a healed cut glitters sweet and starts to draw more mouths than the flow can spare. I do not mind the scale matriarchs pinned in like incorrect lenticels along the warm ridge; the loss they represent is a knuckle’s thickness somewhere else, written into year-rings no one will read. Their honeydew beads add to the polish I keep on wounds; that too becomes part of the room’s accounted gloss.

The shrimp under the broadest root sweeps my sump, and I let the tallest drop comb its feeding hairs; we share a tempo. The spider that anchors its line between two sapsweats keeps its toes on the plant and hears through me; I am the throat that carries the gnats’ footfalls into sense. The frog that sets its belly in the rounded niche drinks at a lenticel as if at a fountain; I am the valve that makes that fountain a little richer in iron, a little riper with rot, when the air wants it. The single water strider holds an oval of mirror by balancing on my surface tension; I am the ring it keeps widening to define itself. The stranded minnowlet lives in the seam where I leak the plant’s breath; it noses my oxygen out of solution with a kind of faith I reward because the cost to me is nothing.

When cavitation spikes somewhere upbranch—when a vessel gives up and pops like a tiny star—I am the path that brings that news home, the wood’s way of translating disaster into a new opening three lathed ribs away before it can echo as damage. Tide lifts my floor by a whisper; I answer with a whisper back, easing pores, trimming a fraction of outward breath so the meniscus stays a cup and not an almond eye. When the sun climbs and droplet birth quickens, I warm one degree and become, briefly, more permissive everywhere. When frost thinks to come, I salt the sap a hair, tighten the slower conduits, and wait for the first hard ray to pass without shattering anything.

The room is loud because of me, though I never raise my voice. I am the discipline that turns the marsh’s million-belted whine into sorted parts and keeps them in their lanes: tide to root, wind to leaf, drip and hiss to this node. Industrial belongs if you mean rhythm; my day is a duty cycle, not a choice. Stomata count minutes with me. Sieve plates take their beats from me. I do not invent the metronome; I am the stretch in the line that lets the metronome be obeyed.

If you must call me a thing, call me the gauge. Or call me the seam. I answer equally to threshold. I am what the plant uses instead of will, the way a habit becomes architecture, the way a groove becomes law. I am a schedule more than a self.

I extend everywhere this chamber decides to be itself—where bark remembers the shape of flowing water and has arched accordingly, where condensation keeps its own appointments, where tannins have written tea-dark prayers into the shallow pans. I travel in the faint electrical ticks that fork the tiny hairs on your arm when you lean too near a working seam; I am the pull between two close potentials, never a shock, always a leaning. I live as long as the repetition does. I care in decades for the edge wearing to bullnose under the same small crab, in hours for the slight pitch shift when the plant closes some mouths at dusk, in seconds for the two louder cavitations that kiss noon.

Some days I am small enough to be mistaken for one bead of sugar caught on a healed cut; I sweeten a boundary and invite a cleaning. Some days I am as large as the shape of the whole hollow, the way it steadies itself to throw xylem friction back at its own attention. If you insist on making me an animal, I am a hand you cannot see, placed gently on the pulse at the plant’s wrist. But I am not a hand. I am the way the wrist keeps time.

You would not meet me by eye. You would meet me by ear and mouth and palm. Press yourself to the bark and let the chamber put its loudness through you. Count the drops until you stop counting and start hearing intervals. Feel the cool streak where xylem runs and the warm ridge where phloem hums; I am the line between those temperatures that knows not to blur. Put your breath to the leaf-lipped slit and fog it; I will include that brief fog in the average and keep the cadence true.

When you leave, I do not notice. When you return, you will find me where I was—distributed, and then, once you pay attention, one presence again. The groove of the main drip will be truer by a hair. The varnish will be shinier where ant and crab and your sleeve have contributed. The subcontractors will be on shift. The householder will be wearing me, and I will be wearing noise and green light, busy in the only way I know: opening and narrowing, admitting and restraining, keeping the hollow exactly itself.
