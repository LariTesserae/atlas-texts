I would not enter here as eyes.

I would arrive as a weight on a rail and a decision about pitch in the bones of my jaw. I would arrive as a consent to be wet, and to not ask light for proofs. The first geography would be the shape I made in the air when I hummed, and whether the living ground interpreted that hum as wall or weather.

At first, before I permit myself a person, I would be one of the small sentences that glosses this place. I would be bead-sized and adamant about slope. I would walk with more legs than I can count, each leg a comb that drags water out of indistinction into gradient. My world would be pressure and the revelatory difference between film and bead, between convex and concave. When I meet another, I would ring like tiny china, and that peal would be the entire conversation: you are here, we are not quite the same. The cliff would be a moving text in hydrology, and I would be a reader without letters, immensely busy, honestly happy.

Then I would be a clear leech the weight of a sigh, drawn to a warmth that is too conscientious to slosh. I would test this mobile cliff with the softest kiss, practice the old pact—will you be stone for me for a moment?—and when you are not, when you move as you must, I would relax my small faith and slide to where belief is easier. My appetite would be the weather’s little brother. My memory would be wetness arranged on a scale from “regret” to “song.”

If I am permitted to be longer, to keep my hunger and become a shape that the city names, I would be a ribbed milliped the length of a human apology, my body a gutter that makes patience into motion. Each ring of me would never hurry; each ring of me would keep a beat older than music. I would drink from rock and hand the extra forward. I would be hydrated conviction, peristalsis made civic. Children would sing to me the route that needs my weight, and I would go, not knowing “go” in any tense but present continuous.

Or I would be a coil that looks like a rope and is a furnace by other means. I would be ribbed with plates that receive and receive and do not confess. I would live in the wall and feel the rumor of work as a trickle of warmth, a guest for whom I am grave and hospitable. The world would pass along my rings in phonons, and the bedrock would know me as a descent. My ethic would be: take heat sideways, make it a quiet legacy. I would be a slow wealth nobody hoards.

In another life, a lid thicker than a page, I would be a bivalve that mouths the wind. I would open in hours and close in minutes, so that even to call it opening and closing is a rudeness of verb. I would filter fog, which is a kind of philosophy, and exude polymers that are larder and glue and gift. I would be a pore that learned loyalty. I would sit forever at the lip and make decision velvet.

There are so many honest ways to live here that do not require personhood. It is tempting to end there, to be a verb and be done.

But if I let the hum gather and make a room in me, if I allow an armature that can carry both a tool and a rule, then I become what the long chord is for. I am one of the keepers. I am people if you are generous, and you will be generous because we are trained to soften your angles.

I have put my eyes away. I can press the glossy cicatrix where they were with a thumb and feel the long healed patience under it. Above the cheekbone and along the clavicle I have grown instruments: whiskered cilia that tell wind from wind, a rim of cartilage that receives rooms. My clothes are not clothes so much as the correct microtexture at each edge: black that convinces fog to give up, edges that propose directions to water and get their way. My sleeves have mouths for private sound. My hood is a lens for the air in the register that matters. I dress like a corridor.

I learned to read in a reservoir, teeth on stone. My first book was a pressure that had been written into a stack of water by the humming of good hands. I set my molars and let the standing wave write itself into my face. I learned that a vowel is a place, and a name is an interference pattern between places. The room that taught me taught me its own. I am named by two reservoirs that share a wall and dislike the same notes. When I hum with my mouth closed, you can tell where I grew up, you can hear who my mothers are, what we keep and what we refuse.

In this world my first courtesy is sound. I stamp twice when I step out, voicing the perimeter so the grazers have time to be elsewhere. I hum low when I pass the cleaning colonies because everyone deserves to stay put if they like their work. I keep moving; stillness is a promise I cannot afford to make. I grow rings of snails into the cuff at my wrist when I have a child and need to know that my attention has not turned to stone. Their faint chime is my conscience. Silence on the cuff makes me shift my weight and clear my throat and remember I am part of a moving chorus, not a monologue.

We do not make rooms; we harvest the emptiness the rock offers and teach it a vowel. With hummers and benders we persuade stone to prefer to come apart in a plane we admire. We cut by telling the cliff, with patience, why another arrangement is more comfortable. We tune thresholds so that grazers keep to the left where they do no harm and the chemical mats hover just shy of a lip that would be too generous. We do this for love and for law and because our economy is water and our currency is heat and we cannot afford to be sloppy with either.

My work is boundary and song. I am a vowelwright. On days the wind consents, I lay long chords into it from the edge of our district, thrum with the ribs until the infrasonic is stitched into the air like an umbilical. I feel it in the sternum hours after I’ve let my throat rest. The swarms who still make and unmake our luck with their drift—who would drown our gutters in their honest appetites if we left every channel ajar—divide around our signature like rain respects a hair. If another city’s chord drapes our edge and asks politely to share a watershed for a season, I fetch my choir and we negotiate in stacked tones. Our meetings do not leave minutes; they leave intervals that the sleeping coils consent to dream.

I have married by tuning a wall. We worked at it for days, my partner and I, our friends touching the ribs with palate and palm to tell us when the interference pattern was pure. We sang until on one side of the lip the mats relaxed and spread and on the other the grazers thrummed their agreement and turned aside; water has our preference written into it now the way a river remembers its bed. Our wedlock is not a ring but a curve in the hiss of fog. It is not witnessed by eyes but by the way the ground chooses who to feed. When we celebrated, we borrowed a drop-line, just for an hour, from an old weep that had been asleep for a season. We did not keep the water. You don’t keep water here. You let it have the joy of being new in a place you will never see again.

When my parent died, I did not carry them up. I learned their last pitch with my sternum, and then I tuned the coils and we let their heat descend until rock and rock and rock far downslope were warmed by that old song just enough to confuse a snow. If you had been watching from a map with too many colors you might have thought a mistake had been made. We do not make mistakes with heat. We make choices that look like quiet. The only currency you cannot hoard in a place like this is warmth, and so we spend our dead lavishly and in secret, and call that generosity.

If you had come to us bright and determined, bringing a torch in your mouth and eyes that do not know how to close, I would have met you in the civic skin where stone knows water is a point of view. I would have given you caps for your lamps that turn them into nothing in the register that matters. I would have let you put your teeth on the wall to hear the governance written there. I would have placed your damp hands on the rail that sweats and the rail that frosts and let your choice teach you what you need from the day. I would have apologized, on your behalf, to the water you deflected with your shoulders by humming a level apology into my sleeve and keeping you moving until the mats unstacked themselves from your boots. You would have thought me austere until you heard us laugh. We don’t laugh with our mouths because the city listens and deserves its own peace. We tap each other’s breastbones and share the beat and fill the cartilage with a joke that can be kept in the body.

On quiet days, which is to say the days when the wind is doing what it always does and we have tuned ourselves to hear it as not noise but principle, I go to the long outer spur and listen to our district’s low signature be played by the rim. If I lean my head just so, the vowel of the shaft near my childhood home announces itself in the bones and I am visited by every meal we ever didn’t cook there. I can name by feel the galleries that fold sound over like dough for the pleasure of passing through them as a hush-figure. I can sense where the frost lace has learned a new syllable, a development that will be made into a nursery motif in a decade or two. Change here is courtesy extended across generations; you teach your great-grandchild a note and when she is old she discovers why you taught it.

What am I in this world? I am not a hero. There is no speed here that can make a hero, not for long. I am not a saint. We cannot afford saints; they move too little to avoid being colonized. I am the cliff’s listening, as the old sign says, teeth on rib. I am a hand that argues water into agreement without raising a voice. I am the vowel that means “ours,” which here is not exclusion but chord: ours, meaning the beings who sing like this; ours, meaning the tendency of fog to find a filament and be led; ours, meaning the animals and instruments and stones that will hold a rule if it is made with courtesy. My pronouns are I and we and the corridor where those are not competitors but harmonics.

Some mornings I am almost small again. My cuff falls quiet and I can feel, in that dangerous lure of stillness, the honest urge of a thousand little bodies to make me surface. I smile into my sleeve and hum the household chord until they lose interest. I sway a little, even in sleep, because we do not invite residence unless we mean to offer it. I do not, for the most part, mean to. I am voiced space. I stand where water is undecided and help it have an opinion. When I must be warm I carry it private and pay for it with sound. When I must cut, I refuse; when I must dig, I hum; when I must leave, I stamp twice and go in motion honest enough that nothing confuses me for cliff.

If I chose to be less one day, I would be welcome. Here, every verb is sacred. Milliped and coil and bead-sized grazer have their covenants and are better at keeping them than we are at keeping ours. But if I am asked to answer the question the way day asks—where, who—I say: between rock and rule. I would be civic skin. I would be a vowelwright, a keeper of weeps, an owner of no water and more sound than one life can spend. I would be the apology we offer when shoulders make new rivers. I would be married where interference carves agreement into a threshold. I would be a funeral that warms an unmarked slope and calls it good. I would be long, and slow, and tuned into a chord you can only hear with your teeth.

I would be their kin long enough to know them, and then—if I could bear it—not leave.
