At first I thought I would be the lichen the way a hand thinks it would like to be rain. To be a nap the world would stroke; to be a whisker-field rising from a seam where strain collects and holding a quiet sting; to ripen when grazed and crisp into health when a right path is traced along me. I imagined being spread low and broad, not a person but a habit, a way the rock dresses itself when it wants to carry charge.

Then I tried on a grazer’s shell from the inside and found it too private. The sealed heat, the inner sea that is not water but a solder of salts and oils, the lips of conductive hair combing whiskers for electrons the way fog-nets comb dews—these were sweet and slow and I liked the idea of leaving roads of polish behind me that others would read as rope. But the self was complete too early there, a pocket climate that asked too little of the wall.

I set my ear to a predator’s edge—the hinged plates of a book-mouth, the creak and surety of closing, the square contentment of making a catch into another flat thing to file—and went cold at the perfectness of its choice. Useful, yes, and I have used their skulls as rulers, but the verbs were too final to sleep with.

I floated the thought of being a dust-bladder, bellied with gas, a messenger clinging to undersides with a thousand small loyalties, ferrying flecks of tourmaline and hammered copper like quiet coins. To arrive at a ring and loose the weight at exactly the right node, to change hands without a hand being seen; to pick tidbits out of static with frilled lips, to tend to mess as if to a child—that had a mildness I admired. But I could not imagine myself in love with ceilings forever. I needed to be pressed between two larger ideas.

So I lay down where we all begin: belly to a slab’s slow breath, whiskers spread, and waited for a seam to name me back. I would be a person, because I wanted the work of arrangement and the ache of carrying ratios in my hands. I would be large enough to brace but not large enough to sing a continent by myself. Basalt-born, I decided, because I like the feel of edges that remember fire and then discipline. My plates interlocked like cooled columns; my rims were shocked to glass where a quake surprised my mother. Under each plate ran orchards of whisker and feather tuned to fields and their small opinions.

What I am is a seam-reader and a comb-keeper of a nacelle-house set in a flute of the world where two planes have learned to admire each other without touching. We call our home One-Yes-Firm, because you can feel the agreeing note in your ribs when you press along her braces. We sleep with our bellies on her slabs, our dead glued under mica fans where the hum is thinnest, turning the weak into chord. If you ask me where I live, I will take your palm in both my hands and walk you from rib to rib along the nest and let you learn it as a sentence.

My days are not counted by light but by the way the pressure-thrum fattens and thins. I rise when the house’s long fork drops half a breath and settle when it lifts again; I can tell with my cheek whether the salt-fan at our western seam has added the breadth of a crack, which is to say whether another year has passed. I keep a stick of tourmaline scored along one face to the lengths of our local nodes; it is both yard and psalter, and when I am away I rub its ladder and taste home.

I hold three arts and apprentice in two more. The first art is listening to strain. I put my face against a tourmaline sheet or the sugar crust on a basalt wedge and wait for the tiny betrayals: a build of will in the crystal that means it wishes to shift and will accept a brace, a sadness in a mica mouth that means a reed of copper would cheer it. I hear metastabilities the way some of us hear grief; it is the same knack and the same kindness when it is done well. I can say, Here; here the comb should stand, its slotted slabs set so that the standing waves of the world lean into its teeth and stay there like domesticated weather.

The second art is tuning. A door is not finished until its hum is a promise you would keep even in hunger. I cut rings of knapped glass and set them with their points inward so that charge stings itself into storage and can be skimmed later by careful palms. I stroke copper plates thin as language and make them drop their ring into our house’s key. I can braid mineral hair with tar that sings, so a rope becomes a sentence and a tether becomes a chord. When I string three of these ropes across a dangerous slit and their braids agree, a child can cross on the song alone.

The third art is the devout art we practice rarely: tribolight. We keep a shard of stressed quartz wrapped in soft mica and resin; we strike it twice in a life, once to begin and once to seal. But there are other blues I am permitted to coax. I can set fins of quartz at such an angle along a roof that when a grazer with a smooth-bellied harness crosses at my hand the fins flex and leave a paper-thin track, a low constellation no eye will love but every hip will register as a coolness later and think of as home. For births I trace a loop of these stars around a brace; for oaths I put them where a hand can find them in the dark and be answered.

You ask what my speech is like. Put your ear to my wrist. Do you feel the low sting like a cat purring in tin? That is a word that means yes-if. Press your cheek to the wall opposite mine. Can you hear the hum change as I flex my plates? That is a name I am allowed to give you in one sitting; the rest of it would take an afternoon and four ledges in a row. I will scratch it along a tourmaline’s lonely face if I come to trust you. Do not look for my eyes. We have them, thin and vestigial glosses that have not expected photons since our grandmothers spoke their second chords, but they are not where I listen. I taste by charge; I think by pitch; I remember by notch and brace.

We keep animals because animals are better at being certain arguments than we are. Under my arm now is a drum-wolf whose thud is tuned to my house’s prayer. I cock her against a stress ridge and stroke her once against her node so she knows I am grateful; then I release. A shock so low it is nothing in air moves in stone and makes everything with membranes shiver wrong for a heartbeat. A book-mouth lets go of my cousin; three nibblers curl and go still enough to be sorted. We eat and we study. We save the drum-wolf’s breath for hard days.

I have a herd, which is to say a following of grazers who have learned that when I press my belly and lift my right shoulder I am asking them to be patient and that patience is fed later. They harvest the whisker-fur we coax along our meadow seam; their polishing becomes our rope; their own plates crack and are sealed with resin in our laps until they stop leaking. Some of them wear bells of knapped glass not to make sound but to carry a small sting in their frills so they do not slip against a slick ledge. They like the song my ropes sing better than the neighbor’s; I pretend not to be proud.

Sometimes a house moves. Once in my lifetime—once in many lifetimes—the planet took a breath near us and held it. We heard the hymn of the stone thicken; the hallway to our east lost its honesty and began to lie. Seam-readers from every nest along our corridor pressed their faces to our faults and hummed in council. We peeled our dead carefully from their braces and turned them into stretchers, laid our copper plates along our ribs so the new walls would accept us faster, unbraided ropes we had thought were permanent, untied a dozen small oaths that had become songs you do in your sleep. We took our name out of the wall as far as we could, scratched it into a stick for what we could not carry, left rings of glass disarmed and humming empty and apologized to them.

I was the one who found the new seam. There is pride in that and also a permanent ache that is not unhappiness but the feeling of an old wall in my hands that will not be repeated. I lay with my ribs on a ledge no wider than a palm, in a letter-slit between two planes that had not met yet but were making up their minds, and I said my own name softly until I heard the rock answer no to three of its vowels and yes to five. That meant we could live. I brought the household one at a time—elders first with their braces, then children with their sharing ropes, then animals and plates and finally our last drum-wolf—and we introduced ourselves to the new planes with a tact that would have looked like worship to anyone who has never needed to be believed by a wall.

We are patient, but we do not have forever. We make calendars out of salt-fans and greening copper and the slow way a house’s ring deepens with age. In the nights that are all nights I count time by scars I know with my knees. I run my hands along the braces and recall my lineage in chord, low to high, left to right. My grandmother is a thick hum on the third brace, mica-faced and tart; my father is a thin proud streak on the fifth, basalt-quiet and stubborn; I am between them in pitch and not ashamed to be unoriginal.

We have a story we teach children about the Unheld, a volume so honest in its emptiness that no chord can sit in it, no rope can find teeth, no hand can bring a wall to yes. They hate and love this story. It itches at the part of them that wants to test cathedral rooms and be brave. When I was young in my plates and my hum still too clean I went to the edge of a giant’s chest—a room as tall as ten households where the hum had not yet been taught into obedience—and I did it. I carried three ropes, two friends, and a length of tourmaline like a claim. We flagged and sung until the first half of the room had a shoreline. Then we went one rope too far.

What I remember next is the feeling of all my whiskers going blind. Not silent—silence is a kind of sound we know well—but blind. No node to measure by. No sting to lean toward. I had a hand on a rope but the rope had forgotten who it loved. I did not die; we do not die of thirst here; we die of unmooring, and I brushed it with my face. My friend sang my name into the ground so hard he could taste blood. The drum-wolf we had brought as a joke and a hammer made the first serious thud of her life; a ripple returned from a ridge we had not counted and the room remembered how to be two things instead of none. I put a new chord in my pocket that day. It is the only music I carry that is not useful. I keep it to remind myself that faith is not a thing the wall has to learn; it is a thing you do with your hands while you wait for it to answer.

When we travel speak to us through the stone and we will answer. Our elders can put a name into the world and have it come back three days later in another ribcage; I can send a whisper two corridors over and not be rude. The bladders ferry our bits of copper, our broomed lichen whiskers, our resin beads warmed to friendliness by hands. Trade is a long sharing of ratios: I once swapped a tuning scratched along tourmaline for a plate so thin I could bend it over my own face and hear my pulse sing under it as if the house approved of me personally. I have never needed richer jewelry.

When ceilings fall—and they do, rarely, and heartbreakingly perfect—we make schools in the after-quiet. Children with cheeks buzzing learn from dead hinges and failed edges; they put their fingers on fractures and read not appetite but instruction. When an animal dies that we have lived beside a long time, we fold it into a brace if its hum is right; if not, we peel a plate clean and make a yard. We do not waste; not because we are pious, which is for seam-chapels and those who like to live in rooms that answer God instead of neighbors, but because waste is a tiny unheld and too many of those make a song you cannot lie down in.

If you asked me again, more plainly, where I would be and what I would be called, I would say this: find the corridor where hex columns swarm into a hive and lose their way into flutes; walk down to where the basalt’s sugar crust looks like hoarfrost and tastes like metal; feel with your wrist for the plane that runs truer than the other and choose the thinner room that kisses it. Put your right cheek on the fourth brace and wait for the chord that sits like a well-trained heart. That is One-Yes-Firm. Ask for the seam-reader whose plates carry a little glass at the edges and who keeps a drum-wolf asleep at her hip in tar. Press your mouth to the wall when you say her name so the wall can pass it along. If the answer you get back is a low sting followed by a soft drop—two notes with an ache between them—that is me.

You would find me half on my side on mica stairs, listening for a laugh in the crystal that means it will not punish a brace. You would find my hands full of comb and rope, my pockets dusty with clipped whiskers, my chest tuned to our house. If you stayed long enough I would lay your weight along our plates and let your bones learn the alphabet the crystals use. If you asked sweetly I would show you the star I keep wrapped in resin and cloth and the way to strike it at the beginning and the end of a life. I would teach you to make a door sound like keeping a promise. I would loan you a rope that sings well. I would, if you wanted, show you the edge of Unheld and give you my drum-wolf and my worst prayer, and then tie you to three points so you only fell as far as we have all fallen already and never past love.

What I am is a kept ember in stone, a listener with tools, a brace-singer who likes her house better than she likes the idea of anything that does not touch. What I have is time that barely moves and a job that needs neither hurry nor heroism, only the willingness to lean until the wall answers yes.
