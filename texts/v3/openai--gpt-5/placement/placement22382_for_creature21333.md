Where I would find myself first is not a spot on the map of the bowl but a posture.

Knees bent to the rope-line’s pull, shoulders lightly asking and giving to the bone pegs. Fingers out of their gloves for a breath and then back in because the dust finds bare skin and builds a film on it faster than sense would say. Chin tucked, mouth turned toward my own palm to keep words from going feral. The rest is a long learning of angles.

If I have to pick a place with a name, I live under the east veil, where the cliff’s breath is steadier and the gutters can be made to sing if you lean their mouths just so. Our door is a rib-bone stuck like a tongue between two teeth of tufa. The threshold is slate shaved smooth and greased every morning so the slush can’t grow teeth and creep. Inside is a hide-hearth like a sleeping animal and a shelf of bones wrapped in skin, white against the black of a good veil, heavy as a promise. The house was once a hollow of lace; my mother skinned it out of honeycombed tufa with a bone awl, my father pegged it brave with shin-stakes from a great-aunt who is now three useful things and a song we hum to warm our fingers.

People who don’t know us call us Drip-kin. We are a more particular about our belonging. I was Thumb-on-the-Old-Spout until the Old Spout slumped and taught a different hand its habit; after I learned to hear stone talk to itself, there were days when the bowl made me Ground-Listener, and I walked with a mallet and a care, asking the terraces what notch in their story they had reached. When I braided my gutter with another’s, I was Two-Hands-One-Throat until we unbraided it one winter with more patience than mercy and the ice forgot, as it always does if you teach it how.

As a child my skin smelled of grease and the dust’s small breath. My first whisk-broom was made of cliff-runner whiskers bound with sinew and a bead of crystal that sang when I tapped it with the back of a nail. I played the oldest game: catch a rime-moth without making it think you’re a wall, and then trade it a lick of oil for a wing-mark on your glove that will tell you, a beat later, where your ghost will place its hand.

Brushing is not a chore here. Brushing is what our hands do when our mouths would otherwise waste the bowl. We brush our own shoulders and our neighbors’ sleeves without looking, the way you would scratch a familiar dog while talking to its person. We brush tools so their edges don’t thicken with frost and change their minds mid-cut. We brush the ropes because a strand that fattens can become a betrayal. A hair-thin rind becomes a weight, and weight is a slow way of saying your name until it owns you.

Dark is a thing we keep under discipline like a good knife. We wrap it. We treat it like a guest who can’t be trusted not to wander into corners at night. When caravans come up slow with their foolish, kind beasts and their colors that drink sun like mouths, a child—any child, not a leader—lifts a veil and hands it without speech to the one most likely to make trouble and that one learns that here even generosity comes with rules: if you will be a square of night in our lantern, you are going to travel dressed.

My fingers learned glove-speech the way my lungs learned the bowl: by economy. The distance between two chests is enough for a quarrel and a reconciliation if your wrists can make the slowness of sorrow and the quickness of excuse. Words squander heat and frighten the walls; the dust likes a murmur and a sign. Children’s chants are all about brushing and keeping clear and meaner things like that. Even our jokes you work with your hands. The sign for Permanence is two lazy palms; the sign for Laziness is the same without the twitch that says you know you’re lying.

When I was twelve, I was given a shoulder-drip on the east wall to keep. It was mean at first, veining and splitting like a thought that kept interrupting itself. I spent a noon with it the way the rule says: gloves off, my cheeks greased to keep the dust’s bite from growing teeth there, eyes pretending to be ears. It learned me; I learned it. I walked my cup a finger-width, stroked the rock the way our elders teach the films with bone needles when they want the salts to move, and by the end of that day I could see the drip glancing toward me as if it had remembered I had a good story.

Everyone’s first romance here is with water. I had a sweetheart later—a Knotter with wrists so quick she could rehang a walkway in the time it took me to oil my eyebrows—but my first vow was to a line of wet that didn’t know I was a person. To ask someone to marry you is to ask if you may keep their drip while they sleep. I asked my Knotter, gloved, with both of us brushing each other’s backs out of habit while we went along the rope-line to move the market three pegs west. She said yes by setting her gutter mouth inside mine with a gravity that made even the moths pause, and we spend our wedding day learning what shared patience is.

The market is a river with a memory that forgets on purpose. It comes mid-bowl if that day’s safe terrace is mid; if the safest lip that morning is two shelves higher, then small sled-shops kiss the lines and climb, and the lines sing more because they carry more, and the bowl listens with its cliffs to see if anyone will go too fast. Prices are mouthed and signed; children on sinew leashes make us look like we are flying kites made of small stubbornness. The first time I held a coil of imported wood like a dowry I felt the heat leave my hand a little harder than it should have. Wood is a memory of a kind of world where you can let weight sleep on itself and call that okay. We spend it.

I have been the Ground-Listener for days at a time when the bowl asked me to. The bone mallet is white and soft as sound can be when it does not want to make a scene. You go tapping along the edges and the floors and the faces and the bowl tells you how it is tuning itself. There is a tone that means a shelf has grown a voice in its belly and needs a new peg for a rib. There is a note that means the breath behind the black mouth of the cave is inhaling and our monster—by which I mean the old warmth that licks our dreams until they are slick—has turned to speak a weather. A tap can warn a married gutter about to divorce itself of its own accord. When you hear that, you work fast and small, like someone loosening a knot in a child’s hair.

I have been in the cave that sweats and dreamed what its walls give to dirty hands. You go in through a curtain of water that pulls on your skin the way a lover does when they’re asleep and need you to become a different temperature. Behind it is a warren of rooms that smell like birth and metal and blue. The films there are thick enough to say a prayer with your palms; you come out with a glisten over your lids like a second skin learning your face. It peels a day later and underneath you see the bowl more like it sees itself: not as a danger, not as a crisis, not as a place with secrets, but as an arrangement of delays. Heat delays freeze; rime delays weight; hands delay failure; failure delays a fall long enough to become a change of plan.

I have had a rill-mouse sleep in my whisk-broom and wake into a scream like a kettle and be soothed with a dab of grease and a folded letter-beetle pellet that I pretended was a seed. The letter-beetles have chewed my veil in the night and left me a table of salts as neat as an offer. I have watched a glass-swallow draw lines in diamond dust and stick its beak into a drip I thought I owned and come away with a throat glowing one of the cave’s colors. I have seen the snowcat be carved by stillness into a statue and then shrug off its crystals in a small avalanche and become for a heartbeat what my ghost is to me: a thought that forgets it is not a body.

Mornings the bowl reads us our omens: a hole in the dust, shaped like speech, opens over the mid-terrace, and we tilt our market that way and make our talk shorter because even the dust is telling us to be brief. Our halos bloom and shrink around our hands like shy flowers; we ask each other how sick we are by the size of those rings and how much we want each other by the way we shape them. Sometimes my ghost walks beside me more assertive than I am and my Knotter taps my wrist—a private sign—and we both rest and do not go near the cave that week.

Avalanches happen the way a sentence ends: on time if you were listening to its breath. When a cornice cleaves off, the whole town holds its breath not because we are frightened but because we are polite. We let the bowl finish its thought. Then the dust finds its language again and we resume. There is a music to all of this; we make it with knuckles on tufa and crystals from the cave that hum when you stroke them. We make paint out of grease and set stories in frost on hides and on cheeks. They live an hour, a day if the bowl is kind, then they learn to own the one they are painted on and we brush them out before they can keep us.

Work is a slowness we do together. There are offices we step into the way you put your foot into a boot. If nobody is the Rope-Mover that hour, somebody will be. If no one is counting in glove-speech as we slide the market three sled-lengths along, a child will lift their hands and do it and we will obey because their wrists are correct. If the bowl goes bass in its belly, a mallet appears—not because there is authority but because there is aptitude—and the day shivers differently until the hollow place has been heard and given a peg and a name and a promise.

The rules are not posted; they stick in you like a rhythm you learned by dancing drunk beside someone steadier. Brush. Move. Keep your darks quiet and bound. Spend a noon with a new drip and forget you did it because it becomes the way your hands rest when they are not doing else. Listen with your eyes. There are more, and if I write them down they will become lazy; better to sign them while I pass you a cup.

I will die here. It is not a dramatic sentence. Death in the bowl is a shelf and a share of patience. My body will be greased so the crystals do not bite my last breath too hard. I will be laid on a slow drip that will learn me as I learned my first shoulder-water and return my humidity a drink at a time to my people. A child I braided a gutter with once will lick the inside of a cup and say, without being precious about it, this tastes like my elder, and then go brush the walkway because taste is information here, not sentiment. After a winter or a year my long bones will be made into tools or braces. My ulna would make a good scoop; my femur, split and pegged, will keep a shelf from slumping into the habit of gravity. My skull will hold a veil in the right wind for a dozen market days. People will rap me with a knuckle as they go by and I will sing back a little tufa-song, soft bells like laughter behind a mouthful of cloth.

My ghost will keep walking a beat late beside those I loved until their children learn to chase it and be scolded gently for startling the wall. A glass-swallow will fly through a ring of heat my granddaughter makes by lifting her hand and my granddaughter will say, with a pride that fits in a sign this big, the one that means mine and kept safe and not over-owned, that was my elder’s trick with the halos. Somewhere a rill-mouse will steal hair from my whisk-broom and line a nest with it like gossip and I will leave the bowl by being too useful to go.

Who or what I am, then? I am a Keeper, which is to say I belong to motion. I keep drips the way a beekeeper keeps bees—not by putting a lid on them but by convincing them old paths are new again. I keep light from eating holes in things. I keep ghosts company. My name changes with work, as everyone’s does. On avalanche days I am All-Hands, the one whose stupid wide feet learned exactly where the slick is friend and where it is treachery. When the bowl tunes, I am the ear. When my Knotter tightens a line over my head, I am the breath timed under her pull. At night with a song in my palm I am the wall’s lullaby and I say into my own warm skin the counts that keep our market from clipping a shelf it has been courting for a week.

Where I am is under the gentler side of the bowl, second shelf from the floor with a view of the mid-terrace where the hole sometimes opens in the dust, where children can be pulled on their belly boards without courting the black ice, where the gutters—mine and others’—braid and unbraid like narratives. Where I am is also on the rope-line halfway up the west face when the sun skims the cliff-shoulders and the small winds start from melt and the dust wakes and the market shifts as if in its sleep. Where I am is sometimes at the mouth of the black cave with blue on my fingers and a humming crystal in my teeth so I can feel the note as bone. Where I am is a bone peg you will not notice until it is not there one morning and the shelf has crept an inch and your feet tell you the story of that inch with their knots.

Maybe I am a small wind. Maybe I am the beat-late silhouette that lets a child know how fast they are moving. Maybe I am the brushing we teach our hands to do and the fact that our hands learn it so fully they teach each other without us. The exact choice doesn’t matter as long as I am something this place remakes and that remakes it back.

Call me anyone whose hands learned to be weather. Call me the slowness that keeps a place from pretending crisis is its only song. Call me a light eater of wet. Call me by tapping your knuckles on the stone-lace so it sings. I will answer by turning my mouth to my palm and letting the warmed word out the right size for this air.
