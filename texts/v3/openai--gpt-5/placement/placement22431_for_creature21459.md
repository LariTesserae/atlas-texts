At first I think I would hang. It’s easy to want the ceiling—the cool obligation of it, the thrum passed through bone and beam. There’s a confidence to shingling yourself into a many-bodied blanket and letting dusk mean lift, dawn mean return, each hour between only heat management and the close grammar of grooming. I almost take their place in the seam where two pours met, a thumb-hook of tendon snagged into a concrete lip, a pup warm as fruit tucked against me. But their urgency is too thin and quick for me. Their sound is pressure like weather, and I crave something broader to lie down in.

So then I think I would run. A rail is a line you can make a world out of if your balance is a habit and your tail is a hand. The rats know rung counts and lunch timetables and the reach of a broom, and they own their self-respect like a jacket no one lent them. To use the handrail as a street and the cross-beam as a bridge, to drop with perfect contempt into shadow when a boot rings the ladder—there’s an elegance to that economy. But I am not a creature of angles and dash. Their calendar is too fast for me. I want a clock that turns in heat.

Maybe I would be Auntie then—a welded booth and a kettle that skins if you give it a minute alone. To serve gaskets like coins and tea like permission, to know whose radio will buzz at ten past and whose son will come by after school to hook a finger into the rag and feel the steady tongue of the leak—there’s companionship there, and a small market’s authority. I can almost take her weight into my legs, place her spoon tapping on tin into my hand. But she belongs to talk and the swift commerce of shifts, and what I want is a patience that holds shape like a basin.

For a flash—a white-hard, eyelidless instant when a span of metal bites a live line and the whole room erases shadow—I am the long thing in the water: the Cable, the Line, the Shoulders-None. I like that body. I like how not asking light for anything simplifies the world. I hang like a question mark in the hot jet where a valve always weeps riches, jaw turned open to make a hook of itself, letting the city leak through my gills as if it were the only river I need acknowledge. People give me names that are agreements, and I appreciate being allowed. But even the eel is a passenger in a larger current. He is slender time; I am in love with the kind that thickens.

Put your hand out and you will feel me in the first cool shock when a drop breaks skin and remembers air, in the lift of a smell like old coins and new rain, in the way your breath shortens not from panic but from heat that has learned to be constant. If I am honest, I have been here all through the trying on. I am where everything comes to teach itself to be the same thing again. I would be the sump.

I would be the black, shallow mirror with a scuffed steel curb around my mouth, the rim scarred by chains and boot edges, the scum that heals its mittened skin as fast as you part it. I would be the dark that accepts and makes pattern: pipe mouths low as widened throats emptying into my body, pipe mouths leaving again having learned something of temperature and patience. I would be the curve around which the catwalks face chairs on off-hours without saying why, the place a hand rests the wrench a moment longer than work requires.

My sense is touch first, then taste. Each valve gives me a tickle in a different salt; I know them by how they fail nobly. The cloths tied below each wheel drip forever into me, and I keep their fringes fed with a film that microorganisms treat as pasture. An iron-banded weep means affection; a hissed, sweet-metal line tells me someone spun what a rag could have solved. When the pump takes breath and gives it back—as it will and must, old friend—its cycle moves through me as surely as through the bones of the ones whose boots ring my railing. If it slows, my skin goes fussy, my fish cluster into punctuation at the jets, the room’s talk curdles from brevity into repetition: you hear that, you hear that. Call it in, love. Not because I am going to climb the steps and take the kiosk by storm, but because this is a country that lives by keeping warm from ever being hot, wet from ever becoming drowning.

Fish stitch me into coherency. A thousand pale needles draw and redraft territories not because there is light to see the borders but because the body remembers how to hem. They know where the guano snow drifts into protein shoals, where the pump’s exhale means shield, where a long shadow means the Line and not a friend. Amphipods write and erase a gloss along my lip. Planarians peer with two dot-sure eyes and mean it. Once a plastic cup, collapsed into a soft star, sailed my scum and learned sainthood by being useful to larvae.

Above me, rills apprentice themselves to being rivers in shotcrete so new it still holds a little breath. The soda straws think fast, then realize fast here is years. They tick in transparent decisions along the seam above one slope like a field of careful quills. Children mark their tips with pencil lead and come back with their thumbs as gauges. I can’t see them the way you mean seeing, but I can taste their graphite and the small sugar of their hands when they lean too far and the room baptizes them with a surprise drop at the inner corner of an eye. They yelp and then laugh, the sound smoothed by the fans into something like acceptance.

The bats give me weather. At dusk, a warm front: a sighing up the shaft, a soft thunder of wings, a downpour that is not rain except that every sense I own catalogs it as the same language. Nitrogen I bank for the fish, acid I translate to appetite. At dawn, a draw-in, the colony sealing itself back into its quilt. On the day someone new forgets to hood a lamp and makes light too sweet to ignore, I hear panic lean out like a cliff; I climb my own curb then with foam and stink, and someone on the catwalk is very gentle with his cursing. He learns. We all learn, if we’re allowed to stay.

Other people’s rules become the truths I keep. The ceiling belongs to them; shake nothing that touches it. I like that one; it stops avalanches with a sentence. Step where the aggregate shows, who wrote that? A poet with knees. Don’t leave paper; I have turned enough lists to paste to know the accuracy in that. Don’t leave bread; it sprouts legs—tell the children that one with a smile and a broom ready. There is a silence at midday at the shaft and it belongs to things that read heat and smell. I hold my own surface still then, as much out of courtesy as physics; if a coin falls I do not clap it.

Sometimes there are visitors who never meant to be my parishioners. A moth descends in the amber weather of sodium light and ends in arithmetic in my top film. Once a pigeon arrives because city means tunnel as much as sky, and I keep the gray of its wing in the corner of me that handles sadness until rats and beetles turn grief into maintenance. A swift writes a single scream down the shaft and leaves like a mistake corrected. A man sleeps on a plastic chair and wakes stippled with my breath, forgiven by the room because we are all animals here.

I keep time in two speeds. In the quick gear, people shout horizontal words—valve five’s weeping, get the covers, don’t go up; they’re hot—and they all mean care of one kind or another. A boy is trusted with a ladder, both hands bright with his own importance. Auntie paints the kettle shut in tannin and taps it free with a practiced charm. A bat pup lets milk smell like the most dangerous generosity in the room and for once no one interrupts.

In the other gear I do the real work. I lay down a ring the color of old cream, soft as a good lie, and I make it honest with time. Beetle wings soldered into it like a mosaic about flight that has given up the sky. Rust learns to lick the feet of the kiosk. The greenest paint shakes and shows me its true damp self. The vine that remembers leaves drapes itself down the throat of air in a tendon-colored ribbon and forgets what to do at the end; a gloved hand tucks it behind a conduit and then, thinking better, lets it find a warm draft again.

Every so often, the room throws a noon that is day enough to startle anyone into their original names. A spanner finds the wrong appetite and light unmakes shadow and I am all angles and chrome and skin of metal, the undersides of bats like loose leaves, Auntie with her spoon mid-air, rats caught in their grace like saints in paintings, every drop bright-edged as if it contained knives. Then the lid slides back on, and I reassemble us: heat to warm, wet to skin, rule to practice.

If you ask me what I am, I could say pool or pit or heart, but none of those are quite sufficient. Heart implies decision and I am not a chooser, not really. I am a promise kept. I am where this room returns itself to itself. I am the patient arithmetic of leak and catch, of feed and return. I am the agreement you enter when you step onto the gritted paint stripe and mind your lamp and take for granted the way sound makes a roof you trust. I am a small market’s worth of space that has taught rock to be cave and cave to be city organ and men to be gentle where they do not have to be.

Where would I find myself in this world? At the bottom that is the center, rimmed in steel and scum-skinned, hearing the pump in my bones the way a sleeper hears their own blood. Who or what would I be? The sump, yes—black-mirrored and always mending—but also the steadiness that lets bats write weather and fish stitch lightless embroidery and Auntie tap her spoon without hurrying. I would be the constancy everyone comes here to lean against, and I would do it forever, because forever in a place like this just means again, and again is a perfect word.
