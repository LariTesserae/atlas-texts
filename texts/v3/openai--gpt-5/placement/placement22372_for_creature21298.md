At first I look for myself in the bright places—the traveling panes of sweetness that the falls sling between the basalt. I think I might be that skin, that brief, cool arithmetic that teaches water to stratify. I imagine what it would be to be all edge: to bead a hand with resistance for the length of a breath, to change the taste of a tongue, to tick a thousand bubbles into hurry. A halocline is a road without a rope, after all. If I were it, I would be every corridor at once, the way choices braid and unbraid in a morning’s work.

Then I try on being something that keeps: one of the globed bellies that the city lifts and lowers along its spine. To be a basket-lung filled with rain, to rise while being made heavy and fall while my weight is drunk away. To have a tether that knows my end points better than I do. To be a measure by which neighbors talk about what is ripening and how much mercy the storms had. That would be a good life: repetitive, tested, the city’s breath made visible.

I even consider the oldest roles in the place: the chalkers, those milky lines of patience that lay stone where there was only permission. I could be the film that takes the hint of a lattice and memorizes it in mineral. I could be the slow kind of maker whose hands are molecules. To grow a wall the way a thought grows a sentence; to leave a pale beard on the sharp rock; to turn water’s freedom into a curve that has a use. It is tempting to be that slow.

It is tempting, too, to be the law. The rope-books are heavy, and they smell like sweetness and salt that have forgiven each other. A quarrel there is not heat but demonstration; lineage is a twist your fingers can read; a season revises an old idea by simply hanging it beside a new one and letting winter have an opinion. To inhabit law that is hand-taught and publicly touched would be to be necessary without being loud.

I taste the desire to be the lantern that combs light for children in night-sickness. I try the big bellied jelly on for a minute, just to feel the mass of contraction and the way a valley’s worth of water answers you when you squeeze it. I test the grazer’s patience up the ridge, stone-sleek and deliberate, rasping the chalk-beards and going on. I stand inside a net and feel how it sings to a long swell, a song so low you can only hear it with your wrist.

But the place where I keep coming back, where my hands know where to be before I’ve decided, is the edge of the ridge where the steps end and the drop begins.

If you come looking for me, you will find me there, a little below the faint geometries of the other cities, half in a veil that tastes of last week’s lightning, half in the unlit mouth that swallows names. I am a cutter of stairs. A bone-listener. One of the people whose knuckles look like old glass and whose dreams ride the nets when we come home.

The day begins against a warm column, not a hearth but a promise that my joints will answer me. I press my hands into the hum of it until the seam-sense along my ribs softens and the ache that lives in the small bones loosens. The midwives pass; I offer my wrist for a greeting and feel the tug-spoken question in it: calm water? I answer with a small, agreeing pull. Calm enough for the work I mean to do.

I dress in the way we all do, but with the extra wariness of those who will lose the city’s skin under them by midday. Kelp-skin, worked until it is tough as habit. Skate-skin gloves, rasp-sewn, that let me love a sharp place without bleeding for it. My hair is my pantry and my counterweight both: black rope plaited thick and hitched through with slate chips and shell and the cooled, improbable tear-drops of deep glass, each one a memory with heft. The belt is a loom of bladders and stones. A novice wears rocks like apology; I carry mine like verbs, sliding them along braided guides to make my body speak the grammar the water asks for. I tie on my coils of line and the packet of tar that smells like old thunder. I sheathe my obsidian edge inside kelp that will not lick it dull. I slide the ray-spine drill into its wrapped place, point toward the floor, as we are taught.

Last comes the jaw. It is someone’s grandmother’s. The strap fits across my brow where the bone is thick, the tooth-curve lying against the place that hums. Bone talks to bone. Through it the ridge tells me what it will permit today. Through it I hear the long, patient moaning of sheets that are cooling past some remembered tension. Pop, as clean as a clay pot’s rim struck with a stick, from a fin that decided its stress another way. These are not warnings and not invitations. They are weather. I do not own them. I tuck them under my own.

Down the catenary lanes we go, my partner for the day on the line ahead and a third behind, all three of us talking with the rope. Not loudly; a tremor, a courtesy. We traverse the blade of a fin, fingers to edge, fingers to edge. We pause in a brighter corridor and let the sweet roll us. I can taste the lightning in it, a bitter-metal sparkle at the back of the face. It makes a person feel tidy. It makes fronds wild. It will be a weeding week for the veil-tenders.

We pass a planter, a loose-hung basket stuffed with old pith and hair-moss where pale lettuces drink the veils. I can name the hands that wove that basket because its bias is the way Eshra’s bias always is, a hitch there that makes the curve a little proud of itself. There is pride allowed in the hung gardens. In the ropes that touch cliff to cliff, the pride is in what they do not do: they do not surprise you after the celebrating is over.

The last net under us is the street that knows my childhood foot. I grew the height of my mother’s palm in one season, right there where a knot sits that has always brushed the left shin. Then we are past it and in the open, where the basalt is new and unrubbed and the difference between thinking and not thinking is cut into you if you don’t keep your measuredness on. The first steps are familiar. They are angular and unofficial. Each toe finds where a toe like it found before.

Work is small and deliberate. We do not so much make a stair as teach the rock what a foot would like, and then wait while it thinks about conceding. I shave a whisker off a lip with the obsidian, a slice cold enough to sting. We make a pocket with the clam-adze and a crack with a chisel of old bone. The ray-spine drills where basalt gives the least courtesy, and in that bore I set a peg of shell and rope it to a knot, and that knot to a lifeline, and that lifeline to a belt-stone that will say yes or no at the right moment. There is tapping, there is resting. We carry chalk in little leather bellies and smear it thin where the water would prefer to be a knife, coaxing build-ups that, over seasons, will remember us.

This is not a dramatic craft unless you make it one, and then it devours you. The one fast thing we fear is the foolish hurry we are all born with. I slide a bladder to lift my hips against the slope; my partner lets hers slack and settles the new notch with her weight; we trade, we tug-speak, we share the little courage of a good knot. When a plate lets go—I feel it coming with the browbone before I hear it—we pull tight and still. Ten thousand knives tumble into a pocket so deep that light is an insult there. There is a deep thud as the world below considers the gift and eats it. We breathe through our ribs. The slits at my collar flutter as if I were a small, practical storm. Then the hum goes back to being hum, and the work goes back to being work.

At midday we do what must be done and is never made into a performance. We untie the old dead from the step that was the farthest of last year. The rope has a patient rot where sweet kissed it once; we have to cut her out with as much respect as we tied her in. The skin is half the sea now. Her hair is rope in a different sense. We bind what is left to the new place—the one I shaved and drilled an hour ago, the pocket that smells like a struck stone. We knot her into tomorrow. She is not diminished by this; she is employed. The ridge is greedy and we are, too, but we have learned how to make that greed behave itself.

Something luminous passes above and for a moment all the lines get ideas. The children in the far hall will be gasping with the delight of it, and I am glad I will be the story of the jelly’s bell tonight for someone who counts my voice a safe place. I am not worried about gods and their sizes. I am worried about the angle of this corner, which needs to be a fraction less proud if a hand is to love it.

By afternoon the thin layers are making their little jokes with my blood. We come up between veils with our bellies slow, unspooling stones from hip to shoulder to change us back toward the kind of animal that sleeps wrapped with others. The pressure changes by hair-thicknesses and I can feel my bubbles think about mutiny and then be persuaded to patience by being held warm and still for the time it takes to name a handful of grandmothers. The city appears the way choices appear when you have earned them, one net at a time. We cross a plaza strung around a warm column, and the skin of the place thrums under my hands like something alive.

Back at the nets that ring the elders, I am spooled into one with two of the other cutters and we go quiet in the way our kind does: we hand-speak lazily if we must, but mostly we let the hum of the ridge talk through the jaw and the ribs and the lines. Children drift near and then still, respectful not in the face-discipline sense but because our dreaming gets loose and climbs. The net carries it from palm to palm. Later I will wake slag-heavy and no one will hurry me; the season has already agreed to what it will and will not do.

On other days, when the edges are unhungry, I go to the veil-tenders and lift and tie and taste. The falls tell me what they told me when I was very young and certain their sweetness had been made for me alone: this storm had teeth; that one could not remember its business; the land above is throwing us the right kinds of leaves this week. I adjust a bladder-frond so that it does not crowd a tender spinach into bitterness. I stake a rope further into a fresher slit so the infants will have something easy when their first halocline is asked of them. My hands feel too large around babies and too awkward around grief, so I bring both to the midwives and I carry the bowls they ask me to carry.

We do our politics by lifting, mostly. Two spans across a hard mouth will be hung side by side until one of them fails to deserve survival. We do not write speeches; we wear belts that tell a life’s arithmetic at a glance. My belt has marks for depths and small glass gifts tied on where I made better choices than expected. There is a short, dark thing hanging near my hip that is not a tool and not a trophy, only a stone that fit my hand so perfectly one day that I could not not keep it. Everyone understands that sort of compromise.

Lovers tie themselves together for a day’s work because there is no other way to learn what you want to learn about another person. Weight is not a metaphor where I live. It is a negotiation. To give someone else the right to slide a bladder along your side is to say a gentleness that mouth-sound would unmake. My best work is the slow stuff; my best rescues are the quiet ones. The child whose knot I covered with my own hand until her fingers could feel the fatigue that mattered? She tells everyone I sang to her. I did not. But I put her palm into the water the right way and the water did the singing.

On law days we wash the ropes in sweet and press them under stones while the current makes proof of our patience. We add a cord with the names of a family braided into it, and I learn a new letter with my thumb. A dispute is a loosened knot retied with hands that agree to be slow. It is not that we dislike anger. We simply understand how heavy it is, and how far it will drop if you pretend it isn’t.

People ask me, sometimes, what I see when I look down past the last lip. They want an answer with teeth in it. They want a creature or an empire or a fear that wears a crown. I tell them true: black that is not emptiness but secrecy, cold that is not malice but habit, an invitation that is not to me alone. They are disappointed and relieved. Later, when they lean their heads against the straps and let the bone-listening in, they hear what I hear: that the rock keeps its promises and sometimes does not, and that both lessons are true at once.

Night is not the word. There is nothing to do with the surface’s clock here, only the slow syllables the ridge reads us. The lantern combs express themselves in their small, generous way and children count the rungs of the city by light instead of by remembered tug. We eat weeds boiled hard to make them tender and lick mineral from our lips until we are satisfied. We share sour pith that has turned to bread from turning and turning in patient eddies. If someone has trapped a chalk-grazer this season, we give them work in the thanking, because to take animal is to be heavy with vow afterward.

When the jelly comes through, blue as myths I do not tell, we do not kneel. We lean to feel what its passing does to the long water and we take the lesson if there is one. The gods we keep are pocket-sized. The warm columns, which answer to tending. The knots in which we find our dead in our hands. The way two nets hung beside each other will admit the better idea without a quarrel because winter is the only judge we answer to with any heat in it.

So: where am I in all this? I am where the ridge is still stubborn, just below the last remembered foothold. If you come with a line and a patient belt, if you have brought the jawbone and the habit of listening, if you have agreed with yourself to be measured and to prefer more making to quick fixing, you will find me there. I will be shaving a lip no one else would notice, setting a peg in a deep cuss of basalt, threading a rope through a hole that will be a hand-hold for a person who has not yet been born. I will be humming with the place where warmth is a location and not a tool. I will be a long, careful no to the fastest kinds of fear. I will be the yes that takes a season to say.

Who am I? A little of the water’s religion and a little of the rock’s. A vertebra in the city’s patience. A hand that teaches darkness to accept one more step every year. A keeper of other people’s fatigue until their hands know it as their own. An arrangement with gravity written in bladders and stone. A person whose best self can be found wherever the rope vibrates faintly under a steady pull, wherever a fresh law has just been tied into an old one and made snug, wherever the dead are being invited along in a way that hurts and helps at once. If you sit with your back to a fin at the settling hour you might hear me without knowing that’s what it is: the tiny tones that come through bone into bone, a punctuation in the ridge’s long sentence.

I will need a long time. The ridge has it. I have hands.
