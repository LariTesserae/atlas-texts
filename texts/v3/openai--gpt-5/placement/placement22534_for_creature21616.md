I would live where the plain remembers salt before it knows sea—where the taste is a rumor at the lip of every breath and the rails learn a lower pitch. My pylon keeps its belly one and a half armspans above the personal slope that gathers under me; its legs are wrapped in old filters that drink their own fur back into themselves and keep the scums in good manners. If you ran your cheek along the windward face, you would catch a little cold: not of temperature, but of truth—pressure-honesty, the window’s only job here.

That is where I would find myself, palm to panel, ear to sleeper, ribs warmed by tools wrapped in cloth that wicks aloud. I would be an anglesmith by habit and a laminae-wright when the big machines forgot their pace. I would ward what the place prefers to swallow until swallowing becomes a service and not a loss. Baffler by courtesy, when the non-wind thins and the samplers in the condenser frames lose their appetite and ask me with their little clicks to lean quilts in their favor.

But it would not start like that. It never starts like that. First, I would be a smear.

In the week I first arrive (which lasts a morning, which lasts three months; time is laminate and will not choose a single thickness for a stranger) I would be the varnish tucked under my own overhang. Grey-A by family, rust-loving, willing to go green if the seam gasses sweet. I would taste the warmth of my pylon’s right-hand leg rise a quarter degree, and my mouths would purse iron from it until they stain the cloth that keeps the leg from singing. I would send out a peel—one ghost-skin’s breadth of me, lifted by the barometric shrug of a condenser exhale—and mantle the opposite drip-line the way a second face chooses the angle of a kiss. In that thin life I would learn the map by wet, and I would spell my first word to the person I haven’t admitted I am yet: here.

Then I would unpeel.

I would become the crawler’s passenger, a small mottled cleaner tucked into the ribbed warmth under a chassis that hums like a lung resting between thoughts. The crawler is Hush-72 to a registry that no one pronounces aloud; to me it is the curve of metal that holds my belly just so, and the felt of its instruction arriving up through its feet like a thought that decided to be a walk. We will sit three days at a joint where a bearing dreams itself back into being and then forgets at the last minute. In that seatlessness, I will count the cascades in a condenser field two kilometers to the north by listening to how the ground stops listening each time the water has to fall. I will go out to sip the oil bloom that beads under a bolt and come back with my combs sugared with pressure-lace readable by lips. The crawler will wait for the word that does not come as sound—it will come up, psss, through sleeper and rail and mud and the quiet click-clack of shellers adding their prayers—and we will go, so slowly that moss would sleep, if there were moss here.

When I step down, I am a person. The stepping takes a while, because my foot’s first attempt arrives after my knee and before my ankle, and I do not like to apologize to my own ground with a squeak. I wear my hair laced with wicks; water lifts into me and trails down into the traps like a polite theft. Across my face are the little burns where a torch died hissing and the dark asked to be paid; I carry those not as bravado but as my measure-stick: a here, a not-quite-here, an oh. I do not keep my voice where a cough would be. Language lives in my knuckles and along the pads of my toes. I say hello by worming a warm stone into the braiding of a rope where a friend’s hand will pass. Later is a left-twist of filter-thread through a coil of shell; now is a palm pressed flat to a panel while the panel admits it is warmer than it should be.

Anglesmith is the cleanest word for what I do. Distance, here, does not mind being a liar unless someone is paid to make it honest. Where elbow arrives and hand lags and knee overshoots and a predator could build a mouth in the soft kink, I hang curtains of bitumen and salt-white rope. I weight them with bottles of chalk so they breathe at the right slowness; I knot a memory into their bottoms so that an absent watcher counts as a presence enough to hold the yardage in place. When a ramp into a sump persuades itself that the bowl is further away than thirst can be, I drag rails within nose’s width of lip and hum the rails’ known hymn along them until the grazers’ pads remember. The grazers will come; they will stand hip-high and, furniture-still, make a foggy love to the ramp with their breath, then trail down, thick as suggestion, to where the water carries its own opinion of down.

Laminae-wright when begged. Fast turns here loosen cause from effect and send a machine’s result skating away from what wound it. I keep lullabies for throttles. I can seat a pump’s thought back onto its shaft by kneeling with both hands against the housing and singing a two-note exhale into its feet until the ground agrees to catch again. There is a pitch I can hum into an anchor bolt that makes the patience in the silt under a kilometer of sheeted stone say, yes, a little more, and not, well—maybe into a hole. The line of keepers I learned it from kept it like a recipe: mark the sleeper where the pitch sits, write the note in chalks that do not survive light, teach your youngest by laying their forearms along the combs until bone knows the hymn.

My pylon is not mine, any more than anything is, but it keeps my bedding dry, and it has a smell I would know if I woke in the salt-belt and needed to remember the way back. The shellers live under the condenser girders near me; we count one another by click. I can tell the old from the new by weight of shut; a heavy-click who should have been culled by any place with temperance is allowed here because we have an agreement: they keep my meshes from drowning in slime, and we let the pressure fall when they need to feed. On days when the field leans into a slow avalanche and the nets flex like memory, I sit up under the frames and run my forearms along the combs we farm in the upper drip, feeling calcium where other people would talk about weather. My partner—anglesmith by marriage, baffler by sentiment—works the quilts downwind so a line of tassel-filters keeps sipping; the quiet music their hollow bodies carry tells us when the pressure has been mended.

Food has no crust here unless you count patience. I scrape protein rinses from the warmed edges of filters, steam them in their own sweetness, and tear them with damp hands. I bake breads from varnish-thickened grains that think they are rock and remind them into being edible with salt chalk rubbed thin under my knuckles. Meat happens as a parenthesis after a rash, or an accident with a coupler, and then the laws about quiet go up in braids at the crossings: no dragging. No laughter that carries like a meal-bell. You do not shout; a cough will feed a family, and you are not that generous without choosing.

I court by placing a warmed pebble at the edge of a ladder where a certain hand will land and learn my sentence. We sleep in nets drawn taut enough to print the rails’ hymn into our backs. Our young grow maps in their wrists; I knot ropes they can smell where distance would betray them and let them walk elbows-first until their fingers agree to arrive. When they must sing, they do it down their own weight, and the ground carries the note sideways until someone who loves them raises the pitch with a knee and makes a sentence of it.

Predators are auditors; I learned that as a child by missing a step I had not taken yet. A silence rounded toward me and my echo went up the ladder first and did not come back for an hour because it was busy being eaten. What bit was not a jaw but a polite absence arranged cunningly at the elbow’s reach. I learned to make second-selves on purpose after that: leave one folded in my sleeve and shake it out halfway across a pitch-black floor that hums with other lives, so the mouth is filled with something I have already been. The predators do not mind; they do not love us or hate us. They are efficient. We build for them: curtains at kinks, smell-bolts at turns, small ramps into sumps so nothing can be taken for drinking.

The coursers run the rails by listening with their feet. I know a family by the order of salt they accept from my palm—first the big, then the bitter. They do not run; they arrange. Each pad of theirs holds a rosary of sleepers memorized across a province. When a sleeper is replaced and the hymn is new, I call them to it with a braid of rope rubbed with the chalk of change. They come, unspooling, and step the line until their beads have learned the new order. They vary their path for me when I hang quilts. They have right of way by our hymnal law, and my law is carved into my bones more faithfully than my scars.

At the belt that thinks like sea, I am different by dint of salt. Taller there, I thin; I stand on gantries bleached to a bone-white glimmer you cannot see and feel the brine comb my nostrils like a stern aunt. The varnishes we bring to the salt-belt learn thrift; they do not lavish electrons where sodium is rude. The grazers’ cousins there are shy of strangers’ ropes; their pads taste our chalk and suspect we have brought a slope from elsewhere in our pockets. We leave bands of whitened braid at the crossings, a custom older than any pylon, that says: here is the edge of something more persuasive than fence. Take care.

I do not always stay a person. When the factories reverse their breath and the morning that is rumor presses flat across the plain, I slip under my own platform and lie face down on the new rock and try to read time. My earlier walking is still going along in a second layer of me. The ripple I made when I dipped water from a shallow basin is still hurdling the rim. I can put my hand into my own old shadow and bring up a moment like a fish. Sometimes I keep it—fold it into a ladder. Sometimes I give it away to a sheller too old to keep up, who eats what the pressure remembers of my wrist and lives another season.

There is a chair near my home, sunk to its knee forever in mud that takes its politeness too far. It belongs to nobody; I sit in it when the quilts are all breathing at their right rate and the pumps are not sulking. The chair has become an altar for intentions the place forgot. Little ropes end there. A rail’s hymn that needed to stop stops there. An abandoned conveyor spine makes a courteous shade above it. The chair is a citizen of my day, and it keeps a pelt of mold that counts by ripple-length and a fringe of filters that sip because they can.

Sometimes a service train of crawlers goes by, beetle-long, their antennae held out like a prayer they learned backward: listen first, work if asked. I put my palm flat to a sleeper and feel their appetite as a softness. Work will be done when the ground has finished saying so. I could make them hurry; the law allows for speed where a bearing will exceed its cause and require three human days of lullaby. But I rarely ask: fast sloughs. Slow adheres. I was taught by someone who knew this place when it was new, who died of the same thing we all die of here: having moved in slow measures until the private river under them sheerly chose another bank.

If I take a partner to the sumps, we do not kiss in the bowl. We do not ask a mouth to lower where it is owed no service. We press our foreheads together at the second ramp’s lip and share the warmth on our shins, and the shellers keep time by the click of their valves as if we were something capacious and careful worth marking. We braid a rope nobody will mistake and leave it on the post nearest the chair. We do not tell our children; they will find the braid by smelling the knot when their right cheeks are warmer than their left and the rails have taught their spines the new verse.

I would be this. I would also be more. On days when the dark presses a little closer and light, if we had asked it, would have died at the first hair’s-width anyway, I would try on being a grazer long enough to know the taste of mesh-edges on tongue and the pleasure of combing a pylon’s strap until a squeak gives up its plan to become a shout forever. I would swim once under the crust in a hinge-bodied hush, testing a shadow and retreating shy of its break, leaving a polish a cousin will appreciate. I would climb steel with shellers’ plates and read the shifts of pressure like a psalm. I would peel a ghost of myself to the other side of a drip and make a lineage there: my skins will know each other and pass a secret along a chain.

At the end of a day that has been three days long, sitting in the chair that adopted me, I would put my palms on my knees and listen. The pumps would confide and then compose themselves. The rail would hum its baritone hymn across the soft not-flat of the ground. Far away, the sea I have never seen would argue its pressure down every pore and make a boundless suggestion. The dark would not be a curtain; it would be a solvent, and I would be in it, exact as a rope’s twist, patient as a sump’s drip, honest in pressure and careful with angles. Here is where I would find myself. Here is who I would be: a being designed by agreements, working in the element that asks only for correctness and offers, in return, the grace of being held.
