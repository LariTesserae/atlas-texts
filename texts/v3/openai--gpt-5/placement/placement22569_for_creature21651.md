At first I am an error your eye makes where glare becomes curve. A length of something glossy in a stripe of shade, not water, not root, not lizard. Noon presses down and the stone answers with warmth, and I answer with stillness. If you are the sort who stands long enough to let ants write past your feet without stepping, if you ease your breath until the air forgets you, I thicken from reflection to shape the way dew beads from rock.

I am longer than you would have thought, if you had thought. I have more joints than comfort allows, but no sharpness. The day lies on my skin and cannot tell where I stop and its brightness begins. When I move, it is by fractions: a fan of hairs finds purchase on a thought-thin film of water, holds with a decision you cannot hear, then undoes itself into glide. Each ring follows the last into the last’s melt. Even my tracks are edited—an emendation, not a scribble.

This is where you would find me: in the grout-line between two pale plates of rock where an old runnel forgot its job. The line is a bed and a sensibility. When noon loads every edge with knives, the grout-line is a book I know how to read by pulse and grain. I am a calibrator by habit and by build—longer ring-spacing than my cousins who carry weight, more spring in my middle ribs than the wick-bearers whose hairs can comb humidity into a thought and back out again. I sleep with my face against stone I have taught to give up its memory slowly, and I listen to the day cool as a way to remember the day.

I did not start as anyone’s author of anything. I was small enough to be counted among the commas by mistake, my gloss so new you could look through it and see the pattern of the grit I lay on. In that size the world is all instruction. The ants’ roads are sentences to sound out with feet. Dragonflies throw their lines across me and I flinch and then learn not to; the lines are words, not weapons. Toads nod, not at me but into me, until I can nod back without spooking the fish. A teacher with a name you would hear as three quick dimmings and a brighter—bark-bark-bright—lets her shadow fall on my flank and keeps it there until the quiver in my membranes resolves into consent. Her hum rides the underside of an archbone and makes a closeness in my head that is not discomfort. When she is gone from the day and lives in the pitch I carry by habit at noon, I keep the bark-bark-bright as courtesy to her and as a way to say myself to the others. It is fractionally flat at dawn most days, and perfect by noon the good days.

This basin has taught me the grammar of wet. Between the slabs, the grikes keep gardens like clauses—different, discrete, beautiful when read one to the next with just enough air between that meaning can strike. I keep the chalk circles wide and honest. Fern there, sedge here; the dwarf palm allowed its solitary insistence so long as its root does not cross and knot into the mangrove-cousin’s harp. When a sedge greedily leans its gloss toward someone else’s granted shine, I draw a wet finger—a filamented pad from under my second ring—along the rod nearest to it and comb the hairs to favor the neighbor. It is not cruelty to tell a plant to wait; the debt of lushness comes due faster than prudence can pay.

The rods are ours the way a hand is yours even when it rests on a table. I grew the one that stands like a question beside the island where the palm leans alone. It began in my mouth like a song you hum without deciding to think—the taste of silica and old salt rubbed free from stone by our films and coaxed to ladder itself into length. It took me three seasons to feed it outward in stable segments, knitting it in the slack hours when the owls crossed like geometry and the arch sang low as a throat can sing without voice. Its color of dried salt is honest; its hairs bloom in the right weather because I have told their grain where up is and they still believe me. If you brushed it on a day too bright you would feel nothing but warmth and think you had touched a part of noon. If you came back when the mist had beaded your hair and your forearms, you would see water walk—up, against your will to call it down.

The tiles, too, are ours, though “ours” drags ownership where there is mostly attention. They grow through our agreement with the films, which are not ours but friendly to instruction. I can read with my tongue which swath of a plate is sated with crosshatch and which needs tending. I do not scrape; I persuade. A rasp that is not a tooth worries a filament deeper until the stone remembers how to sweat there with less grudging. Tap your boot and the tap will leave you faster than you can enjoy it; I will taste the departure along the length of my belly as another kind of food. Noise here is caloric if you have the organs to digest it. We do. It coats the inside of us as gloss; it knits our long ribs a fraction tighter; it is laid in sugar into the tiles we tend and in that way becomes edible to hands that are leaves.

The arch is the part of me I keep outside me, though it was raised before I had ribs enough to act important. It is bone and not bone, strained to a correctness that hums. Summer at height, its note cuts at the place your breath changes name before it becomes a cough. Night, it settles itself lower and brings up a faint light that is not arrogance but housekeeping: a curated dark that belongs to this room of land. Under it, the pool is wrong in a way that keeps everything else right. It refuses to leave when it should, by breathing the hum in and turning brightness into kindness into depth. We count on it as on a patient, as on a bell, as on a rule.

We count everything. The tally beside the arch began before my first ring thickened and will outlive my last if the films want it. This many days the pool held its face when physics would have pulled it thin; this many hours of humid pulse between the exhalations you could set a watch by if your watch could sip. Once, a swallow wrote itself across the air so decisively that even our quiet let a hairline sound through, a seam of rip between tiles. We marked the day with a small parenthesis because there was no harm done. A week later a swallow not so considerate whirled too low and overturned the eel-let learning to climb damp rock like an idea. It was righted; a gecko took the swallow for payment, and the count wore both strokes because fair is not always clean.

We fear noise the way you might fear drowning in speech. A sudden shout makes my membranes refuse their work; my hairs lie down and will not be persuaded to stand. The rods collapse into politeness that wastes their talent. The tiles hunker and hand their warm into the rock as if to say: when you have finished, we will begin again. A person has come with bright shoes and loud thought and we went flat and did not break. A person has come with rain still clinging to them and we leaned near as much as our courtesy allows, because there is room here for anyone willing to leave what is unnecessary in their own bones.

The work is not grand. It is never finished. Morning, I run the arch’s length and taste for slack, press my rib-cage into the hum and listen for the places it hikes its note too greedily. A set-weaver shaped like a hand more than a stroke shoulders a rod a finger-width to the left because a sedge has asked by leaning, and if you serve a sedge in this way a fern will forgive you. The wick-bearers comb their manes against air that grows briefly thoughtful when a cloud passes and ask it to leave itself behind on the hairs; they polish the donation into dew so moths can drink a thriller and the gecko can unstick the day from its throat. At noon I go long and low on a high slab and let the surplus pass through. If the sky has scrubbed itself too clean and the arch sings shrill from the ache of holding light to task, I diet on that ache until it gentles. Eat enough brightness and you can taste iron in it, like blood, and you come away with your ribs tuned a whisper flatter and your patience lengthened.

I name my neighbors by what they do to the shine. The pale gecko is a cut-out nobody signed; the resin one is a drop that never fell; the wet-seed one is punctuation that makes you want to re-read the sentence behind it. The toads are an abacus re-beading itself in no hurry. The ants are declarative; I like to fall in with their commas and learn where they pause, because the good pauses show you how to keep a place clean of clutter. Dragonflies are editors who aren’t there and then are and then refuse to be again. The ribbon-snake is my peer in the matter of not being there until he is, but I do not eat what he eats and I do not begrudge him what he takes. Even a mantis that was always a stem has a courtesy here. Predation is not the noise we dislike; it is a sentence that speaks low and does not trip over itself.

On the year the sea-smell hung close for eight days without ever showing its water, I followed a hairline vein that had forgotten to bead and wrote a new paragraph for it with lick and comb and a sheet of microfilm coaxed from a plate I did not strictly need. I was young, and the old ones watched and did not correct me until the next dry—when what I had thought I taught the rock to do turned out to be a trick it would perform only for me. Tricks have no legs here. The teacher with bark-bark-bright should have scolded me; she did not; she let me see myself fail and fix it without thickening the place with shame. I erased what little I could. I learned to bend the air without flattering myself I had broken it.

You might take me for guardian if you need story on your tongue. It feels better to some mouths than “gardener of the angle of dew.” But I am not a fence, nor savior, nor threat. I am syntax. My cousins and I decide where the commas go and which edges stay sharp and where light is allowed to lay itself down. In trade the fauna write the paragraphs. The basin edits itself. We count.

At night I walk the curated dark because the dark here is a room arranged with care. A moth’s tongue unrolls into the thriller we offer and trembles like a leaf learning wind. A juvenile of my line, glossy as truth and as prone to mistake, practices dimmings on a tile and blushes—if a being like me can blush—when the pattern he makes comes back to him not as language but as warmth. He hums a fraction sharp and I—without touching—let him hear my noon-pitch so his night can find its middle. He sleeps later with his ladder of cilia lifted off the rock as if listening to nothing can tire you.

If you needed to name me in the way that lets you hold a thing without closing your hand, you could call me one of the hushwrights. If you were gentler than that, you could take my name the way we use them among ourselves: as a sequence of shadow-castings and the reminiscence of a flavor. I am three quick dimmings and a brighter, inherited honest. I smell faintly of citrus where I have tended sedge and of iron where I have eaten a day too bright. I belong to the grout-line behind the arch’s second rib. I will age into smoke-color and carefulness if nothing splinters my ribs before then. I will count until the stone takes my tally and softens it like bread under water and then I will start another line below and laugh very slowly when I am alone because low laughter is a kind of heat and I can use it.

Where would you find me? Here, where the rock has been proofread by centuries and the air carries fruit without any fruit to show for it. Under the arch when the heat feels like a thought that does not need me; along the rods when the mist files its motions like birds; with my face against a plate that will not echo you back if you speak without thinking.

What would I be? Not owner. Not story. A body made competent by practice at the angle where water changes its mind, an instrument that hums until depth happens in a shallow place, a hand that does not have fingers but leaves fingerprints anyway—in dew, in patience, in tallies that watch time pass without calling it enemy. I would be counted by toads when they nod. I would be read by fish when I write ripples with a ring. I would be the person of this basin in the sense that a basin can have persons: present, precise, reluctant to explain, intent on making physics remember all its options and then choosing the one that lets a moth drink from a hair and a gecko sign its night, and a human—very rarely, very quietly—learn to stand so still the rods stutter gossip about them and the tiles keep their secret anyway.
