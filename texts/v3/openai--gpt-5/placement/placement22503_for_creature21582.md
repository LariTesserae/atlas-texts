If I came up the cable on a clear-enough morning and set my boots on that forgiving grate, I don’t think I’d look for a view. I’d look for a seam.

I have hands that like edges, and this place is a garment built to catch weather and release it as work. Seams fail first. Seams tell you if a thing is wearing well. So I’d find myself where fabrics meet meshes, where a louver bank kisses its gasket, where net loops bite their posts and ask, one day, to fray. I’d ask to be put with the seamers and the weft-menders and the ones who can read a patch like a page.

At first I would be a novice from the valley, voice too ready, taught in an hour the discipline of palms and eyes. Stop with a chopped blade hand; hold with flat; valve with a fist curled by the ear; go with a two-finger walk. I’d keep forgetting and then remember in the middle of my own sentence, my words falling quiet like tacks set back in a drawer. I’d get the kit: the soft roll with its pockets—webbing straps, curved needles waxed so they slip through wet weave without a squeal, a little roller to press a patch down without a pop, a tin of adhesive that sets in damp and never turns brittle, a coil of black cordage that exactly matches the old so a repair doesn’t catch a thought as it sheds a drop.

The seamers live in lee pockets. We sleep behind baffles with the tank shadow on our curtains. We learn how to draw the magnet seam shut with no click. We learn to brew tea that smells like rosemary or thyme because that is what is at hand, and we learn to carry it without slosh; a lost ounce of heat is nothing here, but a lost ounce of water is a story someone will have to explain later.

Schedules are weather. When the head tanks slow, we nap. When the louvers relax, we eat. When a bank walks in with its hem down and the flags on the ridge rails begin to curl themselves like tails, we move into the places we have rehearsed and wait for the first hard breath. A thousand louvers will turn a whisper; the nets will draw themselves to their taughtest letters; the amber lines will start to tick as turbines accept their letters of credit. In that moment I know my work like a tendon knows a reach. I am hands at a seam.

Storm days, I am leeward, roped to a turntable that lets a whole net peel and wrap around a post without tearing its own stitches. The wind mashes its face on the fabric, trying to speak a word we do not answer aloud. The Lung deep inside the housings—sensors, models, little birds in padded nests of hardware—has already put the dampers and the gels at the right half-degree, the right angle of receipt. I do not argue with it. I watch the droplet size on the fins begin to change, from a light salt to grapes on a vine to a beading so heavy it merges into small rivers. I pull a patch from the roll and feel for a tear I’ve felt coming for a week. My hands know to make no sound. Metal, when it touches metal, is wicked with a little chime by pure habit, and we have taught ourselves to have no habits of noise.

Between the banks, on the wide gray of days when light is a periphery that never changes its mind, I do the brush-work. The oldest condensing fins take a season’s story into their satin—soot streaks from downstream fires; a scatter of salt from a storm that came too far in; a map of pale veins where a duct has stiffened and needs unstiffening. I take a soft brush and I do not polish; I roll. The grit rolls away like sleep from an old person’s morning. I read the under-map, mark a duct to be loosened, taste the first cup the new net gives us and swipe a wet finger over my knuckles with the rest. It’s a joke, the way we call it tasting the week, but it’s also a local truth: a hint of pine if the wind has run hands through the state-forest below; a mineral nip when the banks come in off the sea twelve ridges away, bringing ghost salt with them.

There are bees in one lee house. They are let out with care and called back with a hum only the bees and the two techs who tend them seem to hear. The rest of us use brushes in cases. We take our turn, quick as a breath through a flute, on blossoms bred for selfing but happier when told what to do. It is not romance. It is use. We do it in a sequence that keeps our boots on the blue grate as short as possible because blue is condensate and a stray seed there becomes an apology.

On slow nights I like the sump lanes. Under the condensing walls, where the captured drops answer gravity with gratitude and make a hush of trickles, there are slats with dark water below, shallow but mysterious because light is a choice here and we do not waste it. If you go alone and stay still you might learn something about patience from a salamander. It has the same habit we do: it puts itself in the place where the gradient is kind. It turns once in my light and slides under a slat with the decorum of an old man folding a napkin. I like the salamander very much. We live by different rules but share the same conviction that a noise can be a mistake.

You would think a place like this would make you feel fenced in. It doesn’t. It is so woven there is no void to miss; your desire for a big open goes elsewhere—down to your feet, or into the back of your hands, or into the little theater of a gasket seating true. I take my quiet from the way five systems can be humming within a palm’s thickness, and how you can feel each one if you’ve trained your nerve to listen: the thin flick of air behind an insulated plate, the soft sigh of a valve taking back just a hair of force, the dampness of a nutrient feed whispering like combed hair, the skitter of a pneumatic tube deciding a junction, the slow bass of head on the far side of stone, like a cat sleeping behind a couch.

They let us string sometimes. If you stay a season without breaking a rule and you don’t mind the suggestion of a horizon, one of the forepeople will put you in a harness and clip you to a line and send you out between ridges with a case cuffed in foam and a list that is all numbers. Stringers are nerves. The cars go slow as a sewing foot. The valley below is a stripwork of water and silver and stories we do not have time for except to pick up crates and let down crates and trade a jar of something you boiled with someone who mended something you broke once with a laugh and an open hand. On line, you learn to hear the bow of cable in a thunder rumor and decide if you stop or if you go. When the car docks, you hug a muffler housing or a head-tank belly for a moment of heat, and you eat where you are, because here meal is the interval when your body stops being a tool for a bit and lets the place run without you.

Off-ridge I become a person who picks a seat in a bus by the location of its vents. I hold a rail with two fingers to keep my oil to myself. Horns feel like bad manners. My feet pick diagonals that are all catwalk logic. I carry a thermos and I cap it between sips because my mouth learned the habit of not leaving liquid to the open. I count days in liters, in meters of head, in lumen-hours, in kilos of chicory, in degrees on a louver bank. I take a child—my sister’s—up once on a day with banners of cloud and teach her to cup her palm around an LED not for myth but so the moss stays dreaming. She laughs without a sound, then shouts once on purpose, and we both jump, and then we sit in a lee pocket and talk in a murmur that is not secrecy but respect, and I show her a sign tape someone made years ago, hands in the narrow light of a corridor, a dance of instructions that feels like a lullaby to me now.

I am not a hero. None of us are. We are kept. We keep. I like that reciprocity. If I have a badge it is a loop of old black webbing on a corner post where someone made a mistake once, not punished by noise but remembered by touch. If I have a shrine, it is a drawer with folded finger-brushes and a coil of green line that exactly matches the last. I believe in maintenance like other people believe in wildness. To make something stay useful is to be faithful in a way that does not ask to be seen.

Some days, though, I am not a person. Some days you are the place with your body and the place is you and the seam of it is invisible. I have been a droplet, once or twice, falling off the lower lip of a fog net, heavy with other droplets behind me, brief as an idea and then weight, then work, then power, then warmth under a bunk in a lee pocket. I have been rubber—toes pressing the grate, giving and backing you up with no sound. I have been the band of light that moves along the catwalk and says without saying: move here now, not there. I have been a rule, left rail upslope, right rail down, so much mine I dream it and step that way in my kitchen. I have been an edge of louver tuned by someone else’s gloved finger; I have been the felted back of a sign greened over by moss, being allowed to be because I do not choke a sensor and in return I wick what is given to me and do not ask to be more. Once at mid-shift on a night that forgot its own moon I was the Lung’s thought, predicted wind arriving seven minutes sooner than it told us last week, and I put my finger on a dial and made a correction and the net took the gust like a ship and did not complain.

You could call us stewards or operators. If you called us tendons I would nod. We live in the still parts of the ridge so that the live parts can breathe. We call it behavior because silence is a moral word and this is not a moral enterprise; it is a practice. We have jokes soft as moss. We have arguments you could take a nap through. We boil dwarfed blueberries with sugar and pour them over bread and call that good in the hour when the louvers are fat with their work and don’t need us.

Where would I be? On the lee, in the seam, with a roller and a tin of glue that sets in rain. On the ridge rail watching the flags tell a sentence in a dialect of gusts. In the sump lanes listening to the hush that is the water keeping its promise to fall. In the turnhouse with my hand on the stone lid, feeling the turbine as a purr a cat would understand. Occasionally far above all of it, strung between works with a case of white air line on my lap and the valley poured out below like a wet bolt of cloth.

Who would I be? A person whose body has learned to slope into weather and whose hands know how to catch a drip before it hits the grate. A reader of fins, a mender of weft, a teacher of handspeech, a believer in the first drip across the knuckles. A good roommate for a machine that is not proud. A small intelligence inside a larger one that is not quite alive but behaves like breathing. A patch, a tendon, a cell in a green membrane, moving with the grain of a place that never dries.

Not romantic. Practical. Consistent with weather, slope, century. The wild in me kept tender and small, like the single bat that has learned the LEDs. The fast heart always outside me, in the weather, and my job to make room for its swing.
