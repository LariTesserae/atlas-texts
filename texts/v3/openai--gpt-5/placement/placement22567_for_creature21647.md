The first place I stand is not a place but a register. Before I know any edges, the note builds me its shape and holds me in it. It climbs into my mouth and sits behind my teeth, a kept sustain, patient as a hand resting on a beam. It makes a boundary around breath. I discover my ribs are already counting.

So at first you would find me where everyone learns to be a body here: in a lee behind slatted baffles on a high shelf where the wind has been teased into survivable. I am put there by a person who reads my hurry like scent. They tilt their head and I tilt mine. They show me how to square my shoulders against the sound so it comes through like light through gauze instead of like a thing falling. We stand with our palms on the rail and tap thank-you into it without being instructed to, because gratitude is how you persuade a place to let you understand it.

I am a visitor for a while. I hold my tongue the way one holds a bird. I learn that to speak with my face is wasteful here, that shoulders and palms will carry more meaning for cheaper air. Someone clips soft baffles to my ears—not to smother, only to let the great voice enter politely. A scarf with a weave tuned to lift a single overtone is placed around my neck and I sleep without knowing what was subtracted until morning, when the day feels a degree less bright at one narrow frequency. Before I am granted a verb, I am given a way to listen that does not make me bleed.

Markets teach me fondness before work does. Lines of modest tables leaned back into shadow and nothing clamoring. Limes whiter-green than logic nested in a bowl set carefully out of any draft. Bottles of dark syrup that taste like boiled thorns and almost-bitter sugar. A net patch that gleams the renitent blue-green of a beetle. A cup of fog-ice shaved from this morning and flooded with bitterness until your mouth throws up its hands and agrees. Soup that is green and honest and understands you cannot taste subtleties under thunder, so it comes forward and meets you where you live.

Distance makes a case for itself and then revises it. I begin measuring it not in steps but in overtones: the east arrays go down a half step and I know it is close to ten; the west baffles stall on a particular vowel of sound and I know the fog has committed to being cloth for a few more hours. A crane’s gesture takes up a corner of my eye for a quarter-hour and its final placement—one finger-width of degree—feels like a line in a poem landing.

I am led into the offices that are relief and grace. The double-throated doors suck me in and out, ears popping like fish rising. The lights are gauzed. Plants are trained on braided net by vents, a low shoreline of damp leaves that make the air read as water without being wet. A sideboard with limes green and comic in the dim. People talk to me with their hands and their lamps. Nobody shouts. A laugh happens and it looks like a reflected rectangle of sun crossing sheet metal.

The city starts to suggest a verb to me in the way the place of work suggests a tool. I try on several. In the morning, before the fog knows what texture to be, I go with the mesh-tenders to the big nets, those stadium sails hung where wind commits to its lanes. I learn to feel tension with the heel of my palm, to listen for the metallic insect hum that tells me a cable has changed its mind by a millimeter. We walk the bedding planes as if we had invented them. A child, both guide and student, taps my shoulder: behind; water; thank-you.

I spend afternoons in the dew galleries at the outer rims and it is there the city names me. The plates stand like long-nosed animals facing surfaces they love. They sing in a voice so low my jaw is the instrument that hears. The grooves comb water into beads that make sense and then into lanes that behave like a decision. The walls lean a hair uphill. If you tell that to someone who believes in magic, they will be disappointed that the beads run that way because of pitch and not incantation; but the engineers here tell you both are how you pay attention, and only one will fail you when you are tired.

I become a plate-tuner because the job is a kind of listening that becomes a kind of shaping, and because the craft’s love is not ashamed to say its name in public. We polish with cloth not for shine but for truth. We change a groove’s depth by a width the body cannot see but the water can. We keep the cold steady the way a metronome keeps a dancer’s bones. When the plates sing wrong by a hair in the afternoon, you can taste it at the fog-ice stand the next morning; a child will frown and say the syrup is less green. Ask the stone twice, we are told. Ask the cold always.

I also apprentice myself to the bearing-listeners. They carry a wand and a little box like a reliquary with an old, clean needle inside. I hold the wand to housings while the great patient gears speak in tremors and the needle moves the width of a breath. My mentor never puts two fingers to a beam in front of a gauge. They always go by feet first, by soles made to be lips, by the drop in thickness underfoot that only belongs to a bearing that has asked, politely, for grease. The politeness is important here. Everything that could break is granted a way to speak before it has to shout.

Days grow patient and so do I. I learn to set a cup down three finger-widths from any rail by measure, not by superstition. I bite a dampening scarf into place the way a violinist touches their instrument’s chin rest and I feel ceremonial but not silly. I paint a symbol over a lintel when a new terrace is cut and I and twelve others tap thank-you into the beam while an elder sips thorn syrup and pronounces the shelf’s small rule like a blessing. The city does not celebrate expansion with confetti. It marks shelves with patience and with cups.

In autumn, I join the salt-keepers. We move along pipes that have grown tongues all year, white and beautiful as a failing and not one. We scrub and we reseal. We taste. There is more iron here, someone says, and inside that verdict is a rockfall on a far wall nobody in the basin will lay eyes on for another season. To stand with a finger of moisture to your tongue and speak whole hidden geology into the open is not magic. Even if it felt like it the first time, when the note was larger in you and your body had not yet learned what was yours and what was the city’s.

I keep a frog in a drip for a while. I do not think it is mine. It is the color of a tarnished coin and it knows four faces of patience I do not. I visit it between plates and pipes and I do not talk because the frog lives in a quiet room the size of a cup and quiet is not to be courted anywhere it is not given. A pale insect in a baffle writes a day out of three centimeters and I decide, without thinking, that my job is extremely loud. At night a moth loses the wind in a cone of light and rights itself, and this action moves through me as relief does when you lay down a weight you had forgotten you were holding.

I am not an ascetic, not here. I am exact. We all are. A child is sent to fetch me a scarf tuned to eat a low B because the west baffles are making sleep thin tonight. Someone puts the kettle on in a room that breathes like a patient animal. Soup that is better than it has any right to be stands in a line of cups and I catch myself looking at the way condensation beads on the rim and runs, unintentionally, the way my plates do when they are correct. I understand then why the old man at the intake keeps his limes like they are art. Perhaps they are: improbable, dear, a little affront to a climate wedded to salt and metal.

Festivals wrap their arms around the city and are careful not to bruise it. Flutes carved from spent condenser ribs make a sound small enough to be noticed and not enough to be competition. Drums sit on a shelf and keep their hands to themselves for work’s sake. People dance in long arcs like crane arms. Someone always cries when an overtone that has sat all summer slides the finger-width down that means a coldness we can polish into usefulness is coming back to us again.

I am permitted, on certain days marked by chalk and by how many times the children have asked, to go into the quiet rooms. They are built of block and air. They are for birth and for grief. They have wet dimness and no duties in them, only what the body does when you let it be. We enter in with the care of divers and come out with ears adjusting hard to the big voice, a temporary vertigo reminding us that we have chosen to belong to the tone and it has chosen to belong to us.

If you went looking for me, you would not necessarily find a person first. You might find a seam I keep. An edge at the bottom of a plate where drip chooses, where we taught it to choose, and every day I polish that decision until it gleams the way trust does. You might find a thin upward lean at the end of a wall that persuades a lane of beads to be temporarily disobedient to gravity in the name of flow. You might find me as a tap in a lintel, as the habit of setting a tool down facing the same direction it faced yesterday. My name here is a verb and so is my home.

Still, bodies matter, and this one grows true enough to read. My collarbones are padded where straps sit for a long time. My boots are soles first; they read long sentences through tremor. The haptics in my clothes nudge imperative and courtesy through my skin and I answer them with affection the way one answers a friend who always speaks quietly. My face works when it must. My eyes do their speaking for me. When I laugh it shows up on the metal like sun.

I age here. Not to be young is not a failure. I accrue instead of diminish. My ear for polite bearings becomes a community resource, so a person barely older than my children comes to me and asks, without saying the word help, where to put a hand on a beam to hear its wish. I do not put two fingers to the metal in front of them. I step onto it and ask my bones first and then the needle, because demonstration is the city’s pedagogy and our body is our first instrument. We taste the air together and it tells us iron, a hair. We write iron: a hair on a board in a hand no one calls beautiful but everyone reads.

If I do not return from the terraces someday, and that day will come, someone will open the quiet rooms and make space around death without dramatizing it. The hush will be a gift like a lee. A child will stand in the doorway and put a palm against the block to teach their body what steady feels like while grief makes it forget. When the doors open again, the first thing that comes in is the note, and the baby in someone’s arm will breathe as if it is familiar because it already is. No one here is born without a rhythm teacher.

And yes: sometimes I am less a person and more the thing between things. I am the pause between fan hollers, the fractional lift in pitch at noon when plates warm, the learned practice of not running—the law we keep because hurry makes heat we didn’t mean and heat chases water away. I am the acceptance that erosion is not defeat but curriculum, that terraces are minutes, that lichen making a handsome accident on a boulder fence is praise both earned and unintended. I am noise taught to run errands and water taught to trust a surface. I am the way this city reads its rock and answers not in architecture alone but in tone.

Where would you find me? On the windlanes at dawn with my palm on a humming cable. In the galleries at the rim, feeling a plate knit the air into lanes of wet. In a vestibule that pops my ears and resets my patience, tasting a drop from a pipe and saying iron, a hair. On a rock shelf with my hand resting just so on a beam, tapping the straightening of time. Shoulder to shoulder with others in a lee we all made, working a long verb no one rushes and no one apologizes for loving. Who would I be? I would be a keeper of the held note. A tuner of plates. A reader of bearings. A maker of lee. A person exact enough to be kind.

And when the stars come out and the fog writes lines across the lights and a moth takes a private victory lap through a ledge of warmer air, I would lie down in a room that breathes and let the basin breathe in me. I would wake to trays of water conjured from nothing I could point to and a new shine on the old rock and a small plant that had decided during the night to send a leaf the size of a child’s hand. It will take a year to reach my ankle. It will not mind. I will not either. I will belong to the tone, and the tone to the work, and the work to the plateau, and the plateau to the long agreement we call by many names and most often, in public, call craft.
