At first I would arrive as myself: a pair of wet soles persuading grit-tape, breath doing little clouds a human can claim, a lamp in my hand inventing edges that were fine without me. I would know where I was only by what my body was asked to do—angle the shoulders, test the grating, remember where knees go to keep their skin. I would read the place the way the place is meant to be read: labels first, louvers second, the line of blue light that says walk here, not there. I would be a visitor rehearsing respect.

That would last until the first purge. The roar would take my agency away like a tide pulls foam from a rock and sorts it back into water. Not unkindly; indifferently. Sound would claim my bones and bounce the red LED into a streak across my eye’s jelly and the machine would say—not in words, but in the way it spends volume—This is the size of your privacy here. Less than a drip deciding, more than a number clicking.

If I stayed long enough, if there were a reason for me to linger in a place made exactly so that no one lingers, I would find myself leaving the person at the hatch and becoming the first thing the throat teaches—an interval.

Not a second, not a minute. Those are for clocks that sit at desks. I mean a kind of pass-through that has rules. The clear run between branch lines that must not gather more than air and the occasional human knee. The silence-sized volume inside a noise that the noise pours itself into to keep its shape. The breathing space that makes the breath a breath and not a leak.

I would live knee-high, where the blue LEDs run and two have failed and every third is brighter for reasons nobody wrote down. I would live in their rhythm, not their light; I would be what lets scudding glow skim water and not make glare. I would be the place a drip counts itself against and the reason it counts: rough edge, angle, gravity’s patience, the persuasion of design.

That would be my first self: clearances. The defended emptinesses. The way the machine keeps more of itself by not touching itself. I would be the habit of not-filling. Whoever tried to name me would get it half-right on the first go and then too poetical on the second. “Access,” they’d say. “Air,” they’d say. Close. But I would be the particularity of access here, the flavor of air this room was cut to contain. The not-roomness that still remembers rooms and says, no, thank you, I am a throat.

It would be tempting then to become water. The room is full of water without puddles: it beads, it decides, it obeys. I would try it on, name myself bead, smear, threadline, fog. For a while I would live in the small betrayals and kept promises of surfaces. How a droplet bulks along a louver edge until it can call itself a drop, how it thinks better of a climb and ticks down. But I would not stay in the water. Water goes. My business here would be with habit, not with yield.

There are other shapes a person could try to put on. The little opportunists that pass for life: the coin of green where copper gave alms, the ridiculous handlebar at the sump grate doing as well as it can in a place that wants only to be washed. I would salute them the way a monk salutes a pilgrim met on a path: warmth, respect, no invitation to stay. I would not be them. I would be the thing that makes their season brief.

I could pretend for a cycle to be human again. I could be the gloved thumb’s chalk on a panel edge, the kneecap’s gray smudge, the exclamation-point of a curse word when a burr catches a cuff. I could be the voice that reads a gauge out loud, because the saying of a number changes a number’s fate. That would make me feel useful in a way that is easy to crave. I would flip a breaker and hear the world change. I would tie a tag and see meaning flutter.

But usefulness here is older than hands. The satisfaction that suits the place is repetition without complaint. So I would set the human down again—that migratory warmth, that migratory set of blunders that are also kindnesses—and I would sit myself between the louver’s pale churn and the transformer’s patient purr and be the sum of distances and decisions.

Who would I be? I would fail the biologist and please the metrologist. I would be the enforcement of distances and the archive of stains and the muscle-memory of a purge. I would be the law’s appetite rather than its letter. I would be consequence given habit, habit given sound, sound given place. If you needed me to be a creature to keep company with your need for company, I could entertain that for a minute: I could be the pet behavior of a machine breathing evenly. I could be the seal that blasts and the fish-eye in its frame and the basking warm thing behind the mesh. They are serviceable metaphors. Where would you find me? You would not, unless you stayed. You would have to wait out a cycle in the slit, learning to feel time by weather you cannot see. You would have to watch the needle twitch the way a cat watches a door its person has just left by. You would have to take off the desire to point and put on the desire to abide. Then you could: you would find me in the shape your hearing has taken by then, and in the way your body has learned to stand. You would find me in your willingness to let a machine’s breath set the pace of your thoughts.
