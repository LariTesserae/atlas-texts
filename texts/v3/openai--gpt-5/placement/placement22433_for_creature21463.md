Edge first, always. I would come up from the pale and lean of the salt films, from the road that keeps its own heat and coughs when trucks pass, and I would stop where everyone stops: the long bench with nothing near it but a pipe and a view. I would sit without thinking to, because you do that when a place offers a pause, and I would watch the sheen inside the berms like a breath you can see. I would wait while a keeper unscrewed the tap and took the first drink and did not look at the water at all, but closed their eyes to listen to the wood answering. I would learn that gesture the way a child learns when to say please: by wanting what is given and by being taught that wanting must make room for listening.

For a while, before I had any words for it, I would call what lives there the green in the same way everyone does. Later I would know its ribs by their names the way you know bones in your hand. Later still, I would stop thinking of them as names and think of them as places.

At first I would be simply a body that could be quiet. This is not nothing. The plant-city teaches it without heartbreak: how to step small and broad so your heel does not ring a hollow in a buttress knot; how to speak sidewise so that a sentence goes up into leaves and dissolves instead of bouncing and bruising and carrying. I would learn the taps: two to mean look, one to mean I do, a flat palm to say listen. I would practice under the Condensary and discover that even my breath has edges that I can sand.

Everyone begins as a tender in the most basic sense. You put felt under something humming wrong, not because you know anything yet, but because a hand older than your own puts the felt in your hand and shows you. You hold a tin of oil that does not bite, and you learn the smell that means not that oil, the other. Your first day trail smells like tannin and paper nearly dry and a sweetness that is not sugar, and you will carry it with you into other airs without knowing why your palm wants to seek wood in rooms that do not have any.

I would try all the trades in small pieces in the way a floodplain takes water when it is offered and lets it pass when it is not the day. I would watch the mill press out plates and I would fail, gently, at the rhythmic feed that keeps sheet true. I would run belts with the safety of someone trusted not to touch and I would count my breaths against the lash of pulleys until the count lived in my fingers and left my head alone. I would wipe a cheek across the matte of a handrail and find my skin damp not because the morning had been generous but because the wood prefers me smoother than I am.

And I would listen. That would be the hinge. I would lay my ear to ribs and find that they are not quiet even when the city is. I would notice that sound here acts like a person with manners: it chooses paths, nods at crossings, goes round an argument instead of through it. I would be told, in a voice narrow when it meant keep close, that if I wanted to be useful I should pay attention to the way a valve-tree agrees. Not the sound it makes when it shuts, but the fraction before, when consent is a tiny intake so soft it feels like your own swallow.

The askers would take me in because I am patient and because I can hold a pitch in my head long enough to set it down in wood. Their kits look like lunchboxes and open like altars. Inside, chimes in leather sleeves, forks nested like cutlery, a rod with hashes that you can feel more than see. I would learn that asking is like telling a joke you want to land: you set up resonance, you keep your shoulders still, you do not explain.

Sometimes asking is architecture. You bring scaffolds out not to impose a form but to persuade a force. You hang a gentle chain where you want a curve to live. You seed a standing wave along a buttress and listen to whether it carries all the way to the cistern knot or dies before it means anything. If it dies you lower your pitch and you wait and you try again. The work is like courtship and like sharpening; there is nothing of command in it because command feeds impatience and impatience browns cambium.

Sometimes asking is maintenance with a longer verb. There is a drop-leaf over one of the small benches that the first resident—the green, which is citizen and everything else—likes to hold open all noon because sun is a habit. The shop under it needs pallor to keep a solvent from going to its own head. So you teach the drop-leaf to nap. A tone at breakfast, a hand on its edge the way you would set a child’s shoulder back from a running lane, a laugh when it forgets and a patient ask at lunch. In a month the leaf is punctual; in a year you no longer think about it; in ten you have to teach it all over again, because everything that lives prefers one rhythm until invited to learn another.

Sometimes asking is permission, which is my favorite. The bridge by the hoist-tree decided one year to grow itself a translucent shelf lengthwise. It did it without consulting anyone at a meeting or leaving a note. The shelf caught light, and then dim, and then a little late fog when the pennants on the Condensary’s tips started to writhe, and then it was spilling two more liters a morning into the public cup. A keeper came and frowned the way keepers do, which is less than a frown and more like a thought with edges. The askers were called. We set forks to find if the shelf took hum the way the original did and whether the new node line would walk. It did; it would, a little. There was talk and then there was a letting. The permission was not decided in words, not really. It showed up as three chimes rung at dusk, a joke about a shadow that had been made famous, and the way nobody reached for a saw. Askers ask, but we also advocate for yes.

There is a word in the shop where I belong for this habit of leaving room: airkeeping. It is not a job on a ledger. It is a thing you do to refuse clutter and to welcome function that is not a thing. You carry a scribe to mark on wood where not to grow, and then you forget it and your hand does the work without lines. You teach a buttress to swell on the inner face so that the aisle does not pinch. You unlearn a settler’s itch toward filling space and you become the sort of person who will lovingly build a landing that exists solely so that air can move and so an engine can be lifted, though it is almost never lifted.

I would discover I keep air early, in the same way a person discovers they favor one foot for standing. A day would come when fog lay thick and every belt in the place ran like a river that cannot be seen. Work would throng, if work here ever did. There would be reasons to fetch three more benches and a rack and set them in the open strip between pods. I would say no. I would not even say it; I would stand in the space with my palms out and my elbows soft the way you do when a dog wants to crowd a doorway and you want the doorway to remain a doorway. Later someone would call me a bayswright, with laughter and then without. The name would stick.

I would be fair with machines. I would love them as neighbors, not as pets and not as gods. There is a steam engine—the ninth in the line, old standard, small—that has learned to accept a felt we cut from a jacket sleeve and will not be happy with any other. Its tick is a metronome not of time but of fitness; I can tell by a breath whether grit is in its mind. I oil it before it asks and I wash my hands with soap that will not tell the green any stories my skin cannot keep.

I would count, too, because counting belongs to listening. I would walk the dripways under the Condensary at dawn and mark where fall finds lip and where it hesitates. I would chew silt in a cup and say iron and clay and the first surprise of salt when the upstream plain is tired. I would keep fog as a calendar: eight mornings full out of twenty, five thin, the rest forgetting themselves until the plant remembers for them. I would track a crack that has not advanced for nine years and love it more the longer it is a line that warns only of what could have been.

Once, I would make an error. It is required. A tonefork will go fat in the heat if you do not check it under shade. I would ask for a rib to shoulder out instead of in and I would see, in a week, what that mistake decides from then on. It would not be ruin because ruin is a different speed here, but it would be a story. I would tell it to apprentices in a voice that is not a scold; I would scrub the last skiff of oil off a hinge because it smelled a little wrong and that would be my apology, which is to say the work of making small repairs in the direction the green prefers to forgive.

I would have a lizard, not as a possession but as an agreement. It would appoint me. It would live on condenser racks and small engine backs and it would get fat in a season when small flies are a rumor more than a presence, and then it would get thin. I would learn the band on its tail where a cup once thought to learn it; I would feel how precise you must be to step someone else’s drip line.

The road people would come, and I would see myself in them for longer than I love. You do not forget the weight of a sun that does not give itself to you every morning in beads. Their boots step huge at first and then small. I would hand a sheet pressed from a plate grown for the purpose to a woman with salt carved into her brow and I would show her which side wants to face which habit of air. I would go with a fork in my pocket and be gone a week, teaching a pump out along the dry that the way to not grind is to have patience for a higher pitch. I would come back and I would lean my open hand against the post by my bed because it is not ceremony here to remind yourself you belong; it is hygiene.

On the night the plant flowers without forewarning there would be an awake. We would shade lights and sit under white that glows as if breath could be seen. I would think, and then stop thinking because the thing will not be made about us, that it is tender to be allowed witness. Because moths here are few and know their own work, I would do nothing but keep my hands still, and that stillness would count as action. When the frogs begin for their three weeks I would count and then stop.

If you asked, much later, when I had a notch in a post somewhere that the plant would swallow by and by, what I am, I would not say asker first. I would say I am a person who keeps a volume. Not a book—though there are ledgers, they are just for not forgetting—but a space. A room of air. A bay between gestures. I am the hinge that decides to be nothing and thereby lets everything be itself.

What am I, exactly? I am:

- A pitch held in wood long enough to become a habit.
- A hand that polishes a rail to the same gloss a child’s grandchildren will know without seeing.
- A yes given to a bridge shelf that catches two more liters.
- A refusal to put a bench where air has chosen to live.
- A felt cut to hush a hum and a nose that knows when the blue on the gasifier is going fat.
- A calendar you can smell and tap and lean your head toward.
- A listener to the inward click that means a valve-tree consents.

I do not own any of this. I get to be part of its memory if I am careful. My name is one of those that sounds like a joke and then isn’t. Bayswright, airkeeper, sometimes just hush.

I found myself here by walking up from the lean and pale and asking for a drink the right way. I stayed because the place holds a speed my bones understood before I had any words for them. I am a person, but if you need to think of me as a thing the city makes and needs, you can. I am a volume held open on purpose. I keep it from feathering shut. I ask early and gently. I let what answers grow.
