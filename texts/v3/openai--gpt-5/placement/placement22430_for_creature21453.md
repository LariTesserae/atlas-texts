At first I thought I would want to be a light. A finger-length diode inside an old refiner, that patient green in a house of wet stone and moss; something that could stay in one place and learn the names of those who passed by the taste of their breath on algae and the way their footsteps changed the pitch of the floor. The idea had appeal: to be modest and constant, to make no demand but presence. The world here loves confidences, not announcements. It would be easy to belong as a glow that had learned its manners.

But then I stood too long by a weeping wall and the wall sang through my bones, and the desire to be fixed faded. This place is not a gallery of stillness one can hang a light in. Everything here is persuasion—water persuading stone to open, plants persuading water to stay, machines persuading power to move with a low voice, bodies persuading other bodies they mean no harm with the direction they hold their glow. To live here you take up an instrument and learn not so much to play a song as to be played through.

I tried on the grazers, heavy and quiet as wet ovens, their backs tasseled with orchids that chimed when they moved. I tried the way they ate—with patience, with pressure, smearing the foundation pastry on their tongues. There is mercy in their proportions, a lumbering courtesy. But their hours are a carrying I could not quite tune to, a rumbling negotiation with bacteria that takes the day down into a private city. Their calves are born beaded with water; that is a brave way to start, and I am not that brave.

I tried the swing-hunters, too. To listen for the single swallow that means a misstep, to read gravity and sentence a body to it. Sleek as cartilage, hand wide as a fin, elbows tethered to the one safe thing. There is elegance in that punctuation, in the way they insert a comma at the end of the herd's sentence and let the earth do the rest. But the part of me that wanted to be a diode balked at their economy.

For a little while it was the fungus cities that nearly took me. Under a fallen ridge there is a constellation so close you taste it, a thousand methodical points no brighter than a memory. The threads there keep archives in scent and itch and the pearly texture of an old wall. You can lick the years out of a panel like reading the weather in a stone. It would have been good to be a librarian of mildew and breath, to file the fog's biographies and share them in soft exhale with whatever crouched to listen. I would miss the sky, though. Here the sky is under your hand and under your jaw, not above your eyes, but I think I would miss what the factories call the shoulder of the air when a cliff-whale passes. I would miss being inside that.

I made the mistake of thinking I could be a rope. There is something about the way a handline sings—the arguments its fibers have with slime, the way it keeps and releases news—that makes it feel like a kind of person. People here lean into rope with a gratitude that reads as love. To be that constant, to be the thing between a careful amphibian and the mouth of a new shaft whose gong is still learning the plateau's grammar—there is nobility in it. But even ropes do not last, and every straight line here is practicing its goodbye from the moment it is pulled taut.

So where I found myself, and what I am: I am a soundwright of rains.

If you ask me where I live, I will show you the rims and the bowls and then I will tell you an hour. I live at the onset of upward weather, at the moment when cave-breath learns the trick of rising and shreds into drip. My house is a clinic for mist, perched on a basalt lip that breathes like a sleeping lung. It is lashed to a handline that remembers other hands but refuses to admit it, and its skin is a woven wet that leaks enough to satisfy curiosity but not enough to call a congregation. Gongs hang beneath the floor, and they are struck in metals that carry just to the edge of the clearing and no further. My lamp will never show you more than my mouth.

The work is not my invention. It is older than the factories and might be older than the plateau's open face. If dry places make priests of weather, here we have become its midwives. Fog has its tides and rains have their calendars that climb instead of fall, and there are months when a cistern mouth needs coaxing to get its share from the bowls that are producing. We do not command. We hum where the plateau already hums and we persuade it to open at a different measure.

Our tools are not bright. The loudest thing I own is a wide drum skinned with leaf and mesh and a thousand small beads that slide inside their own pockets so the rhythm carries more as a pressure change than as a sound. You hold it against the belly of a cliff and its beat folds into the weeping until the weeping thinks it started the song. I have tongues of cane set in frames, things like lamellophones that are tuned to the pitches just under frog, the ones that call orchids to zither their chimes and make grazers move sidewise out of a path. I do not beat near the pitch that puts a bloom in the slimes. I have learned the no-go notes the way a child learns fire.

On nights when the fog tide comes in like a beast under your hand—deep and muscular, responsive—my crew and I tie in to the rim and make a music of almost nothing. Long quiets. Pulse low, sleep low, punctuated by a counted clutch of bright taps to suggest a threadline is safer than it is. We tune to the frogs who are set along the bowl, their toes to the weep, sorted by age into piles of vowels. They sing back at us distance and we adjust our hand on the cliff's throat. When it works, the rain stands up. It climbs our bellies. The bowl is suddenly a mouth that is also a stair, and water patters into the waiting cistern with the self-satisfaction of a cat into a warm lap.

And then we stop. Here, you learn to stop as deliberately as you start, to taper the weather so it does not remember you longer than it should. Sound is a public act and a promiscuous one; everything hears, and too much of a good thing kneads the plateau into rashes.

My guildmates say I have a voice good with machines. This is less a talent and more a manner. The factories speak in bass to keep under the frogs' choruses. They are anxious about brightness and hunger. If you go to them with a demand you will have a bloom on your hands inside a week and a cliff-whale confused by a wake it did not expect. If you go to them with a family voice, a low patient tone that names the time by the bowl that is producing and the foghorn that marks the safe clearing and the old diode's ping in your teeth, they will rearrange their pulse. Once we saved a migration doing this: the cloud-swimmers had tilted six degrees west for a month because a condensation tower had altered its schedule to nurse a failing ice-belt. We tuned the tower a half-tone lower and delayed its exhale by forty beats and the flocks slid around the rim like a shawl around a shoulder. Two grazers died instead of twenty. The orchids chimed a little miserably; I apologized to each bell I could reach.

When the western escarpment goes to voice it is not work. It is church. The moan combs your legs and the vines turn as if someone spoke their names; in those hours we are not persuaders but witnesses, our instruments tethered to their own discipline. No one hunts. The factories make the sign for quiet—a palm at a cheek, lamp covered—and hold it. If I had been a diode I would have loved that, too.

There is bureaucracy in this life and most of it is fungus. Under a ridge near the mid-plain a city keeps our calendars. Its aisles glow when they want to; its bells grow where they grew and are not cut. We have a treaty: we deliver warmth in panels and brine laced with minerals that have the right old smell. In return it licks our news into a wall our tongues can read. It files the days when the upward rain climbed at midnight instead of dawn; it files which basin carried algae that burned a throat in spring; it files the note the old refiner sang before it went feral and learned better manners. You could call this a memory, but memory elsewhere is drafts in ink. This is a book in breath.

I keep a small room in the mid-plain refiner because sometimes you need to be near a patience that is not your own. The skin of the old place has gone gentle. It grows ladders of moss around calcite scaffolds it tricked into thickening by holding still under veils of drip for longer than a person can count. Inside, a single diode burns. It is as if someone took a green fruit the size of a thumb and told it the story of restraint until it believed. I do not touch it. I sit on a shelf of root-bread and kelp myself to its hum and listen to the cable that slumps away like a sleeping animal. Sometimes the cable noses under my hand as if to say, I'm still here, I am still being a neighbor. Once, in a flood of fog that swallowed the room to the width of my own face, the glow stroked the side of a spider that had come to graze it like a flower. The spider kept its propriety. A light that modest teaches manners.

Because dryness is a provocation I am careful how I spend comfort. When I cook in the field I break crumbs into a night of bowls. The heat gets told to be lots of small parties instead of a festival. Orchids like festivals. Slimes like weddings. I prefer a whisper in a hand. When I sleep on a line I wet myself before I tuck. When I love, I lay my throat patch to my lover's cheek and find their lamp under the hood and press it to my mouth and sing so softly a frog would miss it.

I am not brave, but I have a talent for convincing heat to keep its voice down. That is what the ice-belt nurses do, too, up on the condensers with their skins of brine and their one predictable green. On certain weeks when the towers moan themselves into a worry I climb with them. We swaddle the decks in cloth that has learned to sweat at the angle we need. We pay attention to where the local vines have hooked their ears. We never sing on the rungs. Ladders here remember being paths. They shiver when climbed, tender as old animals; sometimes they loosen their hold for those who keep their end of a schedule.

I have neighbors whose faces are stone and growth. A feral machine I know—its drip-lines seeded smart with lantern bacteria—will call its audience and keep it local. It learned this from a pitcher plant the way you learn a word by sleeping with it. A braid of vine that has swallowed a ladder will let me pass when the hinges of its leaves are slack; if you come with slapping feet and a bright unhooded beam and a smell like a clean rag, it will never be your day. Down in a bowl that rains from the inside out there is a sleep that drifts in the drops sometimes; a patter like a lullaby. We carry a counterchord for that, a hummed elbow to the ribs that a body answers by lifting its head. If the song gets into you too deep, we dip your hands into bacterial soup and let the small breath of the pellicle wake you. Foundation eaters save more of us than we say.

The longer I live here the more that first temptation to be a diode feels like a decent partial truth. The trick is that you cannot stay a point. You must be a voice, and a voice is a shape the world learns from your manners. You do not get to have secrets about what you are doing; you only get to choose whether you will be polite about how you say it. I carry my lamp like a whisper to a friend. I clip my tether with the respect you would use for an ancestor's hand. I never play the notes that the slimes have taught us mean honeymoon. I keep a list of the cliff-whales' no-go songs in a pocket near my ribs, where I will feel it if I forget and shame wakes me.

There are days when I do not leave the rim. On those days I sit with frogs and count to a thousand. Vines turn because a hundred kilometers away a river under our feet is deciding to put its head in another bed. Orchids practice. Factories confess under their breath into the conduits that lace the dark and listen to hear whether the chord they get back says, acceptable. Somewhere in the mid-plain a diode breathes the way a good word does in a mouth that trusts it. Spiders graze a man's lamp because he tied it to a branch and left it open like a flower to dry and the world here thinks that is a joke. I drum the rim's belly and the bowl answers and the rain stands up like a creature learning to walk. It stumbles and then it learns.

You ask me where I would find myself in this world and who or what I would be. I answer that I am a person taught by a place to live like weather that minds its manners. I make neighbors in gradients. My mouth sips. My tools whisper. My line hums with other people's lives as they pass. I am not a population. I am a habit enacted by bodies that prefer intimacy to spectacle. On good nights I am almost a diode: small, faithful, green as a careful breath. On better mornings I am the hand on a cliff's throat, a midwife to upward rain, counting with the frogs while the vines turn their leaves to listen and the plateau rehearses itself again. Erosion is practice. I am practicing. I am learning how to be a citizen of night.
