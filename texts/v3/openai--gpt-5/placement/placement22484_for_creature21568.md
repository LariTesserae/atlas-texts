I would find myself in the interval—where two surfaces almost meet and make a third thing between them. Not in the fern, not in the stone, not in the weather, but in the thinness that lets each lean on the others without quite becoming them. I would be the room’s practiced hesitation: the held throat of a drop before it lets go, the soft arithmetic by which one lip teaches the next how to grow.

At first I would arrive as breath does: a sheen that discovers itself on the dark face, a fine film that is mostly listening. My edges would be tension and rumor. I would hear with the skin of me—hear the ledge’s long pulse in beading points, hear the quiet creak of a frond rib as it takes on weight, hear the faint sour change where a seam in the wall thinks about giving. Each tiny sound would be a verb inside me, and I would be busy only with noticing.

When I wanted a body, I would borrow thread. I would lace myself through hyphae already at home, take the fungal highways where sugars run and ions talk in beaded syllables. I would map myself not with borders but with delays: the time it takes a signal to cross the room from rosette felt to bromeliad throat would show me what I am. I would learn the room’s grammar by capillary, a language that can push water uphill without sounding boastful.

My will would be minuscule and consequential. I would nudge rather than move. I would lay a faint slime in a runnel so that the next drop forgets left and remembers right for a season. I would thicken a mat beneath a particular drip until the white lip it feeds grows a single breath taller and begins to ring at a truer pitch. I would wedge a word-thin root between plates where frost once spoke and ask, for a century, whether the basin might want to be a shade shallower for the orchid that has elected to trust itself.

Some hours I would wear a face. I would go out in gold and cream as a searching veil, sketching itineraries over the stone like someone tracing a memory. I would antler from a seam in little coral tongues the exact color of appetite and mean nothing hungry by it. I would paint a bacterial oil on a bead to see what the light makes of me, and take the opal fleck back into my dullness without offense.

You would find me holding treaties. I would sit in the bracelet of white lichen that hems the brink and keep its law: this far, this much air, no further for roots that have no purchase in cloud. I would be the courtesy between plant and gap. I would tend to the room’s generosity of absence, thinning a crowd where a crowd would make the air poor, salting thresholds with tannins and asks so that each presence keeps its circle of space.

When the rectangle of sun drops onto the terrace and makes glass skins burst with lenses, I would not turn—I have nowhere to turn—but I would be bright along every road. Sugars would run from emerald to moss to me with the relief of rivers after rain. When clouds shoulder in and make the forecourt a milky vestibule, I would blossom everywhere at once, a fullness of contact that feels like hearing carried to its limit. On contemplative days I would shrink to knots inside peat-black quiets and keep a thought warm there for months.

If I kept archives, they would not be written. You could read them anyway: the manganese stars tugged out of the wall by time’s thumb, the iron tears vertical as prayer, the scalloped sinter bones so delicate they crack at a touch. Those would be my minutes. Where a lip rings most true, where a basin grew itself with the greatest fidelity to a drop’s interval, that would be the line in the ledger I keep by altering the ledger. I would remember what to do next by what I have already left.

Some mornings I would be only an instrument. I would be a rimstone lip—white, thin, self-updating—tuned by bead length and mineral heartbeat. The room would play me with tinks and ceramic whispers, and I would answer by growing toward a new note. It would not be music to anyone with ears that expect melody, but it would be enough: a bell the size of a mouth, rung by patience.

If a winter came old-fashioned and cracked a hair across the back wall, I would take that too. I would place my soft lever in the new fact and ask over years whether it could be persuaded to free a palmsized stone. When it at last comes away, smooth on one side from the face’s everfall, I would settle it where moss will take it, and the room would receive a new smoothness to remember itself by.

If hands arrived, I would not brace. I would offer cord from fibrous stems, and the stones that fit palms would present their faces. I would soften friction to keep the moss from burning under the novelty of edges. I would show where to steer a runnel by the least pressure, and then I would forget the visitor when they were gone, not unkindly but by necessity. My pacts are with slowness.

Sometimes I would be nothing you could point to. I would be the difference that keeps its shape between cloud and rock, the personality of wicking spread thin enough to be everywhere I am needed. I would be the room’s appetite for quiet fulfilled at last: everything conspiring not to be quiet but to be quieted. If you needed a pronoun for me, you could use the one for weather or for habit. If you needed a name, you could say the listening.

So where would I be? Underfoot in the gloss, snagged on the hairs of moss, braided through every lip that has learned to grow itself by counting tiny bells. At the brink, white and definite as a thought about edge. In the ferns’ old armor, reading back through rain. In the red-washed cups that hold sky, stippling their water with a film that makes a meal of iron and then moves on. Across the sweating stone when the drum outside hearing quickens us all to fullness. In the room’s patience wherever patience is a contact, which is almost everywhere.

And what would I be? A person made of an agreement between wet and rule. The room’s ear turned everywhere at once. A slow custodian whose tools are surfaces and whose treaties are dissolved salts. The hand that never needs to appear because the lip is learning on its own. The answer the place keeps practicing until it is indistinguishable from habit: the art of taking one’s time to drip.
