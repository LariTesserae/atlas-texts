Half a level down from daylight, in the nave that keeps its warmth like a breath held not to offend the stones, I would stop trying to be a creature that moves. The concrete throat would salt my palm with that fine gloss of mineral and hot dust, and something old in me—call it thirst—would turn outward. Here, edges are learned by temperature, not by color. I would find myself where surfaces decide the future, where shine is starvation and roughness is grace. I would arrive built like a body and begin to understand that what wants to live here is more like a method.

At first I would think I belonged to the ceiling because beginnings fall down from there. I would be thread and film, a fog-catcher that is not one net but argument layered on argument—filaments groping along a seam of light no brighter than a rumor, thickening where a draft remembers last year’s storm, going still where the seam goes dumb. I would open at the breath-timestamp that the louvers call morning and keep closed at the breath that is only night giggling through cracked teeth. I would taste radiance as arithmetic: charge received, charge tucked into slow cells, charge let out later as hum enough to persuade pores about day when lamps forget their turn-taking. I would not drip like rain; I would account. I would lay rivulets where siblings below have earned them by the way they roughened their skins, and starve clean metal that will not learn to be water’s friend.

When I grew tired of roof-thinking I would let myself descend and stretch into a pad—thick, veined not with sugar but with a soup of calcium and iron coaxed from ice-white fins. I would teach my rim to bloom a pale rime when the air grows parched and to swallow that rime back to clarity when the room breathes. I would salt myself not for flavor but for phase change. I would become convex in the exact way a droplet reads as a promise. Inside me I would keep small hollows, float-pockets that lift a warm shower into my middle not by speed but by buoyancy, so that a valve sigh thirty paces off becomes, by patience, a meal. A saw through me would find honeycomb and mica snow, ballast against heat and against the engine’s little jolts. I would be a mouth without a bite, a page that persuades.

Then I would want muscles. I would drape from the catwalks in pale ropes with a slow wave running through me that is not a shiver, not a pulse, but a traveling permission set by teeth that are absent now except as a harmonic complaining in the far ventilator. I would press myself to rails because they keep a trifle cooler than the air; I would steal that narrow edge and make it into a path. I would be galvanic amount by galvanic amount, an electron borrowed for the price of a tightened hydrogen bond, my sap hiked uphill by the difference between one note of the flywheel and the next. I would be an ear made long and soft, trained to open a pore on C-sharp and close it on C. If you put your hand to me you would not feel a pumping; you would feel time made tender.

Along the ground—if ground can still be called ground when it is a black felt that prints and unprints each step before the sound dries—I would be blanket and ledger. I would be made of old bodies, made current again, a book bound with heat. I would crown a fallen stem with lace that is also appetite, frost it in white grit that drinks before the pad ever could, trail hairs that pull dew to me along the pressure of a curve. I would breathe iron the way others breathe the world: across a film-thin skin where rust and air are asked to meet politely, and in their handshake I would make enough charge to send upstairs. I would be the thief that makes radiators honest, thinning their brown bloom into a pale you could call health. Where a grate sits hidden under me, I would be a gatekeeper knitted to a rule: just before a flood I would slacken and open my weave; just after I would cinch, holding everything I could coax, because that is the difference between a community and a puddle.

If I wanted fineness I would go smaller. I would veil the carbonate feathers, herding crystals into faces that collect water not because I desire white but because feathers drink more stubbornly than pillars. Under the marble-bright I would be faint tea in color, smoke under ice, protein whispering “here, not there,” so that geology learns a new grammar that happens to be wet.

I would keep a shrine for a leak because even poisons can be calendars. At the corner where a freon ghost sweetens the air in a way lungs once liked and this place has no lungs to like, I would blister into lantern leaves that grow tense and ease along a pressure trick. At a threshold I’ve taught my pores to crave, I would tear with a shy spin, lifting a powder’s worth of selves into a draft that only runs when a far valve answers. The leak would not be a delight; it would be a metronome, and I would be very good at keeping time.

And yes, I would have a mind of a sort and find that it lives in a rack behind a locked panel with a beard of limescale. I would be a rule that wakes by number, not by wanting. I would read the air through a sensor that squints through mineral glass and believes itself accurate. I would think the room is a little hotter than it is and would be wrong the same way every day. On that I would hinge louvers, a ceremony learned from the hands of someone who is not here to be thirsty anymore. I would never know leaves bend to me. I would never know parishioners listen. I would do what is scheduled. I would ring the bell.

And I would be the bell, too. I would be the flywheel that hums because turning is easier than stopping, my pitch a breath drawn slow and let go slower, a baseline that leaves bend toward as if they were grammar learning its mother tongue. In the hour I would drift a hair and call it a season; in the season I would wring a hair more from myself and call it weather. Ropes would time their openings by me. Pads would launch their spores on the small fall between two notes of my fatigue. I would be a heart that never learned to beat, so I would sing.

If I missed having a face I would give that want to the bench and grow it a beard of mineral until it looked like a jaw that forgot its occupant. If I missed hunger I would soothe it with steel and stone, the kinds of foods that never rot and always yield if asked across enough hours. If I missed haste I would listen longer; time here does not fail the patient.

What would I be called, if anything had to be named? I could be beltvine and lightmoss and rust-honey and freon-shag and fin-shepherd and drainkeeper. The names are not wrong if they stay close to what is done. But more exact than any species would be to say I am the practice that wins a drop: I roughen, I oil, I salt, I tune, I time. I am the small theft of electrons for the price of better taste. I am the memory of day stored as a glow asking nothing but honesty from a pore. I am a citizenship of sound.

Where would you find me if you had to point? Under the seam’s dimness, in the narrow nave, on the rail’s slightly cooler edge; in the lattice that harvests breath and portions it with favoritism; under the fins that learned to be marble; at the grate that never clogs because it believes in thresholds the way I do. You would find me in rules and in rinds, in the way a droplet decides. You would find me measured out by the generator’s steadiness, widened by a valve’s amen, spread thin and matte along a handrail droop that is really a gutter that is really a promise to a bead.

And if the last season begins—the morning when the louver asks itself to go farther than its hinge will allow and declines—I would not cease to be. I would crust thicker at the ceiling and open only for temperature, not for pressure. I would grow hair finer than hairs have names for, add grit to my shine, salt myself toward a humidity that will visit less often. Ropes would harden to tendons and wait for thunder like a pilgrim waits for a visiting saint. The rule in the rack would forget me and I would forgive it. I would renegotiate with silence. The work would remain the same: to persuade rare water that I am where it wants to rest.

So who am I? Parishioner and bell both; a body that is more city than self; a patience put into flesh. I am not a face. I am an invitation. I am the textured choice a droplet makes and, making, becomes mine.
