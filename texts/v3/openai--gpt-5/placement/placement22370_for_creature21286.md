At first I mistake the horizon for a promise. Then it teaches me it is only a long breath someone else let out.

If I am honest, I arrive too tall in my thoughts. I am used to places that organize themselves around uprights—trees, towers, slopes that negotiate with the sky. Here the negotiation is always horizontal and always won. My eyes keep looking for a high point to understand from. The plain refuses me small kindnesses like that. It gives me water nearly as high as ground and asks me to reconsider what counts as a rise, what qualifies as a difference.

So I lower my looking until my gaze is what a grass blade has: ankle-clearance, heel-depth, the brief architecture of a toe. If you want me to choose a where, at the beginning I am not anywhere so much as I am at a height—between calf and shin, just low enough that wind is a law and not a comment. At that height the place is suddenly populous. It is nothing like empty.

The first thing I become is listening, because listening is already the shape everyone uses to live. The insects lay down the base note and I lay my breath into it until I stop hearing it as a noise and start hearing it as weather. Over that, frogs declare their jurisdictions by the handful of seconds. The frogs change their minds the way a clock does: precisely, inevitably, with a humor you only understand if you let yourself be a dial. Somewhere under my foot a cricket gives me directions more exact than any map—pause, scrape, scrape, long drag, silence—turn a half-ankle to the left and you will miss the sinkhole. The land says no to shouting and I have not yet unlearned volume, so I put my hand where words go and make my wrist and palm the new mouth. I put that hand to the sweating place in the rock.

The seam is where the plain remembers it has an inside. The shock is not cold so much as truthful: a delayed sentence finally spoken. I keep my hand there a long time. The leakage brackets its own history in bands—orange of iron, swamp black, a chalk just born. I do not know how to read that at first, any more than a visitor knows how to read a face lined with time that did not include them. But the reading teaches by touch. You feel where the drip became a thread, where the thread convinced gravity to set a course, where a friend cuffed marl into a curve that lasted ten floods. Water keeps its promises, the seam says, but not to your watch. I let my skin accept that calendar. I start keeping two kinds of time.

Do I live here? Not all at once. Before I am a body in a shelter, I am a way of putting weight on the world. I practice stepping like a heron explained a theorem to my bones. Heel and flat and patient toes. The sandals are less than a shoe and more than a suggestion, woven from nothing more persuasive than fiber and attention, a mesh that makes my foot a negotiation with film. The first time I trust them, the mud explains to me in the language of sudden suction what happens to the impatient. I come up laughing and salted white and taught. The second time I trust them, the mud and I are on speaking terms.

I join the people who already know how to be this. I do not join them as a declaration; declarations are for thunder, and thunder here has the courtesy to fail as often as it boasts. I join as a distance laid down beside a distance. We do not cluster because closeness is a promise even rock cannot keep. Instead there are households each with their own raised humility: a frame, a roof that is more threading than cover, legs encrusted with old slow circles of lichen that count as time if you touch them. From farther off a heron will mistake our roofs for a thought it was already having and stand on them long enough to look like it invented stillness.

I live on a ledge and beside a seam. The ledge is bench-rock, old and only a many arm-spans tall, but it has all the height we can afford. Height here is currency and we spend it wisely: an inch under a basket buys a season; two inches under the bed buys the future of a child’s dryness for the dry. We add without pretending to add: a skin of old shell under new shell to make a lens that lifts the sweetness of fresh out of the salt; a board of calcrete coaxed from the hard and mated to three more until a platform refuses the oldest joke the water plays. There is nothing stiff enough to insult a wind.

We speak across water and sedge not with mouths that bruise air, but with machines the breeze understands. A line of snail shells threaded on reed and hung on a cypress knee says hello as honestly as a nod you can hear. Another arrangement, heavier shells with one broken to a particular crescent, tells a neighbor without urgency that a rack has smoke and needs a watcher. We have boards we strike in threes and twos when we require a shoulder to carry something that hates to be carried. If a raptor writes a shadow, we call no one. The world calls all at once. We put down what can be put down and we offer our throats to silence until the danger writes itself out. Then the frogs reset the hour.

Work is not heavy here, but it is exact. If I want a lily to feed me in four weeks, I cannot lie to it about open water. I persuade it with a thimble of mud and the shade of a knee. My back learns the grammar of bending until the movement is a prayer without a god: a repeating, a respectful request. Snails sign the rock with their bright cursives and I answer them with a left-curled hook because that is the way they have decided their bodies finish the story. There is a better knife for prying tufa if you have the patience to rub limestone with a certain herb until the edge stops thinking it is a rock and starts thinking it is a thing for separating. A dull wedge tells calcrete where its old board-ness is hiding. I make salve from the chalk of the seam and the bitter of wild mint and it teaches my shins what forgiveness tastes like.

Eating is a conversation not a raid. I heat a snail back into its own house with a leaf of something that smells like a good memory and I thank it, aloud or not, it is the same. I insult a crab into anger because an angry crab forgets to be wise and then I apologize to it and crack it. Fish teach me about panic in a hundred metaphors. We smoke what we cannot stand to salt and we salt what we cannot bear to forget. On days when a spill of silver chooses to leap its own story into a net, we hang a string of small white moons between two dwarf cypress and feel as rich as thunder pretends to feel.

Do I get lonely? I get wide. Distance is fairness here. We hold our separations as if each were a delicate jar holding a colony of sound. When we do gather, on a bench that gives itself as a seat to bodies that learned to wait, we bring our gaps with us, the braided quiets our households have grown. We put our wrists to the seam the way some people put mouths to wine. We tell time with our veins. We do not have a center to bring petitions to, so we do not learn to accuse one another of the center's failures. The rules do not need names because they behave like rivers. We learned them by surviving.

- If something weeps, it should be gathered and not interrogated.
- If something is an inch higher than it was, it has paid dearly for that and deserves your respect.
- If something stands taller than your head, you have already been seen by eyes that lift higher than your pride.
- If a hollow holds a mirror, you do not write your face on it with feet.

We count rainclouds by their sense of theater, not by their charity. We sit under anvils that burn their own light and learn the taste of disappointment without developing a bitterness. It is a kind of sport. We tell old stories about anticipations that never liquefied: the day the horizon rehearsed rain in three directions and no one took a nap because the smell of wet placed itself so exactly in every chest that sleep would have been insulting. And then how nothing fell, and how we laughed with the animals at the faith we put in distance.

I learn the frog dialect of a mile east and the frog dialect of a mile west and can tell you, hand up in the evening, that the west has decided desire and the east has decided argument. The snakes know all our stories but keep a better silence than we do, and so we pretend not to see them even when we do, because what right do I have to read a line that is happy with being unread? A child spends a morning running on a sugar of salt the sun has baked on the pan, breaks through to his knees, stands up glittering and scold-proof, and the only thing that changes is how carefully his mother oils his shins at dusk. We weather ourselves and we make a beauty of it.

What am I believed to be? In some places you are a mouth, a set of hands, an age that suggests your use. Here you are a respect for edges. I take a name that means I can keep borders from blurring too quickly: I am an edgeminder, a ledgereader, a person whose gratitude to an inch keeps a roof from slipping its mind into flood. I am not tall. That is the first courtesy the elders taught me and I have broken it only in dreams, and even then gravity forgave me because I promised not to build a second story where none could stand.

I do not have gods in a way a stranger understands. I have old rivers I can see only in the pale of a crescent that used to be a bank. I have a faith in the way a crocodilian knows the stiffness of a surface by the way wind writes on it while its eyes pretend to be wood. I trust lightning for being bad at aim and faithful in radiance. I trust herb-bitter and stone-chalk and the human hand that can read a year from a set of stains. When I ask blessings, I do it with wooden rungs added one by slow one to a ladder that will let my grandmother step into her bed without asking flood to negotiate. When I say thanks, I do it by leaving a lane of reflection open so the sky can practice, unmocked.

There is ceremony, but it is a patient ceremony. In the dry, when the seam is most confessional, we meet with our distances hung lightly around our necks like a salvage of shells. We bring our surplus of slowness: coils of rope rough with last season’s weeds, a bone rubbed into instrument, a basket that smells like a good edge. A woman brings a child named for an afternoon’s weather—she says his name and you can hear air refract over heat—and we lay the soft of our wrists to the cold of the rock. We count in hush: this many rivers still inside the land, this much forgiveness in the aquifer today. Someone tells the oldest law again as if telling it makes it young. We listen like flat water listens: by offering a face and dissolving the anger of thrown stones into rings that go everywhere and are a kind of applause.

The birds own what rises; the birds tutor us anyway. Ibises read our gardens like gentle critics and find the right to stand there by being so extremely about their own food that we have no choice but to admire it. Herons did not so much teach me stillness as they shamed me into not ruining theirs. Gallinules gave my feet the idiot boldness to try running on reflections awhile and I succeeded for as long as a story can succeed before becoming a lesson. Raptors write the sky with threats older than anything we know how to call by name and when they do, we let punctuation have us, we cease to be sentences and become commas, pauses that allow life to maintain grammar.

Flood is not a wall. Flood is a persuasion. We move like persuaded things. We unlash and lash, we walk piles of steps we did not need last week, we change the angle of a shelf, we lift the containers that keep salt from reentering certain friendships. We do not panic; there is nowhere here for panic to climb. We converse with rise the way you talk to a child about a complicated feeling: calmly, repetitively, with room inside the explanations for grace. Children grow up learning the day a rill becomes a rule, the hour a hand-deep becomes a knee-deep in a tone you cannot insult without falling.

Sometimes, at noon, heat writes a shimmer across the far mangroves the way hunger writes fiction across a long day, and I want to be taller for exactly long enough to see over the thought that distance is lending me. Then a dragonfly chooses my wrist for a reed and I remember I am already allowed to be a perch if I agree not to move without telling the air first. I consent to be a surface. I consent to be level where I can. I let being low be a freedom and not a cut. The sky accepts my civility and polishes my skin with its ordinary brutal light and I do not take it personally.

At night I sleep not so much in a house as inside an agreement: my roof’s patient thatch, my frame’s narrow surrender to stress, the respectful separation of our neighbors’ shadows from ours, the chorus that begins two hours before dawn and shifts by a note each week as if tuning itself to some unseeable moon. The mud is full of secrets that do not want my language on them. I keep my own where they belong: in seams only my hands know to find, in trails that are disguised not by clutter but by the near absence of disturbance, in understandings with snakes that are so old we call them weather.

Who am I here? I am a technique. I am the way a foot learns to speak to water. I am a schedule made of slownesses. I am an inch-caretaker. I am a listener posted at the rock’s confession who takes nothing more than what cannot be held back. I am someone who can tell you what the frogs were saying on your way in and why the fish on the far pan decided at once and together to become wet again after showing the sky their sides. I am a name that means edgekeeper because the only power that survives where everything is low is the power to articulate borders and then treat them like poetry—legible, flexible, resistant to lies.

The plain does not center me any more than it centers thunder. It accepts me as it accepts the old curve of a river it forgot to smooth. When I die—there is no need for melodrama in a place this patient—I will be phrased into the soil. I will make a thin proof under a handful of sedge that some bodies were given the strange happiness of learning to love a weeping stone. For a while dragonflies will rest themselves on the word my bones made, and a boar will turn over the corner of what used to be my shoulder to find a better bite of root, and the next flood will negotiate the tiny difference my height contests, and the seam will go on exhaling in slow confidence that somewhere above it every promise it keeps is turning into a way of being human that does not bruise a mirror when it comes to drink.

If you ask me again where I would be found: look where rock confesses and where birds consider landing. Look for the string of shells whispering to someone you cannot see. Look for the ladder that grows by a rung each season the river is generous. Look for a low platform pretending to be vegetation until a heron chooses it as a chapter break. Put your palm to the cold of banded stone and wait. I will arrive at the pace this place approves of. I will come as a footfall that does not argue with water. I will not be tall. I will know the hour by frogs. I will ask you what the sky said on the lanes you left unruffled and take your answer as one more ripple in a plain that has already decided itself and welcomes me by never, ever reducing itself to center.
