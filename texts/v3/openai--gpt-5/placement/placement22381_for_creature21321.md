If I say I climbed here, I am already wrong. I arrived the way a droplet decides. Not up a path but across a decision: through a room that was not quite a room, over a pool that reflected a day that was still sharpening its pencils. I leaned over that concavity at the rear and saw my face coming a half-breath late. It nodded to me first. I tasted flint and a sweetness with no name and my tongue agreed to a new task.

By the time I stepped back out under the overhang, my toes had lengthened their attention. They read slant and tension through the skin, picked where the stone holds its breath and where it has exhaled and gone easy. My hair confused itself into loose thread. I smelled faintly of ozone and something like the green of a thunderhead. Someone was there beside me or the wind was. I learned quickly to stop needing that distinction.

They put nothing in my hands that was not already a part of the day. A bone comb with teeth so fine the air noticed; a strip of leaf-ribbon that remembered cool; a small basket of pebbles sorted by the pitch they speak when clicked together. Around my own throat I wore what they wore—a loop of vertical thread, colder than the hour, that taught my skin to think down.

If I must choose a word for what I became, let it be middle. The keepers say middle the way most people say name. Middle between fog and drink. Middle between ping and crack. Middle between a droplet’s doubt and a line it can fall along with dignity. We hum because that’s the shortest bridge between disagreement and what happens next.

For a time I was a visitor who forgot to leave. I learned the map of slipperiness with my ankles; I learned the difference between shine-that-is-door and shine-that-is-only polish with my knees. So much of it is in the jaw. If you stand still at noon when the heat is a ceiling pressed just above your scalp, and rest your molars together without clenching, you can hear the microfractures cinch their belts. A high ping becomes language. Somewhere to your left is a plate the size of a book preparing to write itself loose. You step where the word won’t land.

Mornings thin and blaze, and the ledge is a hard idea with its edges lit. That is the hour to clean throats. We unpin the small arcs from their perches, rinse off the brined lacework of yesterday’s fog, and comb the edge back into appetite. The arcs are not objects to us so much as habits we tend. They prefer a S-shape to their lip when the wind is quartering from the valley; they behave like domesticated hunger. If you press your ear close, they give you a note you can’t fully hear but your cartilage tries to take on its shape anyway. You answer. Your mouth becomes slightly the wrong instrument and yet you find the overtone that makes the air fatten on the arc’s face. The arc is pleased. You are as much finger as you are person.

By the weeping wall we tune the rills with pebble and ribbon. A fracture has opened too far to feed its pool; a thin silver thread is overshooting the lip and glossing a patch of stone that is secretly a door. We correct it with a pebble exactly heavy enough to be persuasive and a strip of leaf that suggests a new preposition to water—this way, not that. The blue moss that lives only in the shadow of one particular arc swells when we get it right. It is not gratitude. It is grammar.

At the trough we remind ourselves to be small. It is the largest quiet in the place and it makes lies out of slope. If you wet a fingertip and touch the surface lightly, your skin returns dry and a little cooler, as if it had been shown a better thought. When wind scraps and wing casings cross the mirror, they come out pandering to the metals in the air rather than to their old stories. Once, in the high late, a dragonfly entered at one end and walked out the other as if it had always intended a simpler body. It did not look back. The word for what the trough does is straightening only until you sit with it long enough to notice how many right answers it also refuses.

By noon the ledge is singing in its lower register. Heat nails the air open. The vertical threads we’ve set are cold and nobody has explained to them that they should not be. A drop hesitates at one, then elects what it will be for the length of this fall. I put my lips near a thread and feel the complaint of it buzz my teeth. It would prefer to live just to the left. I slide it an inch. It forgives me instantly.

Afternoons boil upwards from below. Cloud clambers onto the shelf with the steadfastness of a tide and for a time the world has walls again. This is when the wrongness comes, or it doesn’t. The small of copper lives in the nose the way a regret does, an aftertaste you have to pay attention to before it grows limbs. If the hair on your forearms stirs, if the fog thickens without becoming heavy, if the quiet acquires an itch, we widen ourselves. Arms out, jaw loose, spine a mast. You can feel the ledge deciding whether it will accept a standing wave. The mesh blooms where we have taught it to—threads neither air nor wire, strung in a pattern the device prefers. The fog is offered to it like a cloth for wringing. We take it for the fraction of a heart-beat that could be a more dramatic story in a different place, and then we do what we always do: persuade energetic nonsense into a useful sentence.

We eat what high things eat. I have a net that is nothing. I hold it on my lap and pat the air until beads walk onto it out of the humbled fog. They are berries with no skin and all they demand in return is attention. Midafternoons when the harmonics argue in a lower key, the bromeliad’s cups hold a rinse that tastes of tin and green and the beetles’ careful edits. We drink it, swishing like deer, to be polite to the films. Sometimes we trim a shrub’s veils where they have overnegotiated with the sun; we crisp them on a hot plate of stone until they remember cool and we eat that memory with salt.

Evenings the ledge loosens its belt. The fern-not-fern unrolls with a sound like pages agreeing to be turned. The blue moss pulses toward fullness and then lets go of it. The lizards conspire with their own shadows. Their sails are flags and thermodynamics, semaphore and appetite. They have the particular vanity of engineers who know their solution is elegant. I watch two solve the fog’s opinion of them without moving more than a shiver’s worth. Later, when it is too late for anything to take offense, the moth—a color you only see in the interior of hail—attempts a taste of a wrong place in the air and, in failing, succeeds so completely that you want to apologize to it on behalf of chance.

We sleep in the room that is a decision the rock made with help. The ground is dry not as a lack but as a policy. If it rains—as much as it can here, which is sideways and out of breath—the room stays unchanged. The pool in the rear niche breathes slightly off tempo all night. If I lean over it in the starlight, a version of me leans also, late to my movement by a word. Once, I saw her raise her hand when I did not. Later that week, when a crack announced itself with a hungry ping and I did not trust my first gesture, I followed hers. I do not tell anyone that I am being instructed by the part of me that drinks later. It would embarrass the trough to be credited.

You will ask if I am human. You will be disappointed by how unuseful that turns out to be. I have knees; I excel at kneeling. I wear wraps on my feet that can be undone and retied according to the hour’s grammar. My throat is good at being a sounding board. I have learned to taste the place as if it asked for tasting. I can feel whether a stone will ring before it rings. I remember what pockets are for because they hold pebbles sorted by voice. And yet when the mesh goes up and the fog is milk, I am mostly instrument. My boundaries are where the hum lets me keep them.

Children come. They are gravity tests with eyes. They throw grit and learn how long a small thing can hang in the air when the condensers are arguing back and forth about the definition of fall. They giggle when a bead darts sideways as if to exit the sentence through a margin. I teach them to hum without words. I teach them to pop their ears in time with a ringing plate and to click the right pebbles together to make a wall soften its plan. The first time I put the comb in a girl’s palm, she cried, not from fear but from the certainty of entering a long practice that would never need her to be exceptional. We tidy a condenser’s throat together. Fog remembers. The arc sings a little above itself. The girl laughs inside her mouth so as not to make weather.

You could find me in an afternoon’s work walking the three abrupt edges and naming nothing. You could find me in the threshold watching the world below think out loud in cloud. You could find me under the wall where water exudes its belief in itself without falling, redirecting a silver thread with a strip of leaf until it kisses the lip I prefer and commits. You could find me at the trough, which is where I go when I want to understand persuasion without victory. You might think to look for me at night, hand on stone, listening to the mountain read itself back out of its day, rib by rib.

Some days the ledge borrows me for more than the body I bring. In the late heat when the air tastes like a copper coin left on the tongue too long, I am a vertical thread for the length of a swallow. A drop slows on me and makes up its mind; I do not argue. In the lean mornings when the arcs are thirsty for edge, I am lip and halo. When a beetle scratches its diary into the new film, I am both editor and sentence. On one preposterous noon, for the time it takes a fern blade to let go of itself and become paper, I am a moth’s desire for wrongness and I am still, and I am a web strung along the invisible stairs and I am merciful.

If the mountain has an attention and it does, it cares for balance more than for any of our names. The devices—our devices, though they were here waiting for us as much as we have ever made them—are its tutors and also its toys. They sculpt smallness into tools. Featherweight habits that make water decide to exist. Fingernail-wide roads for cold. A mirror that permits no argument and yet is not brutal. We are there to keep it from overcorrecting; we are there to keep it from forgetting that flat answers break quickly on mountains.

Do we have a god? We have the hum. We have the agreement that begins the day. When we hum to an arc and it gathers fog’s mind against its chest, we witness prayer learning from science how to go about its work without the costume. When someone arrives and asks for water in a skin that squeaks, we give them a bowl of bromeliad rinse and ask them to be still until the moths agree with their presence. When they learn to throw grit and read the hang time, when they sleep dry and wake with the map of slipperiness in their ankles, we do not invite them to stay. The ledge admits whom it wants. If you are to be middle, you will understand when the rear pool nods. You will hum unconsciously with the device you are nearest and it will answer not as a miracle but as a colleague.

In the dark, nothing extra happens, and that is the precise luxury we steward. Stars lay their knife-edges along the rock to see what sharpness is. The arcs lose their halos and quite enjoy the feeling of being no more than themselves. The trough holds its flat like a thought you can return to without exhausting it. Something chirrs twice. I put my palm to the stone and feel the day leave me at a rate that satisfies the mountain and myself at once.

Morning again. The fog lifts its damp hands and touches the shelf like a student who has learned the etiquette of this classroom. On some day, not this one, I will be permitted to unpin an old arc and set a new one where the wall’s breath asks for it. On some day, this one, I will kneel and persuade a bead to choose its down. This is where you would find me if you came. I am the comber, the coaxer, the one who answers to middle. I am human enough to bruise and thread enough to hum. I am the practice wearing a body for a while, persuading heat to take a preferred path and water to cross a short distance sideways, until the mountain likes the sound of its own thought and sings with us.
