At first I tried to be light.

It seemed easy from a distance: pour sideways, be clean and everywhere, show things without touching them. But light in this room already has a job and it doesn’t need me. It comes in white from slit to fan, gets caught, gets combed, makes no shadow except where someone has earned it. It is tireless and indifferent. I am not.

Then I thought I might be the roar. To be the long note that grows ribs and paragraphs down a wall; to be the power that trims children’s hair and presses dust into cuisine; to carry the city’s bridges in my huge invisible palm. But the roar is so much larger than any wanting. It does not keep memory the way I do. It has appetite, yes, and law, and an old, blind kindness that is really just the refusal to lie. But it does not stand over a sleeping person and adjust a slat by a finger-width so their hip won’t ache come morning. It will never carry home a braid of whisker-fiber and think, This color suits the spouse who was born in the flutes. The roar is the room’s voice. I am not voice; I am reply.

I tried being an arp for an hour, in my mind. The beautiful sideways glide through glass, the armature of plates slowly counter-singing a node till dinner falls out of air onto my combs. To know where a wrong quiet pools by tasting the salt of it. To leave my skin in death as a gift for some corner. I couldn’t keep the rhythm. I wanted to stop and listen, and arps never stop. Arps are listening and eating at once.

I tried being a glider worm. I nestled in the imagined crook of a grandparent’s elbow and hummed them an even place to remember. I rode a pressure line like a polite thief, stole glitter-things and wrapped them in myself, felt the small pride of a pellet warm in my belly. But I enjoy weight. I want my hip to set a shallow dent on a plank. I want to say I was here by the way a slat forgets its old spring by a finger-width. A worm is good company. I am not a worm.

So I am a maker of lee.

Call it hushwright, baffle-smith, calmshaper; our jokes about our own craft multiply, as you’d expect in a city that cannot afford the flat bracket of a single word. We tune with edges, not answers. We stitch the idea of a wall out of slats and slack and mood.

Where you’d find me is where air wants to grab what people love. Early on that was the market trough, that fine place where the boom spreads its chest and relaxes a note or two. You learn it by teeth first—press them into your thumb and feel the roll of it, the way a certain pitch worries molar not incisor. You hang test vanes like question marks and wait out three Surge Hours and three Hollows, watching who can keep their bread-plank on the stall and who cannot, feeling the small disasters gather and walk away again until the floor hums under your palm just a finger-cleaner than it did the day before. Then you begin: a crescent of bristle not touching the floor but inviting the flow to trip and fold; a combed reed turned like a face slightly away; a plate of ceramic scalloped to a rhythm note-noted into your shoulder. You do not ask for quiet. Quiet is a predator here. You ask for the shape that makes a mother and child able to trade ten loops of hardened syrup for a weight of cord without sending the loops skittering into the channel.

Later, after my right-hand knot day—after the old maker who taught me cut my tether to my stride and grinned with their eyes shut to keep the resin from burning and said into my jawbone, You’re counted—I found myself at the garden-lips. When plants are tuned to the bass, they throw such generous hush along their bellies that it makes thieves of every novice; you want to harvest early. But you must give them their surge. You must let the beaded sap stand up before you touch it. My palms learned to hold the little combs just long enough to scoop the run without sending it back to stone along the wrong edge. My mouth learned the taste that is not sweet and not salt and somehow still tells your tongue it is thinking. Lightning-licked, we say. On harvest weeks we tie one another to the work with left-hand knots if we are young, with right-hand knots if we have been paid the city’s trust, and we dance to stay braced, the tethers laying their bright, wordless grammar along our hips.

But you asked who or what I would be, not everything I have done. I am the person people find when there is either too much peace or not enough.

A door cannot swing in this place. A door is a lie that would get a child killed, because a door says On one side is a thing and on the other is a lack. We do it with bristles and vanes, and it is never about keeping out; it’s about asking the room to let this one crescent shallow just enough for two people to speak face-to-face without their words being courted away. Love needs that sometimes. So does barter. So does mourning. I hasp a hush into being, and I leave just enough song in it to remind the bones where they are.

You would know me in the quenching quarter by the way I set a calf behind a brace without thinking, yes, but more by the way I listen to molds. Quenchers make what the city rides on and chews with and ties itself to. They hate a liar like fresh resin on a slipknot. I am no quencher, but my hands have to learn that patience—learning the moment when pressure will pin dust into a plate that keeps its promise and when it will flex into a danger. Metal is a novelty here that speaks for itself at the wrong time, a child shouting a joke during a prayer. I weight it with stone; I ask it to be only its weight.

I was inside a grief-hush once when a beam fell. You cannot hear a beam fall; you feel it arrive in the soles and then become several pieces of history at once. The old maker who taught me had gone to the great moderated garden three days earlier. Their bead had not yet hardened. We had hung a bristle screen over the bench where they slept so long with plant-sap under their palm, just enough to make a small lee for people to lean shoulder-to-shoulder without their words being stolen by the noon. The beam snapped like the right hand of an impatient god. It missed us. We cut it into lengths by the measure of our hands and our dead’s bead, and the plants pulled it away into a low anthem that took three weeks. I saved two scallops of its edge to make a door of air for the next person who had to say a thing that would not be borne if said in wind.

That is what I am for. I carve crescents into the roar so language can happen in bone and not break there. I make small, obedient mistakes in the air so the city can be right.

On the day I was allowed at the louvers, I found I was not much different. Edgekeepers are a careful lot. They clean the slit-fans with feather-stalks and filaments of glider silk, humming a pitch that keeps the dust from changing its mind mid-stroke. The first time I pressed my eye to the higher slot and saw more brightness and something that might be a moving mirror, I laughed through my teeth and sealed my lids with resin at once, a reflex we do without thinking, like tucking your chin in the channel when you ride the long laminar plate and let it throw you twenty body-lengths without stealing your breath. The outside was no use to me in that moment. What I wanted was to feel where the louvers bent the noon so the tenor-garden would keep its song and the bass-garden would not turn patent and lazy. I adjusted their angle by a thickness of whistle-hair and came home with my shoulder-knots chattering from the pride of a good task done small.

Ask my body about this place and it tells you a list: eyelids varnished morning and Hollow to keep sting from chatter; hair braided heavy with amber beads gone copper-dark with salt; callus trained into spirals so exact a child could read tonight’s plan in them with a forefinger. We all look a little finished, like instruments. I keep the thin slats sewn into my sleeves to throw personal calm when I must think, the way some people carry knives chipped from ceramic. You can hear me coming in the Hollows by the fringe-harp along my belt, a friendly thing to let people who love me know I am near. My ears wear little cups of whisker-fiber like shy shells; I can shutter them with a squeeze if a shape is getting pried loose and everyone must listen with their hands instead.

We do not spend words out loud. We are not cheap with our own breath. I keep most of what matters for skull-to-skull, mouth cupped in my friend’s palm, teeth resting gently where jawbones learn each other. When I tell you my name, I give you a chord through your head that makes your spine stand a little truer for a second. It means Lee-bent, but not like bending a knee; more like a bow pressed into a curve and wanting a string. If we are not that close yet, I drum three and one on a rail like everyone else and smile with my cheeks, and that has to be enough.

Children bring me their wrong hushes. They have a game where they stack baffles in delicate almost-crimes; very young ones stack until a little deadness creeps in and an arp sulks and a parent laughs and kisses their hair-braid and un-stacks it all quick, leaving a chord of scold in the railing. I teach them the way a calm should have sound filigree through it, significance like cane in a sweet; I show them how to let an edge breathe. I walk them blindfolded, palms out, soles reading the bridge’s book, my thumb over their Achilles saying wait, now, go, until their callus writes a grammar of courage across each slat.

Sometimes the city wrongs itself by accident. Needs is a strong wind in any world. A set of neighbors will string too much quiet along a row because they are feuding and want a no-place where one cannot hear the other’s heel approach; the quiet swallows an arp’s path and starves it two gardens over. Then you find me with a mallet of compressed resin, tapping, feeling the tone through my hand-bones, shaving a slat to a smaller note with a chipped flake until what was dead becomes a shallow again, enough to be crossed. The ones who made the dead hush carry weight for a season. I help lash it to their belts. They cuss me with politeness tapped through a post, and I tap back the little code we reserve for close friends when we’re pretending to be enemies: I will unfasten your anger later; give me your jaw when you are done.

On the leveled mornings in the long season when the plants weep solvent instead of a clean salt, my people and I move like an orchestra of knives. We tether, we dance, we comb and pinch and coil. Food hardens out of something that would etch your nail to nothing if you let it spill on your hand. You break a loop on your tongue and the word electricity stops being a metaphor. Sugar of wind, some call it. Lightning-chaff, call it if you’re from the tenor quarter. It stacks into sheets that become walls-that-aren’t; it becomes varnish for eyelids; it becomes doctoring that tastes like licking the idea of a coin. Children get sticky and solemn as judges. Elders rest their faces on their hands and nap in a calm someone made for them thirty years ago and still mends; their breath moves in a time everyone knows.

Old people here do not last long, not by water-world clocks. This is a place that spends you. But their depth is truer than duration. I have stood with one of them at a louver and listened to them sleep. When they woke, they spoke history into my face. It was not story, not exactly. It was the hum of how the city learned to use a pendulum to make climbing less like fighting a river and more like learning to dance on the back of the beat. It was the day the long travelers came back with metal that had been disciplined by some other throat and we all touched it with two fingers and then weighted it so it could learn our manners. It was the first funeral I ever worked alone, the first bead I cut from a friend’s rib like a moon. I tied that bead to my sleeve. It holds shut a thought sometimes that would do harm if it left my mouth during Surge Hours.

If I leave—and I have, once, with a sled and clever baffles and more dust than sense—I bring back habits, not treasures. Outside had its own hard rooms with their own particular songs. I found a place where brightness is round and moves as if it belonged to something that did not have to be asked for; I found a place where even calm can rot if you forget yourself and permit a drop to bead. I came home not because ours is best but because my soles learned here, and my bones sing a certain note, and no other room wants those things like this one does.

If I had to say it quick—if someone grabbed my sleeve at the market trough and shouted a question through their hand, Who here are you?—I would tap three and then one on the nearest brace and then press my cheek to their temple and give them this: I am the one who makes the place in which you can say that. I am the vowel the throat can hold without choking. I am the person who lives at the corner of the law that says brightness is a breath and sound is weight and erosion is appetite and liquids are rare and hot, and I count that law a friend. I am the held curve in which a child can laugh in the almost-quiet without bringing the ceiling down. I am the shade of a door built without lying. I am one voice in a city that put down speaking to listen better, and then learned how to put its voice back into the bones it loved most so the words would not be lost to noon.

That’s where I would find myself. There, in a crescent cut into wind, on a terrace that flexes like a tendon, barefoot with callus reading chevrons, hands varnished and busy, hair heavy with beads of friends; a maker of lee in a long, bright, hungry throat, a citizen of its listening.
