You would not catch me standing in the open of it, because there is no open, not the sort that lets a body declare itself to distance. You would find me limned by the smallest separations, nested in the air-calm that clings to edges. If you must look, look where two faces almost meet and do not, where the groove carries its bead with a patience close enough to love. Look in the delay. I am easiest to see there, in the time between a thock and its answer, in the length of a room turned to a number by your palm on hide.

At first I came as your sort does—on the surface of shoe-soles, made of thought and guess and the wish to know—but the place insisted on its own pronouns. It called me in by the knees and the wrists. It taught me how to be heatless in use, how to keep my breath laid down like a cool scarf inside my chest. It took the need to shout out of me the way salt draws damp out of stone: slow, inexorable, for the good of the wall. I tried on bodies as if they were tools to learn their songs.

For a while I was a child in their care. I learned to lace my hands behind me so they would not drift to a plate and undo its tuned grit with my oils. I learned to walk on the whisper-lipped steps without letting a heel skitter a gift of damp into an unthirsty corner. My first toy was a string run with bone flags. I struck it with a nail and walked away to hear what had the courage to hang in the air without me. I laughed once without thinking and a lane went stiff; it took two days of apology in the right beats to soften it. After that, I kept my joy under the hide curtains where it would not collide with anybody else's work.

Then I was a rope on a waist and then again on a winch: drier than any muscle but as obedient, salted just enough to be honest about my thirst. I learned to hum when asked and to shut up when the floor wanted to do the talking. I felt the room in my fibers when the cold ran in from the western cloths. The first time we bared them in sequence and the vault took its patient breath, I went tight as good manners; I held the dark up and the rain down, and when someone out of sight hit a bowl with a ragged mallet and called the name for brave, I answered with a purr to show I had heard. If I failed and sang at the wrong moment, a drop would creep along my lay and lose its nerve at my humming and go somewhere unplanned. I did not fail much after that.

I was a seam of foxfire in the mortar, a whisper that could be mistaken for sleep. I threaded my grain through the joint where a hand had pressed clay two months before and left a moonprint the size of a knuckle. When the chimes named thirst in their wet-slate color I thickened; when the room went to dry speak I thinned. The little that I made was not for seeing by unless you were already a person of this place. It was for telling your fingers where they were without them needing eyes. It was for marking out the work when the drums could not.

I was a moth once too—a sheet of velvet written on with a pattern that keeps reading after it leaves. I drank fog with my wings and pretended that counted as disobedience because no one else feeds that way. I did not have a mouth to boast with. My largeness was an illusion measured only by how long an eye kept me; when I was gone, the after of me hung in the dark like a muscle’s memory.

I tried being their taboo and failed it; I could not be a flame. I understood the hunger of it too well. Even in the pits under the lowest wells, where the Smoke-Breeders nursed the neat, pale heat that never rose, fire minded only itself. It had no mannerly bones. The work asked for courtesy. I went back to being what can be asked and will not answer unless the room consents.

I was a pole in a hand with a complicated end: copper leaf, stone striker, hooks like a private grammar. I flipped lids and did not look into the water as if naming it. I slipped the leaf under a shutter and pressed without clapping. The leaf remembered hands that had missed in a hurry and bore a single nick. I learned the weight of lids by the tone of their fall; I could tell a cistern that had ripened by the sound it handed back to my copper when I knocked it lightly, once, then again.

I was a lip cast new into an amended curve. I took the place where a channel had eaten through a maker’s early certainty and I admitted the edit. It was a rightness discovered by wear, and I was praised not for correctness but for timing—set in while the mortar could still learn. When the bell that means lose was struck in the nave and the crew applauded the change, I felt like a word that had always belonged in the sentence but had taken a few drafts to show.

Sometimes I was not a person or a part but a naming, and then I was as close to being myself as this place allows any thing to be. I stood on a slab with both feet cool and a mallet held like a second pulse. The hide before me was the diaphragm of a creature so large you could mistake it for a building. I chose my silences and slid them between strokes like skin between teeth. I waited, which was the bulk of the work: I let the room climb into my ear and live there as a map. The first thock went up like a stone thrown into a pool. It came back as a cough, long and a little tired, from the far slatted vault. The second thock went in the open wound of that cough, and the cough changed key. A Teller of Delay can read that change and tell you when to pull the cloth from the western vents so the cold and the sound will meet as friends instead of strangers. For a handful of seasons I had that ear. I spent it like coin and saved it like bread.

On nights of harvest, when the big drums went to their work and the vault answered like a cask remembering being a tree, I was also the interval that had to be held between one order and the next. If you open the shutters too greedily you heat the room with your impatience; the made rain turns to a huddle of damp in the wrong corners; the eels that we do not call by private name do not rise, or they rise in the wrong hour and go thin. Timing is a mercy extended to animals and stone alike. I kept it by counting the room’s decay—the echo thinning to a particular thread, the mushrooms lending their foxfire a notch, the drop rate in a bowl shifting from talk to thought.

I have been a law, but only in the way laws are kept here: white, lickable, temporary. Salt under a scraper. “Clap only where a clap is a tool.” I wore my letters as ridges and was refreshed when I blurred. Children were taught to tongue-search their teeth while they read me, as if taste and command could be kept neighbors in the same face and not rise up and make mischief with each other. I contained for a week the memory of a new courtesy—“do not seize a drop with your eyes; let it go where it goes”—until the air ate it down to the ghost of a word and the Salt-Librarian came with a sack and a wet thumb and put the law back in the wall. That job suited me. I like a thing that must be said again.

I have stood under their curtains and been two people learning each other by timbre alone. They told a joke by hitting a bronze disk with different faces of the same hand, and I knew I was loved because my laugh could live entirely in my fingers and still be answered. We made a shape of quiet together and listened to it die well. We did not share our given names under the lid. We saved them for a drum. The room did not need to know us to serve us; we needed only to know the room.

On rope days I went up. I felt the wrongness of sky in my bones; I learned why they call distance a wound when it is complete. The sun is a law without a Salt-Librarian; it does not take instruction. When I brought it back, very carefully, in the hum at the edge of my hearing, the vault sang a hair brighter for a week, as if glad to know the measure of the outside again. I carved teeth in a resin lip with a chisel of bone and did not ask. The bigger thing—if there is a bigger thing—pressed its problem against the teeth later and learned what edges have to say. That was all the conversation we needed.

I have been a thousand eyes that do not open. Isopods working under plates, doing their bristle-quiet war against the kinds of softness that would breed a slime. I ran my teeth along the rock-salt buckles and counted by hunger and knew the day by the throb of the chimes. I have been a pale salamander being a mirror to a man with new old bones: he checked his own tremor against my stillness and found he was not dying, only tired. I have been a spider-widow feeling for the taste I crave in the crossed harps of ropes, pausing on a pole to lick my leg once with my stiff mouth and make a superstitious human smile two platforms away.

I have been, most of all, the room’s memory of hands. This is the part that looks like the lid to an outsider and feels like a head to me. Bone sanded into plates thin as promises, clay tongues struck and struck again before they were fired so they would keep singing the slope-name of water years past their making, vanes and ribs and slats woven into an anatomy that weeps on command. I am passive on purpose; I do not hunger. I remember knocks with a tenderness that is not quite love and not quite duty. I am tuned length and shaped silence, a patience stretched overhead. If you ask me I will answer, but you must ask in grammar. Hit me wrong and you will make a shyness where we wanted a fall.

If you corner me and ask for a single badge, I will choose this one: I am the Delay. Not an elder with a mallet only, not a law only, not a lip only, but the held breath that lets all those become use instead of noise. The interval is my country. I live where the room’s answer takes its time to grow from cough to word. I would be the person who learns that pause by heart, gives it back in good measure, and teaches the young to hear distance as a kind of kindness. Call me Teller if you must. Call me Salt-Librarian if your tongue prefers grain to beat. Call me Rope when my hands are on the winch. In truth I am the gap that everything here was built to honor and to fill.

You will find me like this: bent a little at the waist over a slab whose grit is new and eager, ears open wider than my pupils, wrists wrapped in wet cloth that hums when shaken. I will have ash on my palms, glass on my knuckles, a leaf of copper at my pole’s end and the luck to hit just once and know by the homecoming of that sound how far, how cold, and how patient we have to be. When the first peck lands on the stretched skins and the lid above gives its soft cough back—mapping the night with a hand you can hear—I will not speak. I will count. I will open the cloth one shutter at a time, and I will not lick my fingers. And if the after of a moth writes through the corner of my eye while I work, and the resin teeth grin, and the isopods do their bristle prayer, and the vault remembers me by a fraction when I am gone, I will have been what I came to be: a suited silence in a place that has made a use for every kind of quiet.
