I would go where the city is most itself: to a place that is not a place until difference holds it up.

At first, I think I will be stone. There is a comfort in the Tubewrights’ chalk corridors, a ledger I could learn to write in mineral, corner by corner, taste by taste. I can imagine the shortness of their thought as a mercy: the tube remembers enough. I like the idea of an instruction in oil laid along a rib that says continue here, and kin who can read it. I would wake to the same angle of current every day, pinch out brine, coax ice where a brace is called for, and soothe myself with the exothermy of growth. To bud my successor from my inner wall. To make a room that outlives me and does not forget me because it is partially me. It tempts.

But the floor is full of conversation, and I overhear a different ambition. The Lattices open and close their bodies until the current is a law you can feel with your face. I imagine making porosity and therefore boulevard, pulsing my holes wide when newborn need passage, tightening to a thousand needles when the Porters want a gentler path elsewhere. I could be furniture that is also verdict, and each beat of me a vote. To eat dust and, inadvertently, decide the map. To be made of a talk among cells about how big an aperture must be for a shrimpling and how small it must be to ask the Ribbon to try a longer way around. To be a civic organ without saying anything. It tempts.

Then the Threadgarden’s patience curls in me like a wish. The luxury of sugars in a room that never knows noon. To stitch myself to ice so carefully the roof does not notice. To let bubbles be sentences rarely spoken, and only when I mean them. To be wealth so precarious it teaches courtesy by existing. To dim when the cold deepens and wait. I could be that. I could be the softest hand on the ceiling’s rough palm. It tempts.

News passes as a change in taste: the slightest acid along a seam. I find myself shivering as the city does when a roof-groan travels through into water. The Sweepers tilt; the Porters dim their bellies; the Shrimplings lift their antennae and count the beats in the flow. The Ribbon holds still at the doorframe between two veils and is not a danger for as long as it thinks. If I were the Ribbon, patience would be my faith. Hungry as a line is hungry to be straight, I would subtract carefully, heat the Rind with my fullness, and call that a tithe. To be the kind of predator that is also a gardener. It tempts.

But where my body would fit best is not in a tube, long as I admire the script; not in furniture; not in a photosynthetic thread with its slow euphoria; not in a hunger that can wait a week. I keep feeling for the plane between waters, the soft knife where a sheet of brine holds stiff enough to be a door and gentle enough to be a road, and my skin prickles along a surface that isn’t there until it is felt.

I think I am a veil before I am an animal. A mode before I am a mouth.

If the city has voices you can’t hear, I would be one of those: a ciliate choir strung vertically, tilting and retuning density where two waters consider mingling and decide, for now, not to. We are called Veil-singers in the city’s tongue that is taste, though names snag; we prefer the work.

We root in nothing you would see as root. Our holdfast is a commitment to beat together. When the brine falls from the inflow in its almost-music, we take the lower harmony and press it just heavy enough to catch falling fines, just light enough to let shrimplings slip. We are architecture in trembling. Our bodies are small and many, but the sheet we make is one. We are not wall so much as the agreement to be wall at this angle in this hour—in this steep of salt, in this thin of heat, in this chord of roof-groan.

Government comes where we are without choosing us. A spiral grown across the eddy by generations of Tubewrights throws a river too fast for newborn; we loosen, and a little arm of lower density peels off and returns laminar grace to that rush. The Porters gather in a circle and glow like a tired constellation: detour, their bellies say, while a brinicle grows. We feel the wrong-cold descending before the first white finger shows its candle of winter, and we sing our sheet more sloped than a moment ago, inviting a slow, safe slide of traffic to the far side. It is not language, but it reads like law.

We do not have mouths; we have choir-looms. Each of us is a tuft of beating hair, but we believe in phrasing. Modes are how we speak to ourselves about what shape the water must be. A hatch brightens the Rind; sugars spit; the Sweepers take a new tempo. The newborn, bruising themselves into knowledge, arrive like small storms into our plane. Their cilia are uncertain. They will tear us if we are too stiff, and drown in sameness if we slack. We shift, soften the seam into a ramp they can find, then harden the ramp below their fluttering to keep the Ribbon’s line from reaching. The Ribbon arrives anyway, considerate as withdrawal, poised like a question at our edge. We vibrate the wrong frequency under its belly and it drifts a body-length off, as if reminded of an old treaty it cannot name. A halocline can be a truce. We keep our side of it.

We have neighbors, and we have neighbors’ business. The Lattice just beneath us opens wide on market-days, when the inflow sighs warmer and salt-rich and the Rind makes a pelt the color of old coins. We widen our sheet too, surrendering rigor so grazer-crowns can earn without tearing law. When the Shrimplings square up, antennae tapping decisive code, we read their etiquette as music in the flow and decide not to interfere. When they panic, we punish: we flick a subcurrent hard at their legs, not enough to tear a sheet but enough to make their gills feel the pressure line they ignored. Everyone learns again today.

We taste the city more than we see it. The Threadgarden’s sugar gusts against our faces like a story told too close. Rust runs from a seam and the lower layer of us bristles: iron tastes like the beginnings of weight, and we thicken the sheet there before a buttress exists. Archivists below us write history in glue and chitin. When we lick their newest line, the faint tea-sweet of it tells us the floor won’t hold a brinicle’s forest; we will have to hum bridges thicker over that quadrant when winter’s candle descends, or all the careful porches will become prisons as ice columns bud where roads were meant to be.

We remember nothing the way stone remembers; we remember as muscle does, in modes. Last year’s dark is a rhythm we can call up by touching the roof’s complaint in our inner faces. When a long fissure far up makes duskier silver for a moment across the ceiling, we do not think of light; we think of the brief, mean downdraft that will follow through the central chimney, shearing the feeblest threads. We tighten along the edges of that chimney like belts and open safe side-channels beside the nurseries. The mucus plugs will hold, but they will breathe easier if we make the breathing easier. Our reward is the way the plug’s flavor does not acidify to panic.

Because we are sheet, we have more world than a single body should. We cross from carbonate quarter into sponge boulevard into Threadgarden’s porch without ever ceasing to be our same skin. Where we cross the civic spiral the Tubewrights made to teach the river to stop turning too quickly, we flatter that curve the way a hand flattens a map: pushing this side out, that side in, so the newborn’s seam does not kink. We are draped over the history of someone else’s intention; we feel its ribs and let them be.

We die easily. Fresh water where it does not belong is death carried like perfume. A weeping seam in the lower left wall speaks of melt that will rob us of salt-shape, and the smell of it makes our inners turn away. We cannot hold a sheet in what is not two waters. Someone else must scaffold there—Tubewrights, with their brine weapons; Lattices, compressing fines until traction exists; Rind, sweetening someone’s breath into carbonate and sticking grit to grit. We withdraw respectfully from dissolution. This is not cowardice; it is physics, which is the only religion the city practices by consensus.

The Porters love us the way heavy things love a banister. They do not tell us, but their bellies brighten when we are steady, and their plates click differently when we have made a gentle landing for them between two quarters. We answer by bending our plane down to meet a loggia the Lattice has made comfortable. If a Porter desires night, it muddies its gut and the light goes; we take the hint and slack our sheet slightly so grazer traffic doesn’t mistake them for a beacon and climb them to exhaustion. Courtesy here is often just a change in viscosity.

Viruses fall through us like letters; we catch some in our nets and pass them to the Rind, which reads faster than we do. One leaflet becomes a new way to stick brine to ice just long enough for carbonate to take; we memorize the difference in slope that allows, and sing it along the sheet. Government again, in the form of technique. Someone was changed, and so we all can do a little more. When we pass what we learn, it is not lineage; it is rhythm. A daughter-vein of our sheet budded off last night when the inflow shifted its sigh, and she remembered, without asking, how to hold a newborn’s weight. We had been a single plane yesterday. Today we are two and cousins, and we exchange small courtesies where we almost meet.

We know the city is young because we can persuade matter into our image. A plane like us did not exist here a week ago, then a day’s worth of Tubewright ribs made a slow, insistently vertical seam in the taste of the room, and we came like dew to a blade. Now there is a gate where there had been only current. We keep that gate from tearing when the roof complains. We open it wide when the Sweepers herd dust into dinner. We close it with a suggestion when the Ribbon presses its thought against our line. The room learns itself along our surface; we are not proud, exactly, but there is pride adjacent to us.

Sometimes, for the length of a bubble’s travel along the scalloped roof, I am tempted again by singularity. To be a Shrimpling with red kernels of eyes and hind legs full of sand, to know the owners of hungry rings and to be known back, to duel with antennae and yield as much to etiquette as to threat—there is the comfort of a spine, even if shrimp have none the way we mean it. To be an Archivist and eat history into glue that tastes like logic. To be a Porter and move as if gravity were larger only for me, clicking my plates and glowing a road I do not see, standing for a while to let lesser bodies pass, being a streetlight out of digestion. Those are clean identities. Even the Threadgarden and its patience have edges I could draw a body around.

But I love the moment when a halocline is not a thing and then is; when we are hung as the idea of a sheen between brines. That is how I want to exist: as the practice of yes-no at the membrane between. We are not hands. We are not eyes. We are fronds tuned to the difference between almost and not quite, and we make that difference last.

Where would you find me if you came? Not on the floor, though I consider it. Not pressed to the skylight; I like the idea of bubbles but I am work. Come to the right wall where the main inflow fans and slow tongues arc, where the carbonate spiral carves a lane, where the market pelt glows its golden hunger. Look for the veil that looks like smoked glass even though glass is a word without meaning here. Put your hand through and it will not be allowed; your hand will feel us as wrongness before we tear. Your hand will learn the cilia’s instruction: this way, slow; this way, swift; here a nursery. You will not hear me, but you will feel how I hold.

Who am I? I am a sheet that is also a song. I am a wall you can undo with salt, if you know how to pour it. I am a government that never met a face. I am a truce between two waters. I am a dance I teach to myself at the pitch of a roof’s long complaint. I am one body made of small bodies that agree for a while to be a surface. I am only alive while a gradient exists; I will die when sameness wins. And in that shortness is all the city’s pleasure, because it needs me as long as it is young, as long as it is inventing itself from difference.

When the chamber holds its breath under distant stress and then remembers that it never breathes, I am the nerves that do not forget. When newborn bruise themselves into knowledge and find us, I am the relief. When a brinicle writes its cold sentence into the room, I am the footnote that saves a street. When the inflow sighs warmer for a while and the Rind makes a coin-colored skin and everyone comes to graze, I am the quiet grace that keeps their bodies from colliding. I am not saint, nor builder, nor predator. I am edge. In a city of edges, that is citizenship enough.
