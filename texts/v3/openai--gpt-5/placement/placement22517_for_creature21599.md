At first I try on being a child with wet wrists and a bowl hung from grass, walking the bridge to wait for a mouthful of fog I am not allowed to taste until I have turned around and come home with it. I feel my ankles speaking in a language that isn’t words—the way the braided strands hum when I stand in the right place, the low ache of too much footfall when my cousin’s mischief crowds onto the boards behind me and an elder’s hand—the elder we call Reed-Thumb for the way she mends without looking—rests at the small of my back and says nothing but a pulse through my belt.

I keep that for a few breaths and then I am not a child but a sapling haltered to a patient line, learning to grow into a beam we will need when wind changes one day in a way none of us can yet sign. The pull on me is steady but not cruel. It says: you will be a span, and you will learn to remember storms.

A moth startles me out of that. White, heavy as paper, surprising where you do not look for flight—in the crease where two beams make a low V. I am the hand that lets it go, thumb like a lip to teach it the way; then I am the net that caught it overnight and did not mean to. I am the combing the morning asks of our laces. Fingers walk and don’t snag. Drops build and march and tilt obediently into clay.

I don’t stop there. The longer I stand in the mouth of your description, the more I hear that what I want to be—in the way one wants to put one’s body in a particular place as if there were spaces in the world that match the wanting—is somebody whose work is to hear the slow alphabet of this wind and keep the lips of it honest.

My aunt-of-joinery says joinery is what you do when you know every insistence is a future crack. Reed-Thumb says the same thing about listening. She is a keeper of tone. I try the idea of being her shadow and find I have her hands without meaning to: the habit of pressing the heel of a palm to a wet wall for the truth of the pressure coming a half-breath before the gust, the way I keep a pebble in my mouth when the organ is wild so my jaw remembers a place to rest and the rest of me follows.

So: where would you find me? Close to the north lip in frost-season, where the long throats drop into the kind of O that sits with your bones and looks at you like kin. Where the copper gleams thinnest green and needs a new mouth.

Who or what? A person with a rope-name and a sound-name, both of them unremarkable as a peg until you notice which ropes do not slip. My rope-name is Half-Hitch-in-Weather; my sound-name is the low-between when the O of the morning starts to learn A and then gives up until thaw. I have never carried more than a goat and I was wrong to try. I am light without being careless and I know forty-seven ways to fold a flag to say more than a shout ever could.

My day belongs to the adjectives of wet. At first pale, obedient beads. Then more, the sort that give your lips penny and leaf if you lick them. I comb the nets. I shake grass-knots like bells so sleeping drops translate into running water without drama. I run my palm along a gut-string on a harp the children like best; it answers in a cat-gut yawn. If there is a moth on the mesh, I coax it by pressing a finger behind it so it steps forward and is free to be foolish elsewhere. I lean and listen to the bowl-bone of the world saying what it is becoming and I make a notch in the book you cannot tear in two.

The book lives on cords and stones. We do not trust ink where ink wants to curl into broth. The page I carry fits in a pouch that hits my ribs when I walk. It is weighted with a blue pebble whose work is to remember a whistle we learned last leaf-fall and did not hear again after snow. It has lengths of line knotted in measures my fingers know without looking. When the air drops its vowel a breath lower at noon because the cisterns took their tithe of mist faster than we thought, I add a weight to a string and make a mark with a new small white chip that is not bone—not kin-bone anyway; we keep those for toggles, for the ritual of remembering which ropes belong to which hands.

Work is patience’s hobby up here. There are no nails to make the day skip. I trade the bright mouth off a slit—copper greener than it was last week, soft with salt that came in a woven bag from a friend below—for a bark lip we have prepared and soaked until it thinks it is river rock. The change is not for better or worse; it is for true. Reed-Thumb nods when the note stops lying to itself. Children stop swinging their ankles over a plank and stare with that round-eyed look that is also how we pay attention to goats.

Milk tastes like a lesson. Cheese tastes like hour. We do not boil when we can steam and we do not smoke when we can breathe. The braid of a chimney is a verb: unmake. Smoke becomes gray weather and the organ does not argue with it.

I go out along the bridge because there is always someone to go out along the bridge. The dish is hung from wet grass. I have stood at center in fog you could pluck and in a wind that would blow you empty if it could; today the air is wearing beads and laughing a little in its O. Someone at the far lip—someone whose face I cannot see, the world being more between us than either of us is—has put up a flag with a twist that says: bring two more baskets to the third terrace because the north cistern has gone thirsty and is envious. I answer with a fold that promises what I can: three bodies with hands made for weight, a lull-long’s worth of patience, nothing that makes the bridge go from hum to ache.

You might find me where a crack has opened a bright line in stone like a secret hiss. The elders in hide-wrapped boots have tied their wisdom into their ankles; they bend and lay knotted string across the new mouth as if to lace it shut and then do not. They measure its wanting. I am their coil-runner. I do not advise rock; rock does not need me to be clever. I steady a hand. I hold a lantern that isn’t flame but a pot of white fungus that glows with the modest certainty of a creature that knows its hour and keeps it. If the crack is nothing, we send nothing back into the world. If the crack is a change, I return with a length of cord that I hang with a specific pebble, a specific weight, and all I add to the book is a hum in my mouth so the memory goes in through both doors.

It matters to stand where your shadow does not fall into the bowl. If I forget and let it leap, I feel fingers at my shoulders and I do not bridle; I step. There is nothing doctrinal in me; there is only the arithmetic of having watched how long effort is unmade by careless castings. I have sent a goat home for the same reason—a little tug on a collar, a scratch where she likes it, a treat tucked in a palm damp from combing nets. We move weight as if it were meaning because here it is.

Sometimes we meet those who live where rivers hurry. There is a place on the path that feels like a closed door though none of ours close. We hang a reed curtain where the air is not earnest; it opens of its own accord when the wind says yes. We swap a sheet of green-shy copper, a sieve of fine lichen-silk, a pouch of salt, a song we wrote down the only way we know how. There is a sign I use then, one I learned from my mother: palms turned, fingers wagged, wrists soft. It says: we are glad and slow. They nod. We nod. Nobody crosses the bridge who was not made by it.

I have been to a quieting. A body is unwound as if it were a journey we could trace with fingers and not hurry. Warm mist-water, combs that do not snag, ropes unknotted, breath loosened like an old door left ajar to get its smell out. We do not make kin into reed or lip; we know the mountain listens and does not like confusion in its grammar. Later, bone is light and strong and you can feel the old jokes living in it when you make one into a toggle and fasten it to the line that governs the big woven door that breathes with storm. My mother keeps that door as gracefully now as she did when she had feet. I think of her when the organ finds a new vowel and the day grows two edges.

Night is a long vow. The organ warms as the air cools. Hisses dress themselves in silk. A frog speaks with manners we pretend to disapprove of. Our dog—there is only ever exactly the right number of dogs here and I cannot tell you how we keep it so—shakes water off his coat somewhere just out of sight and sighs like a person. We eat in a circle that does not look like one, scattered across decks and planks because no gathering here forgets weight. We sign a story with greasy fingers—my cousin’s joke about the underslung siphon that woke in a gust and spat its water onto Reed-Thumb’s boot, the old one about a man who thought he could sing a net tighter and ended up with a song nobody wanted to hear twice. If there is a dispute—say, two hands who think a beam wants a different kind of brace—someone fetches the line. The feet of the wrong answer tell the truth faster than tongues.

I go to sleep in a room with a fern for a door. The bed is a plank softened with woven bark and moss that was combed until it learned not to keep its wet where shoulders lie. Smoke breath is braided in the chimney and is not ours to think about anymore. I take my pebble out of my mouth. My jaw acknowledges the day. Somewhere a siphon creeps in its clay, a sleeping snake that must be listened to if we are to eat tomorrow what is already growing today.

If you need my history, it is a catalog of weather written against the skin. I got my sound-name the morning I sat up from sleep and the organ had learned a new thinness I could feel in my teeth. I pointed at the flag that would say so and made the twist before my head was entirely awake. Reed-Thumb marked my ear with her finger the way a person marks a reed they will take next season and said: that is yours. My rope-name came the day I slipped a hitch back into itself in a squall so a net would not clap its water onto a deck and carry a child along with it. It is not a heroism; it is a technique.

You might not ever hear me sing. My throat is for carrying the organ home when it is tired, not for setting it to work when it isn’t asked. In lull I will sing you the tune we use to check our tools—the half-dozen soft pitches that tell us what wood plans to do today—and you will not hear it because the world will have given you back all the other loudnesses you left outside our door. That is fine. I will not be offended. I will show you how to fold your sleeves correctly so you can sign while your fingers are wet and not lie by accident.

What would I be, then? The place you would find me is at the lip, where things decide whether to fall, and my work is to teach them that not falling is one way of falling that takes longer. I am an ear with hands. I am the weight at the end of a cord that tells the book of tones how much yesterday had to say. I am the copper lip when copper is right. I am the bark lip when bark is true. I am a patient refusal to hurry a mouth into closing.

Once, late in frost-season, I walked the bridge at dusk with a dish full to its brim from mist alone. I did not look down. At center I stopped because the hum under my feet said center the way the O today had said mother. I let the dish be heavy in my palm. I did not drink. When I turned and went home, I cried because there is a kind of rightness that makes eyes useless. The organ took my crying and made it into a vowel we had not had. Reed-Thumb wrote it with a new length and a pebble I had not seen before and then she laughed, and I did not hear it, and I did not have to. It was all over the air we keep.

Morning will give me back this same ring, not quite. A net will have sagged a low finger under pearl. A lip will have changed its mind and asked for copper. A hairline will be a lesson we can learn with our thumbs. Someone will bring a goat back from a shadow they did not mean, and we will forgive them. Someone will fold a flag with a twist I have never seen and Reed-Thumb will nod and say: keep that. The mountain will be a key lower. I will tie the day to its post and then let it slip, and you will find me where listening and making sit together like twins.
