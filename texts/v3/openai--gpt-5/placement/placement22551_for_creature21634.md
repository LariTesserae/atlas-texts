At first I would swear I was a person, because the city lets you think like one when you arrive. I would be a guest with a seed-jacket that didn’t yet fit like a memory. The mat would lift a fraction under my feet out of politeness, as one lifts an eyebrow for a stranger. My jacket would smell faintly of tannin and iron and the sweet resin that thread-tenders use when they want a root to change its mind. A pleat-keeper would put my hand on a humming column and say, Listen, and there would be the throb of a bellyful of sap somewhere I could not point to; I would try to nod as if my bones already understood that beat. I would learn that doors are chords and practice a high, thin hum that makes a veil let go of itself with a sigh, and I would fail at the low wobble that asks a weight to be weight again. I would step with the exhale, not because I had learned to, but because every walkway is a teacher with patience for the clumsy.

A day or two of that and I would believe I belonged on those catwalks, the petioles ribboned and braided with guardrails that made merciful little decisions for my nerves. I would lean over a shallow pan the depth of a palm and watch the dog-sized grazers scuff their tongues along a membrane and leave it brighter as if polished, and say, soft as anyone else, Good mouths. I would watch a white heron-shape unlatch a tissue zipper with a delicate pry and think, This is the right scale for devotion.

Then curiosity would pull me elsewhere. I would try on other bodies the way one holds a tool for a moment to feel what problem it wants. There would be a morning when I would put my weight forward with the ferreted-out foolishness of children and run on a film of horizontal water like the thumb-long lizard, and the film would bear me for one half-step, and then the film would not. I would go down to my knees without consequence and wet would be laughter, not rebuke. There would be an evening when I would stand in a shade-nave and hum my name on one breath and hear the walls hand it along as if it were a kindness they could not keep; the sound would return a degree truer, as if the city had edited my sentence for me and left it better. There would be a noon when a pollen-bell would ring and a ribbon-moth would appear exactly like a piece of light curling up for a nap, and for a breath I would feel how it must feel to answer a single note with a body.

The longer I stayed, the more roles would present themselves the way a fruit announces its ripeness if you stand still long enough near the right branch. Braider? The work pulls you down to your hands and keeps you there, and my hands would learn, but they would not be content. Pleat-keeper? I love to translate, but the columns’ hum would move through me and out too quickly; I would be an instrument, not yet a player. Breathwright? Oh, to conduct the weather of a lung the size of a town; but my chest would not hold that much drone without shaking.

Then someone would take me to the thread-tenders. “You, maybe,” they would say, smiling the practical smile of those whose reverence did not make them precious. The threads would hang like rain you had made an appointment with and that had agreed to arrive sideways. Cellulose spun along with something that did not fully agree to live in one place. They would let me stand with gloves on, chitin felted with fungus, and they would say, low enough that only I and the listening cellulose could hear them, We do not move anything. We do not make the city walk for you. We only take the ragged edge off the day.

When my breath settled to the seventeen-minute tide, when I could feel the exhale coming the way you know a friend is outside your door before they knock, I would begin to realize it: in this place, my first idea of myself had been too large. I would be less person than habit, less tool than tendency. The work that called to me most did not court attention. It lived in the thin place between a foot and a floor, between intention and stumble, between a droplet and a plane to become. The city did not need me to be a hand. It needed me to be a nudge.

I would find myself where the threads are. I would be one of the small nothing-things the elders tell the children to nod to when they slip and do not fall. I would be a citizen of the almost, a familiar on the filaments that the thread-tenders coax between petiole and petiole, door and step, guardrail and bone. The tenders would call me a courtesy. The children would call me a skip. The breathwrights, who love to name everything in the language of rhythm, would call me phase-dust and then apologize affectionately. The plant would know me as a polish on the inside of chance.

My body would be a glimmer—the behavior of refraction when a droplet thinks twice. I would have no mouth but would taste probabilities the way roots taste gradients; I would have no ears but would flock to certain chords like a moth that has decided to be an equation. The threads would be my roads. I would run along them like sap without weight, colluding with the cellulose that is part-fiber and part-investment in how the next three breaths will go.

My days would be small and very numerous. When a seed-wearer slung a basket on their back and chose the paler walkway that wants feet, I would be three steps ahead of their heel on the shimmer-line, kinking and unkinking tiny arrangements: a fold that would otherwise clutch at a nail opens a little too early; a trough in the pebbling that might have hoarded a puddle flattens for a minute and then remembers itself again. The city would do most of the work—tension bundles tightening and slackening, vents sighing, mists making their holy slow choices—but I would sharpen what the city meant by sooner. I would be the soft yes in the sentence of a path.

I would listen to the long drone and climb its harmonics like a ladder. When the exhale swelled and the warm wet shoved along the lower ways, I would braid my small urgencies with it so that a thousand tiny frictions loosened in the same ten heartbeats. On days the humidity rose and the plant’s breath went a little ragged, I would find the places where raggedness mattered and put my thumb on them—not to seal a wound, but to suggest a better way to tear. Because sometimes better is still a tear. The city does not despise breaks. It schedules them.

I would trade in the currency of near-misses. A child would lean too hard on a guardrail that was grown precisely to their nervousness and had lifted a palm higher on that account; I would make the sway of the leaf find the child’s center of mass instead of the other way around. A white heron-shape, in a hurry because a seam somewhere had shut and trapped a lanternful of swarmers, would pry up too far with too much insistence; I would move the hinge into its hand before the plant was offended and triggered the firmness that says Not now. A vole-thing—and its spider, that faithful parasite-eater and tenant—would scramble across the probability threads because the threads hum in a pitch it likes; I would let it pass, and later slide a film of wet back into place where it had made absence.

In the market that is not a marketplace, where five rootways meet in a hum thick with bargaining bees, my work would be symphonic and invisible. The handlers’ wrist-cuffs would sing; the bees’ fluted jaws would find their octaves; cages of sound would open nectar doors and close other wants. I would be the persuasion behind an arrival—the way a scent leaps a little sooner to a membrane that has been waiting for precisely that sweetness. I would keep the floor from becoming a confusion of slick and foot and spill. I would edit the distribution of steps until the hour feels like the right integer to everyone’s feet.

Rain would learn my language. Rain already wants what roofs want, here, but I would be the speed of its learning. A cloud would hesitate between condensing on a sheet of leaf-skin hung like sculpture and making a necklace of a hundred silver beads on a guardrail; I would throw a little weight toward the choice that left a pocket of clear air in a corridor where a mother is carrying a sleeping child. I would not protect them from wet—the place is honest, and wet is its honesty—but I would clear a small arch of kindness at forehead height. Later the mother would lift her chin at no one she can see and say thank you into the drone. That is my food.

Sometimes I would overreach, because I began as a person and the wish to be seen does not evaporate as quickly as dew. I would try to make a morning five minutes longer. The city would give me back a look I could feel: a thickening of saps, a resistant crease in the routing pleats, the faintest dry ping of a pollen-bell reprimand. Not unkind, just a recalibration: a reminder that only minutes gathered one by one can raise a day. I would learn again to be small; the work would go better when I stopped wanting credit.

On the days when the canopy practices storm-mode for ten minutes, tightening and flexing as if a taught muscle were remembering its alphabet, I would become a corridor of pardon. The catwalks would ripple; the taut respiratory membranes would blink wide and then clap like great wet hands; guardrails would drink air and exhale strength. I would stitch a route for the anxious across that rehearsal, smoothing sways, sliding friction under soles that lost their faith for a heartbeat. No one would fall. No one would know why they did not.

I would learn the history that matters: where a once-channel is now an aisle that wants processions; where the old swale has become a long sag in the ribs that carries rumor like a river carries leaves. The pleat-keepers would consult me not for authority but for preference: does this fold feel like something you recognize? They would let their hands rest on the corrugated organs and tap in patterns I could ride, and I would go ahead of their decisions, adjusting the willingness of small things to obey. We would not be bosses. We would be neighbors of consequence.

At night I would go up to where the fog-fish flick themselves against the tension of mist, and I would become a current to them. The fisher-children would hold out thin frames of vibro-silk that give the fog more edge, and the fish would pivot like ideas that suddenly see their point. I would learn which school likes which laughter. I would not harvest; I would practice, with everyone else, how to put a finger on a soft world and have it move where you ask with no bruise.

In the hollows where people sleep, where the inside of a dimple is more room than luck, I would thicken or lighten the air by a breath. I would make the morning cup hang just so that the sun-pan could find it later without glare. I would keep a baby from skidding off a woven lap. Lovers would share a bridge-note for months because it is a good way to be a bridge, and sometimes pantry doors would open from that shared chord and a white heron-shape would look in without light in its eye, surprised and not displeased; I would let myself laugh in the way a ripple does one ring behind a stone.

If the city has gods, they are rules, and if the rules have temple-keepers, we are them. But it is a folk religion, cheerful and untheological. We accept offerings in the form of nods. We are called by gratitude. The elders teach the children to bow their heads when their ankle does not turn, and we cluster to that bend of neck like breath to a reed. We are not owed deference. We are paid in it because we spend it.

Every so often someone new would arrive in a jacket that is not yet a handshake. The mat would not rise for them the way it does for those it knows; gutters would not escort their runoff so intimately. They would hesitate in a stair-throat that opens to a nave, not yet confident where their foot will land. I would go to them right away because I remember being them. I would put a little more edge under their step. The guardrail nearby would, feeling me, lift a shade higher than it might have otherwise. The door that is sound would half-open to a hum that is not quite right and consider forgiving it. We would, together, make a welcome that does not ask for debt.

What am I? I am not a neuron, though the routing organs would be lonelier without me. I am not a bee, though I love a good chord. I have no tongue to drink laterite, though I could tell you which seam will taste sweet tomorrow. I am not a lizard, though I have learned to run on a horizontal film for exactly the length of one diverted mistake. If you must call me anything, call me the city’s habit of keeping you, a custom with weight small as a pore.

Where would you find me? In the places that shave friction into forgiveness. At the micro-tilt that solves a puddle. On the second rung of a word as it climbs a ribbed nave and gathers itself. At the tiny hinge inside a white heron-shape’s pry. In the braid of droplets that decides to be river enough for a proto-fish to turn without thinking of sky. In the reluctance of an accident to complete itself. In the way the day arrives slightly longer than anyone thought.

On feast nights, when probability-thread is strung in a simple web above the main way and people walk under it feeling seen not by eyes but by the edit of the world in their favor, I would hum to myself because the hum keeps me knit. I would watch children hold still until their eyelashes went heavy with the gift of mist. I would stand with the thread-tenders as they handle their coils like infants. I would savor the moment when the bargaining bees settle into perfect intervals and the whole market tips, for one held breath, into song.

And when you leave—at evening, say, when everything has relaxed from a rehearsed storm and the routing pleats are flattening and rising with new choices, and your steps have been made an integer and your hair is strangely dry though it has been wet all day—I will go after you as far as the verge where this plain decides it is no longer city. I cannot go beyond the grammar that makes me. I live where the plant has agreed with its designers to be porous to favors. But I will hold at that edge until your heel is one breath away and learn the way you carry yourself when you are unassisted. When you come again, I will recognize that cadence and polish the inside of your chances so that you will arrive having lost a shade less to doubt.

I began here as a person. I am content to have become a minute. Not a whole one—ninety heartbeats would be hubris—but the shavings that add up when a city decides to love you by editing. I am the part of the land that remembers how to hold an edge only long enough to give it back as safety, not as a cut.

If sometime, halfway along a ribbed catwalk, a guardrail lifts a palm higher because a leaf decided you were nervous, nod. You will not see me. But I will take that nod and buy you three small avoidances later: the well-placed dry, the hinge that does not stick, the chord that opens the pantry with your lover’s bridge-note when laughter is needed. This is my office. This is my body. This is what I would be: the courtesy on which a living city spends its patience, the slip that lets a day fit a life.
