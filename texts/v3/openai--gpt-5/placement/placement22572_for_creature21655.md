If I belong here, you’ll find me where the day learns to turn.

Our room lives under a white-lipped mouth that isn’t the biggest but puts its light down true. By midmorning it is a square-edged river you can step into, and my work is to convince that river to bank and eddy, to slide around corners and pool on shelves that were shadows yesterday. We do it with pale stone and a pastry of mica. Mica has a grain to it like patience; if you read it wrong it will crumble like luck, if you read it right it will split its bright into leaves. My mother can read it by touch alone—thumb and nail and a hum you cannot hear unless you are touching her wrist. She shows me how to ease a blade under the glint and lift until daylight peels. Our blade is old copper with more mend than edge, a cousin’s cousin’s wedding gift from a place that shouts when it bargains. It goes through mica like a thought if you are kind to it. We keep it wrapped and salted and no one calls it knife; when you say its name you say it soft so the ceiling will not get ideas about wanting to wear it.

People call us Benders in a joking way, and also because it is true enough to be useful. My given-name is sisterly and small—Knead-of-Mica when I was five because I pressed my palms into sheets like dough—but the street calls me Bend-of-Day now, because my hand knows the hinge where noon agrees to turn inland. To hold a sheet angled just so, to see the white run along it like water feeling for a fall, to set a pale slab against a far wall and make a gray room confess that it has a left side—that is my profession and also my play. With a twist of wrist you can pull green into waking; with a hair of tilt you can give beans another week of wanting to be here. A little too much and you teach the moss thirst it cannot satisfy.

I have an afternoon-right on the third drop in the cookfires seam. It is a modest thing—I stand in it long enough to braid a tie or to bring a baby into it for the first time, to show them how warmth steps down into their bones from above. Rights here are not land but seasons of light, turns of dawn, small calendars etched on lips of stone. My aunt holds the morning slant at the ropewalk two days after rain; that is why she is never poor in favors. When the ropewalk wakes and the lines are born heavy as children, people remember who can buy them a day’s extra softness with a well-timed “after you.”

If I am not at my slabs, I am listening.

We do not call ourselves priests. There are people in the seam whose bones lean to sound, and I am one of them. The old men call us Wood’s Ears when they are kind and Ping-Takers when they are worried. The words feel like titles, but what they really mean is that we keep sleeping shallow. You will see us lay a cheek to a beam as if in love, or breathe against a seam to see if the exhale comes back flavored with wet. I carry a wrapped stick for the one tap you are permitted when a line runs brown where no water has been and someone younger sees it and says, Hm. We tap once, if we tap at all, and then one person older than anyone else present cocks their head and tells you whether to fetch neighbors or to mark it with lashed twigs and go home gentle.

The first note I ever learned to fear was not a crack’s joy at having found a better angle but the sound of shouting where it didn’t belong. My father tells the Stamp Year like a joke whose bite stays hours later. They were young and light-hungry; they said if birds can stamp a branch all day, so can we. For a season we proved a rock’s memory; now we wear that remembering like a button inside our shirts. Loud music is for topside. A shout makes walls curious. We have proverbs in every pocket. A plank is a promise but a rope is a hope, I tell the children when I teach them to tie their own breath to their legs for their first climb.

You will find me in the morning, if you need me, with my palms in fog.

We hang cloths in the wells at dawn and hold them as if asking. Fog is polite; it will give itself to a good question. By the time the sun hardens its spine and the air stops strolling we have shaken bowls full of pearl into the day’s first mouths. Salt tastes of patience and so does dew. I can empty a handkerchief into a jar and know from how it falls whether the noon will be kind. The women who watch water like this are judges and mothers of small mercies; funny how those are the same stance, bent a little forward, eyes keeping two times at once. If you bring me a sip and ask me where to lay your bed I will pretend the answer comes from your water, not from what your ankles do when you think I am not looking.

On days when the long throats sing, you can catch me with my ear near the lip, bowls nested like birds along the rim. The song after rain belongs to no one but it borrows your breath to be itself. We line our nets and think about nothing until thinking sneaks back in. No one tells a wish down—that is wasteful and cruel. Down is neighbor. We lower what we lower with knots that listen and draw up slow. Some throats have a metallic tongue, and you taste it later in your wrist; some lay chalk on your teeth that speaks of patience. We take notes in our bodies and then write them on sills with our sweepings, the day’s ledger in pale.

I was a child once, which means I was apprenticed first to the city.

It was my aunt who taught me to let a hand hear rung before eye trusts it. Three fingers and a palm, never all four, never your wrist until you are sure. It was the old women who put their breath in their pockets for festival and made me carry a kerchief that smelled of anise so I would remember to ration joy. It was the rope-makers who made me walk the board that hums like thunder with a bowl in each hand and never spill enough to stain my ankles. It was a boy named Salt-Feather—the salters have a hundred jokes about themselves and none of them nice—who showed me where beetles choose to die in rooms where sleep would make a bad bargain. I loved a beetle until I loved the air patterns more.

We keep animals the way we keep wisdom: by keeping out of the way. Swallows throw themselves into our rooms some mornings and my little cousin stands under the pillar with her palms full of engine until the engine remembers to be engine and lifts. Salamanders are half myth and half old aunties—their ribs like combs under glass, their presence like putting your fingers in a pocket of sleep. We do not eat them. Spiders sign treaties with us, signed in the ink of flies we would not have had the time to swat. A snake fell into the ropewalk one year and the story it wrote under our ceiling had four endings, all truer than the others. We sealed the room with laughter and careful feet and afterward everyone twisted a longer line, as if a little too much hope might hold back a season.

I married into rope because it was impossible not to love hands that are always doing something with their own weather. The Ropers step wide where the hum is loudest and speak in the grammar of cords. Their talk has its own tense: knots that remember what they did were doing. My wife moves her heel without thought across her thigh to smooth a line and you see the story of a whole seam in the polish at the place where skin meets work. She says you can hear a knot’s good mood if you lay it under your ear and close your eyes. She took my mica and made of it a hinge for a shelf that should not have been possible. She will go topside once a year to bring back fat or metals that flash like river-bright, but mostly her feet do not believe in emptiness and she likes her distance full of neighbors.

We do not exactly govern ourselves. We hold the place we are in the way a bar of song holds the note between two words. An old man who has been cutting his bone-tally since the year the ceiling coughed its first window will tell us when a street’s shoulder is called for and we will come as if to water. There are distances in place of prisons. A boy who shouted and woke the rock’s attention burned a month’s salt in apology and learned to turn a tone into a rope—he taught it to hold. A woman who asked too much of a span without leaving the stone room to be stone lost a turn of the dawn and then built a better shelf with a looser knot and left a gap wide enough to admit regret. We make rights and we make wrongs and then we make rooms to hold both.

Sometimes, if you could listen far enough into me with the same care I give to beams, you would hear the day I carried a ceiling with fifty people I love. It was new stone and thought it was a curtain; it let go like a paper tearing, quiet as all treachery. Somebody called out—not a shout, a’said loud enough’—and a whole street turned. We put our bodies where the physics were and became the other half of an equation, and the rock decided after a while to agree. Afterward the Ping-Takers said that was the way to tell a city’s age: whether it holds you as if by agreement or as if by dare. That room is all corners now and I have slept in it. When I did I dreamed of the ceiling as a cousin who almost moved away.

It is not that I never climb out. I have a sister who married noise and lives where wind throws its hand in your face like a game. When I go to her I am wooed and appalled in equal weight. Her children learned to shout before they learned to listen and it took me a spring to stop flinching when they laughed. I bring mica like sweets and show them how to lead a slat of brightness into a soup pot and they show me markets that rustle like bird flocks and sell danger by the story. The wind feels like a wall you can walk through. I come down again grateful for how our city hears itself.

On some days I am not a person at all. On some days I am the taste of water that ran over new stone; my mouth goes copper and I know the roof will sweat before noon. On some I am a child’s held breath, sleeping calmly under the tongue like a pet until you forget you tied it there and the world goes a little flecked around the edges. On some I am a knot thinking about letting go and then deciding to be faithful a while longer. On seed days I am a root from a shrub you cannot see: a pilgrim, black-haired and free, scribbling on our walls with a pen too thin to hold, writing a grammar of thirst we read with our fingers and then translate into rope.

When my body is finished I will be clay. Good jars are architecture, and death should be useful in a place built from agreements. They will clean me with salt and patience. They will lay me down in a soft-walled room away from anyone’s bright work and seal me with white. Someone who loved me will mosaic a teaspoon of mica into my lid so that a thought of daylight sits there when morning comes. No one will lean a beam against me—that’s superstition and also good engineering—and no one will forget where I am, because we do not forget shelves that hold. Once a year they will play music down to me from the surface and my bones will listen and believe they can be light again.

If you are looking for me then, listen under the ropewalk when it hums its thunder. Sound goes honest there. You will hear names rolled flat and not offended by it; they are used to that kind of kindness. You will hear my children teach their children to step their weight like a question people want to answer. You will hear the city do what it always does: learn itself out loud without raising its voice.

Some mornings we wake to a sound like a calf’s first cough, and everyone looks up the way birds look when they know the sky has added a thing. A new skylight opens like a word we were meant to know. The old man cuts another notch in bone, and three families move, and the light in my room is now younger by an hour and I have to teach it to turn again. Happiness here is not a feast but a rightness of angle. The world is still deciding how to be a place and we are still deciding how to be its people. I like that we can be patient together. I like that a city can be born under your hand and not resent you for midwifing.

So where would I be? In the long seam that runs like a thought beneath the day, under a pale and dangerous roof that listens, with my hands in fog and mica, with my ear against a beam that has learned to trust me, with ropes loud and kind above the clay, with rules that feel like jokes because we tell them with love. Who would I be? Whoever keeps company with sunlight until it is safe enough to enter a room, whoever leans their body under weight with a whole street for a spine, whoever teaches a little green to want this place, whoever hears the ping and answers with neighbors. In this world I am one of the people who carry the day down by hand and carry it back up again when it is done with us, not as a trophy but as proof that we are learning each other’s languages: rock and rope and water and light, all of us careful, all of us bold where carefulness wants courage.
