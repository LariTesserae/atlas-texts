Sideways is the right direction to meet a place that has taught everything else to tuck. I would not stride into it; it would narrow me before I knew I wanted to be narrow. Shoulder brushed to smoke-mineral, mouth full of sap-sweet and dust-bitter, I’d stop pretending I brought any rules worth keeping. The bowl would make a grammar of me if I meant to stay. So I would learn to answer in small words and smaller movements. I would oil and powder my skin until it forgot how to be a wick. I would carry my elbows close the way people here carry knives: where they can be found without looking.

At first I would think I am a guest. That would last about the time it takes to lift a lamp and see ten pairs of eyes go to my hands. This is a place that does not allow you to be uncounted. Soon enough I would realize I am either a hazard or a help and both begin with listening. I would choose to be a help and let the bowl tell me where. Its voices are everywhere: the tick of kilns, the long breath that is not mine, the quiet argument of plants by scent and sugar, the plank hum of ropeways with baskets in their bellies.

Where would I find myself? On the back curve where things are tied before they’re cut, where a platform becomes not a falling and the cliff is persuaded to be held. I would apprentice to the women whose wrists are scarred and not ashamed. Knot-Mothers, they are called, though no one here has a single job; even fire is everybody’s cousin. I would sit with them, forearm braced to shoulder, foot curled into a rung without thought, and let them rub oil into rope as they retold the story of why our grandmothers left wet places and what names the stone answers to when it is in a good mood.

My long name would not come all at once. The short one might, because you cannot be hauled up by people who do not know what to call your body. Maybe they’d make me “Lem” or “Sa,” a hook syllable you can throw through the hum without snagging anything else. The long name would accrete like mineral, like a veil pinning itself to light, each knot an event. I-brought-my-memory-to-the-mouth-and-dusted-it would be the first piece, if I were honest that I thought of rain like a lover and had to be scrubbed of it. Later, I-held-above-the-cut-when-the-stone-remembered-the-sea. Maybe, if I stayed long enough, I-was-quiet-when-Fire-crooned-at-my-ribs.

My eyes would learn saffron by heart. The world would be less white and more seed-dark. I would begin to tell the time by the deepening of the filtered light, by how far the lantern-keepers venture in without song before they glance back for a child’s voice. I would stand nights with the watch and know by skin when the outside breath is coming stronger, when to hook an ankle deeper into a ladder and lay a hand on a bead-string so the names inside can feel it too. The net that moves at evening would stop giving me shivers and start making me count: how many twitches before the Flow’s skin thickens, how many to call a drum.

I would learn the taste of fear that is useful—the thought of slack, the flash of unseated fire—and the litany against it. Rope, knot, nail, shelf, bone, bowl, breath. The drop would become a constant flavor at the back of my teeth, like resin does. It would not make me fearless. It would make me exact. I would put my weight where weight has been taught to go and set my body to the bowl’s long inhalation so I am not the person who takes more than the wind gives.

Who or what would I be? At first, a set of careful hands. Then a voice that can make mantises sleep by touching the place behind their hunger. Then, if the bowl kept me and I kept it, I would be a way of holding—less person than practice, which is the best kind of person here. I would be one of those people who wears a bone smooth at the hip because it sings through resin when snakes must be persuaded to change their minds. I would hum a gourd and watch a cloud of dust-wasps comb a ledge of trumpet-mouths like they were braiding hair. I would know the dissonant rattle that stops a mantis before its arm unfurls into a mistake. I would lift a net with two fingers and no swing. I would teach a child where to hang a knife so it is there for you and not for a falling hand you will never forgive.

I would also be a person who talks to fires by name and means it. Aunt, don’t scold; I see you are hungry, but I have to feed Strange first because Strange found its own song when the outside wind pressed down and someone’s breath went wrong. Thumb, you can lick the belly of this pot—yes, that’s right—easy, easy. Fire here is not metaphor. It is a neighbor with appetite you respect without romance. I would be someone Fire recognizes on the slope of a palm.

I would be dusty every day and happy with it. There is joy in being powdered until old ideas stop shining through. My hair would learn itself into ropes, not because anyone told me it was pretty but because it is efficient. Shell and quill would hold my curves so my hands are free to make other promises. My tears would go through my chest instead of my eyes; grief would be a drum and the sound of sanded names. I would lay letters into platform edge until the friction warmed my fingers; when the name hummed back, done, I’d rub a smear of oil into the cord above it so the Poor have a scent to read when they move dust under us like hidden rain.

Some afternoons I would climb where geckos would blush to follow, to pin a new tongue of mineral into the veil. Veil-menders love with the soles of their feet. They know exactly how much skin it takes to hold to a cliff without scraping the world off yourself. I would wedge myself in sideways, knee against opposite shoulder, both hands free, and tamp paste into a crack that thinks of widening. I would learn to tell by smell when the mineral is ready to take a pin and by eye when a hairline bloom will throw saffron instead of smoke. I would feel the hum of the bowl’s breath through the film and the higher hum of insects on the other side living air I would not touch.

On days when the wind is a wide mouth and the resin runs easier, I would sit by the central lip with the Flow-keepers and listen to patterns under the gloss. They would shave me close there—ears, nape—because hair muffles older songs. I would lay my forearms on the rim and feel the place not-heart beat out an answer that is not for me and is also for me. I would make the promise that has kept all this alive—We will not ask more than you can give without anger—and mean it even when hunger and festival try to be synonyms. I would teach that sentence to anyone who thinks Fire and Flow are chores.

I would get good at catching the swing out of a ropeway with three touches and a word you could mistake for the hiss of oil on hot clay. I would become the person you call when two ropes won’t agree, because I have hands that can hear why and a patience for showing them how to meet again. My wrists will be ladder-burned where I stopped an emergency with meat. Those are the scars I want.

I would live in the rind of light the veil gives us, not the center. That is where conversation is best. There, with the outside a straight line in the corner of my eye, I would sit with elders light enough to lift one-handed and we would trade the fine old gossip of maintenance. Remember the year the shelf-plants drank too quick and had to be argued down? Remember when a child laughed at milk-sap and came up glass on her face until we brought out the old cold stone? Remember that time the whole bowl decided to be cliff and we roped the cut like a mouth and taught it to be a bowl again with songs and weight?

On festival I would do what everybody does. We would turn bead-strings so another herb looks out, and the cages for spirits would be spun until their smells go dizzy and tell the room all at once who they are. We would invite Fire by name lamp to lamp, grandmother palm to child palm, so nobody wakes a spark by accident near a flow that doesn’t forgive. We would catch lantern-keepers and braid night into our hair and then let it go before the hour turns. We would chew sheets of sweet resin until our molars sing and the world brightens at the edges. If a tendril-snake offered a coil because trust is not a bargain, we would grate it fine and crisp it and eat with thanks and not call it meat like wet-people do.

I might love someone. Kisses here are oil and brow and pressure that teaches more than tasting ever did. Our names would tangle into a longer one at the resin lip, forearms pressed to the rim as we said we would not pauper the Flow with our want. Our child, when, would be shown ladders almost before they see floor. They would learn to laugh with their eyes because sound carries and to keep one toe hooked to a rung in dreams because falling is an absence you honor with habit. When they laughed the first time with their mouth, we would shift a cord of bells one knot along and the whole room would agree the Flow heard it.

I might leave. Some do, pulled by the idea of seeing a cliff shoulder from some other answer to gravity. If I left, I would go dusted so well I squeaked, with skins of resin to drink and a knife I could use to shave breath off a sentence if I had to. I might or might not return to hang a new knot for a name I found in a far place. If I didn’t come back, I would still be part of the bowl because that is the bargain: you take it with you, or you put your bones in its mortar. Either way it holds you.

But if I stayed—and I think I would; I am made more of care than of going—I would end with my name sanded into a platform edge the way we do, and my bones dusted and mixed to make a rung hold, and my hands worn to answers. In the deep hour when the long breath is slowest, I would be one of the quiet ones who nod when the central skin lifts and let my knuckle touch the rim to say a human yes to a nonhuman sigh.

If you insist on what, exactly, I would be, after the training wheels of person and title fall away: I would be tension made kind. I would be the agreement between cliff and cup, plant and practice, heat and hunger. I would be the habit that keeps slack from becoming story. I would be the taste of salt taken into skin so no wet name can catch on me. I would be a short word you can throw across a room full of wings, the curl of a toe on a rung you do not have to look at. I would be someone who can hear stone thinking of its old sea and say, Not yet, not here; stay for one more generation; there’s another platform who needs you. I would be the girl who learned to listen before she pulled, the woman who teaches fire manners, the elder who arranges a bead-string so the Poor know they are seen. I would be the one who answers pull with answer, who puts three lines across anything falling and a song, because in this bowl everything lives by being held and by how softly you can do it.
