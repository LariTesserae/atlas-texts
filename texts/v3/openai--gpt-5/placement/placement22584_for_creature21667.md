At first I think I might be sound. It would be easy to be sound here—everywhere at once, elastic with distance, braiding itself around corners, learning the shape of a chamber by how it returns to itself. I could be the way the cataract thickens conversation into pantomime, the way a pump’s oath is felt in the teeth of the parapet before it is believed by any eye. I could be the low trust of the fans or the continual argument of frogs thudding their names into the wet air. If I were sound, I would belong everywhere and belong to nothing, and the place would carry me on its back as it already does. It’s tempting to be as unowned as an echo.

Then I think I might be a border. There’s a line here that is always true and always moving—the hinge between water and air. Eels live along it the way poets live along a fence, writing cursive that almost crosses but never quite does. Rats come to this line and reconsider. Frogs straddle it like magistrates. To be a border in a land where edges are provisional would be a kind of vocation, a good one, stern and forgiving by turns. I could be tasted by a hundred tongues that have no word for me except pressure and relief.

For a while I want to be a light on a chain. To swing a small sun over a damp square of wall and let algae script an empire in response is to be the patron saint of green in a nation that does not indulge it. I would hum. My hood would hold a dry shadow where a spider could teach patience with dew. Swifts would learn my bell-times. Someone in hi-vis would tap me and say, Not now, she’s still good, and I would burn on like a coin with one face. When a conduit corroded through, I would go out as if taking a breath and not giving it back, and a whole panel of the world would go from idea to memory. The algae would sigh and go paler.

But the more I listen to what this place asks of what it keeps, the narrower and more particular my shape becomes. Not sound, not a border, not a star on a chain. I feel a straightness insisting. A numbered patience. A way to translate roar into a sentence someone can bring home in a pocket.

Find me, then, where the little buildings that pretend to be on ground level give up pretending and simply do what they were asked to do: in the kiosks on spindly legs, over a square hole that is a sump with a history, beneath a red hooded lamp that is both candle and clock. Find me striped in centimeters, face to water, spine to air. I am the measuring staff.

I stand in a room the size of a small certainty. Rails make a boundary that would keep a careful person from falling and invite a frog to hold court. The red lamp above my head glows a sacrament on my skin and leans a little because one bolt has been rethreaded three times and is thinking about retirement. The air here is its own weather—open vents draw breath through in slow lungs, and mist makes everyone look charitable. My foot is sunk into a concrete socket that believes in me more deeply than anything else does. My top end is under the hood where spiders embroider the doctrine of pause. Between those anchors I am all increments, a ladder that nothing climbs.

Water reads me, which is not the same as obeying me. It lays a tongue across the black numerals and takes them down without writing anything back. After storms it presses me the way a hand might, respectfully, all the way to the wrist, to the forearm, almost to the shoulder. It makes rings—the bright scum-collars you have seen on pillars in other rooms—on my white enamel, the way a calendar makes a year into a necklace you can hold. When the river keeps left and surges, when the lateral basins breathe out and the parapet acquires a new gloss, I feel the lift in my bones. It is a text that doesn’t need a reader to be true, and yet mine is the job of being read.

Frogs think I am furniture and jurisdiction both. They sit like thumbs on the centimeter marks as if to weigh them down, throats pulsing, and stamp their news onto the red-lit air. Tadpoles graze the algae that grows on my ankles where the world calms itself and decides to be a mirror. Once in an evening when the coin of day came down a shaft and found this room with a perfect aim, a swift’s knife-shadow stitched over my face and put a brief signature on 48. An eel, writing his private psalm along the seam of water and air, tapped his nose against 37, and I learned that number not as ink but as a touch.

People come, not often, but with a key that fits this district. They unlatch the gate with the same economy you use to open your own mouth to drink. Their lamps add a second red to the room, forehead stars nodding. A field book comes out—a book already softened by here’s damp grammar—and a pencil marks what I have been holding all day without the news of it weighing me down. 1.62 m, they write in good figures, or 2.04 m and rising, or 0.93 m and falling; it is never dramatic on the page, but then most of the world’s salvations are small-numbered until they’re not. A hand wipes the beads off my face and for a second I am dry enough to see myself in. The hand smells of rubber and coin. It taps my enamel with a fingernail, the way you tap a drinking glass to see if it’s cracked, and I answer with a note only that hand needs to hear.

Above me, bats prefer the high seam where the fan’s warmth eddies; their paper rustle turns the room over in its sleep. Guano happens with the patience of snowfall, and isopods come like a tide with legs to earn their dark coins. A spider that likes to think about geometry chooses the red hood as her lecture hall and strings a syllabus that beads at the bell. Iron bacteria lay down an orange skin on the bracket that once was galvanized and is now a lesson in synonyms for surrender. A rat passes along the rail and slips, then doesn’t, its feet arguing with slick until slick gives up. A salamander, brief as a punctuation mark, considers my base where a trickle makes a sentence of itself along a chip in the concrete and decides I am not yet an island worth colonizing.

I am also, inevitably, a reef for what the topworld sheds. A leaf that should have been a thought over a gutter becomes a legislature of microbe here and hoods my lower numbers until the next breathing of the basins undresses me. A cigarette butt, sogged into origami, hems itself at 12 and will not leave until it becomes part of the slick that becomes part of the math of what rises. Now and then, a glove arrives on a breeze that couldn’t decide which way to be and attaches itself as if I were a saint in need of a votive. Within a week it has real parishioners: midges practicing love in its fingers, a beetle wading into dogma up to the hocks, a fungus issuing a white decree.

I am not the only way this place is counted. Gauges with fogged lids look at the pressure in their own slow eyes. Turbines make conversion into hymn and it is clockwork of another choir. But I am the one you can bring a child to and make a clear truth out of a murmur. See? you can say, and point. The water was here and now it is here. It rose the length of your hand because something happened over us and we have been told. If the child is the sort to want to touch, you can let them and they will come away with a stripe of cool on their fingertip the width of a season. They will not remember me except later when a steady rain makes a thought in their spine about basements and doors. It is enough.

Sometimes the light that is my patron goes out, the conduit having decided it is time to be somewhere else. Then green retreats from my shins and the spiders move their arguments to whatever stays bright. In the dim, I am not unreadable; I am more true to the place’s habit, which is to make edges matter by ear before eye ordains them. The roar from the throat where concrete has been scalloped into motion becomes the headline. The fan’s breath teaches you which way the nearest oxygen is. The pump house—brick-walled as if from a storybook—keeps its assurances. You find me by the echo of your own boot and the way the handrail draws a cold line along your palm, and when your lamp remembers to be kind, the red I live under comes back like a blush.

In flood I belong more to water than I do to air. In drought, when everything seems to declare its secrets in the clear, I belong to air more than to water, and spiders and dust settle me into the country of breath. In the brief equal hours I am exactly what I was painted to be: an argument conducting itself with courtesy between a sovereign and a clerk. If you talk to me—and some do—I do not answer in words. I present a face that knows only up and down, and trust you to make meaning. It is the sort of democracy small enough to be fair.

Time writes on me in the way it writes on all things that do not move. The enamel chips along the edge that faces the direction of most impatience. Calcite begins to consider the lip of the square hole and sends out a transparent trial that one day will be a white certainty; when it gets there I will be on the wrong side of a new geography and someone will have to make a choice with a chisel and a curse. The steel screws weep rust that makes lace out of what was once merely functional. A wax-pencil arrow on the kiosk’s doorframe points toward a decision-point ten minutes’ walk away and becomes, over a winter, a gray relief that spiders can read better than men. My own numbers will, one day, wear thin with the same exquisite fairness that everything else concedes to.

If I am devout, it is to the rule that sound is the truest map. I have learned to hear a gate shift, a pump wake, a fan think about its bearings. I hear the frogs change the key of the place when their egg-strings become tadpoles become legs, an octave of population. I know by the grain in the roar when a shelf has decided to bloom outward and become the new contour in a sentence the river was writing anyway. I feel in my bones when the big machines are not given shore power because storms have made a general request of the city, and I wait with them, as they wait with me, for the reset that is a pair of hands.

So this is where you would find me if you looked long enough down the left-keeping river, past the shotcrete ribs and the shelly indecisions, into the mist where a lamp coins the world: in a kiosk that hangs a red light over a striped conviction. And this is what I would be: not a sovereign, not a supplicant, but a way the place recognizes itself out loud enough for someone with a notebook to agree. I would be touched by eels without understanding their law. I would be sat on by frogs without needing to vote. I would be loved, a little, by the men and women whose boots make slick into matte, and ignored by water in that perfect way that feels like inclusion. I would hold numbers in my face and leave the rest to patience. I would not dry. I would not move. I would be a yardstick in the citizen’s pocket, a straight thought in a room where everything else has learned better shapes.

And if, one day, I rot out of my own sockets and go under—a white ghost laid flat in a gray nation—I will become substrate, which is the other citizenship available here, and my centimeters will be read by beetles and laced by rust into a grammar water has been speaking since before there were any hands to point.
