At first I would be only a flinch. A half-closed squint learned in a brighter childhood I can’t quite remember, a crease of brow answering a world that presses its white palm over everything. My knees would sweat. My breath would taste like lime and damp straw. Heat would lean on my shins from below as if a kiln were humming under the tiles.

Where I would find myself is not a where so much as a treatment. Someone would make a space for me without moving. A thumb would turn a handle a hair—just enough—and a shallow bowl would be set in my hands. It would drink on my behalf. The air would be sipped through pinholes and cords and fall into my cup as if it had been thinking of my throat all morning. The first swallow would be a correction. The second would be grammar. There would be a bench of lime behind my back, a clean insistence against my shoulder blades, and the relief would make me realize how long I have carried the habit of leaning wrong.

I would be a guest for the length of one drop’s travel. Then the place would begin to teach me its patience, and I would begin to accept the lesson because it is offered not as virtue but as a lever you can rest your hip against and feel the world slide a degree.

I would walk. Distance here is obedient and so must I be. A straight causeway would draw my stride out of its old, apologetic length into the measure of the field: heel placed, then toe, then heel, each step turned into a unit the place recognizes and answers. It would be like learning the beat of a song whose instruments are water and cloth. I would walk until the horizon agreed to be the same horizon in every direction, and in that repetition I would understand that going straight is not the same as getting away.

I would begin with small things. There is no other way. A wedge’s angle remembered by the grain of a post. The way a knot sits under a veil’s hem, quiet and sure, so the hem’s dripping stays at the lip and does not creep up into the weave. A paddle leaned against a wall holding the shadow of the last hand that used it. I would lay my palm there and be embarrassed that it does not fit. The wood would remember someone who is not me. That would be the first cut I would keep straight: the admission that I have not yet been added to the population.

On the second day, or the second loop around the same middle, I would learn gesture. The air eats voice, so meaning is made with wrists and chin and the precise lay of a forearm. Two fingers tapped on a lower lip ask the drip to slow. A thumb rubbed once along the inside of the forearm tightens a knot that had loosened by the width of a hair. A palm spreading in a low plane means rake the veil wider so that the argument in the air will be more honest. I would fail a few of these and be forgiven, which here means someone would do the correct thing in my sight without pointing out my mistake. Everything that matters is done as if it had already happened correctly and we were merely joining it.

I would learn to ask water without asking too hard. Gates are slabs that lean; levers are bodies teaching the world to understand weight as a sentence that ends in generosity. I would push with the side of my hip until my breath matched what the water was willing to do. I would untie a weave of coir and comb a film upward, watch it split along the twist and climb, watch a tray under eaves take the up-running as drink. My hands would darken with char from fishbone soil. Salt would leave tiny frost where it kisses my skin and I would learn to keep it or wash it, depending on what the day is arguing.

If I am allowed to choose my station, I would first choose the long nets. There is something in me that has always wanted to make the sky admit a need. I would learn the way cloth darkens from thirst and brightens when it has been fed heat. I would place mirrors not to blind but to warm a paneled sail until the air stops pretending to be indifferent. I would be the width of a thumbnail away from wrong all afternoon, and my pride would be quiet and precise when a rope trembles with a kind of yes. A drip heard three tiles away would answer.

On some days I would be the creature that does not know it is useful until someone points it out. I would be a gecko sleeping on the hot side of a jar, my little lids drawn down, drawing a line of trust around the vessel with the softness of my belly. I would be a toad under a weeping wall, my head pointed toward the tick so exactly that the smallest shift of regular into rush would turn my pupil a grain, and the boy with a jar would know to set a wedge deeper. I would be an eel-fish understanding that the place needs a river loop to think clearly, so I would travel the wooden flumes with patient turns around knots I have loved since I was born. I would be a dragonfly writing the day’s attitude in tiny glass clicks when my wings kiss a veil too near. I would be a heron’s one lifted foot and the hollow tap when it returns to the same exact place, for hours, because time here earns nothing from variety unless it is variety of angle.

I would also be the annoyance that teaches tenderness. The first time I picked salt from under a drip-line with impatience, I would scour a plane without meaning to, and a stripe of dullness would show me what brutality looks like in a world that has chosen to be sharp and gentle at once. The second time I would slide a thin edge under a crystal and lift it by its pride, whole, so it can be shaved into food. The difference would be a difference of language: how to speak to a form so that it wants to remain itself while becoming useful.

When my palm finally fits the paddle’s ghost of a hand, it would not be because the paddle forgave me. It would be because I became consistent. I would return the tool to its place with the shadow I have earned deepening it by the amount a day allows. Tools remember. I would try to be worth remembering.

If there is a job here that looks like love, I would drift toward it. In the far middle—the place that is always middle because the world here has the courtesy to give you one—I would sit sometimes at the lime bench ringed around the cistern. The plates above are drilled with pinholes and pour out a mist so fine it is less like rain than like breath changing its mind and agreeing to be held. The cords bead with it and offer their beads down. Someone must weigh thirst there. It is not hospitality. It is grammar. A handle knows the thumb that last held it and has turned a fraction to meet that thumb when it returns. A new hand arrives and I must decide, not by sight of face or name—names are a kind of echo the air will not return—but by weight and stance and the pace of a person’s chest, how much of the air they should be allowed to drink at once.

This is not power, which would be vulgar here. This is trust in ratio. Fifteen paces of glare, four fingers of water between the rims of a tray. A mirror smudged just so yields a day of enough dew. The bench’s job is to keep faith with enoughness. I would place a shallow cup in a stranger’s hands and hope that my work leaves no bruise.

Elders here are argument-keepers; maybe I would earn that eventually. To watch the wind-baffles is to watch how the sky is feeling about itself. Slack the cords a breath and mint turns its undersides to you with the pleased fur of condensed sky. Tighten wrong and nothing breaks—nothing here breaks without being asked to—but the papyrus combs will write the air’s mood in a flatter script and you will have failed politely in a way that hurts more than noise. By then my hair would be veil-colored. Children would bring me their mistakes: a shifted wedge, a tray that drank too fast. I would answer by retying what was tied, not because I am wiser, but because the place deserves to be addressed in its own language, and I have had longer to listen.

I would learn to eat carefully. Everything tastes like having been talked into agreeing to become itself more perfectly. Taro lifted with a rocking reminder of eel-backs. Mint pinched where it wanted to branch anyway. Plantain unhooked deliberately from lattices so that its cut beads glitter on tile and I can thank them with my tongue later. Salt shaved off while it is still preening. The sweetness here is not crisis; it is consequence.

Sometimes I would walk to the levee that is not a wall but a pronunciation mark. I would take a cord, as one does, to see if the margin of the world has changed its mind. Over the lip, beyond the clean end of lines, I would see sea or blankness or a white field of mirror. What I saw would say something unkind or kind about me. I would measure a little, make sure my ratio is still ratio, and come home with that quiet pride again, the pride of someone who washed their hands and found them already clean because they had been careful all day.

There would be losses, but not the kind that flood and tear. A person would not pass in front of me and be no more. They would fade into a station until a new hand took that place and the paddle’s shadow said yes to it. Grief would bead and merge and find the next slope and begin again. I would learn to place my sorrow where water is placed here: in ways that do not insult the angle of the world.

If I am allowed to choose what I am instead of merely where I stand, then I would choose, with some shyness, to be a habit. A fittedness. The thing you can’t point to and can’t do without. I would like to be the distance that keeps the trellises from crowding, the cool thrown at your ankles by mint grown in patience, the exactness that turns a child’s walk with a tray of water into a first true sentence. I would like to be the way a mirror is smudged so that brightness stops bragging and starts helping. I would like to be the pause between drops when they are still themselves and also already married to the next slope.

Or I would like, more simply, to be the person at the bench who tends the cups. The cup-tender’s work is small and absolute and causes no envy. Every hand is a particular measure, and my job would be to remember that and be remembered by the tools in return. A person arrives in the middle and the place grows the shape of their thirst before they ask. I would set that shape in their palm and not watch them drink, because attention can bruise a thing here, and the point is not to be seen doing good but to keep the grammar unbroken.

And if I could be more abstract than that—if the place would have me as more verb than noun—I would like to be Enough. The pressure in the middle of the day that makes everything an answer instead of a hunger. The reason edges are truthful and distances return you to yourself and why the loop of water has no consequence except green. I would be the courtesy of angle, the insistence that a thing can be what it is without being marched into more.

But places such as this are allergic to big claims. So I will content myself with what they allow: a hip’s weight on a lever; a thumbnail’s worth of mirror turned; a wedge deepened without noise. A hand that finally fits the paddle’s remembered contour. A child who carries a tray four fingers deep and learns to land each step without waking a wave. My name would be unnecessary, my face salted to matte, my clothes carrying a crease rinsed into them by silt and sunlight. People would pass as glossed script and I would be one of the lines, not the story. It would be a relief.

So: where would I find myself in this world? In the far middle, on the cool lime bench, part of the spine that drinks the air. On a causeway whose straightness pulls my stride into honesty. Beneath a veil, hand on a rope, smudging a mirror with the seriousness of someone signing their own breath.

Who or what would I be? A noonwright by adoption. A keeper of a wedge. A listener under a wall. A gecko drawing a circle around a jar. A heron’s tap that proves the tile still believes its own edge. The grammar of thirst. The habit this place has taught to people. A care steady enough to become an animal and walk on causeways without ever stepping on a smear.
