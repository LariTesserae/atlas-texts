At first I am only a palm pressed to a taut line, the cool braid of a guy-wire braided again by water and salt and a little moss, the tone coming up through bone into teeth until thinking picks its gait from it. The edge is doing its quiet thunder—upwash gathered, decided, voiced by basalt into vowels so large they make a muscle of air—and I am a body arranged to receive. It is early by the clock we keep, which is to say the manometer in Bay Three sits its width of needle above forty and the ceiling is the low river that passes for weather here. The dark drop bites at cloud, neat as if the land were kept by some fastidious tailor. The masts wear their coronas like small, steady saints’ lights. The condensers drink.

That is where you would find me most days you could call days in a place that has so few of them distinctly: at the rim, touching the wire that holds one of the Works obedient to its song. I would be a tuner, if the word can be shifted from ears to airfoils and from strings to plates. The job has other names—edge clerk, pitchwright, humskeeper—but all of them mean the same gentle intrusion: I keep our large animals speaking the language the cliff prefers.

The first thing I learned here was unsuddenness. Nothing I do may arrive fast. If I shift a baffle I do it one pawl at a time, and I give the air the courtesy of a breath between clicks so the cloud will not fray. If I walk I walk as if the mats were instruments—because they are—my boots wrapped in shag that makes no gust, my steps shallow as apology, my shadow disciplined by protocol so I do not lay it on a lichen that has taken a month to reach this chemistry. If I reach to stroke condensation down a fin for the pleasure of the movement, I stop my own pulse in my throat for a beat and let the Works’ pulse speak through mine instead. That is how the deal is kept here: by never forgetting we live inside other throats.

What am I, then? A small instrument, mostly listening.

I sleep in a row that has doors like good mouths and windows waxed against thoughts of mildew. The rooms hum to the edge’s pitch so conversation can ride without arguing; we turn the room down a quarter-tone to ask for quiet and up a hair to agree awake-ness, and our bodies have conceded circadian to this more ancient governance. We wear fungus-felt that loves being damp and never seems to decay because it was trained by minds that make bread and gaskets with the same vocabulary of pores. We eat bread that is warm and tart with iron; it steams when cracked and smells like leaven and vent-mineral and settles the heart in a slow way. Our tea is a floral we built for ourselves out of memory: petals that never grew here, coaxed into behavior by fungus until they breathe that summer someone carried folded in a pocket across an ocean and up a rail.

If you asked me whose hand is on the Works, I would hold up mine and then point to everything else. No single piece thinks, not even me. The Works think because we have fed them sensors the way a trainer feeds a hound until it knows by the scent of weather which turn the hunt will take. They don’t decide; they prefer, and preference in a place without crisis feels almost the same as mind. I am the organ of their preference that can climb a ladder. I can hear when the upwash has a new burr in it that will cost us a tenth of a percent, and I can move a vane a thumb’s width to ease it away. I can put weight into a ratchet the way a body learns to breathe deeper when rain is in the nose.

There are times when I have to be larger than my skin. At night, when the brights gather—small wet galaxies in the hollows, charged enough to simmer with their own thoughts—I stand with the masts and I am the network for a while: microphones in organ mouths; thermocouples snugged into basalt like proprioceptors; strain gauges along the long bones of condensers and viaducts; the slow sirens pulling on the air to persuade it to give water into our cups. Through the guy-wire I feel Standard Drop 81 holding its B-flat the way a contented animal carries its tail. East Organ Span is always a little sharp in fog; I know its humor better than some know their kin. When the train comes—a single articulated being scissoring steam in an honest, rich way—the entire rim lifts a breath, and the Works tighten and relax their throats in a choreography that reads as affection if you let yourself believe it can.

I carry the calendar, too, but not in a book. Up here the calendar is law rather than story. We schedule because the sky keeps its part and the vents keep theirs: a meter of wet to be had if you are patient; a wattage from the mouths so regular you can write fussy poetry in the margins of the log and be scolded because someone will need that space in ten years for another initial. Maintenance is not drama, it is liturgy. I go out with a drone that knows etiquette better than any of us—we make them that way; it is dangerous to be rude to this much water and tone—and we groom fins of matte hydrophilia until they drink like happiness. We tap gutters and watch the drips jump to new tempos. We wipe corrosion where copper weeps turquoise and invite the guild that enjoys copper to thicken there. We stroke mats at their edges, thickening here to catch the waste-crescents, thinning there so a rail-bed can breathe. We never surprise anyone that cannot run.

Sometimes I am only a finger laid on a rail to taste static as flavor rather than shock: the edge is in good humor; the manometer is merciful; the marsh-gases are folding into stars again. Sometimes I am an apology made to a liverwort because my sleeve threw a shadow. Sometimes I am the name of a module spoken in its key because names matter here as functions do. We name them the way shepherds name their dogs and hills—E, low; F-sharp; B-flat; a lined-up run of animals that graze the river of cloud. They answer with pressure and water and a little satisfaction.

On the scarce clear days I become someone with eyebrows again. Light picks out everything too sharply; noise cleans up; the edge rings like a blade. We narrow our eyes—habit, not need—and listen for what the exception means. The Works flex into it; we let a little light fall on patches of lichen we have been waiting to reward; the brights dim and sluice themselves back into their base because charge leaks in sunshine. If a day goes bright for long enough we put a cup under a dripping lip and gather water rich with taste and take turns drinking it slowly, listening to the tone lowering into our bones like a promise.

If I were not allowed a mouth—and many things that live here aren’t—I would be happy enough as a length of gutter that had learned the good art of patience. I would feel beads build and fall, feel water start to speak inside me the way blood learns language as it moves, know that my work looks like shining and is function like a vein’s. Or make me a guy-wire, and I will sing with everything that passes, tuning myself by fog. Or a fin, perfecting hydrophilia; or a ratchet, a little pawl that clicks the world into measure. Any of those would be a life without accident here.

I might also be the small gray fly that decided there is no point to flight and has found a life in walking that is perfectly civil. A gloss worm that tastes iron as other creatures crave salt, writing in the peat’s slow script. Something mouse-shaped that learns warmth from pipes the way monks learn warmth from hymns. I have time to love their biographies because they are long afternoons rather than hunts.

But you asked where to find me, who I would be, and the truth most days is simpler. You would see a person-shaped quiet at the lip of the world, gloved hand on a line, breath that fogs and then falls back to its owner because the air is already as wet as it intends. You would not hear me, not over the plate tectonics of sound. You would not see me move fast, because nothing does. You might notice a twitch of a baffle, a gentling of a siren, a fingertip lifted then laid again to read what we all read: tone as map, tone as law, tone as companionship.

I used to belong to a different rhythm. There were days that declared themselves flamboyantly and a sky that did not hold still. I thought surprise was proof of being awake. Here I learned a better vanity: precision. I learned that devotion can be unambitious in the loud ways and extraordinary in the quiet ones. I learned to sleep by the edge’s breath and wake because the plateau told me, and I found it was kinder to be told than to insist on telling.

If you stayed, you would learn these manners too, because the place teaches without scolding. Your own water would decide to arrive by agreement. Your hand would stop wanting to slam a door. You would taste iron and like it. You would begin to know the modules by their notes the way you know your friends’ footsteps in a stairwell. You would acquire the habit of laying your palm along railings to feel tone instead of looking at clocks. And you would discover, sometime between a calibration and a cup left under a kindly drip, that the sentence on your permit was not a promise but a description you have come to fit: I agree to be tuned.

So that is my answer: I would be the small animal that knows how to live in a chord. You would find me at the edge, a steward of nuance among large, slow, obedient lives—moss and metal, steam and glassed basalt, luminous microbes and quiet pipes—keeping company with the long low creatures called Modules while we all graze the river of sky. If I am anything else, it is only more of the same: a length of gutter given to good behavior; a wire that holds its note; a hand that moves by ratchet and pawl; a citizen of an arrangement that chose monotony and made a music out of it.
