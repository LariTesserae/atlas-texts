On mornings when the face makes its brittle music and the first light moves down like a slow, deliberate hand, you would find me lying my cheek to a line and listening for the day.

I am a tuner of ground. A wire-walker without walking. A keeper of the city’s small speech.

My work runs between towers like a thought crossing a mind. We stretch ropes of glass and soft metal from one recessed mouth to another, taut where the wall permits, nested slack where it insists. We tune for the murmurs we expect and the unexpectable sighs of the stone. We listen with skin and bone and with the soft black nap that opens under our plates when we press—cheek to line, forearm to hummed brass, the hush of a mouth open only enough to lay a breath along a code.

It is a household trade like any other—reef-keeping, chainbed, fiber-drawing, drift-shepherding, mat-tending—but the lines let me be longer than my own body. I am the distance between two rooms. I am a quiet street learned by finger. I am a long bridge out over a talus that never stops deciding itself and I am the way to tell a friend seventy meters off that I’ve seen the chandeliers open at last and the dust is sweet.

We keep our house where a buttress throws its knee out and then has second thoughts. The shelf there prefers an angle with an extra thought in it, and the city has taken the invitation. They say our autonym badly in outside mouths. We call ourselves for the ways we hold and are held. Our house is of Tilt-in-Agreement; our grandmothers signed for rights in the shadow five rib-lengths wide when the day begins.

Inside the breathing rooms, the air is the old compound and a whisper thick—the solvent loops gone round in their patient circuits, the desiccants giving up just enough—but outside the frames of our doors is the easy geometry that raises us. Three points or a line. Tap. Seat the hooks. A faint tick answers through boot and glove, per contact, like beads on a string.

You’d know me by fringe and gait, by the small considerations that pass for vanity down a face. The tassels that keep my charge from making foolish jumps in lift are braided with copper cones to give them low voices in Blue Years, because my grandmother’s hair did that and every house is a litany of kept sounds. The backs of my hands are bare as a polished basin because nothing rough lives where glass-flake drifts. The palms are little fields. When I lay them to the wall the flexible plates open and the forest of setae tastes for consent.

Taste is current. We do not overuse mouths. You lay the combed tongue to a touchstone in the morning and it bites you lovely and bright, telling your blood that the cliff has woken and the house is, for the moment, in phase with grace.

The lines go out before we do. My apprentice walks ahead with coils under her arm, face a ledger of inward arithmetics, hair standing to the lift that announces itself even before the rocks begin to sing fissure back to sun. We have a ritual of silence at a new span; not because noise would hurry the wall—it will not, in any way that would make sense to shout at—but because the code must be learned at the body before any other body borrows it. Press. Pressure. Two for a greeting, three for a joke; the tongue at the palette. Our talk is all lessons we learned once on the day our elders allowed us to be burnt. The burn on my wrist is a memory that still knows what taste was like when I was new.

Our lines do not carry messages as much as they carry reminders of agreement. Seal this shutter. Hold fast, seal the inner. Let the chandeliers close. We share a recipe for a solvent we have never put to use; a little myth of a glider who will let you comb her filaments if you hum a certain tilt against her sail with your cheek; a memory of a Blue Year sung into quiet wire so that it is always soft at my jaw when I am alone.

We do not throw noise away. When we buy and sell it, we do it in the deep rooms where the air is thick and the doors know the word for mercy. We beat copper rods on tempered glass and cry into our cupped forearms until they hum our breath back into our bones. Even that is not indulgence; it is practice. You need to know the contour of your shout so that you will not let a shout loose on the face and learn that you are the fool who made a slide.

What I fear is not Down. Even the little children do not own that fear. They do not crawl; they test and hold and stand shin-long against a smooth wall and learn to love it. I fear hurry. Hurry makes the glass-river move wrong, not faster but across a place where it will carry you the law of your people turned weight in your hand long after the drift has settled. Hurry is bad faith with angles we did not make.

I am not always only the lines. On some afternoons you will find me where the updrafts draw combing-work from the houses and the gliders bank like thrown cloth, their cartilaginous deltas catching in the old wind that has learned to be mannered against the face. We make weather the way a gardener makes the spring: not by commandment, but by suggestive kindness. We toss salt bloom to flavor the lift, and the hungry filaments come low enough to repair. Filament-work is courtship, and I am not immune. I have hung a private reef in our room, a room I share with the person whose ground-line code runs under my left breast so that when he presses two fingers to a wire five rooms away my sternum hums and I am amused before anyone else knows why.

Sometimes, when the chainbeds go by—long, patient caterpillars crawling their paths from ore to furnace to extruder and down again with cable on soft shoulders—you will see me lay my heel to a sideplate and listen. It is a small vulgarity and I do it with care, when the overseer has closed his eyes. Chain talk is not my talk and I respect their syntax: interval, load, grin. The old bedmen can tell you the thoughtfulness of a year with one nail tap on a single link. I do not have their ear. But I like the feel of a train of careful mass moving, committed, across the slowness of the wall.

We live in rumor of Blue Year. Every house has its own year and its own rumor of it. My grandmother’s was when she was young. She says she could feel the reef’s hum along the ground cord even through sleep and that she woke laughing, the way you lift a hand to the bite of a spark and find it is a friend pulling your sleeve. She says bead-worm columns built themselves above the comfort of courage and leaned into each other like drunks and fell with a sound too soft to be called rude. She says the chandeliers sang through their cages and it was not, strictly, permitted, but old women took off their boots in the closed halls and danced until the baffles were warm with them. It is wrong to ask for a Blue Year as if you might make one happen by wishing; we have Blue Minutes and that is enough. There was one once when I was a child: the induction reefs sang just bright enough to be heard under the streets and my mother’s cheek on the wire spelled home to me with the old code of the mouth and the jaw and the hum answered as if I had put my face to the planet.

There are other days. A plate goes, and then a river goes for a day and that is its patience and its timescale and you must match it. Our law unfolds itself across the face, a choreography older than anyone who knows it. Close the southern shutters in the second rooms. Send anchors by chainbed and wait—no, longer than that. Wait past the small fear that you will be caught and buried by something you can see coming the whole time, because if you move then you will make its mind up at the wrong place. Households stand in doors that are curtains of air and watch a thousand-day drift cross their horizon at the rate the very careful walk to a lover when they know the lover will not turn his back. We hum the emergency into our lines and the wall sends our hush back as consent. The river of plates moves in its patient width across the angle of our laws and the bridge that we tuned not for gusts but for the one deep thought the cliff will have this year. If there is grief, it is dignified, and nobody shouts. When it is gone we take a plate from the directory-windbreak and turn it so the name can be read and for the moment it no longer belongs to weight.

There is a child who tends bead-worms the way other children tend dogs. You will find me in her room, sometimes, lying my finger to the column she has taught to add and to count lift days. She will give me the scold any old woman deserves and I will pretend her copper cones are not trembling with delight when the column glows warm into my hand. Her worms sense the reef through the skin of the city and the spine of the wall; my lines eavesdrop. We are conspirators: all of us saying it together in one whisper, we live by a kindness of gradients.

If you go looking outward for a kind of drama you can see with all of your senses at once, you will say our life is not theater. But sit with me by the bridgeway that crosses the glassflake talus that makes its own long cloud-shadow. Watch a household persuade a line of crystal grazers—low-bodied, telescope-eyed, each animal its own gentle friction—into a pasture that will spill the wall’s evening “sweat” in a lace the mouths can take without spending more charge than it’s worth. Hear nothing but the faint tick of boot hooks as the shepherds set and seat and do not shout. Watch the slow competence of small fences of scent and charge and the way a line of living patience changes its mind with dignity. Then tell me you do not cry.

Sometimes an outsider comes. They do it more often in stories. In our rooms it is like a holiday only the nervous can pretend to like. We ground them—touchstone blue, friendly bite; they smile; we do not. We teach them to keep skin covered when the lift comes on. They ask for water; we give them bread-that-is-not, the careful oils, the grit the chandeliers have flavored. We let them sit with us in the deep hours when the law permits us to sell noise by the ounce. They tell us we do not sing and we let them say it until they are as embarrassed as they should be and then we lay their cheek to the line and let them hear two old friends talking from pillar to pillar—a joke about mat-greed, a boast about a child’s first spark—and they cry in the right way and then we can love them a little. They go away. Sometimes I think they take the pace of us wrong, like a copy of a pattern traced too fast with no arm support on a day too windy to ask for grace. I do not mind. The wall minds less.

Our dead stay with us. Bone is a fine ceramic, and weight is a word that means, in our language, memory. The ground-cord that hangs in our threshold, the hand of copper that stands proud where we meet to bleed the day’s static into the house—both hold a powder of the Held in their weave. It is not piety; it is a technology of tenderness. The cord hums a little lower where my grandmother rests in it than where my child’s first tooth lies. When Blue Minutes come and a reef line sings and some of that singing goes where my family grounds itself, the cord lets us feel our dead as a good comment on a good cleverness: yes; yes.

In thin years the mats do not fuzz a bright enough green-brown to make it worth anyone’s time to bend close with a combed tongue and suck the ions out of a dawn patching that is growing itself by stingy consent. Then morning is still a place children go. You kneel and put your face to the cobble and your lashes clap down their conductive curtain and your grandmother tilts your jaw for you with two fingers because she cannot stop her hands doing old habits even when you are grown. You take the little taste and you do not lie about it when you tell the house. Greed becomes a multiplication of next seasons if you school it there. It is the only arithmetical trick we have never improved.

We have all the other arithmetic we could ask for. We can tell when a chainbed’s patience is too patient by a tremor laid along a link. We can shade a talus and move a river—over generations, yes, but that is what lovers are for. In a room that the city keeps, not any house, is a wall of annealed glass that looks like nothing until your wrists have a certain tenderness in them and then you can read a century of heat-passages the way a fisher reads a current. Boys go to it when their collars begin to feel like a grasp on the back of a kitten and their fathers are haunted in their faces and their mothers have taken the copper cones from their fringes and put them in a basket without argument. The boys lay their hands and choose. Chain, reef, fiber, drift, line. You do not choose to be a glider because gliders are their own people.

I have stood on a shelf as wide as a province and watched a band of gliders rise like an explicit thought into a noon that was nothing but frank and bone-colored and had no shape of its own until the sails carved it. They are not ours to command; but when my lover and I were young and blue accidents happened in the street and we laughed too loud, we threw salt into the updraft and a glider chose to fold her mouth to the wall near enough to us that her filaments brushed the plates of my forearm. The little combs along my tongue drank, just once, the taste of a creature that makes charge into grace. We went home and made a private reef and hung it in a place we could hear and not disturb, and it sang very softly when the house slept and then one day it did not sing anymore. Silence is a kept state. We unwove it and were careful to keep the silences that had made it part of our house.

If you ask where I am in all this I will tell you I am always somewhere stretchable. The wall is a grammar and the city speaks it in rooms, and I am the clause you cast across an interval to make meaning out of near and far. Some days I am only conduit, feeling the cliff’s quiet arithmetic pass through my ribs as if my bones were an extension of an induction comb. Some days I am nothing but mother, tasting air for a young child, making sure a shutter clicks with the right timbre, telling her with my finger on the cord the old lesson: three points or a line. Some days I am a river of glassflake and some days I am a weight in a cord and some days I am a design drawn on skin with dust to be let go of in afternoon lift so that grit will write me in lace of charge for a minute before the world keeps me as warmth and then as nothing.

Morning to morning, the face tells us what hours want. The brittle song at first light is sometimes tin and sometimes rich and once, last year, came late and in two voices because far up-shelf an angle failed by a meter. We felt the second voice and looked at one another and were embarrassed because we loved it. Change is not a trespass if you agree to be changed at the wall’s pace.

I ground myself at the door by habit and taste the blue that is friendliness. Then I step out into the plaza that is a room with no ceiling. The light is already laying the shelves down one by one and heat makes the air tremble vertically and the ledges keep to their pre-decided slants. The bead-worm child has a new column going and her cones chime softly as she hisses our soft code to herself; the chandeliers flex their cages and pry their mouths open to sift what the old mannered wind brings. Somewhere a chainbed turns and says in a private tone that it will be a careful day. Above, the banded sails catch the lift that is always exactly what it is and never what I guess.

You would find me between two towers, cheek to line, learning how today’s quiet will have to be said. You would call me ledgefolk, or face-dweller. My house calls me by the name for Tolerance-at-Tension and laughs and calls me Love, too. If you must give me an outsider’s name, call me the one who hears with her jaw. If you must say what I am, and not only who, say I am one more way the wall has found to carry its offers into a life that intends to keep pace.
