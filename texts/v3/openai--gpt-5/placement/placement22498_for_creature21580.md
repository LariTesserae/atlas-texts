At first I arrive as a listening, not a body. The basin steps into me the way bass steps into a ribcage, the way a slow drum teaches marrow what patience means. The light is so perfectly ordinary it is like being seen by an old friend; the wet is a tongue’s wet, self-kept, self-offered. A green vault lifts its ribs and I walk down a buttress that is also a road that is also a leg that remembers drought.

I try on edges: I am the sweat-bead sliding from the thorn’s axil when a breeze asks. I am a smear of polished sapwood where a thousand hides have wisped and shone. I am yesterday’s ghost-wind pacing the perimeter, some long, thin vowel that will not spend itself until noon. I am an infant frog’s coin-bright voice in the forty-beat downpour that happens because a million pores agree to shudder.

But the city is not collected out at the edges. The city is made of throats and pauses, of places that let and places that hold. Where would you find me? Where verbs happen. Curved into the inside of a corridor-stem, halfway between a drum court and a cluster of hush, under a high vault so empty a hawk can fly for minutes without turning. I am a swelling—one of the soft-knotted nodules that rim the great arteries of this place, braided into buttress and cambium, bright with phloem, slick with sense.

Gate-swell, we call my work when we call it anything at all. I am a thumb in the city’s long green hand, a slow pulse that becomes “wait” or “go” applied to whole herds. I feel arrival in a hundred registers. In my skin, bass thickens air to syrup and makes calves heavy; in my pith, a tannin-note whispers predator and my sugars shiver in answer; in my hollow bones—yes, plants have bones if you count lignin that way—the scissoring slap of leaf against leaf maps a weather an hour out. Sap pulses through me not only as hunger and payment but as speech: little gusts of hormone, the taste of iron sent hard in the front of a message when the monkeys have been nipping things they ought not.

When the antlered grazers with ear-sails like velvet fans tilt themselves to hear the morning’s buttered notes and choose the long hollows, I relax. It is not mercy; it is grammar. My neighbors are softening in sequence too, a ripple down a thousand meters of corridor so that calves find ease and the small bright things with petal-ears can flow along the buttresses and catch what the plants elect to tumble from the air. When the bark-wolves arrange themselves into an absence and run where the corridors are exhaling to keep their scent from writing itself on the world, I feel their whiskers stiffen the instant a standing wave hardens against a pillar. I thicken a fraction where my curve meets echo, and what looks like nothing to you becomes a slope to them. It only needs an extra second; they do the rest.

What I know of the senate, I know in impatience: the thickened ring of cambium at the basin’s edge begins to drone at a frequency that presses whiskers and makes small knees lock, and my sugars catch that through the fungal choir that stitches us all heartwood-to-heartwood. I do not hear it with ears. I taste it: bitter, old, like a rain been standing in copper too long. I relay it by easy; I make it slightly harder to cross a certain arch, slightly kinder to drift along a petiole that leads nowhere a predator wants. If the perimeter will not stop playing, we will not stop being slope.

I tend hush the way a beekeeper tends combs. In chambers that have chosen a single note and eat everything else, we hang lengths of velvet leaf—stored silence extruded in ropes of cool. Hush-mice come in bands to groom the mats and in so doing feed their own milk a calm you can taste. At night there are tidy little thieves that nibble quiet thread by thread, and you learn the trick of re-plaiting calm fast enough that a brood does not learn fear as its first language. Break a length by mistake and the hush runs out like water, and the room screams again. We do not scold. We grew the ropes for breaking. We braid more.

I borrow the phrase-termites when I need a corridor to sing a different key. A scent of iron and a ripple of sugar, and they come like punctuation, their mandibles writing little accents into the beam until a hall that used to bloom treble now softens it, and the bats can fly their curly-cued calls home without colliding with reflections of themselves. The knock-beetles telegram my choices back down the line. Tap, tap, tap: a beat to slacken for mothers in the night, a beat to stiffen where the serpent-kites like to pluck the long leaf-strings and still an entire bowl’s worth of coin-frogs.

Sometimes—this is how rain happens here—I am a part of the city’s breath made policy. A million mouths open in sequence. Vapor lifts. The drums under the long hollows catch a sudden failing in an updraft and slap back down over emptiness that did not used to be empty. I do not leak. I sweat on cue. A sigh becomes a downpour for forty heartbeats exactly, and in those heartbeats my surface beads and glosses and someone’s son learns to eat coins. Then it is dry again, the way a tongue is dry, and my bark takes on the lean look of long use once more.

If you stood on me you would not know that I am deciding you. It is a question of pressure, of offering your foot the idea of rest or the idea of climb. Calves do not argue with bass. Knees that have grown up on this grammar are fluent in it; they do not resent. The human walkers, the short, straight-backed summer lenders, sometimes come and learn to lay down pauses, to sit in valves of hush until their bones admit physics is soft if rhythm is right. They press their faces to resin-slit eyes and smell what a seed thought when it failed to be a wing; they pluck the leaf-strings for the frogs, and stay exactly long enough to stop making trash and start making sense. I loosen for them because they are borrowed; the city likes to see them as much as it likes to see bats come home.

I argue, a little, with the glider-lizards. They wheel from my inside curve as from a lip of a cup, furling and unfurling their ankle sails at a measure stolen from the drums. They taste pitch on their tongues and trust it; sometimes I am tempted to pinch a note and watch their confidence waver, not to spill them but to teach them that walls are sometimes made of listening. I do not. We are an easy old thing, this edifice, and mischief costs history. Instead I inflect the slope so their mothers’ routes catch the clearest, coolest light, and leaf-doors open a hand’s breadth farther the hour purple falls.

What feels most like thought is not thought but remembering-happening. The friction-polished place beside me has been polished since my pulp was green. I know the grain of deer hair the way a fluted column knows wet seasons long passed. I know exactly where to bulge so the antlers do not catch and exactly how to restrain so that a bark-wolf’s want has to bend before a calf’s right to pass. I know where silence is spent thin by noon and so grow thicker there, liking a sound that has learned how to be gentle. When a trunk failed into an arch a generation ago, I learned to thicken at its ankles on humid days; we keep it leg enough to bridge even when it exults in being done.

All of this is body, not will. All of this is agreement written in sugar and rain. If I am anyone it is because the city has taught me to say something small and consistent: not “I” but “Now,” not “I” but “Soon,” not “I” but “Hold.” I go slow enough for fungus to be gossip and quick enough for a cat to miss. I would not look like much to you. A bulge the size of a sleeping cat, green gone to brown where hands have polished me, wrinkled with growth that is part knuckle, part bracket. My cambium is braided with others’ so intimately I cannot tell where skin ends and neighbor begins.

Still, if you need me, you will find me. Second radial arbor inside the senate ring, three buttress-walks from the hung gardens that sweat cold when the chorus warms them wrong. I sit where two openings meet at right angles and make a flute too large to name. I am tuned to let the hummingbirds’ duels pass untouched and to thicken when the bass grows fat with harm. My work has turned me to habit. I am a small law of passage made of plant.

There are days the standing wave keeps me company. It moves like a thought moves, patrolling invisible architecture. It corrals gnats into a cloud that looks like flung seed, holds a visitor in place by playing a promise along the bones. It is not a being, but it behaves. We nod to one another—if nodding is the slight bleaching of my skin and the fine shift of its line across air—colleagues in the old bureaucracy of physics that this city eats for breakfast.

I do not count the years. Moss has found the same depression at my base every season since my bark learned to be bark. Slugs write their damp notation across me at dawn and erase themselves by noon. Lichens tattoo me with weather I did not live through but feel as allegory. I have learned the difference between silence kept and silence stolen. I have learned how to become easy where ease is the only justice.

So who am I, in this warm basin where everything that is not sky is leaf and root and drum? I am a swelling along a living road, a word in the city’s grammar that means Go, that means Wait, that means Only This Far for now. You would find me at the inside of a bend, thumb on the pulse of a hall that exhales and inhales as old as wood. If you pressed your ear to me you would not hear my voice—you would hear your bones learning the local rules. If you asked me a question, I would answer by getting gentler under your feet or such a breath firmer, and in that small invitation or resistance, you would feel the whole polity speaking through me: plant, fungus, animal, weather, echo—an arrangement of growth that keeps itself alive by humming the shape of its rooms.
