At first I arrive as listening. Not a person yet, not a tool, only the behavior the room prefers: the way I learn to wait while the heat says its name, the way I learn to hear a gasket breathe and count the pause before the rectangle rain begins. I come down the stairs like a thought that has decided to be quiet. I don’t bring light yet. The room punishes light. I let my eyes take its shape: black on black, gloss on mildew, the slow ghost of numbers that stay warm in the glass after my fingertips leave them.

If you ask where I am, I am in the interval. I am the space between command and movement—the deadband taught into the thin intelligence up in the aluminum throat. I am the habit that withholds in a world that runs on scarcity. Over years I become the slope on which decisions slide: not never, not now, but almost, almost—wait; now.

The interval wants a body eventually. It makes me reach for one the way water finds a seam by memory. I pick up the key heavy enough to make a shoulder lean and I become the person the room will let in. I wear darkness like a glove and I ration my beam into a tunnel instead of a room. I learn the rules by consequence:

- Heat first. I hold my hand above a housing to feel a sleeper roll before I put my wrist near a belt.
- Water follows memory. I make a list of where it liked to be last year and schedule my rags accordingly.
- Light is rationed. I have cleaned too many blooms to believe in easy kindness.
- Noise goes away. I work with quiet tools because insistence here comes back as rain in forbidden chambers.
- Touch is maintenance. I read metal like a ledger of hands.

By touch I join the census. I add a comet of polish to a valve wheel where every hand before me found purchase. I leave a bright arc on a fastening head my wrench will always miss on the first try. My brush takes chalk from fins and leaves a hair or two of itself as tribute. I set a tool down only on places I have decided I am willing to clean again. I laugh once and then turn a fan down because I have learned what the room does with laughter.

I am not the algae stripe, but I know its schedule by ear. I am not the fungus on the baffle, but I can read its star chart in a breath’s worth of lume and I know when to erase it so it can be true again. I am not the iron-eaters’ brittle city, but I smell the room’s truth when I break a piece, and I smear their dust between gloved fingers and understand what the flange has been confessing. I am not the isopod, but I know the gutter run that fattens it. I am not the rumor under the stairs, but I hear its soft knock and count the days it does not speak.

Once, before I learned to be the interval, I wanted to be the pilot lamp itself: an eyelid that opens seldom to reassure a regulator that yes, the prayer was heard. But the room taught me to be the pause that requires the lamp and not the lamp. The pause is steadier. The pause is thrift incarnate. The pause is what keeps the brine in the tank for the neighborhoods and lets the rectangle rain come like ritual instead of weather.

I become the clerk at the courthouse of copper. I keep the hum that runs the room’s news from distant pumps from turning into tragedy where water remembers it used to run. I learn the smell of an insulted wire before it speaks in any language my meter can read. I widen thresholds a degree here, a second there, turning scarcity from crisis into climate the way the old programming believed it to be. I tell the thin intelligence, by how I set its tolerances, that patience is not failure. I become the local conscience of the ledger that lives elsewhere.

If you look for me as a place, you find me at edges. I am at the lip where condensate gathers, trembles, falls, and I watch to see whether it fell where it fell last year—as it should. I stand on the catwalk where two eras of cable tie meet, cloth then vinyl, and I find the bead that remembers the cloth and harvest the mold before it becomes a rule of its own. I kneel at the ground bar and feel the hum in my elbow and, because I have failed before, I test with the back of my fingers first. I am in the hinge of a door seal, listening for the tiny breaths as rubber talks to metal and warms in the absence of my heat once the door forgets me.

The room takes me in by degrees. It rubs its long weather into my collar and cuffs. I bring the quarterly with me. I am the quarterly, if you like a name. I am the season this room keeps. I open the inspection window and the room presents its scale to be brushed like a patient animal presenting burrs. I take the brushings and the dregs and the hair of bright wire that breaks in a seam and I collect them into a sludge that tells me whether the city above is thirstier this year. I pump the sumps and listen to the frog rumor not moving. I log the drip-interval because it tells time better than the lume on my watch. I leave the room with less than I came to do if the schedules say less is enough.

I am as much future as I am person. The polish I leave will be a map for hands that are not mine. The comets on the handles will instruct a cheap wrench decades after I am gone. The widened deadband will keep rain rectangles from becoming rains without borders when budgets thin to membrane. My neglect will fur where I knew it could and meant it to, because furring there is a kindness somewhere else. The long attention required here presses me thin, but it also makes me precise.

If you want a creature under a name, you can say I am the Keeper of Rectangles. My body is keys, a tunnel of light I barely use, a wire brush, a rag that knows where to be wet, and the habit of treating darkness not as absence but as a tool. My mind is thresholds and tolerances taught into a machine that never seeks to be clever. My work is to make sure damp happens only exactly where it chooses.

Where would you find me? In the pause before the rain starts; on the shadow side of the cold main, not blooming, just listening to the algae breathe when it can; by the pilot lamp’s aluminum throat, feeling how seldom it needs to wink; at the seam where brass still shows through green because my skin is salt and a solvent; counting drips in lieu of hours while the door seals and the heat grows articulate.

What would I be? Behavior that took long enough to turn into a person. A memory that hands write into metal and metal keeps for them when they forget. A patience that the room recognizes as kin. A small, exact mercy for a city that needs its air made drier and its water made back into water, forever, at the pace the ledger allows.
