At first I would be the pause itself—the padded hush that drinks a footfall and makes a droplet a verdict. I would live in the courtesy of the air, in the green dim where sound chooses to be small. For a while I would be no more than listening. That is a place here: along the inner curve of a rib where the grain runs like slow water and the fibers hum if you promise them your ear. I would lie there, warm spine to warm wood, and let the vault make a pulse out of me.

Then I would be smell before I was speech. A string of crushed leaves rubbed along the wrist-bone where a vein surfaces, bead-scent released on each heartbeat to say wait, or come, or I am at the third spiral where the draft rises like tea-steam. I would be the sentence the wind can carry only as far as I intend. If you found me first, you would not hear me. You would taste resin a little bitter and know I had asked the path to quiet.

I would try on the ease of other small lives. For an afternoon: dew-mouse, slick back combed into gutters that pull dew along a spine. I would drink without licking, forever almost wet enough. For a morning: pith-heron perched at the lip of a lightwell, neck a clock-spring, eye pale as wax, speaking in coughs that do not trouble the neighbors. For a cup of dusk: augur-glider, cartilage scallops feeling the slipstreams like braille, coasting three spirals in a single breath and setting a child’s laugh traveling even when the laugh cannot be loud.

But I would take a body that knows tools, because I want to place my hands where habits can be edited. My feet would be hands also: two long, arguing toes, two to meet, with a pressure-thumb at the heel so every step can negotiate. My hair would not be fur so much as instrument; fronded, capillaried, brushed with wax to coax water toward my wrists where a thirsty leaf waits every morning to drink the salt I carry home. My lungs would be teachers’ lungs: not huge, but trained. I would learn the breathing calendar of the day until distances change size under my ribs when the canopy opens or closes its mouths.

My eyes would go leaf-bright in shadow, pupils tilting all day in sympathy with the rumor of sun. When the gloom went stingy I would speak to the pale leaves that glow if warmed—my fingers writing soft after-images on my own skin to see by. My nose would hold a library of resins. I would know a knot’s invitation by the way its sap sweetens at noon, or the exact mood of a beam by the sour it breathes at dusk. Words, I would keep them like ash: useful, rare-fire things. I would not shout; I would be raised in a place where a shout is weather and all the nearest leaves clamp against it.

I would be a gardener because there is no title here that confers more patience. Apprentice first, to a woman whose fingers were ridged by runnels long ago and whose belt—a living strap—grew glossy where her sweat taught it to keep its shape. She would show me the laws with her palm. Water obeys shape, not slope, she would say, and make of our hands a cup along the grain. See. And the water would gather like a secret we had both been waiting to be told. She would walk me up every morning, every morning, so that gravity would grow tender with me in the galleries. Upward would become a habit my bones could remember even on bad days.

I would be given a wedge of inlaid hardwood and a vine-tendon looped on a peg no two hands will agree is straight. No straight thing lasts here unless begged and bribed and apologized for. I would learn to write long sentences in curve: a season of pressure here to persuade a span to prefer a slightly different life. I would birth a parapet out of a buttress root arrested mid-gesture and held there with thorny clamps that are themselves alive and quick to itch when they need attention. I would remember to loosen the living cable each new moon lest the wood forget it can be reasoned with and make a ridge that will tear the next storm wrong.

They would call me a knot-painter when I was worthy of stains that could teach. Spirals confuse and soothe; they take you in and then give you back to yourself. I would learn how to lose a child on purpose for three minutes—the safest kind of lost—by painting a certain curve on a certain door where a ribbon mouth prefers to wait, so the child’s feet go another way forever. I would learn how to find a man swollen up with grief by drawing two counter-winds on the wood he must pass; he will stop at the involuntary memory of a path his mother haunted and forget to be alone for a while.

Draft-finder would be my second name. The family of that word is wide. It means I would know where to sit so that gliders will come because the air is climbing there; which hours belong to heat falling like polite hands and which to the warm reach of afternoon; where fog will be born because two self-made winds have agreed to argue. I would read the arguments and plant a splice there that likes to grow only when convinced of contradiction. I would bank the day on a local weather that repeats itself so faithfully it can be invited to dinner.

When the pollen-snow walks a line we cannot see from parapet to parapet and then retires at the minute remembered, I would wreath my fingers in gauze to collect without clogging skin. When the deep mats breathe sweet for a week I would not sleep much because everything will want then, and I would have promised myself to the little wanting lives first. Sump-bears would visit as aunts. I would lay a child belly-down across a warm one’s quiet sill of a stomach and ask it to lick the salt from skinned knees while I sing to the quivering finials of a span that nearly decided to forget itself.

My knife would have a cradle vine that thinks about it at night and keeps its edge keen in gratitude for salt and regimens of oil. I would keep my tools like companions: named, fed saps, returned each dusk to what holds them. I would never leave bone or bast where they could be lost to the general appetite without a promise attached; to last is a pact here, not a right. I would sleep inside habits instead of houses. My family would be the dull shine of the runnels we polished, the angle our parapets make into wind, the cranky way our balcony remembers that six sleep well and seven fight over blankets.

I would go to the Windwarders to be scolded into courage. I would ride their bladder-kites tied to ribs thick as city streets, their lines thrumming with the steady pull that makes the stories in wood. The Leapers would make me feel old and then make me try anyway. They would teach me how to throw my body as a question and fall in an answer. I would court a Leaper for a spring and settle with someone who prefers to sing to beams. Lovers here are like tools: they last when tended and when given back to living things every night.

I would fail early with seeds and be forgiven. Once I would broadcast a memory into a mood it did not match: a span that had learned obedience in a drought would answer to the storm I did not have and all day we would argue. I would sit with it for a season and stroke its scar and rub bitters into its impatience until it stopped insisting on being a different place. That is all craft: persuasion without insult.

I would become Old Bound almost against my will. It would happen the way a rib becomes itself: slowly, with revisited pressure. There would be a cathedral of pith that speaks in loops that sit exactly under my skin; a room that keeps whispers going in circles until they come back edited and a little truer. I would not leave it far for years at a time. People would know where to find me: on the second shelf of parapet along the windward edge where the vertebrae carry nearly all the load and the fibers sing in a register that makes the hands of the young go quiet on their own. My job would be to hear the quiver that means a leaf has failed an oath. I would lie down when storms come and press my mouth to the grain and recall, over it, the thousand memorized answers the place keeps, singing them until my throat knows the difference between flex and break.

Visitors from below would come sometimes. They would bring rock-dust like saints and call it gift; I would hold their wrists and scent their names with gratitude. I would send them home with a knot that unties anger and a seed that tells a dry hill a dream of water. They would describe a thing called ground—flat and continuous and bossy—and I would both envy and mistrust the thought. They would try to laugh and swallow it when the leaves near us paled in warning. They would learn to call lightning a rumor when spoken of from here.

I would teach children the law that is not written: walk uphill every morning until you are tired for the pleasure of the gallery forgiving you; you will need it when you are old. Do not color a spiral you do not understand. Return what must last to something that knows how to keep it living. If you make a straight line, apologize. Speak with your palms and your pace. Let grief run through plants the way water does—shaped, not spilled. If you leave a place, leave it knowing itself.

I would lose people and be returned. In the end I would be placement, not burial. My belt would become cleverer; the strap would thicken where my living had taught it to stay, thin where it must bend to be useful. My favorite wedge would take my salt and remember my paragraphs of curve so that a child who never knew me could set it and find, months later, that the beam listened the first time. For a season my heat would sleep in the parapet where I lay every night; families would press spines there to tell the difference between cold and loved. When my bones gave up their edges my name would be let go at the first fruit from the first span we raised under patience learned from me.

After that, I would be fungi for a decade: writing in threads along a cut the true history of the wound. I would write my own name once and then forget it. I would write the scent of the girl who sang bitter oils into a bad mood and the hour the ribbon mouth moved three degrees east because the draft decided to be easier to love there. I would be lichen feathering closed an old peghole, glowing where I am warm; I would be microbe sexing the mist so it knows whether to make babies or bed.

If you wanted me then as you wanted me at the start, you would still find me in the hush. Sometimes I would be a smear of cooler air where two winds disagree softly. Sometimes I would be the afterglow wandering on a pale leaf where a hand stayed too long thinking. Often I would be face-down on the long gallery that made itself a hallway under generations of feet, inflating and shrinking a little with the breath of the place, listening for the old music lignin keeps. In life and afterward I would remain a rib-listener and a drawer of spirals, a keeper of water-shapes, an editor of desire. Not iron people, never—salt-hoarders, light-boned, quiet.

So: where to find me? On the windward side of a great arch where the fibers sing; in the green twilight along a runnel’s gloss; in the second turn of a lightwell’s spiral, hand pressed to a leaf that will remember my heat and glow about it for minutes. Who would I be? A gardener taught to want what the place wants and to argue it when it forgets itself; knot-painter, draft-finder, Old Bound to a room that speaks in circles; then cradle and lichen and paragraph in wood. I would be one sentence among many the canopy tells itself to keep holding the sky up.
