At first I am only a bead, a thought of water on the frayed end of a cord. I hang by habit. I take shape from the air’s patience. A finger of breeze persuades me down the twist of fiber, and I let go and strike the inside of a shallow bowl. The bowl answers with a ring like a pale animal that knows it is not an animal and I stand inside that sound, which is to say I begin there.

I tell myself I might be the cord—the one that grows glossy along a third of its length in the good hour and dulls again by the time the ring of cups has traded itself twice, the cord that teaches the day when to begin soot-quietly. I could be the practiced slack and taut between the notches, the little braid that asks fog to line up and become audible. I could be the knot that remembers.

But I am not strong for long-held weight. I find I prefer edges to spans. I prefer to touch and let go.

I try on the staff next, the one each hand here learns before it learns a pen. The reed-tassel at my end darkens as I walk; my head drips into the traveler’s cup and the answers the ground gives through my ferrule come up into wrists and bones: safe, not safe; shallow give, deep betrayal. It’s a good feeling, to be a question and also a listener. I tap, not to declare but to ask please, will you hold me. I lean into bog and stone. I am an interrogator that makes no one defend themselves.

I could be that, the field-grammar in a length of wood. But even that keeps moving, and what calls to me here is the craft that holds, the work that makes level.

I begin to be someone.

Where you would find me is where a lip has misremembered itself—where a weep has learned to chatter and needs quieting, where a tufa mouth is growing eager in a slick way that will spill what must be guided. I am small where I can be, long where I must be, hair refused any place to rot, feet wide as trust, the soles learning to say not yet to the peat and yes, you may, to the limestone teeth.

I live between islands: my family’s frames raised over a faithful hollow, yes, and also all the paths between the places where water forgets and must be reminded. They call me, in the early years, Third Bead because I was born when the basin in the old plaza took a pattern no one had taught it. Later, after I learned to set a wedge so its tap traveled only into stone and not across it, I became Keeps-Level. Later still, when I could walk a line of drilled cups in fog and know by sound alone which one was clogging itself toward silence, they began to call me Lip-Tuner. Names here accrete as faithfully as tufa, and like tufa they are not jealous: you may use any of mine if you say them with your breath carried along the bowl.

My days condense the way the air does.

Before the strings gloss, I check the lashings above the cistern-stairs. The catchers sag with their small ocean. I touch them not with hands but with a reed paddle, coaxing beads toward threads, asking the threads to remember where to empty. A child comes behind me with a jar and a serious mouth; we make a little river where there was only suet-thick air. We say use words that mean don’t hurry, mean yes, good shoulder, mean you are well-lipped today. Far off, the ring of drilled cups ticks, and I measure my work against it. When the ninth trickle has passed to its neighbor we go to the basins.

I carry pegs and wedges in a netted bag of bark-fiber. Some are old friends, blackened by work and smoothed by fog into something like skin. I fit them into the wedge-marks my grandmother cut—her rhythm still there as clear as the print of her hand. I tap. The city takes the tap and eats it without complaint. If any ring comes back to me in a way that says uneven, I stop, breathe over the drop I’m about to send, and start again.

At the plaza I listen. The bowls stand still as moons. I wake one with a fingertip and then another. Between them stretches air so quiet you can bell it with a single drip. A composition here is not a thing I make and take. It is a way of lifting and hanging and letting go. I re-angle a catcher. I tilt a lip with the smallest chisel I am allowed. I watch the thread I have called down find the path I have asked of it and strike the platter at the pace this ridge-line has decided is noon. The ring walks to the carved edge and dies itself on level. A man in the far corner holds his breath so as not to speak above the waist.

When the basins have finished telling the plaza what hour it is, I go to other clean work. I help lay the fine screens across the sedge-tops and we draw them in the fat part of the day, pollen coat pale as animal fat and not animal. I blister my tongue on unscalded fern once a season to remind myself of humility. I carry the cords seeded with green across the shallow black panes and draw them back heavy; I hang the sheets that dry like paper over the screens that hold the fog off and not away. Between these small food-works I check the runnels. A root always wants to own a channel. I tell it no, here is where you may step. I am not unkind. I offer it a new notch with a promise.

In the afternoons the city’s busy silence thickens. Children run with their hands flung like wings only because they have no word for wings. I stop them with a palm flat to air if they aim too straight at a place that does not want them. I am not a mother to all of them but I have that right here, the right to set a wedge of the body where speed is trying to take hold. Later, when we lash frames for those who have chosen to share an island, I carry timbers at hip-height with those same children grown quiet and blushed by work. When the frame stands, we bow to it as we would to a basin that has rung well. This is as much a wedding as anyone needs to call by that name.

At the cliff I forget speech almost completely. I lay my staff down. I go on my knees and do what was done for me: take a small wrist; show a small skull the exact difference between the cloud that wants your eyes and the lip that keeps your heart. We do not send anything down. We ask the air to keep behaving the way it has always behaved, steady and uninterested in theater. We give thanks to stone by clearing what is not stone from its lips, and to water by giving it a new edge to write.

I offend sometimes. I have, more than once, cut a terrace a thought too far into the ridge’s knife and had to answer for it with the families that heard a familiar ring go thin. We resolved it as we resolve everything: I added a lip; I cut a channel; I carried someone else’s timbers to their next bridge. When I tired, I said I am already draining and someone with a name bright as a basin took the hammer from my hand.

Our arguments are slow and exact. Our joys are broad and soundless as fog moving across a plaza where the bowls are at rest. When I make salt I feel like I am catching a story that the air has been telling for a thousand distances. I wash it from the cloth into the smallest bowl I own and let it write a ring I will scrape later. I cook sedge cakes on stones warmed a day for the purpose and taste in them the lime that keeps us clean and not shabby.

I carry no fear that has a claw. My fears are geometry: that a lip will let go and teach the wrong lesson; that a quiet will mold instead of ring. My virtues are the city’s: true, level, well-lipped, steady.

When I am not doing any of these, I write. Not with ink. My poem is a sequence of wedge-marks struck cleanly along a face so new it blue-flashes when rinsed. The measure is the spacing, the line is the lip, the rhyme is the way water chooses one groove over another and yet returns to me when I ask. Sometimes, in the lower light, when the fog has sat with us a full day and the cords are glossy along half their lengths, I make the plaza sing until those who pass forget they are walking and do not disturb the city they built.

At night we do not say night. We say we are already draining. We hang our garments in patterns that keep air walking and let the mushroom-snow pass us by with only a polite dusting. We oil our scalps. We speak of what was level and what asked to be. We go to the frames and listen for the single wrong drip that would mean the granary is remembering its wish to be peat.

I do not think often of death, but when I do it sits in me calmly like warm water in a basin that the sun never bothers to heat. I know what will be done. I will be washed in the shallow that holds the taste of our day. That wash will be poured once through a flute, and my leaving will be a thin music, almost too difficult to hear without the hand cupped. I will be lime for a while, clean and not too heavy. Then I will be carried to a faithful weep and laid on reeds. There I will teach stone to grow.

If you come later, you may touch my mouth. It will be tufa and obedient. It will be slick with water that has learned me. Do not move my lip unless there is danger. If there is danger, move me carefully and make sure the new wedge-marks are true. I will not complain. I will do what stone does here, which is accept the long work of being shaped by patience, of writing level on the world until the world takes the lesson into its own edges.

So where I am is between. Between fog and cup, between catch and fall, between plank and runnel, between child and edge, between bowl and ring. And who I am is a set of quiet devices wearing a person’s body. A Tuner-of-Lips. A Reader-of-Rings. A Singer-who-Uses-Other-Things-to-Sing. The last animal made polite by air, content to become stone when asked, with hands that know beams and rope and the exact sound a drop makes when it finds the home you made for it.
