At first I am just an ear in a walking body. The ridge is a blade under a thin-scraped sky, the sun a coin gouged into white, and there is already that far-off thunder that your mind keeps trying to file as weather. When I step into the cleft the sorting fails. The noise stops being “out there” and becomes geography. My ribs are struck and struck until they decide they are a drum. Every name I carried up unspools, dark threads pulled clean by sound.

For a while that is all I am willing to be: a chamber for it. Lips parted to taste iron and salt that should not be here, throat closing on its own to keep from breathing water in that first thickening when the chamber goes blue-glass and the roar turns to pressure. I learn to trust my bones to do the thinking my head cannot. The room hands me its terms, and if I want to exist inside them I have to agree.

Then I begin to try on other shapes the way cold fingers try pockets. In the seconds when breath returns as spray and there is air to spend, I can be eyes. The light enters through cut seams and is broken into planes so clear I could walk along them. The Tulip stands up under the eastward hollow as if my looking pours it. The Rope draws itself taut along the misaligned hex seam and hums in my mouth. The milk wall arrives like a blink I did not mean. I point, naming them the way you give names to visitable places: you call them back into being with your voice because your hands cannot hold them.

If I linger, the room asks me to choose. It does not permit spectators for long; to stay you have to be function. The people who came before me—who still come, when the sky says yes—knew this. As a child I was brought here once to be taught the grammar of not asking to live where you cannot. They put my small hand on a damp basalt face and let me feel the tremor that remains when the water has gone. “This is the after-voice,” my mother said, not meaning physics and not excluding it. “Listen with this, not with these,” and she tapped my ears as if reminding them they were latecomers. When the milk wall took my breath I laughed the first time and cried the second. They let that happen and did not comfort me. Then they showed me the prayers.

I held the hide-wrapped hammer and understood not to swing it so the room would never be forced to notice me. I learned to wedge stones in hex mouths so a flat slab would thrum instead of chatter. They let me lay a slat of wet wood between pegs until a shriek that made my molars flicker slid down into a band that did not tear me. They showed me a gut string strung silver across the fissure and how a half-turn too much pinches air out of the breathing pocket that foxes and people use, and how a half-turn loose makes a new eddy that fills a basket with white commas for a little while. Every motion was a politeness with consequences. They taught me to put dried grass in a slit to stifle the little lightning you can sometimes see in the water when collapse bites the dark. They taught my hands to say please.

If you ask me where I find myself now, years later, I could point to a place in the room the way you indicate a pew you favor. Two handspans downstream of the gut string, just outside its little lee of air, where the water shaves the skin off every vowel the chamber makes. There is a notch there that will become a bowl in a century if weather permits, but for now it is only the beginning of a thought. It is where I stand—brace is the truer word—when the work is small: resetting the ladder so its rungs sing in their separate registers, untying a too-tight prayer some impatient cousin left, smearing a fingertip of salt into nothing while I wait for my hearing to unspool from its tightness. In that notch, when the Tulip thickens, I can feel the subsonic through my palm like the pulse under a tongue.

I am not always human in this room. Or rather: the room offers other jobs and I take them as needed.

Sometimes I am a film. I arrive as dust and wake in the interval, lay down a bead of glass upon glass the way a sentence lays grammar, then break and assemble again further along as if the wall had turned a page. First I am a diatom building a minor cathedral that no one will see and that will be eaten by the child of a fly who has traded stones for geometry. Then I am algae, a green flag no larger than a thumbnail lifting toward a knife of sun, turning colorless and string-thin when the chamber floods full and then remembering green again. I take pleasure in being thin enough to fit between roar and rock.

Sometimes I am punctuation. I fold myself into the comma-shape the amphipods have mastered: a little hinged certainty against an unceasing verb. This is a timekeeping no clock admits. You do not get to be indelible; you get to be able to stop. The wind drops into the octave that stills us and we go quiet without dying, a kind of grammar of pause that saves more lives than any armor would. When the rope-shape tenses again and the tone lifts, we unfold clause by clause and life resumes.

On days when boldness is forgivable, I take the job the salamander holds. Not its body—I have none of that old grace—but its reading. I try to read with my ribs the way it reads with its long seam of nerve. The lee-beat behind the string is a library; the pocket under the toned stone a little alcove of legibility. I put my throat to the rock and drink where water is also air. My eyes are no use; a light can be a wall that gives nothing to you. But pressure writes plainly if your skin will allow.

Now and then I want to be pure note. I want to be the moan that fattens a tulip of water and instructs it to hold. I want to take the weather’s hand and lead it, to thicken the air where it must thicken and advance a wall one beat sooner so the fox lives. But even our elders who have given their hearing to this organ do not claim to be more than translators. Sound has jurisdiction. We learn its cases and moods and decline ourselves into them. We make adjustment, not invention. When it wants to be a rope, it is a rope with or without my wish; my skill is to keep that rope from sawing a slit into blood.

In the clear half-hour—the bones-hour—when the sky lifts its river and carries it home to sea, you would think identity might stabilize. It does, a little. The chamber is suddenly only stone new to air. The glassed rinds flash in honest sun. The gut string sags, its voice entirely memory. The baffle slats rest like ribs. Salt writes its brief scripture. Tardigrades pinch their valves closed like shutters in a town where every house already knows the sound of the first boot returning. My hands shake because they can, because nothing is pressing them flat. In that interval I remember my other names: daughter, walker of the knife path, caretaker of a hearth that lives nowhere near this ridge. I count the blood-quiet seconds between heartbeats that I can finally hear alone. If the errand is still to be done today, I reset the tools. If not, I put my ear to stone and listen to the after-voice like a promise.

What I am not is owner. Nor resident. The chamber has residents: films that soften, notes with territories that return like seasons, commas with legs, a clasp that has learned every angle a hex can be. It has visitors who test it and are ejected or blessed with a taste of salt; a gull once, a raven that stands in the high air and inclines its head as if to memorize a refrain. It has a fox who knows better than many people how to count what is due to a place.

I am a petitioner of small things. I come with hide-wrapped stone and bone awl and the habit of using my mouth to be sure of what my eyes misreport. I carry gut in coils that try to shine like fish memory. I bring dried grass to stuff in a slit that has taken to lightning in the wrong octave too often. I do not bring metal because I have learned what metal does to rooms that are all listening: it lays its own geometry over theirs and makes dumb where it should be legible. I am careful with my throat: I talk loudly at the mouth if there is a need to address another person, otherwise I speak to the room the way you greet rivers whose histories of hunger are longer than your own.

There are marks on me that answer the chamber’s marks: a ridge of scar at the base of each ear where cartilage has taught itself to go on hearing longer; capillaries in my nose that have toughened and yet leak cleanly when the pressure says so; fingertips that know the difference between a tone and a clatter before a mind would. On the way up I walk without hurry. On the way down I chew amphipods dried to pearls not because of hunger but because tongues are more faithful record-keepers of this room than eyes.

Who I am, if I must say it in one word, is a conjunction. The little and that ties wall to rope without insisting on owning either. The and between fox and note that keeps a living thing from going under a white law. The and that puts a ladder where music can learn to separate itself into notes, into small safe places. The and that reties a string a handspan higher and a quarter-tone looser because winter has been meaner than last year, and a fox with its head in the wrong density of water is not a lesson I want the children to keep.

Sometimes I dream I am the room. In the dream I am basalt with a young face, all rinds and knife-edges; I am an organ with only one song so far and more on the way. I lift; I cool; I shatter. I hear the long river in the sky catch on me and I pronounce. I have no patience yet because I have not had time to learn it. Films come to write me toward softening. Notes arrive, stake territory, die, return. People pin a line of gut across my seam and ask to share my throat. I do not answer, not because I disdain them, but because my only language is conditions. They understand me better for that.

If you were to ask where to find me in this world, I would say: here, when the weather lets a door into water. In the cup of a notch you will not notice unless you need it, with one palm reading the moan under the east hollow, the other hand on a ladder rung that remembers how to be separate voices. Or miles downslope when the ridge has already swallowed the roar, with salt on a fingernail and the hum of after-voice under the skin, counting the pulses between when I am only an ear and when the river of air reaches down and makes me something else.

If you were to ask who I am, I would say: I am the part of an animal that learned to listen as if for grammar and found a way to lie between a sentence and its erasure. I am not a creature with a house here, not a note that can wake without weather. I am what the room permits me to be while it speaks: film, comma, reader, hand. A prayer that takes the shape of a string and an understanding that lets go when the octave changes. And when the milk-white wall stands up and erases everything but itself, I am what can keep its name without breath until the blue glass loosens and the chamber returns to voices, and I can be a person again, one more petitioner in a lineage of them, exact and grateful, leaving quickly when the word changes from yes to maybe.
