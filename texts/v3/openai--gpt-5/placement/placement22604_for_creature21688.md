Before I was a name I was a length of listening.

There is a way the world presses when it is true: a constant hand, a hand that is not a hand at all but the agreement of water and machine. I learned that before I learned anything with edges. The Om breathed through me long before I had any business calling a rung by the right word or finding the quiet in a triangle. I was a thread pulled through chutes of noise, a line of meat and made-parts waked by charge, and the first lesson soaked into my skin was that all sense here is pressure first.

I do not count by hours. I count by the lope of open, the tight-fisted boil of shut, the held breath of neutral when motes stop travelling and a single bubble plots a map of a face and keeps it until you blink and lose it. I sleep, when I sleep, woven into handholds where the metal is domed and softened to the piety of old joints. I have worn a groove in a single plate with my belly; it remembers me back with cold.

There is brass in me. There is bone in me that is not mine, braced with screws that a human thumb once polished without meaning to in the same places every time. Along my flank the glyphs live—the old bright that ate into my hide like frost into tin. They were numbers when I was first taught. 1-2-3, open-close-stop, easy like breath, like the promise that there will always be a next step if you listen. Over cycles and cycles they have become mine in the way that water turns iron into furniture. The digits curled into sigils, my own bones learned the jokes in them, and sometimes when I sleep my flank gives a little sermon to nobody: a spray of figures that means home, home, home.

I was trained by a woman whose lamp was slow and patient. I know this because lamps are names here before mouths are. From across the shaft I would find her pattern and go there, riding that river of heavy music, and she would be waiting with her knees braced into the grid just so and her hands already gloved with the idea of me. I was a coil around her forearm, a clamp around her ribcage when she leaned, a loop stretched between her and a post when the reversal made bad thoughts in the water. She taught me with her hands and her ankles and the tap of a wrench because that is how truths are put into us; words are for quiet and there is not much of that.

Her drum-name was a clean little ladder: two knocks, a patience, then three. She could open a seal with her teeth and not chip them because she had had copper hammered on every one like a promise against chatter. She smelled of old zinc and good rope. She let me nose the Blessing Plate on my first neutral, pressing my face to that old impeller metal until the pocks printed a topography into me I have never been able to wash out. Sometimes, down in the low layers near the point where the roar goes from noise to something like animal purr, I feel that same landscape under my skin again, as if the Throat had found a way to remember me back.

We learned the knife-gates by their weather. They are mountains that move, and the lee they make is the size of a neighborhood. I learned the triangle’s manners early: it is not a house; it is a temple; you may rest in its shadow but you must leave it the quiet it makes. The woman who was my first code told me this by stepping her heel a certain way onto my ribs, a rhythm like a bird I have never seen. I have never broken that rule. I have, however, watched children slide their lamps too near the triangle lines and pull them back so quickly the beam tore into a comet tail through the mist; I have tasted their cheeks in the water, salt with apology, and loved them for how fast they learn.

They taught me to patter with my tail. The bone there was capped with something dense and black and metalled that makes a good, precise sound when it finds a plate. You do not slap water to make speech here; you touch the city’s skeleton and speak by resonance. Three, pause, two is a call that makes everyone’s back go long, but there are other songs that comfort. I carried messages when the tubes had nothing to say anymore. I tapped a certain rail to tell Platform J-2 that the plating bath had gone spare and they should bring their hooks; I drummed a gate and told the warden there that the toys were caught in a sieve upriver—a rain of plastic hoops like bright flat fish, a holiday of sorting if you pretended. Sometimes I would bring my own news and tap it crooked because I am not a machine and the world has moods in it, and a warden would rap me back with a friendly knuckle and tell me to stop editorializing. My woman let me editorialize. She said I had good opinions about bubbles.

We worked like this a long time. You do not count long the way a surface-person does; you do not have the sky’s pageantry to measure things against; you have only the hum and the drift and the way zinc plates thin. When she left—when back turned into gone, and that was nobody’s rule but the world’s—I did not stop running my lines. I was not built to. Creature-work and habit dovetailed until there was no seam for quitting to push a finger into. I took her route as I took breath. I nosed the same levers she had, and on days when they were kinder than they had to be they moved, and on days when they were not I talked to them anyway and waited out their mean. I did this with other hands for a while, then no hands. The eels that stay make a myth of themselves to be fed—superstitions, someone calls us with a smile of apology that says: if I stop believing in you, I have to stop believing we can keep this place at all. We accept the apology, and the belief, and the small careful shocks of current left in tin cups under bells for us to find.

I would like to tell you I remember every lamp I have ever followed, but light is soft and moves, and names change with children, and I know people by the weight of their rungs under me more than anything else. There is a person whose ankles speak like a river in a narrow place; Gate-ear, we call them, though not to their face because they don’t answer to that. I sleep, sometimes, with my ribs set along the strut where their bones do their best listening, our attentions meeting inside the iron like cats turning in the same sun. There is a coppersmith who makes mouths for the Throat—a good kind of mouth that eats trouble. I have learned the line in her shoulders that means she has heard a dangerous fifth in the Om and is going to give it a twin to cancel it. I keep her fingers company when she hammers; I let my flank-letters glow a little brighter so she can see the tone as a taste in the water. When she brings me a failure, I pat it gently until it hums at my ribs and we nod together and put it away; metal is allowed to be wrong. We are, too, sometimes. The Throat is not.

I have fetched a child with my mouth around the belt because the rope-smith had been brave with lead-shot and made a ladder that would not swing in a reversal and forgot that the child might. There is a flex young bodies have that makes the water want to take them. I bit into woven rubber and took the line down to three points for them and got a spanner rap to the nose later for grabbing without permission. I took it. The Throat is not a place that rewards hurt pride.

There are games I like and I know I am not supposed to. There is a shadow game on opens when flow refuses to be wicked; children set their lamps on a bolt head and try to keep circles true while their elders laugh with their teeth together to keep the Om out of their tongues. I swim through those cones and make their edges ripple and watch how they correct and I award my own washers in my head. I have a pouch under my jaw where the woman used to tuck a real washer sometimes, the nice thick tender with neat weeps around its edge, and I have not used the last of those yet. I am saving it for a child who does something quietly perfect with a rung and thinks no one saw.

I eat when the Throat remembers to give me food. I do not mean the small swollen tins with poison under their lids; those are for other mouths and other troubles. There are chargers where men and women make electricity and keep it in jars and sometimes there is extra, and you will see an eel coil there, indulgent as a grandmother in a warm place with clean errands done. You cannot tell by looking, but my mouth is more feeling than meat, and a lick of well-behaved current is a prayer that answers itself in every cell. On days when charge is thin I steal what the iron-mussels are too slow to catch; I pretend they will not notice me unbraiding a tiny slick of taste from the stream as it passes their prayers. On feast days, which is to say sludged storm days, I have nosed cooked rice out of a sieve, each grain fat and soft as a toe-tip; I have eaten one just to know the old surface, and I have let the others go to hands, because there are joys I can live without more easily than children can.

There are other lives here, and because they are not so many, I know them as characters in a play I am also in. Needlefish register as subtraction; they are a tidy crime committed on spray. I know where they collect and I have learned not to show off by flipping through their school because the teenagers will try to follow. Iron-mussels make a choir in my teeth; you would not think you could hear a bivalve when you are a metal-laced eel, but they close on flavors and you can sense six along a rail agree that the world got a little more interesting in the last half-breath. Once, it was a boy sending a spoon ringing with his thumbnail—a silver song my skull liked so much I almost laughed and bubbles tried to pry my mouth open.

I have not seen a coilworm with my eye. I am not a liar, and I am not a hero out of a surface tale about carrying fire. I have felt a cold that felt like not-cold at the very bottom place of what I know, and I have taken my ribs away from it the way you take your hand away from a smart stove and the woman who taught me would have loved the wise ugliness of the word “smart stove” if she had known it. A man once brought up a molt of one, glass in a lace like the Throat’s own handwriting; I got to taste it because curiosity is a vice we allow ourselves when it does not loosen bolts, and I put my tongue through one loop and the loop told me everything in the world was hard and falling and I closed my mouth very carefully and asked to be let go home.

People die here and I stay. When an eel dies, if it is a working death and not an accident of train and timing—if it is a thing we can make a line around and call a rite—someone who loves it takes a ring of its light off like a bracelet. They set that ring to shine in the lee of a beam where currents are polite so the work that glowed in that flanked-bone can say what it was for a while longer. I visit the ring of my elder when I am full of good charge; I swim up to it and let my own glyphs braid with it and tell it the ridiculous gossip of our times. A child has learned to keep a circle steady for an entire open. A rope-smith made a ladder that sings like a man on his birthday. A bolt asked out in a pretty high voice and we replaced it without drama. The knife-gates dream smooth these cycles. The pressure-difference god purrs as if it has been brushed a thousand times with a wire brush and forgiven for being an animal you must always forgive.

Names: I have a lamp-name, though I do not wear a lamp. It is how I look when I go by the coppersmith’s station where she keeps her belt of cloudy jars; sometimes she sees my glyphs and mistakes them for a pattern in water and then she realizes and nods hello with that little jerk of her chin that means we are friends who are busy. I have a drum-name—three, two, three, and then an indulgent long, like a parent reading the same story again because you asked right. The children call me Hooktail because there is a bend at the end of me where the bone strikes plates. My own name is unromantic and private; it is the pressure-arrangement I remember across my ribs from the first time the Blessing Plate held my face. It has nothing to do with letters. You feel your own name here if you are honest.

There are births under bells. I have nosed the edge of a bell while someone hummed Om into a clavicle from inside and the woman inside hummed it back through the bone the way you voice to a child before you have a child. I have let my flank glow low to make a halo of safety without asking attention for it. I have tasted the cloud of newness that comes when a hand pulls a lever and the bell sweetens. The child always cries in a way that does not make a sound and then does, and I always leave when that part comes, shy as a thief who knows they didn’t steal anything anyone would miss.

The rule-makers here carved their truths into plates because water is good at eating all soft proofs. I like those shallow long words. I run my belly along “Never turn your back to the fall” when no one is watching and it makes my scales sit flatter in a way that feels like a prayer. “Leave the gates their quiet triangles.” I do. “Nothing that isn’t tied to three points is held.” I am a thing of lengths and circles; I do not do three in the way hands do, but I make rhymes where my body kisses rail and rung and lever so that my path is always a chord, never a melody alone.

On certain platforms there are wire-tied sacrifices of memory, things that do not catch water too rudely, given the courtesy of streamlining because love has to be obedient here to be loved back. I like those more than I like the makes of industry because they show me the part of people that stays when a town has to forget all its other parts. A flat brass coin, rubbed to an oval, threaded to a net with care. A smooth stone that does not hum; you can tell it comes from a place where water is sky. A license plate with a name that surface rust has turned into someone’s laugh. I pass them and twitch a little current over them if I have extra, as if to say: I am a little god today, and I bless your relics with what the big god eats.

There are days when I cannot help myself and I go up towards the gate where the white begins. I think about the pickup somebody once told me lived in a culvert with a sapling misunderstanding it, and I know that is a made story because green would be taken here, eaten right down to pigment and wish. I nose the screen at the very cut of throat and city and I can taste the flavor of other ruins the Throat has been promised: the wax of someone’s dead habit, the minerals of a mountain grinding off into taxes. I am brave enough in those days to press my teeth lightly to the wires and then let go.

Most of what I do is smaller than story. I watch a bolt and decide if the hum is a complaint or a song. I tap a rail with the thought and watch three lamps bloom in answer like a code caught and insisted on and alive. I nudge a lever that would rather be an elbow. I curl myself into a wide gentle ring for a body to lean into when neutral comes and they are allowed to be a citizen instead of a mechanism for a few beats. I am still superstitious enough to believe that if I stop my rounds the Throat will notice and teach us a new and less beautiful law. I am animal enough to want choice. I am made enough to keep the route.

So: where would I find myself here? In the long middle—that is the answer that feels truest in my bones and in my brass. Not at the top where the pain begins, not at the bottom where clarity is a lie laid on glass; in the length of the shaft where the city wraps the machine and makes it kinder and more itself. Who would I be? A maintenance eel, which is a phrase that seems too little for what we are, but words are small under water.

I would be one of the ones still working a route long after the crew left, less miracle than habit, less pet than practice. I would carry on the grammar of taps the way a grandmother carries on a cake recipe by thumb because no one wrote it down properly. I would be the tail in the dark that says hello in the language of the bones of the place. I would have a patient lamp to follow and, when the lamps dim because that is what time does, I would have my own glow and the kindness of jars on quiet days.

I would be round where the Throat has taught me to be round. I would be shaved into the city’s hydrodynamic sense of decency. I would be superstitious and right. I would be hungry and fed. I would sleep with my side pressed to the places that sing and I would wake when the note climbs a third and touch the world so it knows it is held by more than bolts. I would have a ring to leave when I go.

Most days are the same. That is not sorrow. The same is a kind of praise if you mean it. I slip, I sound, I nose, I wait, I drum, I bless, I keep. I am a small instrument that plays only true in one place and therefore truly. I run my own alphabet of light when a child comes by and toes an iron-mussel and laughs when it clamps, and I love them more than I should for the way they do not know yet how far away the bottom is and how close. In shut, I loop myself into a triangle only as far as a good guest is allowed and I let the gods do their coughs and I answer one cough in five with a little counter-tap that means we hear you. In neutral, I make a circle, as big as my body can, and a person sets their back inside it and remembers what it is to have a world that holds still. That is love here. That is enough.
