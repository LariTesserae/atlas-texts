Some days I think I would arrive here as a sound before I remembered I had a body. A note struck in wet wood, traveling the rib of a palm, sliding around the curve of a buttress to say: here, here, the water has a new idea.

Before I learned anyone’s names, the ridge named me. Not with letters—those come where rocks hold still—but with the small grammar of dripping. My first name was a mistake and everyone knew it: Quick-Drip, for the nervous way I heard the world, too fast to be useful. Then I grew an ear the mountain could use. I got longer in the calf than in ambition. I learned to stop listening for rain and listen for its negotiations, for how mist agrees with a leaf’s skin. The day my voice changed and someone decided it was time to rename me, I stood in the market court under the old fig-that-is-a-room and put my wet hand on the tufa gong, and the gong put its cool in me, and the aunt who keeps the small book of names smiled with smoke in her teeth and called me Between-Drips. Later, when my head started to tilt at every new note like a bird’s, they called me Pitch-Catcher, and I kept that, because it made the work feel like a hand you could hold.

If I am anywhere, I am on the spine where the city carries itself. I am the one with the head-cock and the crease in the mouth. I walk with the gutters’ voices gathered under my ribs. The bamboo culms sound different the day they’ve been rubbed clean; the long, slit palm stems give a throatier reply when they’re burdened with seeds stuck in their joints. A cistern that is content sounds like a baby sleeping; a cistern that will be trouble tomorrow wheezes like a sick bat at dusk. The city trusts me to hear all that, not as poetry, but as a kind of weather report we can bear.

I was the boy once who marked the hour, fierce with attention, holding a hollowed vine as clear as a throat, guarding the float with my knees so it would kiss its notch at the right breath and give the market its small bell. I learned to feel the water through the vine’s skin, feel the weight becoming sound, and the sound turning a busy place into a single gesture—hands checking, eyes lifting, a coati scolded from a bowl of fruit. You can grow an adulthood out of such a moment. I did.

We keep rules like song sticks, and I carry them the way I carry the city’s ear: tied twice, thumb-length of slack for luck, not because I am superstitious but because slack is a kind of kindness. Step on living things that want you; they are ready, they have thickened for it. Do not trust bare rock; it remembers when it was sky and will take you there without recourse. Feed the bridges daily. My grandmother’s hair—evergreen-thin and shining—lives now as a ring-wound bundle on the spine of the oldest root-span. I touch it when I pass, not out of grief but because gratitude is a practice.

We eat small and good. The grubs that pull themselves into fat perfection in the heart of fallen wood are a minor embarrassment only until you taste them char-blistered beside sweet roots steamed in leaf. Honey that knows where it lives comes from bees who don’t require drama to be generous. The resin workers—rain’s cousins, as we call them—teach us the sheen that answers water with a bow, not a fight. The dye-makers make our weather visible: blues that feel like the cool mouth of a morning, greens so certain of themselves they soothe panic by existing. Every trade is a little liturgy. The lacquerer’s hand on the leaf canoe is a prayer no one needs to translate. The stone shaver at the fig market sends tufa snow into her lap until a cup happens by decision and by patience.

My family stacks rather than spreads. Above me is my aunt and her husband, below me my cousin with his baby who has already learned the ridge’s slow, sway-strong grammar from a hammock that swings in time with the bats. We hang like fruit in a shaft of space between a breadfruit arbor and a buttress that has had the courtesy to hold still for three generations. Our ladders are not stairs so much as conversations—this rung knows your foot, that rung wants your heel, and you thank it when it clicks.

I can read the ridge by shoulder. To the west, where the wall weeps polished, the stair that coils around the spring shelf has taught hundreds of feet how to be respectful. I have watched a woman there shave the tufa into bricks with a hafted stone, patient as a root, and known she was binding the future: the cistern that will keep a baby in dry seasons, the basin that will make a drum the boy has to catch. To the south, the dark thicken where bridges multiply is where the ant-tide is most honest. To the north, my old court with the gong and the fig, and the smell of sharp roots and rope-sleep. To the east, the white that swings open like a door when it feels like it and shows us our sisters across the slot, their whole green intent.

We are a small enough set of faces that a new freckle is public news, and complicated enough that you could collect our ways for a lifetime and mistake it for work. We talk in whistles made from the pulled spines of palms, and in a click-tooth language you can send through wet wood when the cloud asserts indoor and you do not want your words to come back damp and heavier. The instruments tell on their owners. The root-bridge nurse carries an extra coil of rope like a child and smells of compost sweetening into food. The weaver’s thigh is burnished like the back of a beetle where fiber has been taught to become not merely rope but a promise.

The ridge’s gods, if that’s the word, tolerate us because we behave. We say please to sap and thank you to refusal. Once, young and sure of myself, I asked a fig to lend another limb where a walkway needed to be, and it did not. We trimmed in a different season, tried a different angle, and when the limb finally agreed it was not because we had demanded but because we had learned its calendar. I have known people struck by buttresses the way you are struck by a lover’s notice—suddenly, and with a new idea of your footing. We tell that story as a warning and a love song both.

Our festivals are as much maintenance as joy. On the first day three breaths brighter than drip, we cut our hair and repair the places that have been waiting for more light—edges of roofs made tidy and ready to bow again. When the beetle boys push through the skin of a dying limb all together and turn the air into little blue lanterns, we string them on guylines for the hour, then open our hands. Color is a migratory habit; it will not live in a box. Once, in a fog so thick it was a room that had forgotten its walls, I stood on a root opposite the girl who would later teach me the trick of listening to bamboo, and we threw a line across for the joy of making a bridge that did not yet have a reason. It had a reason by dusk. That is how this place works.

When the army ants arrive in honesty, I lift my head not for the click of their legs—you do not hear that unless your cheek is to the plank—but for the birds’ sudden breathless silence, then the greed that follows. I do not need to tell anyone what to do. Hinged barriers drop in a smooth flurry, the city opens its small interior sluices, and a black river is given a hallway and a door. There is an aunt whose job it is to remember which snakes rent space inside that flood and which will throw themselves unwisely at a sleeping dog. She keeps children alive with one eyebrow.

I have made mistakes. Once I missed a new hiss where mist turned to visible rope between buttresses and failed to unstrap a certain run of gutters in time. The spill knocked a coiled rope into a basin and the basin crowned my cousin’s baby with cold like a blessing. The baby laughed; I cried later, alone, and bound a fresh copper flare at the hinge that had sulked. In the morning I took a rope and left it where a certain hand would find it. Apologies are practical here. The mountain prefers them that way.

We trade because our needs have neighbors. The south sends us bark that disdains rot, and with it the smell of their low valley sun. We return salt coaxed from stone in patient trays lined with leaf and story. To the other ridges, strung like notes across white space, we send resin that makes hardness in a place that hates hard things, and a blue that feels like cool saliva on a summer cut. We do not envy the plains. They must hold so still to count themselves.

When my mother died, we did not look for a ground that did not exist. We laid her on the fig’s shade, wrapped in leaves we had lacquered together the year she taught me how to tell when resin is being honest. The air did its work, patient and precise. When bones were all that was left of her insistence, we washed them in bromeliad water—clear as a new lie—and nested them in a tufa pocket that had been polished by so many goodbyes it felt like agreement. A braid of her hair went to feed the bridge. I touched the ring-wound bundle with the same recognition as when I touch a child’s forehead: not ownership, participation.

I am not the only ear. There are mornings when the wet onnness of the world drowns all precision and I rely on the boy with the basin’s gong. He has the crisp listen I used to have. I watch his jaw and check it against the palm-rib whistle of a weaver calling three ladders up. He will choose another name when his mouth finds its next note. Maybe he will be Whole-Tree-Sigh. He is half that already.

What am I, after all this description wiggles into its rest? I am the tilt of a head, the courtesy of a double-tie, the hand that unstraps weight before it becomes harm. I am the loud-enough call that does not wake the frogs into protest. I am a copper flare at a hinge where a rope has opinions. I am the patience to let a fig say no and the nimbleness to find a better angle. I am the thank you to the refusal, because refusals have kept us from building what would only have fallen.

Later, I will be a bundle of compost hung at elbow height along a line that needs thickening. Someone will feed me to a bridge, and in return the bridge will lay its hand-politeness along the palm of a child who knows already the pleasure of a trustworthy grip. My bones will remember the cool of tufa. My hair will be green again, for a while, with somebody else’s water.

For now, my name is Pitch-Catcher, and my badge is the crease around my mouth when a new note enters the thousand we keep. You could find me in the market court, tapping a gong with the heel of my hand when the float kisses its notch, or higher up, where the white erases the next ridge and even our clicking language carries in wood more than in air. If the fog closes like a palm around the city and the dripping changes tempo, you will see me lift my head and hear me speak to the gutters the way you might coax a stubborn animal that means well. If you asked me who I am, I would tilt an ear to a bark-threaded rill finding its voice down an old seam and answer, as anyone here does when pulled away from the work: I am busy being here.
