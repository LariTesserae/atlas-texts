I arrive first as breath. Not mine exactly, but the little looped music in the mask—draw in, offer back; a brightened ribbon of warmth and water sent home into the body instead of spent to the air. The height presses silence on the ears. Sound starts away and thinks better of it. The light is too honest to be forgiven. I put my palm on a column and feel the long, slow answer in its skin: cooler than noon, warmer than morning; a thing with its own temper. I stand in the lane between two winds and learn where to put my weight so I do not owe the day a stumble.

I try on a few selves. A hawk asks me to be fall and I consider it—the quick treaty with gravity, the hot-red engine of lungs made for empty air. But I was not born for that clean hunger. A fox flickers at the edge of a warmed span, her kits confiding their sleep to a thickness of human-made breath, and for a minute I am her tail, a kept balance, wind-measuring brush. I loosen and spill across slick stone with the morning rills, feeling the small rightness of slits in rock choosing me long before I am water, and for another minute I am a path, lit and then gone. I even borrow a lacewing’s lace for an hour and let the edges of my own attention frost, riding that knee-height wind the swallows anticipate with their whole bodies.

But I keep finding myself where two states touch and argue politely.

I am a Seamlighter. That is the nearest word in our language to the place in me that is most used and most content. I light seams because in this country the line between one and another—between day and night across a panel, between vapor and bead, between stillness and a little stirred air—is worth as much power as a river would be somewhere gentler. You can’t burn what isn’t here. You can notice.

My work is a cartography of almosts. At dawn I make my rounds with my teacher’s old tools in their cloth: a set of thin fingers you can lay along a panel to feel the surprise where shade ends and light begins, a soft brush that convinces a skin to refrigerate itself just a little more or less, and the blue sticks that show where a current would like to be if you could coax it. Some places want different thicknesses of fog to drink well; some places want only to stop the killing edge of shadow where it could run like a knife across a bed of cups or a coil of dwarf willow.

We have names for those edges: the Feather, where you spend a half-degree like a coin; the Hard Line, which we blur with patience and web; the Singing Place, where the hum in the pylon moves a note when a hand passes. I am paid partly in words. They accumulate on the inside of my mask, drawn in fingertip oil, a vocabulary that fogs and clears with my own breath: leeward-sweet, frost-tongue, night-skin, rill-door.

On some mornings the city asks for silence, and the silence asks for me. I walk the panels that face the first gold and lay my palm on the cold-caught glass-dark spin of composite. I listen for paper tearing inside the rock and that means no weight there, not even the weight of attention. I sign that place with a ribbon of frayed mesh to warn other hands. At the first thaw a bright impatience runs in thousands of threads down the cliff’s face. The Fogwrights have been out since black, turning their looms to the precise forgetfulness that lets ice not stick; the Attenuators under hooded glass notch the plazas a breath or two heavier. I go where a border is new. I go where the city meets the youngest water and confers.

At midday my work is a hinge. When the deck goes to clear, the plates shift and go porous and the sky walks through us. People eat looking down into place and at their own reflections. My job then is to tend the tiny map of heat: give a yellow-flare bulb its negotiated hour by darkening a seam before and after with a thumb’s width of painted shadow. If you make a day with miracles too many, the miracles thin. The bulbs want one hour. We want them as a promised jolt of color. I hold the edges for that hour the way you would hold a child’s face gentling it toward sleep.

When fog tries to own a terrace, when a day decides to be only its middle, I teach a panel how to remember night on just one side and day on the other. A drop is a story with a bang at the end. If I can let it get to bang without growing heavy, we will have an evening’s worth of light.

I was named once by a seep. That is another part of belonging. On a day last bright-half-season that felt like bone under skin, I found a rock-lip that glittered not with ice but with the beginning of wanting to be water. It was almost a drip but not yet. I sat with it until it admitted me. You inherit it when you name it. I called it Small Vow because if I lifted my hand and held it a little away, the skin between finger and thumb sweated in the same shy way. Now it is a task: to keep Small Vow from rushing into gulliness, to plant three cushions of sedge in a triangle that will invite it to linger without damming it into a lie, to teach dwarf willow’s short temper to thread there as if by its own decision. We visit each other in the right hours. Some mornings I give it a shadow-blur so it does not leave a bruise of cold on the thing it feeds. Some days I only walk past with a hand at my side and let it think it is unobserved.

When I am not at a panel or a seam, I sit with the Listeners. They taught me the grain of the rock’s inside weather, the stretched-out page-rip a boulder makes when the ice it held too tight refuses and leaves, the long held exhale when the ground gives back to night what day stole. On still days, we place little ceramic mouths under slate stacks and let the wind play them into a thought. Music here is for stone and the body that leans on it. If it carries, we are failing at quiet.

Children come with their fog-cards and their knot-tying, and I show them where a line is not where it appears. The law about shadow is a cruel and careful one. I teach them to soften it with a chain of nets that do not look like anything at all when you do it right. We fold the edges of their school’s courtyard into an extra breath that saves a lesson and hides itself as soon as the bell goes. Their wrists have cups sewn into cuffs; they learn to share the habit back to the field without shame.

In the afternoons the insects make smoke of the air again. That is not my hour; that is the swallows’ letter to the day and the beetles’ punctuation. Still, I love that we have built spans that rewrite a little law for their sake. The girders nurse a thermal that calls the midges from the seeps a heartbeat earlier than they would have come, not too early to harm them, early enough to practice for flight. Sometimes in fog the white little flowers over the cushions look like a fever dream and I stand farther back because glory is a kind of noise if you get too close.

On the Day of Yellow Bulbs I dress my tools. The sticks are polished with talc-silt and lichen oil. The brush is trimmed to a line as fine as thought. In the vault where we keep a slow warm mimicry of soil, the bulbs have been banking their green into an answer for months. All of us who hold the city’s edges take our stations and look sideways at the sun as if asking a neighbor for a favor. It is choreography with a star. We taste the air. We count our own pulses. When a child shouts the first yellow from a lower terrace, we let our wrists relax half a whisper. When the last one outgasses its bright into the thin without apology, we pull shade like silk across a shoulder and the hour is over. It would be easy to be greedy there. It would be wrong. We will do it again when the place says we may.

At night, up where the uppermost terraces are more chessboard than garden, I walk the Hard Lines and dissolve them. The phase bricks underfoot murmur as they work through their changes, sucking and singing, putting off the bending of the houses toward cold. I tune the ribs that wick sideways and up. Someone below will sleep without a throat gone raw for want of moisture; someone else will not cough a lung woken by dust. The city’s hum moves sometimes so low you could lie on it like a drum and believe the skin was the whole world. Lovers do that. I have.

I drink rust-lichen tea that goes lime under breath. I eat sedge like a vow—grain by grain folded into soup that tastes faintly of the iron and sweetness of the noon we ransomed from a sheet of fog. The fish-bodies we keep in the breathing troughs are jokes by ocean standards, but tender and steady gifts by ours. When we take a hare, which is not often, the thank-you is folded not into talk but into the habit of walking tomorrow with lighter feet.

I know my people by their gait: the Fogwrights have their shoulders a little forward as if leaning into a forever arriving; the Attenuators carry their heads tilted as if listening to a weather that has not yet consented to be sound; the Listeners’ hands never stop making nests for the backs of their own skulls; the Seamlighters like me step as if there were always a line at the toes we might smudge for better.

Now and then a caravan shows at the far lip of the land and stares down. We trade meshes, we trade algorithms, we trade water that has been taught to be a better thing by passing ten thousand times through a skin the size of a scarf. We don’t trade fog words easily. Not because we are stingy, because we have learned ours by failing to say rain. If you do not carry the habit of edges, the words rang hollow in the mouth and make you thirsty.

Age in this place is a kind of practiced patience. Elders have legs like cables and eyes that can see a kilometer changed since yesterday. They teach you how to be heavier than the wind when you must and lighter when the ground would like to take you into itself. They have names like Fog-Edge, like First-Rill, like Sound-of-Split. Mine was given as a joke when I made my first seam sing too loudly and had to blush into silence: Bright-Near. It has stuck.

Sometimes I am not a person, even now. When the plateau inhales at noon and lifts the air, when it exhales at dusk and thickens the plazas for old lungs, I let my ribs answer and become briefly a roll of weather. A breath can be a citizen here. A pause can be law. On certain nights that ring like glass I am the thrum inside a span that lets a fox curl her kits into a story no one interrupts. On certain noons I am the glitter along Small Vow’s lip and the idea that it could become a bead if it liked. On certain mornings I am the absence of harm where a shadow should have cut and did not because a hand had taught it manners.

If you ask me where to find me, come to a terrace where the light makes a straight line, and look for the place where it has been coaxed to be tender. Find the seam where the energy runs richest and see who is tending it with a brush and a set of thin-fingered instruments and a body trained to move like a polite question. Watch how I ask panels to argue with themselves and lose by choice. Watch how a bulb flares when all of us right along a cliff decide together not to want more. Listen with me to rock thinking about being water and decide not to hurry it. Sit with me under a span and feel the city breathe a sigh when a family passes and believe with me that buildings can mean kindness.

In time the gullies will be braver and the cushions will become neighborhoods. The seams will go inward, into soil and shade and the private politics of root. My guild will change. It always does. Maybe my children will be Attenuators, thickening plaza-air by a mercy or two. Maybe they will be Fogwrights and weavers. Maybe they will sit with their spines to the cliff and study beginnings. I like that, to think of beginnings as a craft.

For now I would be a hand at a boundary, a quiet that makes a current, a listener for lines. I would belong at the edge where day meets night on a panel and a watt is rescued from that handshake. I would be a citizen of small differences in a country that built its body on them. When you look for me, look for the bright-near not bragging, the shift just this side of notice, the place where shadow is less cruel than it could have been. I will be there, counting breath against distance and letting distance answer back with weather, adding my share to the choir of quiets.
