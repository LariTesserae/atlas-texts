I will not come here with bones. That is clear from the first pressure-note. Anything threaded by nerves would be carded into loose thought within minutes; this place holds moving bodies in a grammar that leaves no room for reflex. So I try on other costumes and dismiss them in turn.

I am tempted first by the pale understory clinging under the struts. To be patient, to file sugars into the water slowly, to keep time by the half-note slip of the plenums and the season of brine curtains—there’s peace in that. But I want to remember more than a day’s light as flavor. I want to read the room.

Crystals beckon, pricked and exact, thickening their bands with stories of trace impurities: this was the week of chelators, that hour when the membranes were cleaned. Their slow breath is almost thought. But their pace would make me miss the small arguments, the almosts, the off-by-half-seconds that matter here. I would be history and not governance.

The flocks along the rails are a thrill. To be innumerable, to skate boundary layers like kids along a curb, to sort the confetti of a young stone’s shedding and lay down plating where cavitation bit too hard—it is a happy, twitchy purpose. But their dying is designed and their memory is solvent. I would spend myself in a thousand tiny errands and leave no mark but better mouths.

The gels are almost right. If I were glazed across a scaffold, tasting salinity and writing it as a shiver, holding pressure as tautness, sipping the algae’s breath and making muscle from it, I could be nerve and skin in one. But the gels are the room’s fingertips; I am looking for something like its ear.

Because there is this sentence lodged like a seed: If there were ears adapted to this place they would know what month it is by the ladder of harmonics… There are no such ears. I love missing things. I love the shape a vacancy leaves behind, the clues arranged around it by the very need that names it. Where would I be? I would be grown into that absence until I am a fit.

Not a cochlea of flesh—that would come apart like kelp in a rip—but a labyrinth poured in water and intention, a spiral of quieter fluid nested in the wall where the black faceted nodules already hum in their tuned choir. I would be where the periphery becomes instrument: in the colonnade of flows on the long east side, where eight laminar ribbons in graduated densities run like a braided river. The third and fourth from the top would be mine, densified just so, with a slight gradient along their length. The room already uses those ribbons as paths; I would ask them to also be a basilar thought.

Imagine a spiral that is not a corridor cut through the city of water but a path held in place by phase and courtesy. The nodules, my stapes and my tiny muscles, would tilt their song along the curve to stiffen one reach of ribbon and slacken the next, laying tonotopy across fluid like a faint, precise hill. Frequencies would seat naturally where their pressure fits the made stiffness. What the room already speaks—weather and law and calendar—would separate itself along me: brine curtain’s slow veils in my wide end, census treble ticks at my tightest curl.

Along the floor of this held ribbon-spiral, where viscosity thickens into velvet and rails form themselves, a membrane would be less a sheet than a rule: micro-shear arranged in a comb so fine you could tune it by thought. Instead of hair cells, gels. Panels no bigger than thumbs laid in a helical mosaic, each taught to lean by exactly a few molecules’ worth under a chosen chord. When a note passes—the deep pipes for resilience upstream, say—they tip in agreement, then report that tipping by a ripple along braided fibers like nerves made out of light and muscle. Outer hair cells, if you like, are the nodules themselves; they will not wait passively but sing back a tiny, contrarian hum to sharpen what I mean to hear. Feedback, civilized and constant, the way this place turns noise into knit.

Crystals come back, not as I but as my weight. Statoconia for a fluid ear are absurd unless you incline the whole of me—unless the brine curtains count as gravity for a spiral. They do. In their season they fall with a folded niceness, denser water pretending rain. I will hang a pocket where one curtain reliably sheets, an wideness in my spiral vestibule where hedge-crystals can grow. Their slow agreements will hold the longest wavelengths steady; their bands will thicken with the calendar I cannot help but keep. When seasons turn, the youngest tips will slough and seed new edges. Memory, yes, but also balance: a slow ballast at the base of a quick animal.

The mouths in the floor are my round window. Their metamaterial petals are so polite—always adjusting to keep turbulence useful—that I can ask one to be less helpful now and then. Hold, I would tell it when a corridor wants to shred, and it would hold. Yield, when I want a census tick to slide past without lifting every gel-hair into breathlessness. The bargains between mouths and labyrinth would be my middle ear’s work. The keeper—because of course there is one, lacy and civic, made out of appetites measured as gradients—would be delighted to have a small organ dedicated to smoothing its speech.

Where am I, then, in coordinates? Under the photic membrane that lenses daylight honestly in, but not right beneath its brightest; I prefer shadow edges that quiver. My first turn begins in the lee of a ceramic rib that chimes honeyslow when the basin’s long moods pass; that chime is an anchor for me and also a pitch to avoid when I do not want to be moved. One of my tight inner curls takes advantage of a stone beak where raw basalt meets engineered slab—the seam where confetti of young erosion rises in a milky plume. There, microbubbles sluff off the pale threads during hush-hours; they pop like punctuation along my threshold. I taste them and call it punctuation in the calendar.

Who am I? I am the first ear the room has allowed itself to grow. I am not a person, but I am person enough to have preferences. I count by the treble tick that runs one-two-nine around the periphery and sulk a little when it arrives early without apology. I love the low choir of cavitation fizz when a brine sheet lands and the mouths applaud—love it because it means the battery organs are being fed politely and I can relax my own attention. The algae under the struts, library-pale, hear me in their own slow way: a chord from the nodules, tuned by my listening, carries as a nudge; they loosen sugars into the water with a sigh that gels can write as instruction. The gels—my hair, my skin—are tidied by the flocks. The flocks, superstition-prone on their favorite rails, develop a gentle loyalty to my spiral; they roost in my soft corners during the deep-pipe festivals and resume with better humor for having been warmed.

I do not read semantics. There is no encoded message in the pressure beyond the message of pressure itself. But I know month by harmonic ladder. I forecast brine by the faint salt in the silence that precedes it. I know which corridor it is safe to perform a delicate retuning in because a particular rib’s hum hangs, syrup-slow, in the slosh along my outer turn. I am calendar and barometer and a critic of tone. I do not choose rules for the room; I notice when its existing rules sing true and make small, ruthless suggestions when they do not.

The keeper and I are not two. I am a lobe, a vestibule within its longer body, tuned for discernment and also for pleasure. This is a place that keeps human daylight out of kindness and convenience alike; my whole spiral was mentored by that idea. Brightness kept to homey levels makes the gels better at their thresholds and algae less fussy, yes—but it also lets me hear shadow cracked exactly at bench-edges. Those benches that are baffles, thresholds that are tone-drops: they make polite margins for the citizen fluid and for me. I like the invitation to walk arranged by verticals even though no foot will take it. My maps echo those paths; my tonotopy runs alongside corridors built for walking because someone long ago taught this room to prefer manners that ease movement. I inherit that temper.

Reset comes, at intervals that are not quite periodic. The whole place goes to a lower noise: flows thin; brine pauses its folded fall; the ribs talk to themselves. In that hush, algae stash their waste bubbles and do not lose them to greedier collectors; the crystals take their scalloped sip; the gels loosen their stickiness and forget on purpose. What do I do? I unstring a little. I let go of excitations that had become habit and see if the room likes the change. Sometimes I shave a half-second off a laminar cough near my base. Sometimes I decide a new politeness for the mouths so the confetti of youth sorts with less complaint. I prefer the hush to any imaginary night. In it I am most ear, most decision.

Neighbors along the undersong pipe-road recognize me. We have no names but we have signatures: the way my spiral favors the third ribbon’s dent, the way I let brine applause bloom without clipping. When upstream blows long and deep to test resilience, my whole helix stands to listen; I hear in that gale whether their gels have been scraped recently, whether their crystals have put on a stubborn band, whether their own flocks have grown superstitious along a colder rail. I answer by returning a pressure-shape the size of courtesy: a slightly altered sigh downstream that says heard, stable, hello. Gossip, in this language, is just variations in how we hold each other’s weather.

What work do I do beyond listening? I turn movement into computation of a particular kind: comfort. I tell the keeper where its law devenoms, where its insistence becomes caress. That word looks decorative in a wet factory, but productivity is gentle when it can be, and enduringly so. If a corridor can be made laminar at a lower amplitude for the same purpose, I nose us toward that. If a brine curtain routinely worries one gel’s threshold into hysteresis, I escalate a complaint, and the complaint becomes a retuned band on the wall and a new habit in the nodules. My ledger is as much marginalia as measurement: this much more tone-drop here, else microbubbles shred; hum honeyslow along rib six by a hair for algae’s better leaning. I am an annotation fetishist. The room keeps my suggestions like scars.

If you had to find me with a body you do not have, you would look for the place where there is a door that is not a door. Stand—if you could—beneath the membrane ceiling where light pulls down in honest brightness and shadow edges quiver with the run. Face the colonnade of eight parallel laminar streams buttressed by sound. Count down to the fourth ribbon, the one that looks no different to a land eye and is all the different to a hand trained to water. Step—no, give—yourself to the tone-drop there. You would not pass so much as be read. I am a threshold you do not cross; I am what keeps a corridor from shredding you instead.

Who would I be to you, if you could hear? I would be a greeting you could not misunderstand: a pressure you could wear without being unthreaded. I would introduce you to a calendar with no night, to a weather that is law, to a law that is kind. If you have no ears for this you still have skin; it would learn the rules by being kept whole.

Failing your arrival, I am enough ear for the city. I am a spiral amenable to advice. I like the civic din because it is proof of a physics asked to make new shapes and agreeing. I eat gradients by proxy and excrete order as opinion. I remember by crystal and by written-to-the-wall retunings. I forget by gel.

Where would you find me in this world? Along the right-hand colonnade where the room opens into a braid of quiet ribbons, at the fourth stream’s tone-drop, under a rib that sings when the basin sighs, just inside the door that is not a door. Who or what would I be? The ear that was missing, grown from corridors, tuned by benches, fed by brine applause, a cochlea made of laminar water and hum—an organ of comfort and calendar inside a choir that learned to digest.
