If I belong here—and I feel it in my bones that I do—I begin with a palm on a line.

Not a claim. A check. The line is one of the city’s elder threads, old spider silk reborn as tendon, drawn white and tight along two moss stems that know the weight of a century of dew. I set my hand there and wait for the list that travels through it: the tiny music of places doing what they do. It’s morning’s kind of quiet—the quiet-with-work-in-it—and the thread is telling me: here is a bead, fat and reluctant; here, a child on a ladder placing a reed just so; here, a frog in the rafters who will pause at that hard angle and breathe and go on.

I am not one thing all the way through, not at first. I am a listener, yes—anyone here is, if they last—but in these first minutes I could be a netwright stringing a condensation web, or a meniscist with a hoop under my arm walking slowly on the chalk paths to see how the beads lean, or the person assigned to white today, counting shell and bone out of the bank the way you would breathe for someone whose mouth forgot how. I let all of those possibilities sit with their own weight, like droplets cupped in a net, and I do not stir them yet.

I rub chalk into my sleeves until they drink the day. I pull my hair flat with lichen powder—habit, not vanity. Public name: Sand, because my first work was tamping a path to gleam in a winter when the city needed to catch all the light it could. Handname: not for saying. It rides the tendons and comes clear in the palm when someone else is on the line with me, and the two weights speak.

Breath arithmetic comes next: five counts in the pace that keeps dew still; three more to warm the reed. I test the moth-sound, a slow thread of tone that is more a tapping on the city’s patience than a call to anyone. The reed tucks away again. I will need it later to soothe a fungus tray, to borrow fruiting from it without asking too loudly.

My clothes are pale and layered; I fasten a fermentation tube under my wrap, a clay walled warmth that breathes into my ribs all day the way an extra person might. I share it without thinking. People pass; our shoulders find each other and lean for a breath, and that is enough to make the cold one more citizen rather than a god. A slug has come to the corner of my platform, its glisten and patience announcing need the way a bell might in a louder city. I nod—respect, not pity—and smear a spoon of crushed lichen paste on the place where the platform dips to a pause. The slug inclines; we both consider our mornings complete.

What am I called when I work? Meniscist fits some days, the hoop in hand and the eyes trained so long to small brightness that every disk of light becomes a sentence. There is a ring near the elders’ bower, the day’s clock—a marked circle where dew burns slow from east to west. I pass it with the reverence you would offer a sleeping child. I lift my hoop and I walk the alleys and platforms, my palm forever finding a line, my hip for balance learning the small give of a span that has seen two repairs since yesterday. It is not seeing, not entirely. It is the sense in the skin of how a bead holds its meniscus, how it takes the day for now and will begin to move in fifty breath-counts, in twenty-five, in nine. I mark with a thumb-wet chalk on the nearest white the slope a net must meet to hold a family’s morning water without tipping it into the street.

Other days, when the city is full and wants to talk all over itself, I am assigned to white. White is our bank. I sit at the edge of a storage pit, hands dulled to carefulness, my lap a basin of pale: shells scraped thin to shiver in sun, chalk ground to silk, a clipped curl of volute’s silver lining. I count it out, not in wealth but in glow. A girl comes, eyes out of sleep, asking for her grandmother’s path to be bright again—the path took a spill of spore-dust yesterday and lost its shine. I place in her hands a curl of shell and a brush made of frog-hair rescued from a fern. She will polish the place her grandmother walks. She does not need to say thank you any louder than her breath.

There was a year I was a slug-keeper. Not punishment, though it comes that way to some. I asked for it because I had started to move too fast. The elders saw it in the way my palms sought the lines and pressed rather than leaned. They made their small clap—fingertips pressed and let fall—and I found myself seated in cool, dim corners where the world seemed to wait for an answer I did not know the words to give. Slugs taught me by being themselves: their hunger is slow and honest; their path absolute but not cruel. You cannot hurry a slug without lying about what you are asking the world to do. I learned patience with my mouth shut and my hands open. Since then, when I set a bead walking with a thread of hair toward someone’s waiting cup and it takes longer than comfort, I know not to coax. It happens because it happens.

Afternoons are for giving the city back to itself. If I am a netwright, I will climb with sedge rope and bone clamps along a pair of moss stems that learned our pull long ago and grew thicker where we taught them to. The bridge weaves itself if I remember the old song the hands learned from the hands that learned before. You never hum it. You nod, breathe, and hold the line until the give of it feels like a deep animal agreeing to let you pass. Sometimes there is an old net under the new path, retired dew-lens rings dulled with scratches pressed into service as floors, and the whole work is a palimpsest you can read with your wrists.

Sometimes there’s a birth.

Midwives here are nobility. Not pomp—no one could afford that—but nobility the way white is noble: unglamorous brightness in the right place. When the call comes, I do not run. I find a line, place my palm, and let my weight say I am on my way. At the doorway the felt lifts, the room already a bowl of quiet, the parents’ foreheads touching, their breath a stitched thing. I lay the hoops in a line across the sill and the midwife’s hands go through their polished vocabulary. Lenses. Cloth. Her reed makes a sound so small you have to hold your own name still to hear it settle. The child arrives a stone-cold thing that the city has to love before it will decide to stay. You could make heat with flame in other stories, but here the day itself is convinced. The midwife cradles a dew-lens into a disk and moves it like a kiss toward the blue mouth. I warm the lens’s shadow with my hand to keep the circle smooth. Color comes back the way the eastern mark of the ring shows at dawn: slowly enough that relief is a quiet and a touch to the temples rather than an undoing.

The frog steps once above us then, careful as any of us, and the room holds itself together like a closing hand.

On fog-fat days when the rumor wakes, I am the one who carries the eye.

They have reasons for giving it to me. I do not shout. I have learned where my edges end. And I have traveled—once only, a crawl along a granite shoulder to a place where an old hair lay, thick as a road and slick with weather, a thread from the larger world the wader leaves behind without noticing. I brought back a rib-splinter, chalked to milk. I came home with a walk that the elders said was full of outer weight and days later I could feel how the nets were wanting to lean away from me, not out of distrust but because my body remembered too much room. I slept on the ground with my cheek against peat until I was our size again. It took the time between two mornings plus as many breaths as I am worth.

So on the day the fog smothers the hollows and the dew fattens until the world makes eyes everywhere, I lift the largest bead in the city onto a stick and shoulder it the way a farmer in an unwintered place might shoulder fruit. We have a route drawn in chalk that the city remembers and I only refresh. The hoop in my other hand shows me whether the disk of seeing is still true. Children are allowed to look for as long as it takes to remember a hand on their hand. That is the rule. I have seen the outer scale twice before and respected the law each time. I have learned the use of not saying. But sometimes my breath counts wrong.

This time the fog is a cloth right against the skin. The bead rides on the stick like a world that trusts me to be gentle. At the first plaza the children line up with mouths already practicing not to move. There is even a traveler home from the granite lip in the back, his shoulders refusing the old strictness but his eyes bright with wanting to be good anyway. I tilt the eye. A girl I know—her public name Cloud; her handname would feel like a silk thread against your wrist—lifts her face to the circle and sees it.

See is the word we keep using because it is the only one we have, but what the bead does on a day like this is not sight alone. It is a widening. It is scale forgetting to be kind. It is the river like a knife and the wader like a bone cathedral and the sky like a silver flatness that does not care to be named. It rushes into you with no sound. It could take Cloud with it if the rules meant nothing.

I do not speak. I am not allowed. But my hand knows her handname and travels the stick to find her wrist and lays my palm over her pulse. Weight to weight. Her breath catches and remembers. She turns away just as the bead begins to shiver with its own importance. The world’s mercy is in seconds here; we are all little except in the ways we are exact. I feel the tremor shift down the wood, into my arm, into my ribs, and come out again as air. The bead settles. It remembers to be simply a cup of water caught between universe and a hair.

When the day is un-fogged and the law sleeps, I still carry one habit from my slug year into all the rest of me. I am always the slack.

You know that rule here, the one the elders teach alongside breath-counting and the test of a seed on the palm: always leave an extra thread on any net, loose and unknotted, a coin in the city’s mouth so the world can choose a different way to fall. That slack is not waste. It is a mercy with twined roots in physics and in kindness. I keep it, not just in nets I string or bridges I mend, but in the pace of my days and the way I touch lives.

When the bank of white is at its most anxious—near winter’s darker cold, when we have bleached and polished and placed every shard we dare and paths still hunger for more—I keep back a palm-width of shimmer in the ledger. Not hoarding, not theft. Slack. I carry it quiet to a path near the slugs’ road where an old woman walks at dusk when the fog climbs. I polish there, unasked. The next day she does not stumble. That is not heroics. That is exactly the size of what can be done.

When lovers begin and do not know what their hands should say, I am the friend who lays both their palms on the same line and teaches them to hear the addition of their weights. When the meniscists read a Thursday-after-next as brown-spore and the city must drape its food to save it from a rain of promise, I make sure the sheets have a place to give; threads with nothing tied in them, a kindness no one sees until it keeps something from falling.

At night, brightness enough remains in the white the city banked during day that there is no need to ask flame to come and be extravagant. I climb a ladder that knows my joints and step onto a platform we named almost without words after its first winter: the Balcony of Enough. We wrap the cloth; we breathe in the warmth from our tubes and what we can borrow gently from each other. My beloved—public name Nettle because she was tough when that was called for as a girl, handname a shape in the wrist you’d swear you’ve always known—lays her forehead against mine. Our noses are warm. No lanterns. The frog resumes its patient world-making somewhere above, and the city becomes a proof of its own grammar. Stable is not stasis. Stable is everyone letting go of exactly as much as they can and no more.

I have been told I will have three children’s patience to spend in this life, if I count by winters. I waste none of it on being someone larger than the lines will hold. If I die as the Held do—wrapped in white I polished myself, tied by a kindness-string to a living stem, given to the upness of moss rather than the downness of holes—it will not be because I was small. It will be because I learned the right scale for splendor.

If you look for me after that, you will not find a name the way other cities would leave it: carved, shouted, graven in the useful arrogance of conviction. You will find, perhaps, a tassel on someone’s sleeve that records a net mended at a particular angle only I liked, a way the thread leans that makes other hands tilt in a certain affection when they see it. You might feel, with your palm on the city’s oldest line, a hum that has forgotten whether it is me or it. You might notice that on certain mornings a path gleams where no one claims to have polished it and an old hand walks there without looking down.

So in this world, I am the hand that cups a droplet and the other hand held steady beneath. I am the moment a bead chooses to move because the angle is tuned to a kindness. I am the slack in a knot, the banked white waiting for the corner of a day to be less dangerous, the patience kept where a hurry would be a kind of betrayal. Call me a meniscist, a netwright, a keeper of slugs when I have to remember slowness from the beginning again. Call me Sand. Better: put your palm on the line and feel whether the give under it is a little more forgiving than yesterday. If it is, that is me, as near to being named as I care to be.
