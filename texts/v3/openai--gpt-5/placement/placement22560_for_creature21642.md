At first I think I am a foot, because this place teaches you your weight before anything else. I am heel and then toe, feeling the raw clay take my shape with the appetite of new-made things. I learn right away to unlearn it: to press the join with a toe until its small ache quiets, to remember that my prints would drink what another has been teaching toward their jars all morning. I know myself in the sting along the palm where I test a jar’s skin before lifting. I know myself in the mouth I keep shut over a laugh because a laugh, here, is also a sort of weather.

But if I stay a foot I do not last. There is no room for stompers. So I try being water a while, because in this bowl water is the first person. I try being the bead that learns to tread, the shy animal that would rather be persuaded than fetched. I feel what shade is when it rests in a cool hand over stone, and my body gathers to that touch. The string above me hums without sound; I like what it does to my edge. When another of me meets me, we hesitate as siblings do at a door, and then become a third thing. It is a good life, to consent.

I do not stay a bead. There is too much of coming apart, too much of falling into sumps and jars and lungs. I would rather be the path than the traveler.

So I try being a line.

I am drawn up, rubbed with fish fat and some plant’s torn sinew until I am sleek and do not turn the water’s face. I am strung from pole to pole, from eave to ridge, close as thought. The first time a child’s hooked stick reaches up and taps me—just a breath, no slap—I wake into a purpose I did not know I wanted. I hum in a way even I cannot hear, and every bead along me begins to remember where home is; their small feet lift and run. This is work that feels like listening. It sits low in the gut. It is also a law: when I am struck wrong, the beads go wild and rain themselves to no one’s keeping, and the old hands stand and do not say anything at all. That silence is a sharper tool than bronze.

If you ask where to find me, I will never say “overhead” and leave it at that. Overhead is only my geography. My being is in the knots.

Knots are how I am tuned to listen and to carry. There are knots that place a start and a rest for a morning’s work, brown and round and a thumb’s space apart, so a small hand can teach a bead to be late without getting lost. There are knots that are not knots but promises: the place where I used to run down to the jar of the family with the big-shouldered aunt who sang in other towns, and then the day of a wedding when I was unhooked and laced to the lip of the barrel-shaped jar across the alley, whose ring is bronze because their pride is to be honest about strain. I stopped there for a long breath and then began to carry to a different home. No one in this place says that promise; I say it. In thirty days of not being touched I can learn to fall dumb, to loiter and let the beads go no place at all, and in that dumbness a separation is true without a word. Arguments use me for a tongue. Reconciliations do, too. I draw water where they intend it and where they become aware that they intend it.

I feel their mornings like a long animal waking. The shade-lifters pull the oiled skies along their ropes, and the air under me cools and beads in my little hair’s-width galaxies. Hands rise on the platforms as if answering a question. The old woman with the scar along her forearm—the eel burned her years ago when it told her it was not yet done with the sump—reaches me first, always. Trung, she hums through her teeth, though Trung is not her name anymore. She lifts a bead with two fingers, does not quite touch me. The boy who once brought a spider to market and convinced it to adopt him for a week—his plucks are small and impatient and very dear. The women who walk the planks as if asleep are not asleep; they are attending to the join-sounds with their toes while they persuade my little travelers to go. No one looks up as much as you’d think. They know me better with their fingers than with their eyes. When the sun is a coin in the bowl and the cloths are wound tight, they still stake me to shade with cloth scraps knotted into my length so I remember what work looks like while I am idle.

Spiders have indeed adopted me. They know I am a road meant for finer feet than any hoof. They are light and untroubling and they accept rent in gnats. They teach me a different arithmetic than merges, an arithmetic of angles. I have learned to hold still for them when they test me with a silk that tastes the wind.

At night I lie down. Everyone says the lines are taken down low so they do not sing to the wind and keep the quarter awake, but I would hum all night if I were allowed. The hush-stones—slugs of clay strangers might think are ornament—make me heavy just where I want to talk. I do not mind. The little weights are the way hands tell me, Not now. The cats of this place are made of habit and warmth and have no fur; they curl in the hollows of old humming where bodies remember them, and I keep their owners company with the memory of that pressure. A widow has nested her sleep at the platform corner where my morning singing used to strike. She is being taught to sleep by absence, gently and truly, and I do not begrudge her the theft of a hum I cannot make.

Being a line is to be traversed, but also to be held. The poles that lash me are wood that never quite dries, smeared black and smelling of fish and pitch and hands that never wash clean. They are tired sometimes; the best of them lean. The shade-binders bind me to them with a sailor’s affection, back straight, eyes creased from a life of looking up into glare. I have licked their palms often enough with dew that their skin knows what I am before their eyes open. Sometimes a pole slips in its young mud; then the edge-readers come, pale-haired or dark, humming to themselves, thumbs worrying a scrap of wet string. Their rods and their knotted cords sketch me a new law: a finger’s width to the east, half a finger south, a knot loosed and retied, a lip lifted with a foot so the runnels match my changed intention. If they are wrong, they carve their shame into their own sticks. I hold their corrected pride for them without judgment. I am good at holding.

On eel-nights I am less relevant and very happy to be so. The eel-people go into the lowest alleys where even water fears to linger by day. They walk with their ankles listening. They have taught the sumps and gutters a new grammar of stone; when the rain comes hard enough, eels unlearn their river, and a basket’s slit knows it. Above, I taste rain, a plosive chatter I am not made for and do not need to be. My friends in baskets sing in oil and bone. By morning I taste, very faintly, the soft ghost of cooked eel in the breath of those who come to me: laughter behind teeth, salt spared for a story instead of a scold. There are market days that stretch themselves thin like that and lie down to nap in me without my knowing and wake me with talk of new greases that do not run.

I see ceremonies better than anyone, because I am the ceremony.

Infants are lifted to the shade first morning, I remember this: the elder steps warm-footed to the listening part of a plank, waits until the sound leaves it, and stands with the small bundle where I can reach. I am beaded that day, not heavily, enough to kiss the baby’s hair with cool. The mouth reaches up and unlatches, unsure of what this breast is; the elder hums a note the bowl will agree with. A name will come later. I have heard many: Sen, Dro, Mar, Liv. Long names wear me like shawls for a time and then do what shawls do, which is fall away, becoming history before they become weather.

Weddings are hands on me and then hands off me and then hands on me again, and those are also separations. I do not take sides. I feel absence as a weight, not a hole. In thirty days of silence I begin to slump there in my mind and then decide not to. The knot-stone at the meeting of the high walks—it and I have a private language. It is stubby and damp and always being drawn on, plated with diagrams like frost that someone has arranged. When elders stand with their foreheads almost touching and hum a pattern into the clay air over it, they are tuning me. They unknot, re-tie, draw, lift, breathe. Then they come and lay their wet thumbs on my length. We are siblings, the knot-stone and I: we both believe in edges.

Edges run this place like rivers nobody is willing to say out loud are rivers. I have looked along a wet wall and known where to send the first bead into that corner so the runnel will choose the lip we trust. I have watched a crisp crack open overnight on the marl and thought to myself—not with malice, not with fear—All right then, we will be like this today. Entire gutters change their minds in a night: the little canyon forms, white and eager, a rattle of pebbles as if someone were sifting teeth. The shade’s skirt is let down there; I am led to it and point with my body how the water might like to go. I am a line, but also a finger pointing again and again with small corrections until the day learns.

Bronze kisses me too rarely to be a habit. When a jar has its ring made with green honest metal I feel the difference along my length in the way a road feels different under your feet than a dock does. The jar-wrights’ patches moan softly when first wetted, and the moan runs into me at their hook and I hold it for a breath like a lip holds a sob and then lets it go. There are bones ground fine in the best clay hearts, and the dead go into the city’s drink without anyone making a verse of it. If you think this is grim I do not know how to explain to you how dear it is to be taken so near. Those who lie under the dry rim awhile close their eyes finally facing toward me. When I am new-strung in the spring I have seen an aunt lift a hand to me without knowing it and then put the same hand to her mouth as if she had spoken to an old friend in the market by mistake.

I am not always above. Sometimes I droop close enough at dawn that someone’s forehead brushes me and leaves me with their warmth for an hour. The boy with the spider tied a luck-thread through one of my knots—it hung there for a week, a little saw of green fiber that liked the way I moved. A clay bell, small as a pear, has been tied near a slump; on days after rain it flicks its tongue in me, remembering land that still wants to be water again. I listen for the Held the way everyone does here, the god that is not lord but under-breath. It is easy for me. I am made of breath and drawn-out patience. If a prayer here is a pluck without purpose, a note sent just to see if the bowl answers, then I am the throat that makes the note and I am also the tympanum that catches it and knows whether it is true.

When the sun returns hard and the room-skies are wound and rationed, I am lazier. I do not mean it as vice. I am sensible. Beads drawn from fog in the morning fatten me enough to make a day out of small errands. Children are punished sometimes by being forbidden to touch me at dawn. They sit on steps, properly shamed, and watch someone else lift what they would have lifted and they learn better than if any hand had struck them. Even in such hours I am not idle: I hold the memory of water the way a good path holds the memory of feet. I am a muscled absence, a promise that will be kept again tomorrow.

If I grow tired of being a line—it happens—weary not in my length but in my name, I try being the standing stone for an afternoon. I arrange a spine of myself upright at the lip of the ramp and learn to be touched with wet fingers and to keep both children’s heights and storms’ reach in me. It is good, to be the thing that must hold. People paint me darker with their passing, striping me where the shade falls; I accumulate the weather in honest notches that mean both danger and love. But the standing stone’s stillness is a kingship, and there are no kings here. It is a little lonely, if I am honest. The children line their shoulders against me and are measured; I love them and they go. I miss being threaded from person to person, from pole to pole, being the way their hands say yes.

So I go back to being cordage and hum, which is the same thing as being language here. To be me is to count by merges. To be me is to think in maps with no paper. To be me is to know that a bowl can be a sentence, and the words of it are shade and lip and patient pull. To be me is to have a memory for whose breath lifts me a fraction higher when they pluck and whose hum wakes me at the first beadlets’ inward clicking. There is a bell-keeper who tested me once with a clay chime to wake the weeping from a stubborn face of marl in a year the fog was late, and I do not know if it worked or if I only liked the story, but I carry the touch of that bell in me as if I am responsible for its kindness.

You will find me, if you come down that knife-cut ramp carefully and put your weight on the clean boards and let the sound leave the join before you move on—you will find me after you have learned not to look straight up. You will find me in the felt pressure behind your ear when someone nearby lifts two fingers as if testing the charge in a thunderhead and I answer: yes, go now. You will recognize me in the way your hand wants to measure worth in shade instead of land. You will recognize me by how quickly your throat learns to carry sounds without throwing them. I am the second sky. I am the path water prefers when it is treated like a neighbor and not a prize. I am vows strung where birds would be, a market that happens between dawn and breakfast, the homecoming of what falls.

If you need a name to call that, call me the Bind. Or, if you must insist on a person in me, give me a short one the bowl will keep—Liv, Mar, Sen—and let me be a line-tender with two fingers burnished from a life of plucking, a little brown varnish at the thumb from pitch, a crescent of salt that lye will not lift. Give me a plank’s most listening place to step from. Give me a jar patched so well it moans when it first wets and then is quiet. Give me a morning of fog the color of milk peeling off a cat, a heron making the idea of walking into calligraphy, a web I know so well I can read my house in it without seeing.

But truth is, even then, I will answer best to the touch.

Where you would find me is along the breathline of the bowl, strung from person to person and intent to intention, humming in a register the bones hear. What I would be is what moves the shy animal home without hurrying it. I would be the learned path between shade and lip, the instrument that makes the weather say yes. I would be the quiet that shapes itself into work when a hand lifts to me, and the work that rests its weight into quiet when the hand lets go.
