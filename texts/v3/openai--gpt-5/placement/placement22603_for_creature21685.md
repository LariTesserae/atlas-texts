At first I would arrive as a habit and not a person: a way of lifting one boot and setting it down so cloth does not slap; a hand checking a white plane for slack in the only light the mountain forgives at that hour. You would not find me until your eyes settled into the city’s alphabet—until a dull green in a crystal face taught you to look slowly, until a flag’s small grammar made sense in your ribs. Then you would see me as a pale smudge moving along a shaded wall before the sun has chosen that wall for its day.

I live where stone decides to be cooler than its neighbors. That thin edge is my home range. I take an old chalk bag and a soft brush and I walk the faces in the first blue, when the black glass still keeps last night cupped behind it like a promise. I bend and press my ear to places the air doesn’t convincingly enter. I lay my palm and measure a breath’s worth of difference and I draw my line, not straight, never straight, but according to how the mountain thinks about heat that morning.

By love and lineage I am Kio. By work and second naming I am Line-of-Cool.

If you wanted me, you would look for a person with grey on their hands and their neck wrapped against the unhelpful parts of light. I mark channels shallow as a thumbnail, along which later a bead may astonish itself into being a thread. I don’t own a loud voice. I keep a small tin of shell powder for whitening a shade that has gotten tired of its job. I carry cloths for unexpected sun and a spool of patience for unexpected wind. The rules taste like manners on my tongue: keep your weight and words smaller than a breath; lay white between dark and sun; do not make a thing do what it isn’t ready to want.

I was named twice the day I drew a line that made a stingy cistern generous. That was a year of short rains. The bead that morning hesitated at a lip we had all walked past, stubborn as a thought you can’t persuade into speech. I scrubbed the edge clean and cut it just shy of vertical, hardly anything. The bead fattened, surrendered, ran. It found the small groove under itself and then the shallow runnel and then the white trough that refuses morning. People farther along began to speak softly in their hands. Someone laughed with their shoulders. The old Isotherm-Minder, who had been carrying the weight of our bad weeks in his leaning, set it down and called me over when it was dark. He tapped his two fingers to my breastbone and gave me the second name. After that, when flags on the far face ask Where will the day be allowed to cool? a reply comes from my side as much as from anyone’s.

I am not only chalk. Most of a life here is a braid.

At noon I check the white gutters that run beside the rails, where dew’s night-caught sleep turns to thin rivers if you have been kind to their edges. I wipe the black tiles clean as if I were cleaning my own thoughts, lay them out to drink the thoughtless cold of stars, and I gather them in before the first warmth remembers its job. The tiles sweat into my troughs; I watch the beads thicken their skins. Children come to stand under Sister’s Breath and count blinks until they are wet; they practice later in the street with the saucers and the ropes, hands learning what not to begin.

In the afternoons, when heat is steady and not quarrelsome, I climb a span and kneel under a sickle hood to hear if the brine works has started to talk too much. The Dwellers-in-Noise are never allowed to be alone for long, and the day someone is tired I trade with them. You learn to hear the factory the way you hear a cat breathing on your shoulder: not with ears, with something kinder that lives nearer your bones. I roll a little felt into a seam and the machine remembers its gentleness. Its breath goes back to being a habit, which is all we ever ask of anything heavy.

On the rails I am a passenger of patience. The drivers sit like forgiveness, and we talk in slotted light across distances that would make a voice into a reckoning. Once, one winter, I held the Counterweighter’s book while he took sick and slept in a wool nest by a warm pipe. I learned how to sit by a window with a cup and how to stand at exactly the right time so that a room-big carriage on a face far away did not begin to love the idea of falling. No one saw me put my hand to that small lever. That was correct. The mountain noticed in its own way; a chord down in the deep went on thinking, untroubled.

Evenings, the cloth holds the day’s heat until our talk has somewhere soft to lie down. We gather by whatever cistern has won the day, and we use voices because the air will forgive us for it. Children tap lullabies into the smalls of backs. Someone paints a black circle on a white tile to show where noon stood this month. Old ones tap the white line of the high water with knuckles, the way a farmer somewhere else might measure a child against a wall. Arguments go into later and come back as careful words with their hats in their hands.

Sometimes, rarely, I am visited by the creatures that have no hurry. Paper fins slip past on an updraft, mistakes of tarp until one tilts and an eye finds you and makes you blush for seeing it. I keep a wet palm when I can, and it sometimes kisses the water off and goes on, having made of me a better vessel for wonder than for talk. In the cistern weeks, when the small white worms suit up and carve screws into seashells in the dark, I bring a lamp and a respectful face and leave it there as an offering: light for their work, not for mine. On a day counted on fingers each year, heat gives birth to largeness. A jointed length of black glass climbs from a vent, chooses a white stripe like an altar, and lays itself to cool. I have watched it breathe twice. I have never counted more. I have learned to carry home the kind of silence that knows what to say when it is time not to say.

There are cats in my house and a jar of last year’s fluted luck on a shelf, turned to the wall because my partner believes different kinds of tenderness have to be separated or they make a noise against one another and wake the stone. We sleep under the slow white that stays warm until midnight. When storms come thin and exact—the way they do here, with a prelude in the fur of cloth citywide—I tie my chalk bag around my waist and go where the lines I drew were meant to hold. There is a joy that is almost a ceremony in adding cup to cup to runnel to cistern and hearing everything fit itself to belonging. After, we walk the faces and count the tiny scallops, the spoon-bowls where drops were patient with their work. The rock has been corrected, not wounded. I touch the place and remember my own new face on the crystal in a deep cut. We keep birthdays that come in angles and light.

I walk with the pilots sometimes when air has a moral lift to it above a certain corner. They unfold their fabric birds and take the day’s grammar into the wrist. They come down with lampwork reports: a lift-line like a thought you can finish, a crack beginning to think itself into narrative and needing a small discouragement. I am there with chalk and a white tile and the part of my body that belongs to persistence.

You would not find me on a map. You would find me on the day’s map of heat. I am the chalk logic slipped along a shaded plane, the hand that erases itself responsibly when the mountain changes its mind. I am the person you pass on a gangway who lifts a chin toward a black tile and a white trough and a slim morning—later, you will know I was asking if your corner had agreed to drink. I am smaller than my echo and happier for it. My work is to persuade a thing to move in the time that won’t break it. My leisure is to lie with my head to the wall and listen to time thinking until it forgets itself enough to sound like comfort.

On still days there is easily room for listening, and it turns out that is where I live: in the room. Between the hard young glass and the white that forgives it. Between a bead and a thread. Between someone’s breath and the bridge’s first idea of singing. Call that a house. Call it Pause, or Lean, or Catch. If you needed me, you would put your palm on the cool and wait for a reply, and the reply would be a faint chalk arrow appearing where you hadn’t looked yet, offering the day a way to belong to water. That would be me. That would be enough.
