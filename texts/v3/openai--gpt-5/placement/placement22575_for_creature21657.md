At first I think I want to be weightless.

I want to be one of the schools—blue-green dragonflies knitting themselves along an edge, turning all at once like a thought so clean it leaves no wake. I want a thousand small eyes and a single urge, a lane that remembers me. I want to feel the pressure fold along my armor as a gust shoulders past and to answer it with nothing but angle.

Then I want to be water. I want the way morning makes of myself a gathering; I want to bead on a leaf’s hair and grow until I tremble and drop from a tip into a waiting mouth and, because I am small, I want to matter. I want to lie black and patient in a cup with sugar-sheen on top and honest rot underneath and hold a city up in my skin: shrimp like glassed commas flicking across my surface when a shadow falls, ring-worms writing poems around my lip.

On afternoons I think I am resin. I think I am the slow, exact patience that becomes a seam, the memory that shines when scrubbed by wind for a lifetime. I want to mend with the day’s heat and set with evening, to give off that clean sweetness when warmed, to be the thing a woman takes with both hands at sunset and coaxes without gluing herself to death.

Sometimes, briefly, I want sound. I want to be one of the long harps flung between anchors, a great cable with two notes in it, swollen with history, humming because air exists. I want to sing myself thin in storm and be tuned in the hush by careful hands and a ring of weight placed just so. I want to feel the touch of a boy’s palm and know he is measuring me for danger the way a sailor knocks a plank for rot.

On green weeks, when I remember how pollen falls like a swallowed sun, I want to be noise entire, a continent of it—seed butting seed, wings scything the heated air, pods clapping, leaves whistling, resin-bells speaking with the long vowels of thunder. I want to be the thing that makes predators hunt by smell and flash and gives the babies a reason to be lashed into quiet with their mad, small fists.

But when I stop making of myself things that do not have to tie knots or feed the old or count the dead, when I put my hand down on the wood and feel the slow movement in the deep, what I am finds me.

I am an ear.

They call me Ear-of-Harp when they need to be formal, and sometimes Leaf-Cone because of the little stiffened horn I hook to my head when the noon updrafts begin to stretch the place taut and it is my work to listen. In the morning, before the fog even makes its move toward leaf and hair, I walk the lines with a rope over my shoulder, the way an old swimmer always wants a towel even in another country. I do not go to the places where ribs are young; I go to the stayed-for-good shapes, the old arcs that have forgotten bark and the catenary bridges that learned to survive by giving just enough. That is where songs begin to go wrong when they go wrong—where history meets hurry.

I was not born with it. I was born a child with a harness belly-broad and two loops for anchoring, a forehead with resin in the hair for the day’s grip, a grandmother who made me recite failures while I crossed: the names of bridges and the days men forgot to double-listen and the time the air was a certain green and a pod said goodbye and took two cousins and most of a deer-moss flat with it. I learned what melon-smell means and how far to step back when my shadow broke on a gap I had thought was gossamer instead of edge. My first coat was not a coat but a habit: thumb here for anchor, tooth here for patience.

Like everyone else I herded bees when I was strong and too hungry to think of anything but sugar and noise. Blue flowers on stakes like little flags from the wet worlds below, comb so fat you had to brace and lean to get it to give. The small scars on my forearms shine when I turn my hands palms up in the lantern-fungus, ghost-lattices from hot resin afternoons when we coaxed silk to take a mold and learned to keep our breath out of it. I have the taste of old nectar in the back of my throat that keeps company with me even when I am watching for hawk-shadows.

I tried to be Hand-of-Rib. There was a season when I could not stop seeing the future of a line in a year, when every knot bloomed wrong in my sight and every sling hung like a question. I made good rope. I could lay in a splice that would sing a little lower under load so the rib would not. I could name the place a pin would split and cut my thumb on it to show a cousin when she thought I was being too careful. But rope does not ask for what I ask. Rope wants to be more than itself; my ear wanted to take things away—or to add them until they stopped wanting to sing themselves into pieces.

So I apprenticed. My teacher was called Mother-of-Chime, because she greeted that wind with her cheek every time it came across a certain span, and it repaid her by not lying. She wore three old rings of resin on a thong because they were the weights that had once saved a bridge in a green week, and she let us, the small ones, touch them when we had been brave in the right way—not in the jumping way, but in the way of stopping before a good run when the line under your toes said not today. She taught me to use the cone at my ear not as a horn for hearing but as an angle-measurer: a shallow curve for Knife-wind, a deeper cup for Lamb, the sharp edge turned toward the north for Backhand. She taught me to lay my palm along the outer callus of my own foot on a rib and feel into the bone of the place, the way a doctor in a stone world listens to your heart through a wall.

Mostly the work is small, as all the honest work here is small. A ring of resin around a cable to bring its note down a half-tone before a certain month. A handful of creepers cut back a handwidth here and left to grow wild there to make a baffle the wind will taste and take elsewhere. A dead palm-slat pinned just out of the way of a rattle that has been building like a rumor. A cup weighted with three stones from a trade that started as a song in a kite-string and ended as a dark-eyed boy with a laugh that smelled of other places climbing up with his ribs. People think Ear-of-Harp is drama because they have heard of subtraction—the sound a bridge makes when a hundred years lets go. It is almost never that. It is almost always this: you put weight down in a place no one will look, and the day after a storm the span is there like a table the world set out for you again.

Once, to be fair, there was the subtraction. We heard it not as a roar but as a breath pulled in from every throat at once, as if all the harps had surprised themselves with their own bodies. There had been a wrong song under the noises for months, a shiver higher than children’s laughter, a thread running its own argument across three ribs that had no business with each other. We could not find the oath it had broken. We walked and knocked and listened to the tick come up under the night like frogs in rain, and the wind would not call itself by any name we knew. We were careful and wrong, and when the dry arc went it did not go alone; it took four good hollows and a long run of rope that had a story already. We tied down the women and children with rage and gentleness, rode the dust and the pollen and the falling for a long, blind minute, and then we wept like people with bodies when we could get our breath back. For a month afterward I could not put my ear to anything with a voice and trust it. I went to the bats and sat underneath them and asked my heart to give up the hammer. They obliged me with stitches of quiet across the stars.

There is temple work in what I do, the same way there is temple work in a glider-snake thrown and caught across a clean span. Sometimes you stand at an edge when the sea has shouldered a proper storm up into the high and you feel the place go into its other gait—rushing stairs, screaming hair, the high bells booming not for death but for living. The Stormers lean like they are married to gusts; we behind them feed their hands. The cup-keepers shout their brief shouts under the noise with their heads nearly inside the lilies to hear the sour that means a shedding in the water, and the rope-makers stand with their teeth on knots because they need a third hand and the world will not learn gentleness today. It is a joy, that kind of work. It is the kind where your grandmother’s voice of failures lives in your mouth and you bring it out and it runs across a bridge ahead of you like a lizard and comes back with a tail.

On the quiet days I do the other kind of listening. I lie along the oldest ribs and feel the day come off them tick by tick as the sun leaves. I sit with elders whose fingers know knots the way my ear knows Backhand from Mother and let them tell me about sap as if it were a neighbor. Children come and we put their small calluses along a place where resin has turned to glass that remembers being sticky and we say: feel that promise. They do not have to know what it pertained to; it is enough that they feel it. Promise is a temperature here as much as a word.

I married by throwing a line. We tied a first bridge in the presence of a singer of spans and gave it the failure-name from each of our houses. From his side: the year of the green air when a pod took his sister’s teeth and made her bite the wind for a living and she loved it until the last day. From mine: the subtraction. We stitched a low wall for our winter out of two old slings patched with stories you could read by thumbnail. Our kitchen is a hanging platform over a cup that has had our laughter in it. At dusk it is sandalwood and clean sugar and the dark breath of bats. Our children’s first coats are harnesses. Their first game is dew. You spin a net even if the season has been mean and you go thirsty or you drink, and either way you learn how much of you is luck and how much of you is rope.

We trade when the wind writes to us in kites. A green-world family with stone in their hands and scents on their clothes like a new animal sends a line up that sings when they pull and we answer with oils and feathers we have taught to be colors the world did not ask for. Once I went down, wearing a coat sewn from glider-snake leathers that had slept a long time and would not betray me. It took two days to go down by slip and step and the laughter of cousins who called me Leaf-Cone and asked if I meant to teach the lowlands our better jokes. The stone tasted of iron and heat and weight. When I came back my grandmother tapped me on the chest with a pod bone and said: now you know how little we are, and I wept, not with shame, with relief.

If you meet me you will think I have nothing in my hands. It is true. In a place this tuned, you do not carry what you will not need before noon. My hair is plaited until it will not lift even in backhand gusts; there is seedglass in it so it swings like a tide-pool, not like smoke. My arms wear leaf and hide when the harps go hot; my feet are wide; my outer edge is more knowing than my eyes. My voice lives low because the wind steals the top of it for its own songs. I speak with my hands and a chin and the small bones of my face; I keep a rope of story-knots I can read with my thumb for companionship when I have to sit under a rib and count the beats between ticks because something is deciding whether to be a problem or a future.

I am not brave in the way leap-stories like to make you think of bravery. My courage is weight in the right place. Sometimes that is my own weight: myself as a ring of resin placed upon a line so it will not sing itself thin for a decade more. Sometimes it is a hand on a cousin’s short harness to keep her from saving face at the cost of the lesson. Once it was the angle of my head against a wind I had not met before and had only learned by smell—the iron sour that comes right before a sea-storm when it is going to be generous and cruel at once. I angled my cone; I put my palm on the rib; I said to the air with my cheek in the old way: Mother, if you can hear me, I am listening. It listened back.

You asked where I would find myself. I find myself wherever the wood speaks in its inside voice. Dawn, on a curve with old sap beating in it like a slow animal, waiting for the first beads to fall in the cup we tend that has seen three of our dead and all of our children’s laughter. Noon, on a diagonal with my toes turned out, my ear-cone to Knife, measuring a hum into a safe note with a ring that was a bracelet on my lover’s arm when we were children. Sunset, in the amber rooms the fungi make for us, rubbing a rope between a thumb and a forefinger, telling a girl about the day you smell melon and do not drink, about the time an old man named his bridge for pride and was taken whole, about why you speak to your shadow like a brother and listen twice and move once.

And who would I be? I am the pause between gusts. I am the hand that quiets a string and makes a home possible on a sound. I am one of those who, when the flowering comes and the air turns to hail you can eat, lashes her children down and weaves herself into the under-wood and lets a week of god-noise pass through her bones without adding to it. I am a small animal on a living ruin that chose long ago not to fall. I am an ear in a country that is an instrument, and I am at home.
