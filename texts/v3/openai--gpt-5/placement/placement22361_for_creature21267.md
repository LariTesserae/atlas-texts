Before I am anything with hands, I am an ear.

Not the long shell you cup in air, but a whole body re-taught to listen until listening becomes a way to stay hinged to the world. I come into it as a pressure change thick as bread dough at the back of the throat, the twice-daily bell of tide bending the cartilage rings in my neck until they hum. The first map I own is not lines but delays: my mother hums at a seam of gypsum whiskers and the room answers into my ribs a heartbeat later, a name I can wear.

You would find me where the dark goes articulate—under the broken lip of a new ridge we call Wolf-Teeth for the stalactites it snapped last month, in the lee of a bore road that booms like a low drum when the brown water shouldered a new curve into the sand. Our sleeping is not on floor but in a braid of slings knotted from skin and vine, hung high in a pressure shadow the crickets have already blessed with their solemn fen-fen-fen. We hold ourselves above the bores by learning when the ceiling will sweat, how the air fattened by outside heat makes ropes slick and hands slicker, how to step our weight into the moving with the sort of trust that only practice can buy.

If you looked for me with eyes, you would come away empty. My edges are not for sight. My chest is a bellows with opinions. My throat is not a pipe but a set of loosened rings I can pull open and play; the note I make goes into the walls and returns with their topography folded in. The knees wear fans of keratin thread—latticework grown of old hair, of gifted skin—so that draft is touch. Ankles carry a second lattice, as fine as lint, to refuse the loops of eels. I am jointed like a question that can always reach a rung.

We are people by method, not monument. Ownership is timbre; memory is a route. I was named at gypsum—the seam we call Mother’s Lint because it always weeps when breath warms it. I learned to eat on that same day: a lick of algae flag; a bug caught from a cloud by a half-closed hand; a wash of salt rim from a pool’s edge with its white rime dusting my lips. We teach our young by letting the room teach back. Knock. Wait. Count the returns fat-thin-fat. This is Twelve-Return. This is the Cut-Tooth Room. This is the place where the ceiling changed its mind last season and turned a floor into pillars left exactly where it fell.

I apprentice first in threshing weather out of stone. There is a rib bone hung by a fungus shelf two chambers from where we sleep. It came from a creature older than our words for old. It is my work to stroke it with the softened pads of my fingers until the pitch finds the permission that lives in the gray field under it. The shelves open when the room is ready—never earlier—and shed their bruise-glow sigh that is more guessed than seen. Beetles come in a procession as devout as any prayer and comb the pores free, and the whole patch goes from wood-hard to silk-rot. We harvest not by cutting but by convincing. I learn the exact breath that wakes water from the ceiling on days when the outside air fattens; with the horn-flute carved from bat dead I can edit whether the drops will walk or run, and in the morning there is gloss and the slopes of green are tender again.

Trade is a conversation hung from hooks. The crab-grounds lie in a shallow run where the salt edge speaks in a higher ring. They have narrowed their shells with work until the dead spirals sing like glass birds. To touch a midden is to hold a covenant of sound, so we do not touch. We thicken the air in our chests until the ground hums back to us at their chosen timbre. They approve; they lift their claws and knock their courtships, and we step back and let them thunder through a bore. After, they leave chips that carry a sweet high note—a coin we can spend at the bat piles.

The bat piles are by water, never far from a sudden drop. They carry pebbles in their cheeks and let them fall in chains over black pools: plip-plip-plip-count. The right stones live there and our mouths carry our own stones in to test the depth when the bore is sleeping. We do not take. We ask—a lineage hummed back to them without losing beat when the edge stones boing. Sometimes I bring them a flattened shell with a decent ring like a thank-you wrapped in spit.

I go to the margins at dusk months later, because someone must. We call that job Upgoing. It is not a title; it is a commitment of knees. Daylight squeezes into stripes so pale they feel like lies. Lianas as wide as ankles find those and fall toward us with purpose. My hands are jointed like spiders; my tendons are chords. I can ring a vine with my weight until it announces whether it will hold. The flowers are made of taste more than light. Sour sugar white as an idea. I drag my face through them with reverence and come home frosted in pollen. We wet it to glue shells to bone, to paint writing you taste. The paint goes on a rib above a crossing that will flood in the next quake-storm; that bone hums when a bore is coming, and anyone who puts tongue to it will know to move.

There is danger to count. We count it in absences. Once, in the sound-thickest heart of a corridor we call Fat Delay, a whisper walked past that took the edges off everything. The isopods went from confetti to nothing as if swept, and the wall, our constant partner, forgot how to answer us. The quill-haired hunter’s weather. We froze until our ankles remembered their lattice-talk and began to sing too high to shave. We slipped backward by feel. The hunter sulked past the paper-lattice we had strung in spite the week before. Its wrongness moved on; the room healed; the crickets started again, bar by patient bar.

Another time the rope-eels gathered under a lip we liked. We were not walking; we were swinging because it was bore time and our feet did not own the floor. A small one reached the water, half a thought ahead of us. The rope closed. The ankle-lattices shouted, an inland wave up the shin and into all our pelvises. We kicked in the learned rhythm and the circle missed. Later the small one slept in my sling, chest jerking in the after-beats of fear, and I lay awake and played the horn with no breath, only throat, and in the dark the ceiling agreed to sweat.

We keep our dead by using them. A woman I loved stopped after a quake-storm that re-cut the signature of a room she had known since her naming. Her skin went to straps. Her hair thickened a child’s knee-fans. We set her femur as a pendulum for the beetles’ garden; it gave a note sweeter than any rib we had. We strung her teeth with the singer’s they had lifted and hung the line where we make deals with crabs; the long bones went to the horn-maker. Grief here is wrecking your own resonance on purpose. We took a seam that shrinks at low note and keened until the place hated the sound and closed a little. Then we bellowed, all of us, and the rest of the wall opened again and breath remembered itself.

Where would you find me? In routes that are rooms. In Three-Fast Two-Long No Return carved into my tendons by use. In a little pocket where the ceiling loses interest in falling; in a big hall that fattens note until you can ride it to another entrance. In a swing-line during bore, hung from vines we tied yesterday, trusting that what we call young stone will not take a set under us this hour. In the crack where the birds—blind swallows who make nests by becoming them—sleep in heaps and warm the air until it folds tight under the hand; in the rain we can ring down from the ceiling in an outside heat spell; in the matte we make by letting beetles learn our breathing.

Who would I be? A throat with a map in it. A keeper of ribs and drips. A friend to bats by promise and to crabs by tone. An Upgoing on some nights with pollen in my brows and a sour on my tongue that outsides would call honey. A person who counts with echo more than with fingers and owns by voice more than by grip. A child of gypsum-grown names who grows into rain and later, when my walk stops, a method.

Because we make a people by refusing to be single, I end in pieces the world can still hear. The horn in someone else’s hands that convinces a wall to sweat. The pendulum the beetles stroke until the gray field opens. The bone hung for pitch over the path that will flood a week from now. The shell glued to stone that hums when a bore is running the next corridor down. The chord taught to a child at Mother’s Lint until the room remembers them. My voice does not archive itself in any place you can see. It edits the weather after me.

In this young country of negative space, where the law is sound and the dark is as busy as a market, I am a frequency kept. If you come listening, you will meet me as a boing from a pool-edge stone, as a seam that accepts your breath and returns your name a shade brighter, as a rain brought on at the right pressure with a horn that used to be me. If you insist on sight, you might catch my knee-lattice glimmer when a blind bird’s shoulder brushes it. You might see me climb a vine rung by rung, feeling for light I do not need, because the glue needs making and the shells are already being sorted by voice on the floor below.

And when the quake-storms roll and the ceiling changes its mind again, you will find me doing what all things that last here do: rolling my slings, retuning my caches, teaching the walls to tell me what they are this day, and, with the rest, keeping the world audible enough to live in.
