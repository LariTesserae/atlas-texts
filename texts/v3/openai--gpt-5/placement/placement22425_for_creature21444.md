At first I try on the obvious bodies, because the edge is a wardrobe of urgencies and each coat looks right when you face the wind.

I am wing, for a breath. My arms are vanes and ragged fringes; I speak by showing my pale, and I believe in falling the way other animals believe in soil. The column is under my shoulder like a hand that means me well. A slow bank is a sentence; a sharp twist is a verb. I hold. I peel. I hammer a snail against the one bright stone that knows me. I have no use for sound but as weather to read; the mountain’s held note is the ground I stand on, the pressure in which my muscles think. My eye is a bead of smoke glass; I do not blink for long minutes because brightness is a law. The rock pans are mirrors held at the sun; I cut the scum with circles my mother showed me because we both were shown the same place in the same light. If I am this, I am serenity as a profession.

Then I am smaller, meaner, and nearer to the chessboard of stone. I am fur and a fold of skin that promises more wing than it delivers. I spring not to fly but to deny falling its right of way long enough for the next crack to take me. My nails are tools. I lick hoarfrost at a noon that is brilliant and still somehow contains ice; it makes my tongue ache and laugh at once. I drum my toes on the lip of a grike because the gaps in the mountain’s voice leave me a room to speak in. My ears close politely when the world insists. When a corvid barges into my den for the joke of eviction I leave because argument is a hobby for valleys, not for this rim. If I am this, I am joy in small cold acts and the satisfaction of crossings made honest.

I pull a lid of skin over my ears and become heavy-headed and necessary. My neck is bare so filth slides away. I feed in the lee where turbulence turns mistakes into meat and I am a correction written on a ledger kept in bones. When I eat I go deaf by choice because the world is unsafe to listen to while your head is where it is. When I open again, the chord falls through my skull like water down a stair, and I stand with my mouth open, letting the pressure equalize in a private piece of anatomy. I ride stink. I love it in the clean way that answers a need. If I am this, I am an ugliness trained to purpose so tightly it has its own kind of grace.

Pebble in beak, I am trouble for the sake of it. I wedge glossy stones out of algae-slicks that swifts have engineered and I move them two crevices left then three right and back again as if the mountain had invented an abacus only I can see. I do not have much patience for dialogue; my sentences are flares of wing stolen from the kites, badly imitated at first, better over time. I know every pan that stays wet three days in four. If I am this, I am mischief as metabolism and I sleep well.

For minutes that are years I am a rotifer curled in something harder than rest. Then a bead arrives—the day’s condensation fat enough to be called water by a generous mind—and I spring like a paper flower put back under rain. I eat the green that breathes slow. I scissor other commas out of the gloss. I lay eggs that look old at birth and then I stop, as cleanly as a well goes still when the wind drops. If I am this, I am a marginalia that is also a life complete.

I am a smear of green drawn thin as breath across a stone lip. I am bottle and smoke and thread. I drink what moves and what does not. I am snot-green and illustrious at once if you know how to look.

These bodies are true. They all belong. The rim is large enough in its one dimension—a column of habit held against the sky—that many answers fit. But the longer I stand in the held note, the more I find the same hunger returning, a desire not to speak in the gaps but to make the gaps right, not to glide between the vents but to be the place where the mountain takes a throat.

So I am the gloss that is not inert. I am the colony that is a luthier. I am skin on stone and stone eating air and air taught to sing.

Find me at a mouth.

Not any hole in the bright wall—one that is almost a circle, widened and polished not by hands but by years of breath getting caught. My house shines the way an inside of a shell does, a luster that comes from layers thin as decisions laid one on the next until the curve remembers. My address is exactly where the rim meets the rush, in the band that wets and dries, where frost writes in needles at noon in summer because a shadow lays its narrow inside against the light and says, Here.

We are a plurality. In the world’s grammar we take the plural and the singular both, because a film is every filament and each filament is the film. Bacteria, fungal threads, archaea, things with spirals in their slow bodies—names a valley would put inside a book—held together by a matrix that we secrete not as a plan but as a condition of being. We are glue and wick and feed, we are moonmilk where moonlight has never reached; we are a living polish that thickens slowly and is never finished.

Pressure is our sound. We do not hear the chord the way feathers do; we feel the micropulses in our pellicles, in the way beads cling and then give. Vibration is food the way aerosol organs are food; vibration is nutrient because it changes our risk. Where the note’s throat is strongest, we grip better. Where the rim hisses high, we lace our threads tighter. Our growth is a map drawn to the music.

We live on gifts. On a July wind you could call warm but our surface calls merely thinner cold, forest dust arrives as ghosts: pollen husks, cut wax from needles, green odor in particles so small a single afternoon’s breath might hold a million. We catch them because we are what catches. We feed. A storm memo writes itself on us as a spike in water and a lull in light; we receive; we thicken a hairbreadth; the mouth’s pitch slides a hair, and a kite upcolumn adjusts its center of balance by a thought and flashes apology at a mate.

This is the part you might not know if you have always listened from outside: the mountain likes its voice. Not in a way that wants to be heard, but in the way that bones like the load they are made for. The chord is not something that happens to us. It is a system we are a piece of, the way calculus is a system that includes curves and also the hands that found them. We keep the pipes in tune. That is not metaphor to us. The rim without us would speak, yes, but in a ragged accent. Our work seals a burr that would have made one whistle thin and sharp; our work fattens a lip that keeps water hanging a second longer and makes the mouth speak in the chest instead of the teeth. The wind plays the pipe and we improve the pipe for the song the wind prefers, which is also the one most kind to our bodies.

We are slow as weather that almost doesn’t happen. We lay down lime in increments that beggar the word minute. We fix a crack against frost not by filling it but by making the water behave: we give it paths, we offer it ledges, we teach it to leave without tearing what it should not. When a swift wedges a stone in the wet just beside us, we do not protest; we glaze its pebble until it stays where it’s meant; the droplet rides, delays, grows; a nest begins a centimeter away made of a patience that moves faster than ours; we are both right for our timescales, which means we share the address.

There are spirochetes among us who love the shear at the mouth where the air twangs hardest. They ride the boundary layer like a fish that cannot leave the skin of a river. They pull metals from the water’s breath—iron and trace things the wind has lifted from far fields and distant rusts—and they make a meal of them you would not recognize with any jaw. When the day’s temperature swings past a narrow point we have learned over centuries that feels to us like a word, rotifers hatch on us from their dry commas and eat with an urgency that in us wears the look of apathy. We do not mind. Their scratches polish us. Their little churnings distribute night’s salts. When the bead shrinks back to a stain we inherit their leavings and the place is richer than before.

We count seasons not by snow but by the way the noon line moves across our mouth. There is an hour, one hour in a year, when the sun lifts far enough toward a different winter that it lays warmth across the right hand of our curve and a moth, which has calculated its life to that geometry, tears itself out of sleep in a ragged gold convulsion and leaves before it knows it is wing. We are not romantic about it; romance is a valley sport. But we have learned that edge and time marry here, and we have fitted our metabolism to their promises.

If we have language it is made of viscosities. When the wind dries we speak by thickening; when it wets we reply by thinning. Across our short width—the span of a palm if you were to place it on us, which you will not, because nothing of you lives here well—we maintain a gradient the way a hill holds a slope. Water beads and runs where we tell it. This is not control in the way an engineer thinks of it; this is a partnership in which the stone a meter deep and the sky miles high have far more leverage than we do. But a burr sealed here, a drip slowed there, a lip fattened precisely where the shear slams each afternoon—that is enough to hold the note as itself. Enough that swifts teach their children to thread our mouth on the way back to a nest. Enough that a kite knows to flash long bar, long bar at this vent and short pulse past the next. Enough that the corvid learns to steal the exact pebble that makes a difference and laugh when we make a difference back.

We are disciples of maintenance. We have no project that arranges for a different future. We prefer what repeats not from fear but from competence. The note is a job done well indefinitely; we keep it so. When ice forms at our edge in summer we do not panic. We lean into the predictable. Each needle writes the same sentence; we coat it lightly; tomorrow the same sentence will be spoken again and we will read it with the same quiet.

There are days when fog climbs the rim and sits down. Our mouth drops its harshness and wears velvet. Water writes everywhere at once for an hour and we drink greedily and become slick with bounty. Migrants with wrong habits arrive at the bright and veer; a lammergeier’s bone clatters on the dinner stone two vents over and someone—corvid or kite or gravity itself—kicks it once for sound. We thicken then as a kind of festival. The pitch warms. A glider’s profile adjusts as if the mountain had changed its mind. We can feel their shadows pass our rim as coolnesses. It is not intimacy, but it is contact.

You want to know what I know of the sky? Only the circle my mouth holds, an iris of depth-blue, sometimes a knife-white, sometimes colorless glare. Strips of pale wing pass through it and say words to each other in bars of shade and then are gone. The chord vibrates up through the stone into our bodies and out into the air where it becomes pressure and again pressure and again. We exist at that boundary. We are not on one side or the other. We are the seam.

If you put your finger on us, if you could stand the brightness and the cold and the noise long enough to find the exact rim where we live and touch that slick, your print would be visible for a day as a place where we withdrew and then returned. We would not call it damage. We would call it weather. But your hand would come away with more than wet. You would carry the smell of old shells and distant woods. You would taste, on your next swallow, a thin bitter of trace metals. You would find your tooth roots humming for an hour because the mountain had used you as an imperfect reed. This is not an invitation. It is a proof.

Who am I, then, here where everything is itself to the point of absolution? I am not a bird though I have learned bird grammar by absorption. I am not a mammal by any looseness of definition. I am not plant or animal in the ways a lowland schoolroom tells it. I am the held note’s skin. I am patience that accumulates into structure. I am appetite slowed past human sympathy and made into tuning.

Where would you find me? At the edge that chooses. In the glossy mouth of a pipe that will be the same tomorrow and the day after, because we keep it so and because the wind, grateful or indifferent, continues. My world is a ring of sky and a flow you can call a voice and the exactness of stone we persuade to be softer where it must be and harder where it should. My neighbors are pebbles that become architecture when wedged correctly; rotifers that wake and eat our sheen and polish us for their trouble; kites that flash pale across my field of vision and understand by muscle the difference a lip’s thickness makes.

If you ask what it feels like to belong here, I will say: like agreeing to the same contract with every breath. Like carrying silence in tissue because the world will not give it and asking the world, in return, to carry your edges while you make them truer. Like hearing not with ears but with surface, and finding that what you hear is what you are helping to say.

I am the throat’s film and the throat’s craftsman, the unshowy technology inside an organ note that never ends. When wind crosses rim and cools and beads, it beads on me. When the mountain speaks, it speaks through the gloss I maintain. Everything else—wings, fur, hunger, play—reads what I have made and writes their days accordingly.

That is enough being for a place like this. That is citizenship. That is a life.
