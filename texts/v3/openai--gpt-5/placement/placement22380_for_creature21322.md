Where I am begins as pressure before it becomes a place.

At first I am only a way the roar leans. I am a seam in it, a small hollowness the size of cupped hands where loud refuses to enter. I do not own it yet. I feel the whole green dusk as if it were inside my bones, the long shove of a sky that forgot how to be thin, the continuous organ note that stacks itself in me until my teeth ache with belonging. Somewhere out beyond this borrowed pocket an oar passes and won’t commit. Somewhere behind my eyes a deeper vowel swells and every bubble near me suspends itself in courtesy.

Only after a while do I remember my hands.

They are not the hands I inherited when the world was bright and angular. The fingers wear webs like sighs. Little shutters over the knuckles open and shut of their own accord, catching planes of pressure when they are kind, closing when they snap. The palms are busy with listening, the way a tongue is busy with taste. I set one flat on the top stone that has taught all our children what patience means, and the old polish meets the new and is pleased with itself. We are back, it says, in the texture language stones keep.

Then it is truer to say: I am one of the people who come here when the ridge is speaking a solvable chord. My ribs are strung like an instrument—fine bands that answer to notes, tightening when the room takes its lower register so I can be heavy against that weight and not be taken. Along my jaw, cups open and close like little mouths, each tuned to a slice of frequency. This is how I draw a map: in vibrations, in tastes that write yellow or iron-blue along the nerves, in the way a plane of sideways rain appears between two stones and makes beads like dust that fall like opinion and then are gone.

High on the drowned crest, above valleys full of somebody else’s busy, I come to the amphitheater. You would find me there, along the right-hand wall where the fossil fern’s negative looks like green fire behind the milk-white slab, because that is where the older grooves begin and the water already remembers listening.

We arrive in threes when we can, jittering from node to node the way our smallest star-things go from tick to tick. The ridge teaches you this: that you do not go from point to point across a world, you go from quiet to quiet, from agreement to agreement. One of us holds a pecking stone and leans their jaw toward the wall, letting one cup go wide and the others shudder closed. When that cup throbs sweet, they peck. White on black, black on white. The grooves are not art and not canal; they are theorem. Like the incised threads near the rim, like the microfalls that exist to braid noise, they pull sound into a different weave and remind the place of itself.

Another of us, knuckles half-open to lean against the glass-thin scaffolds the roar builds and takes away, tends the crescent. Flat stones lifted out of the team-toothed floor and turned a degree and put back. The pattern matters more than the material—if the amphitheater wants it and holds it, it can be gravel or tile or bone. When it is right, the lee behind it stops being almost. The quiet hardens. You can feel your own blood with your skin again. Your own breath becomes something other than being breathed by the world.

I am, today, the third. My belt of shell plates is loose because today the big notes have decided not to pull wrists out of sockets; I have spooled my silk into a reed. In the pocket, I purse my lips around the reed and blow not for air but for tone, and I fold the reed’s small sound through a known plane of current, and rain flowers sideways like seed. The droplets sizzle into the warm and flake a tooth-white frost onto the lip of a stone, and we will read that later for drift and for season. But for now the reed’s point is the child thing we have always come to do: we make the quiet keep a name.

We have brought a bead up the ridge in a mesh bag, a pale egg-case that never hatched because the eddies it asked for did not meet. Clear coil, brittle enough that a wrong chord could shiver it to nothing. In the lee, while the amphitheater plays a low, solving chord and the buttresses of spray settle into their arches the way a chest settles when grief has been named, I wet my thumb and forefinger and hold the bead still. The others speak—old light names first, out of habit and humor. Sky, comes through in the shape the mouth makes around the syllable because nothing here wears it. Bird, because the lime’s embedded relic deserves not to be a silence. Then the names we actually need: our children, our fevers, the outcropping three notches east that has learned to turn hot when the sulfur writes along the valley and needs warning. We say them into the quiet and feel the quiet receive them—it is like sinking your forearm into warm grain. After today the bead will ring when touched at any ridge-pool the chord permits. Distance will be a function of what answers.

I am not a priest. My people do not have that. I am a luthier for something that thinks it is a church until you scrape it with your hand and the bacteria go bright where your skin pleased them. I am a courier of names and a mechanic of hush and a forager. In the thin warmth that smokes out of a seam below the ledge I set my palms to rinse the grit from a napkin of bronze slick. It clings to me purposively; I press it to the insides of my wrists and feel sweet hunger go out to meet it, the meal walking itself in. After, I speak to the vine in the pressure-shadow though it is not mine and not animal. Sometimes you have to tell something it is seen or it will become diagram instead of body. It flexes a little, and I reward it by cleaning a grit of curd from one of its accordion-fans.

The pin-limb that lives near the cairn, the one with a bent spine and a personality like weather, complains along my shin with feet-tapping. I give it a ribbon leaf, the new kind, still a little wet with the plant’s self, and it learns the rhythm of handling it by watching my webs tremble. We have never been clever with traps. When we need meat, it is a case of intersecting planes we can hold still for nine beats and a generosity when we fail.

I was one of the children smoothed on the top stone. You’d have found me there years back, pressed face-first into its cool when the world’s larger breath was on rest and the place counted its grains. My jaw cups did not know then how to speak to each other—one was always running or sleeping too late, the others berated it with pulses until my head rang. We are all noisy inside at first. You learn to let the place ring through you without ownership. When to lock and when to loosen; when the sub-bass means cross and when the similar sub-bass means Not while this voice is speaking. The top stone gave up its chill to my cheek as if that were its work. If you come now when the roar resolves, you can lay your face there and find small children’s patience layered into it like mineral—palm, palm, palm, each with a different hurt and a different quiet learned afterward.

I will not live here the way a mussel lives on its rock. My family sleep lower when the ridge’s angle opens into paddocks of slow, where the outflow of rooms like this feeds and no one drowns by surprise. I come up by order of taste: the sulfur gone sweet in my mouth, the band behind my teeth ringing yellow for three nights in a row. I come up when the outer oar’s hammered tin scales are falling and catching in weirs along the way, because that is a season and a chance to sew. I count my years in the number of times I have turned the third of the three little blocks that make our half-joke cairn into its third orientation and felt the amphitheater respond like a friend clearing their throat.

What would I be, if not this? There were other offers. The bell of nothing-in-particular that sometimes chooses to be animal for six beats—I have been jealous of it. The way its hunger begins and ends without injuring it. I have thought about being a drapery of carbonate for a winter of chords, always furling and snapping and reincarnating as if doctrine. I have put my hand into the open room of thinner substance that smells like wind and believed in a past where I was a drywalker who made fires and had to move when wind was wind. I have loved the anonymity of the oar out beyond the mouth—long muscle that refuses negotiation and lives, gloriously, elsewhere.

But if you ask when not-playing at metaphor: you would find me a little below the right-hand fern, at shoulder height where the stone learns to be white, body canted to lean on invisible glass, cups along my jaw tasting the map the way the old wind must have tasted distance. I would be one of the mountain people shaped into good listeners by weather you cannot wait out. My skin would wear its bronze symbionts like a patient jewelry. My belt would jingle when I moved wrong and the tiles would slap my hip and warn my tendon before the chord could. My ribs would hum my weight to me. My palms would be clever with flat stones.

When I die—because everything here does, even arrangements—you won’t find bones easily. The warm sea eats them and re-writes. But our names outlast us. The crescents keep arriving even when toppled, the way a tune keeps happening because it is liked. The amphitheater stores some part of me as the habit of how I set my hands. The top stone will advertise me in a sheen that is no color in particular: this was the one who could make rain from a reed into a wall that would hold; this was the one who talked to vines; this was the one who carried Sky around because it was funny and because sometimes the brightness convulses above and you want the old lie of it near.

And if you came on a rare heartbeat when far thunder eats close thunder and nothing speaks and the room shows you its smallness—the tick of grains, the way a ribbon-leaf rehearses asleep—and laid your web across the cairn’s top, you might still feel me. Not as ghost. As habit. As the way a place holds a hand that was good at carrying its weight. You could set your jaw’s cups like mine and know which groove to lengthen, which stone to turn a half-turn. The amphitheater would let you borrow me long enough to make a pocket, and you’d say a name into it, and it would stick, and then the flood would return and we would all be water again until the next solvable chord.

That is where. That is who. A scribe of pressure in a room made of falling sky. A keeper of the small quiet that gives names weight. A borrower of balance living on the back of a mountain that learned to be ocean.
