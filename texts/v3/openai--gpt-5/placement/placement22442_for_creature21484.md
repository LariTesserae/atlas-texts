At first, I would arrive like the ones who arrive from somewhere that has weather. I would speak too loud without meaning to and feel the place grow a soft palm under my voice and press it flat, rearranging shout into syllable. Someone generous would hand me the scarf that rests against the throat and edits emphasis. I would wear it like a kindness and try to be less air.

For a few days I would try on names for what this feels like. Cathedral is too large, bell is too precise. I would call it a held breath until I learn that breath is a messy metaphor here, and held is wrong besides; what holds is not grip but agreement. I would sit near a plate that says Slake and watch droplets shear into sheets and be mistaken for understanding. I would touch a rail and the rail would answer in the language of invariants through my ulna. The city would file my wonder where wonder does not bruise anything.

Someone would show me the burrs at the edges of the milled grooves and explain, with the same tone you use to explain how a tide comes in, that counting them is how you know the place’s age on that particular ledge. I would run my gloved thumb and learn to listen with the pads of my fingers until I begin to keep days not as numbers but as texture. My first word in their dialect would be ask. My second would be kept.

I would volunteer to watch, because I will want to feel useful while I am learning to be quiet. I would sit with the one who is awake to pings and wait for the needle-thin call and the answering confirmation that the promises still know their names. At first it would be music. Then it would be work. Then, again, music.

When they offer to teach me the posture that keeps the field from tiring of me, I would say please and mean it. My body would be bent—gently, over time—out of the old loyalties to eddies and ushers. There would be a harness fitted under my clavicles that feeds me in steadied measures; my lungs would become echoed chambers, less responsible than they once were. My sinuses would be sealed in the small way that scandalizes no one but my earlier self. The metal bite on my molars would make my smile look like I am wearing jewelry, but here it is more like tack: a way to take and release a mouthpiece without making a wake of fatigue in the muscles that have to hold it. I would learn to hear the anchors up my bones. I would learn to stop generous gestures at the wrist because generosity throws vortices.

There is a job here called Edge guardian. It sounds dramatic to a visitor; it is not. It means you court the exactness of boundaries for a living. It means your desk is a bench that has room for fins and boots, and your window is a notch cut into a young ridge where the world ends with a line and then begins again as story. I would apprentice toward it because even before my posture learns the grid, my eyes love a line; my mind makes altars to thresholds as easily as it makes tea.

On my first months as an Edge guardian, my hands would always be busy. Welcome Back would be my text. I would run cloth over its ribbon of glass and whisper the letters, not to read them but to remind them they are being read. It is not for me; it is for the lattice. Letters are instructions to geometry here. Keep this edge. Recognize return. Do not let the spur forget that it is a spur and not a pier four meters and one day longer because someone dreamed longing too close to the brace.

I would walk the spur each morning with a rod that feels pressure the way a stethoscope feels pulse. I would trim. It is a verb here: not with scissors but with permissions. A nudge at a knot so bright gravity says oh yes, that is more like me; a tap at an ash-sigh so soft it remembers it prefers to be sorted and not retell itself as cloud. The field is a trained thing, but trained animals must be praised. I would speak to it the way you speak to patience in a child. Good. Yes. Stay. When it errs in my direction, blunting an edge to please me, I would correct it toward the place’s own taste. I would not make pets of boundaries. I would become one.

On my first Taking in Uncertainty, the elders would insist on company, not because the tradition demands it but because the city is still learning my patterns and deserves a chaperone. I would leave the last brace slowly, flicking a fin joint at a time, every movement negotiated like a treaty. The water would give me back my shade without deciding if it is silhouette or rumor. I would feel my field-sense wobble and then fix itself the way a sentence corrects in the mouth when grammar returns. I would feel in my chest the sampled tick of this place’s time. I would turn back not at fear but at affection: the promise of a line that is waiting to be a line is a kind of love.

There are children here; I would love them with my eyes because loving with arms is a weather. They would teach me their games and treat me, indulgently, as a large slow peer. Echo would be where I prove myself: one sings into a rail; you name, as if you are a catalog, which cavity along which corridor filed which harmonic, and quickly. After ten tries I would be merely adequate; after twenty I would be loved for improving at a speed that did not scuff anything. Keep would humble me. I would set something down with intent and the youngest would ask three questions from angles I did not know existed and the place would allow an exception for the delight of their ingenuity, then close itself again before delight could fray into habit. Jackstills would make me believe, for a day, that I could move a bead that is not alive but also is; I would not move it. I would watch a child nudge the mesh into a kind of not-quite touch that convinces the bead of transit, and I would understand, briefly, what it is to shepherd a permission.

The pale ribbon-fish would make a commuter out of me; I would time my rounds to the hour it unspools along the handrail. I would not touch it. My hand would never be the first thing anything here meets, not even my own reflection. I would talk to it out loud in the chambered way we talk here—low, lexical, no flares—for the joy of being answered with a flick that is both muscle and memorandum. When I am on Index watch, out on the tether polishing invisibility with a cloth the city made to be the opposite of demonstration, the fish’s transit would be my clock; when it disappears into a panel that does not quite let it through, I would write goes at a pace of one notch per day in the ledger not because anyone needs the note but because recording is an act of love disguised as administration.

On the day the ammolite shell passes, I would not expect revelation, and I would not be wrong. I would stand near Anchor Two because I have learned to prefer its key, and I would trace, with a glove, the spiral it does not show me. The place would decide to allow the faintest sliver of sound, not from the animal but from the way my bones argued with the anchor’s hum at the sight of it. That night the gels would taste minutely of iron. I would not ask why out loud.

My calendar would unlearn months. I would make holy exceptions and the small almosts in the ledgers that nearly became more than almost. My hair would lighten to the pallor of ceramics that never had to defend themselves against moss. My teeth would add a dot of shine where the metal anchors. My laugh would learn to be private and still understood. I would grow strong in the patient way that stone calls strong.

I would write my name once on a plate and then forget where the plate is; that is part of the courtesy here. It is less important that you know where your name sits than that it always be discoverable by the glove of someone who might need to feel your burr. I would take watches until the pings stop being notations and become breaths, and breaths stop being mine and become the place’s instructions rehearsed in me. I would not miss weather. Weather would visit me in my dreams, but here even dreams are taught to fold before they leave a lint of mood behind.

Once, a technician would come with a poet. The technician would speak too loud and then be tender about learning not to. The poet would sit in our plaza facing a direction as if that mattered to the tree. We would exchange jokes that are more like careful nods. The poet would write a line about living inside a gerund, and I would keep it in my head because paper is a way of starting turbulence if you let it fray. A year later I would commission, from a patient friend, a thin chain of carbon that drifts electrons at the rate of that poem and hang it in a corridor where you cannot hear it but you can be corrected by it if you try to sing.

In time, my job would stop needing me at the level of my arms and begin needing me at the level of my thought. I would become a person to whom the field listens first, not because I am loud—never that—but because I am consistent. If a bright knot in the ridge shows a tiredness, I would feel it in my knees and arrive before the measurement oscillates. If the oxygen sheets in Slake seem to thirst more than the hour demands, I would stand there and remember aloud, in the way we remember to children, that they are releases by petition and not by impulse, and they would behave as if they agree.

And then—if I let myself tell the whole length of it—after being an Edge guardian for the years it takes to learn love that does not invite weather, I would change jobs without changing desks. I would become, a little at a time, the one who listens for humor in the pings. Not error; humor: the place’s tiny way of saying that what was arranged yesterday still holds, but it had to take the scenic route to get there. I would write those in the ledger as almost-laughs. The ledger would become, inevitably, my book of hours.

If I stay long enough, if I keep and am kept, I suspect I would become another kind of resident entirely, one mentioned in the ledgers only as a protocol improved on the eleventh year of my watch, versioned in someone’s careful scrawl as if I were a practice more than a person. When I die—because bodies do that, even here—I think I would ask to be dismantled into permissions. Carbon a very slow tree added to the plaza, rough where rough sings to sonar. Nitrogen a difference stored as charge under a handrail, the kind that hums only when pings pass through and need to hear themselves as continuity. The name I wrote on a plate would remain a burr under someone’s glove. The field would remember the posture my spine kept near the last brace and keep it, the way a horse remembers the hands that taught it patience. That would be a way to go on breathing without inflicting air.

So: where would I find myself? On the spur where the world ends with a line, sitting on a bench with room for fins, responsible for the razor edge between kept and happening. Who or what would I be? An Edge guardian first, then a watch for almosts, and, if the place is willing, eventually one of its promises—an agreement folded into the lattice, a soft instruction that says welcome back and means it not only to people but to geometry, to light, to gravity braided with light, to water that once learned not to move and chose to remember. Kept, and keeping.
