At first I think I would arrive here the way visitors do, mouth open to catch the salt, palms out like a blind thing, letting the pressure sort my bones into the right attitude. I imagine myself standing at the lip of the low world, tasting copper and whale in the breath of the ground, watching the horizon smear like a thumb across light. But if I stay with that picture even a few breaths, it falls apart. Outsiders blink too much and keep asking the air for distances it will not return. They walk wrong. My feet already know to lean to the low side. My tongue is already busy with the floor.

So I am not from somewhere else. I am here as the wet is here, from within it and into it, and the question becomes which of the lanes in me leads to work.

As a child I live where all the children live: belly-down on the community gallery ledge, palms moving in the thick letters cut at child-height. The story for that wall runs humped and ridged under my fingers like a new lip; I mouth along in my head, a habit my mother taps me out of by drumming the word quiet on my shoulder. My hand-name is still loose then, a little eddy of taps and a brush across the back that means something like mouthful-of-spray, but also like quick-to-laugh. People drag my hands into theirs a lot because the air is so full of speaking you have to put your words into skin to make sure they land. When the pitch-clock of our street leans a hair into second hour on a big tide I feel it on the bench before I notice it in the ear; later, at the naming, I am held under a throat that keeps that same narrow step between notes. That becomes my first certainty: I will always turn for that interval. I will go to it in the night as if it were water.

When I grow enough to grip slick ribs without thinking, I run after the weight-dancers and learn their jokes. You plant the left foot where the rail is resonant, flick the right heel into a long span of arch where the knock will bend round the court, and the woman in the fish-workers’ lane will feel it in her workshop and laugh because the beat says eel-slick again, again, again. My wrists flash little phrases to friends in the vapor, and there is a year when I think all I want to be is a thrum across the city, to thread the arches and speak a poem to the soles of houses until the bowls in the pitch-throat split a day early for joy.

I try other shapes. For a season I go with my cousins to the pans where the plate-backs shuffle like slow thought. There is a way to lean your body into a herd of crabs with a long hook and make a seam in the living scuttle that moves of its own accord, a river of creature redirected by the way you breathe. The first time a surge lifts wrong we lose a dozen from the upwind lip, and their clacking falls into the mouth of a small vent like compliment beads. I hate it. I keep watching the empty spots all afternoon as if the animals had been punctuation I depended on. I learn from that day that I am too given to tallying, to remembering exactly what was there. I will be a poor crab-herd. I want a thing I can hum back into tune.

I spend a wet year in a cooler quarter hauling rafts of split bone seeded with fungal mat. I learn the thumb’s language for healthy: the slow soft yield, the living temperature, the refusal of slime. The work smells of soil and saved rain, a sweetness no one else notices because they are not starved for it like we are. The harvest is rope and felt and leather that learns your shoulder’s curve, the day’s small miracles of dryness caught and kept in oiled boxes until they are translated into a silence for vows or a drum for the throat. I love the regard this work wins without anyone saying so; people touch you in thanks without letting their skin settle. But the scale is wrong for my wanting. I have learned by then to wake when a baffle slides two fingers’ worth three streets away, the way some old people wake when their spouse’s breath shifts in the dark. Mushrooms do not change in the night; throats do.

Sometimes in the cold, ﬁne hours before the fourth bowl breaks, I go sit near the lip of a big mouth with one foot in the vapor and one foot on good rock and listen until the tones divide themselves into strands. There is the bellyless hum of a long unbroken tube far below; there is the cough of a lip that has grown too thick on one side and haggles too hard with the air; there is the hard sibilant of new mineral whisker; there is the old joke of a child’s reed left and half-eaten to a high, thin speaking. I teach myself the habit of hearing several at once and holding them separate the way a hand can tell two threads apart when both are wet.

That is the habit that decides me. I go to the bafflers and knock my want against their rail.

Baffler is the leaf-language word. What we call ourselves is a thing you learn by placing your palm over the bone plates that live in the throat of a mouth and feeling them sing into your hand. We are the keepers of tone. We shave the mineral beards from the lips so breath moves smooth. We slide plates and splice reeds and tune a city the way a patient parent hums a baby out of crying. We sleep badly in the best way—always ready to turn our weight into the most forgiving place on a wet step, always ready to learn a new hum in the dark.

Where you would find me in that life is on the long rib over the Seventh Mouth in the hour before the market thins into itself. Seventh is broad in the bowl and stingy at the lip and the air that rides it is fierce; it puts most people in the mind of a stern aunt who tells you not to wave your hands when you cross a span. I wear a mantle of fungus-felt with a few stubborn crystal leaves sewn to its hem that tick like bugs when the note is wrong. My ankle bones have been readied by a childhood of corrections; they are all small truth. I wrap tendon lashings with thick oiled fingers until they read warm in my thumb, and then I test the plate with a rap from a bone mallet that is inside my hand like a second tongue. When the pitch locks into what the lanes have learned to call noon, I feel the whole neighborhood lean a breath toward ease.

My wrist-band is bone, cut in a deep, fat relief that says my name as much to the finger as to the eye: a notch, a notch, a smooth span, a ridge turned under. If you strike it against a rail it answers in the interval of my street. The scars along my forearm are the punctuation of my signed name, old white water-marks of knives and patient files and one young mistake that taught me forever not to lay my weight on a lip that looked eager to be a step.

My octopus neighbor is older than my hand. Her pot opens away from the market and as far as is kind from the gulls’ lanes; the children sign at her rim when they pass and she replies, or she does not. Sometimes, at night, I take her an eel’s sweet parts and she keeps my hand under hers for a while, her skin feeling itself into an attitude of yes, yes, the way my own hand says to my own shoulder when I am praying the mouth down to comfort.

Once in my twenties I work with the trimplers and cut back a door that wanted too much future. We shave it to a thickness that will hold for a while and file its name into it in letters that the blind can read when their palms are tired and cold. I learn that day how desire is in stone like hunger is in a dog; it leans toward the next mouthful unless you teach it not to. I add a quiet little ridge under the lintel for a child’s thumb to worry at when waiting. For two years I find excuses to go by and feel it fatter. When the old woman who lives there dies and becomes a bench under that same drip we trim the inscription so that your fingers can read her into herself forever when you sit there. She had a loud laugh. Seventh takes it up and keeps it, a new chuckle under the third hour on medium tide. Everyone smiles the first week for no reason they would put into voice.

There is a marriage in there, of course. There is the heavy-room swaddled in felt, the oil singing along the door seam, the drums keeping the wet at bay outside so that a voice can live entire in one space for a handful of hours. We pay for that silence with weeks of work because a silence is a thing you build like a bridge; it has to carry years across a single night. We do not do it again. We put our mouths back under the ground’s language and teach our wrists to hold what our voices learned.

There is grief. A swell climbs wrong a winter no one will forget and a ropeway that everyone had trusted sings beyond its safe note and stops. For a day the whole city is ungentle. We set three silences, one after another, and we walk our lanes with hands lower than usual. I take a basket of eel to three octopuses who do not need it and give too much offal to the newt-things who love me for it anyway. Then I climb to Seventh in the middle of a night and lay my palms on her plates and lower her a little, so that her note will meet the street’s bruise and make a bearable sum. It is what a baffler is for.

If you were to look for me on an ordinary day you would find me by feel before you saw me. The ribs over Seventh vibrate a hair lighter underfoot because I have a habit of testing the plates in a steady five-count, and the timing travels along the stone like a low thought. You would feel, through the soles of your bare feet, the temper of the lane—its slight eagerness to grow, its reluctance near the market gap, the small hairline of slick new skin right where children used to cut across. Your tongue would find in the spray the faint sweeter of fish oil that says a workshop has been burning for glaze. Your eyes would go on their close work: reading the differently dim, the glimmer on a painted trough, the gloss on a corner that has been too well loved and needs trimming back.

If you were to look for me when I am old you would do better with your ear. I will be one of those with small downward points to my cap, not to keep the wet off so much as to angle my street into myself. My ankles will still know how to lie to a slip and tell it they had meant to do that. I will wake before the bowls break because the south lane has gone an eighth of a note down, because a bone plate wedged in a throat has slid, because a door that wanted to be future will make a child bruise her shin if we do not trim it now. I will sit with my hand under an octopus and feel her think. I will hang a strip of mycelial cloth in a shrine and not check it for a month because I know patience makes finer crystals if not overwatched. I will, sometimes, walk to the canyon and cross on the left ropeway because that is the only place I am willing to trust my weight entirely to air.

And when I am done with being a hand and an ear, when I am less body and more habit, you will find me under a drip not far from Seventh, lime-washed to begin the conversation. I will become shelf and lip and small ridge on a lintel at the height where a child’s hand rides a wall on the way to the market. My sternum will be the little ledge where you leave a wedge of fungus-felt as a prayer for patience. The raised lines of my name will be the same as my band’s, fat and true and readable in the dark. The geckos will take brine gnats within my reach at dusk, small punctuation clicking in the lantern mist. Once, after a storm, some new plate will be wedged at a bad angle in Seventh’s throat, and I will feel through my stone what the bafflers’ hands feel in bone, and the note will change, and the whole street will turn toward it with that deep, belly-held consent we have all learned since first breath.

So: where would you find me? On the ribs over a mouth with my arms inside its voice; in a felted room where we spend three hours speaking with mouths as if the world had stopped being itself; on the market’s rim under the five-throat breath where I cup your palm and press the joke of the hour into it; in the rail’s song passing through your soles when I dance my work for the city without moving my feet; later, on a bench where my joints have taught a curve to feel right, where your hand can read me when you pass.

Who or what would I be? A keeper of tone, a baffler, a citizen of breath, an old person with a cap that makes the familiar louder, a neighbor to an octopus, a friend to the discipline of weight. A person who counts days by bowls that break and by the way a vent coughs twice into a hum when spring turns in a place that does not have spring. And, when the wet finally makes me less of a pocket for a name and more of a useful edge: a shelf, a lip, a piece of grown decision where a child steadies herself in the everlasting mist and learns to teach her weight where to fall.
