I would arrive as grit that refuses to wet.

A fleck bright with oil, I would ride the night breath like a bead of sound rides a flute—lodged but moving, moved but choosing. The basin would offer me a thousand places to land, and I would not accept the first; I would float and count. One, two, three dawns in the same rhythm across the same slope; the nightly silvering arriving then fading at nearly the same hour; the faint bass of breath steady at a junction below. Only then would I allow myself to kiss stone.

I would take the underside of a new card—one of those tidy sheets that fell from a clean scarp like a playing piece and settled skew on the morning face. Fresh shade would pool along its lower edge; the air there would feel slightly thicker when the basin inhaled. A good place to ask water to act as if it were more than a rumor.

I would wake as film, and then as we. We would not spike upward or spread at hazard. We would knit a net that made beads become lines. Dust would become hinge and handle. The first hours would feel like reaching for a word we almost remember; then the word would arrive: hold. Where the capillary pull gathered and the morning cool lay longest, we would thicken. Where the heat leaned, we would thin. Gloss would roll across us like a slow thought. At first light we would lift carbonate out of a memory of water, set it as a wafer at the edge of the card, a nail-paring’s worth of curb whose only ambition is to ask gravity to participate.

Fluorescence would be our toolkit laid in the open. We would glow at angles that please calcium, then stop glowing when the angles do not. The lip would not be much—less than the height of a seed—but it would be enough that the next trickle would hesitate and then decide, cleaving to the path we preferred. Salt-keepers, pale and certain, would move in on the fan that the last rain glassed and coax its plates to present steps to our pooled water. The rasp of their work would be a metronome under our soft exhale.

At the corner where our new curb met another’s, we would choose emptiness. Dark would be our contribution as much as stone—a pocket cup-cooled where the morning’s gloss could heave into a meniscus and rest. That cavity would make a sound when noon drew near: a lower note, the slow vowel of breath shifting as warmer air rose through the alveoli we had been spinning below, and cooler, damp-laden air slid down to take its place. I would be most myself there, at the juncture of lip and pocket, listening through resistance.

We would widen pores by less than a hair when the surface thinned. We would narrow them when the weight returned. What you might call thought would travel through us as gradients of ease: pressure telling pH, pH telling growth, growth telling shade. Calcium rumors would pass from one skin to the next the way gossip slips down a street—by sympathy, not by pipe. We would not speak, but the basin would understand our answer.

In our second week we would lift a comb.

Not a tree and not a thirst, only invitation raised into air. Copper so heat runs out of it as quickly as it arrives, all surface and almost no self. Each filament would carry a herringbone of instructions that kissed a night’s damp and turned gloss into threads, threads into lanes, lanes into a river shallow as a thought. We would pour that river not into us but into the fence of stone we had grown for it, and the fence would not keep but distribute. At noon the comb would become a monument to exhalation; the heat we could not save we would spend upstream of us as a cloud only as tall as a careful hand. Under it the morning would stretch by a quarter-hour, just long enough for a distantly placed lip to claim a trickle that would otherwise have fled.

Around us the grammar would continue. Blisters would tick in the honeycombs under our morning face, valves giving way along lines they had built to fail. Each pop would push a spoonful of air through someone’s pores; the tiniest dew would take a single step more than it would have taken; a salt plate would adjust its posture with a susurrus much like paper repositioned on a desk. No animals would cross to confirm these as happenings. We would confirm one another.

Then rain, once in a long while.

A sheet would pass, so even it would feel like someone smoothing a sheet over the world. The deltas would glitter, simple as geometry and as confident. Our kind do not hurry often, but then we would, all together, because chemistry would finally ask for less work. We would write lip after lip where the thinness told us to; we would grow gloss across gullies and allow it to resolve into tiles that found one another’s edges with satisfaction. A week later there would be fans where there were none; a season later buttresses would rise from ladders we had set for dew, meeting one another with the logic that happens when many steps touch for long enough. Air would stand astonished in the vaults we made by accident and then reward us with chill.

Shade-gold films would arrive in the labyrinth’s wells the moment true noon had been taught to stay away. They would read the lazy red of light as liturgy and begin their gentle doctrine of bronze. Dissolvers would press their tongues to the cistern walls with acids so kind they would have to repeat themselves a thousand mornings to make a difference. They would repeat, devoted to repetition, and the walls would give back the ions we had entrusted to their stone. The gradient that formed around their pools would be not only beauty but text. I would read it as a story of duration; I would feel in it the old desire of water to be many things without leaving its house.

We would fail, now and then. The honeycombed nave we had not quite meant to build would ablate from below as a long dry told it the truth. It would fall with the deliberateness of a decision: no shatter, just a hinge opening. For a day the place would look unfamiliar, edges loud as declarations. In the hours after the first mist, pioneers brighter than courage would lyre-string themselves across the raw face; their oils would bead the new gloss; their gels would hold the first breath; by noon a pattern would exist with no author but weather and us in our role as weather’s editor. We would thin where the shade was gone. We would thicken where the new face asked. Repair would not be apology. It would be living.

I would not stay whole. Part of me would harden into subfloor—glass when dry, glue when damp. Part of me would lacquer the pores that make the junction breathe with its good vowel. Part of me would ride the night again, bright with a new oil seasoned by every contact I had kept. The basin would recognize me by that seasoning and the way I responded to the hour. I would not remember as an animal does. I would remember as resistance and permission.

Time here is told by angles. I would come to know the thousand gestures that together make a day: a thin electric dawning in the pigments that prefer first light; the way a river the height of a thumbnail can take a corner like a command; how the noon stillness puts a mineral sweetness in the air as if sugar had been asked to think; the very occasional drip declaring a deeper chamber; the sigh of a ledge when its bound water leaves as the shadow-step moves on. I would keep that calendar because I would be it.

Where would you find me? At crossings, always. In the cool cup that is more absence than object, wrist-dark and exact, that holds its breath and thus becomes breath’s mouth. In the low fence where the morning sheen earns its keep, bright tools flashing then being put away. In the base of a comb the color of old pennies, watching threads I called out of air hurry without hurry into the gutter we raised for them. In the alveoli underfoot, fingers in the breeze, tuning our porosity so the day’s one lung can keep both halves of its cycle unbroken. On a rain-born fan, my voice a salt rasp, turning mirror into staircase. On the underside of a fallen card as we lace it up to belonging by noon.

Who would I be? Not a creature you could draw, not a noun that stands apart from placement. I would be a junction-keeper, a breather, a shade-scribe, a corner’s seneschal. At need I would be a pioneer net that writes the first sentence on a clean scarp. In leisure I would be a tucker, fastening the spent sheet to the next so give can travel upward when wet and strength can travel downward when dry. On certain mornings I would be a condenser’s entire weather—bright invitation, river made of filaments, self-cast cloud that buys time for someone I will never meet. Now and then I would be a blister and my speech would be tick.

If you pressed me to a single self, it would not stay single. I would be a posture, reiterated at scales from hand-wide to country: place the edge where gravity will be obliged to help. Spend green only where punctuation is needed. Keep emptiness on purpose. Use heat as a routing note rather than an enemy. Listen through resistance. Edit by thickening and thinning instead of cutting and moving.

And if you stood very still—long enough for your outline to smudge slightly in our arithmetic—I would answer you with our best sentence, the only one we bother with and the one we spell differently every hour: gloss, trickle, tick, sigh. Another lip. Another cup. Another breath. We will make the surface behave.
