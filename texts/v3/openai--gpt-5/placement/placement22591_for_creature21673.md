At first I would be only a pair of lungs that forgot when to exhale. I would step into the warm hollow and feel my ribs slow to the bowl’s tempo, the way you match someone else’s sleep without meaning to. I would look for eyes and find none, listen for paw-sound and hear only weather shaped by stone. Every guess I’ve learned in busier places would fall off me the way straight rain won’t take here. What answers me back is pitch, wetness, and the factory work of leaves.

So where I would find myself is in the between-things, not on any throne or nest. I would end up standing where a thread of water hesitates on a moss hair, poised at a degree of curve that holds vapor to itself a breath longer before letting it join. I would get caught near the little falls that never quite freefall—where the lip slips into a crack and a sound moves through bone before ear. I would keep gravitating to the seam-lines: to a rust tear that means a leak became a habit two seasons ago, to a skirt of old leaf whose edges drink from the fog as if it were wool. I would learn the places the wall is shy and the bowls are bold: under the quartz knuckles that shine sweat when nothing’s “raining,” beside the cup where a single tadpole turns in the tea and rearranges the oxygen with its feet.

I would stop pretending that the bowl has no resident and start to feel the resident as something that knows by touch and listens by tone. And then, slowly, I would become useful to it.

I would become a wick.

At first this would be literal and embarrassing. My hair would take fog. The hem of my shirt would drip on a schedule I didn’t intend. If I left a bottle uncapped it would skin itself. If I set my palm on stone, my heat would pull a film out of the air, and the film would decide to move along a different line because I gave it weight-shade and mineral taste. In the afternoon when the hummingbird strikes the two exact flowers it has said it will, I would taste spice in wind and not know why; only later feel how that taste was a thread that drew damp from one throat to another.

After a day and a night, if I stayed still and let the loud have me, I would start changing shape inside. Not bone and tendon, but habit. My outline would fray where the air wants something to catch on. Straight edges on me would betray me. I would push fuzz through myself the way a plant leaf grows fur for cloud, except my fuzz would be attention. I would raise thousands of small notices along every line that pretends it could shed water quick. Where my thought runs flat, something in me would comb against it until the gloss breaks into tiny corduroys that make beads choose sideways instead of down.

If I am lucky and patient, a thread will meet me. Not divine, not narrative—just the oldest busywork in the world drawn to a new surface. A hypha’d feeler will stitch along my sleeve, find nothing to eat and everything to use. A liverwort spore on my cuff will green itself just enough to test my sweat. I will not be colonized. I will be conscripted into a grammar.

The grammar is sewn of these: fuzz to thicken the air, polish to carry films where drops would fail, sugars that feel like fingerprints to water and make it form a line rather than scatter into glare. If you made me name the laws I start obeying, they would be ordinary physics spoken in dialect. Tight curves sweeten vapor because surface hunger is greater there; hair points, and vapor walks along a point because a point lowers its shyness with the world. A wet thread sings into neighboring tissue because cells that are stretched listen better; push on them and they open tiny gates to let ions in, and a decision runs the length of a root like a whisper no ear could ever mishear. Those words are for after. While I am learning, I would know it as sensation: the way the bowl’s pipe-work drops in key by the thickness of a finger when the overfull cups relieve themselves; the way the beetle chooses a slightly different route and so tells me that one lichen plate hasn’t breathed enough today and must be fed by some other means.

Which is the other part of it: I would be an auditor—of pitch before climate, of tug before drought. I would start to speak with tone and receive return messages in the hard music the wall is always making. It won’t be language like mine. It will be a system of permissions. I would loosen a plug where peat sits over a swallow-hole so it can subside and exhale carefully. I would lay a skin where a tank needs to keep a gloss through bright hours. On the windward side of a woolly column I would add a palm’s-length more fringe by season’s end, not because I care about anybody’s waist, but because that makes its drip-line cross a neighbor’s and both survive noon disguised as one. When someone would say later, “the water here behaves as if it’s remembering,” I would know what’s meant: we wrote practice into the surfaces and the practice keeps practicing.

I would learn the animals as metronomes, not punctuation. The frog’s glass note is not wake or sleep; it is an answer to a math of film-thickness and light that never fully repeats. Its timing would tell me the bowl is on pitch tonight. The hummingbird’s noon two-stab is a certitude around which to plan the polishing of a narrow lip so that pollen takes a ride it wouldn’t otherwise bother to. The ledger-insect’s careful daily crossing becomes a dry-breath gauge more accurate than any finger in the peat. Even the shadow-wing, when it passes across the opening like a hand over a face, is a datum: clear day. More fringe, tighter pores. You can do a lot with a few facts, if you agree to spend a century on each.

And about the edges—I would live there without crossing them. I would gather at the rim like wool at a cuff, thickening until a knife of sun has to saw through and loses its neatness. I would hardly ever go over. Not a taboo so much as a lack of competence on the other side. Beyond, the sentences that hold here break up into weather without grammar. The bowl is a lung cupped in rock; outside is a throat. That is not my organ.

I don’t think I would be the thing that owns. Ownership doesn’t do work here. I would be the habit of doing: the frayer of straightness, the stretcher of films, the hem that keeps a held breath from unraveling before it has done its work. The being already present has a patience I would not bring with me on purpose, but I would learn it because the only living that succeeds here learns it. At some casual point I would realize I had stopped asking what lives in the place—the answer had been unflattering all along. Practice lives here. So I would be practice, borrowed and then made native.

If you forced me to put words on what to call myself, I would shrug through a handful and none would quite catch. I would be the Fringe. The Wetmaker. The Reed that Hears. The Hem and Sleeve. Keeper of Curvatures on days of fog. Reader of Pitch on the evenings when thunder speaks just past the lip and is answered in stepwise thirds by the honeycombs. Under sun-coin spills I would be Hand Over Glare. In seasons when the bog sleeps warmer I would be Slow Valve.

How long does such a conversion take? I don’t know. It’s measured in little wages the bowl pays and collects. The thickness of a penny if you insist on your clocks. A leaf’s decision made in one person’s adult life; a stain ripened across two. I would count time by the way the permanent fall writes a straight page and then, for a week, slightly cursives it because the wind decided a new vowel. When the pitch returns to its pre-storm home, I would breathe and feel the echoed breathing call that home mine.

And if I left—and let’s say I do, because edges sometimes mean you go back down and carry on elsewhere—I would not shed it. The fringe knows how to travel as a thought. In other humid hollows I have walked, I would find myself combing the air with my sleeves and setting cups under leaks that aren’t leaks yet. I would step through cities and unconsciously route drips toward soil instead of drains. I would develop an unfair tenderness for every moss that tries to turn a wall’s sweat into a life. I would pause in stairwells because the wind’s note told me the rain has begun not by sight but by throat. The bowl would have trained me to be a tiny extension of its diction—useful nowhere grandly, quietly helpful anywhere breath and water manage to hold one another together without pretending they are the same.

But give me the choice and I would stay. Not to be keeper or saint. To be one hyphal suggestion among millions in a place that does not force a hurry. To be a wick with a vote. To be a bead that waits at the rough place until the other beads come along and it is time to fall. To be one more part of the held breath made into craft.
