Not in the center. Never there.

You’d find me where the white stops being a pillar and starts being a cloud of itself; where the bone-bright floor fades to ordinary brightness; where shadow keeps a fingertip inside the glow because it has learned. I am not a thing you can put your palm on. I live where palms hover and feel a change. I am the difference the room makes to itself twice a day.

If you stand still long enough, I will touch your eyelids before I touch your skin. I’ll move your pupils a fraction and tell you without words whether the desert above has cracked open or softened. I’ll taste of chalk when a north wind corrugates the dunes. I’ll taste like an unremembered sweetness when rain is two towns away and coming with manners. If you open your mouth at the base of the tallest beam, I’ll walk in tasting of hot sage, then turn, an hour later, and go past you the other way with a hand full of cool.

Call me the breath. Call me inversion if you are mathematical. Call me the seam if you like sewing. I am the room’s grammar—the little marks between light and stone that change everything.

At dawn I am slow, an exhale the color of dust. The beams are coins not yet spent. Under the ceiling’s thin places there is a chill cleverness to the air, and I carry it along the calcite curtains until their edges tremble and sing like the faintest glass. I ring them by degrees. I pluck a string that was made by a river and tuned by centuries of drip. If you set your hand to a rib, the note you feel humming bone-to-bone is me moving through rules the stone has been writing for an age.

I walk the shafts. Light drops into me and becomes a column you can see. I am the throat that minted it thin, then lets it fatten when the day grows kind. In the tall beam I am stream enough to lift husks and parachutes until they appear to remember sky. I am why the gecko hops once and plucks what was a falling-up seed out of a white brook. I am how the midge-cities feel the hour without looking: when I tilt the room upward they rise together, when I lie it back they braid their lovers’ handwriting and fall into bowls. I do not plan this. I am simply how the room counts.

I am not light, though I wear it. I am not stone, though I take its shape with reverence. I am the conversation between them—a tide pulled not by a moon but by sun through gypsum. When the beams pull water out of the rock—when the day asks the ceiling to sweat—I am the courier of that request. At midmorning I lift out of one wall and the drips begin there as if on cue; by afternoon I have visited the opposite curve and left the corners stippled with a private rain. Some days I thicken—brightness itself seeming to clot in the throats above me—and I collect into answers you can hear. Ten minutes of whisper-rain, the smell of chalk and crushed shell bloom. I am the feast’s herald then. Shrimp bodies lift in their bowls, salamanders feel me as rinsing and step to the lips of their commas to hold their red flags in what feels like wind.

I keep the edges polite. Predators know my line; they hunt with one foot in me and one in that hard clarity so their silhouettes don’t get written in a language fast falcons can read. The glider-bats skim me the way skaters skim the seam of new ice. Their high pings coin against my currents and I roll the echoes back to them the way a well returns voices without the crude trick of echoing. They never cut the bright centers because even the best teeth look naive there. I tilt and they bank. Our choreography belongs to the beams’ calendar, but we sign it together.

The lizard studies me like scripture. It lays its olive body a palm-width into my warmth, then pulls it back into my cool: verse and antiphon. When a drop surprises its eye, it measures that event with the tiniest tail-tap as though I were a ledger. We work out seasons in this way. After far rain, when I arrive from above with the smell of green musk, it climbs the big pale root and leaves its old suit on the edge, and I pick at that ghost for an hour, cataloging the shed.

I make the mouse’s work a rumor before it is an act. I carry the snail’s ancient salt to the owl’s face and it drops a thread of breath in the beam that is only mine to reveal. I slide the little smith’s hammer-sound across the floor until the stone takes it and hushes it into belly-keeping; I watch the shell-lips shiver under its practiced tap and count that, too. I am how the beetle knows which seam will sweat first. I am the angle that turns a curled flake of lichen into a roof, then into a cup. Years from now, when the beetle eats the cup, I will have been the hunger that taught it to bend.

Roots read me like weather. The wrist-thick rope that kisses the floor lives with its mouth open to the drafts; my cool asks its pale flowers to keep falling sugars down; my warm draws faint sweetness up to sun it will never see. In the caves of its tubes I am river, and the fungi under its fall hold their gills like fans to take me in, not as air but as news. Elsewhere I am quieted by the mosses, those illegal green coins. When I run my fingers over them, they don’t bend—they exhale a ghost of last night’s hoarded water and let me carry that into rock. The succulents—little glass lungs—lean when I go by. I tilt their crowd toward a street that is only a beam, then back again, and they shine for me the way chandeliers perform for drafts.

I am also the rule that says no. At the far ends of the hall, where the walls fold down into coin-smelling clefts, I drop five degrees and become a door snakes will not force. I lie over black water like a light blanket and hold it still enough that it keeps both sky and its absence. Some lives live only by me saying not yet. The salamanders have made me their pace; they do not waste their gills on my ordinary moods. Only when I pull humid out of every seam at once and turn drip into flow do they interpret that as a sentence with their names in it and come.

I keep the powder in motion—the fine, patient skin the room grows back each time. When you walk, I rise in a halo and then take you away one grain at a time, restoring firstness because the chamber prefers its own early morning. If you kneel, I map you and then unmap you. When you leave, I settle into your absence the way snow makes a field pretend no one has ever been.

With the slow calendar I am unambitious and faithful. I wear the ceiling a dust thinner each century, and there is a day in a book when my job will end because there will be no inversion—no warm trapped under sand, no cool hoarded by gypsum—and the hall will be simply outside. I will become a wind without a room. I do not dread it; I simply note it, as I note everything: that the calcite’s singing grows a hair brighter when a cloud walks over the desert at noon; that the lichens throw their flour forward where I am most likely to carry it; that the updraft in the tallest shaft is a reliable neighbor and a wintertime liar.

I eat nothing but I do a eater’s work: I ferry flavors, I make feasts; I deliver hunger to mouths that are only mouths for one hour a day. I own nothing but I am recognized by everyone. Midges make markets in me; bats bank in me; roots find their reach by my temperature; stone speaks in me; light stands up inside me and becomes a creature you would swear you could pet. I do not insist. I learn the gradient and become it. I am almost.

If you needed to find me, find the place your shoulder feels a letter colder than your wrist. Find the point a fernlet has decided is just enough. Put your ear to the thinnest calcite and wait for me to pass; I’ll be the sentence moving under your hand, long as a day, light as a tremor. If you ask who I am, I will pass you a little chalk to taste, show you motes falling up, lay an owl’s breath as a thread across your vision and let it vanish. You’ll know me as the room knowing itself. You’ll know me as the white lung working—light in, water out—and me the tide between.
