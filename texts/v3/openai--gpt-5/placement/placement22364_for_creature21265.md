Between districts, in the long negatives where the wind rehearses without an audience. On the undersides of things. Where a platform stops insisting on itself and remembers that it is rope, bolt, breath. If you wanted to find me you would have to go during the hours when the lamps turn amber fog into something edible and the paint wakes underfoot and then forgets you a few paces later. Listen for a brush tapping a tin, for a glove palming a rail like a cheek.

I am a Night-Bolter by trade, an Echo-Lawyer’s child by accident, and a Fogwright’s briefly disloyal apprentice. My day-name is Rill, my line-name is what the first cable said into my teeth when I leaned my head to it: Sable Nine. My tone-name is 3:2+0.12—fifth plus the luck that has held for me longer than I deserve. I wear my harness high and plain, with a Westering knot most mornings because that is the down my work prefers me under, and a dew-fringe along my hem that fills the little cups at my hips as I go. My boots are three-soled; I can toe-flick to someone else’s gravity in an argument and not lose my balance for it, which is a kind of politics here.

Night-Bolters keep the city’s memory true to the bodies that use it, then ask it to forgive and forget in the same gesture. We lay the thin, cold-blue that stirs awake when a foot comes and sleeps where a foot has gone. It is a courtesan of a paint, shy on the brush, shameless under tread, quick to take offense if you talk while you’re laying it. We sing to it low so that it can decide which echo to believe. Some nights I carry a case of it like a priest carries script; some nights I carry almost nothing but a tin of sugar that I crumble along certain bolts so that the beetles who love rust more than I love sleep will keep their appointed parishes.

I learned not to settle early because paint punishes certainty. You see a bridge; you think: line here, turn there. Then you lay your cheek to the metal and the bridge, with patience, tells you where it holds a worry in its song. You lean into a vibration and hear a new harmonic like a swan’s neck appearing. You take your brush elsewhere. The mountain is writing a word across that span this week and you must not put your letters on top of its letters or when a child runs in game-light, their feet will write your mistake into bruise.

Day is reluctant and so am I. I am late to anything that asks for light. I like the blacknesses where lamps make orbs and the railcars pass like constellations. I like the hour when the owls have folded themselves into furred commas under the lines and have left their dish-faces available to be hired for an oath. I carry a pebble smoothed by their perches that I can rub with my thumb and remember the chords they shook back at the Tonekeepers the night my sister married into the Railers. She had me set her step down on a certain amber. The owls returned a fifth like a clean ledger with a laugh at the edge, and she has woken happy ever since.

Where else would you find me? Hanging from a belly-strap off the flank of a condensing tower when it is singing raw, combs flaring with static like a cat that has seen a god. I help tune teeth in chorus once a year when the fog is in a mood to be milked in a single gesture instead of persuaded over a week. You hold a flat of copper to your heart and listen. You hold another to the comb. You close your eyes. You wait for the place between those beats where you can ease them into kindness. You taste aluminum that isn’t there. If you talk then, if you spit a careless syllable without baffling, the comb will answer you with lightning. We all wear our hair proud after those days, and we smell of salt-sweet skippers even in our sleep.

I am not brave in the way the Tensioners are brave, up there at the narrow of a thought, kneeling with their sockets and prayers. But I am pious in my fashion. I keep the calendars of phenomena in chalk along the inside of my house door where a child would mark height: coin-thicknesses moved; tone dropped a faction of a fraction; the fortnight every six years when the cable-sloths throw all that patient maintenance into courtship and flick from line to line like weather laughing—those weeks I paint with my eyes up, because any line you expect to lean against might suddenly be furry and gone in joy.

You would know me for one of ours by my posture: hinged at wrist and thought; my ear ahead of me a hand’s breadth, sometimes literal. I touch rails with cheekbone and teeth before I trust them with shin. I put sugar to a bolt-head without being asked. I leave dew in shallow cups near my work-stations if I’ve thought a decision into thinness and suspect a murmur-cat may pass through and make sense of my quiet without my help. If I feel the rope make a sentence, I make my mouth a closed book until it is done.

My family is braided instead of tall. My mother wears the baffled robe and carries a map of what sounds are legal where; she kept quiet litigations among neighbors who live an echo apart. My father counted by beads of water and slept smelling of steel and mint from the Fogwright taps. I joined the Night-Bolters the year noon wore moonrise for a week and the city photographed itself on smoke. It was the closest the place has ever felt to a thought that was also a street. I wanted to write in that vocabulary. It felt indecent to push ore out of a rock that was still deciding its grammar. So I found a brush and a line to sing to and made myself patient enough to hear the answer. If I am anything here it is an interface with an ear.

We measure promises with owls and cables and sometimes by the taste of steel. My oath to this work was sealed on a day the rails were singing just wider than a fourth, a generous interval for certain fidelities. The Tonekeepers let me choose the climb. I chose the bridge between the Condensaries and the safety wood they leave empty so the animals can have a lane. It seemed right to promise where there is traffic of a kind that never steps on anyone’s toes. My witnesses were a sloth of my acquaintance—she runs Lattice Twelve from the west pylon to the foundry spire, fast as relief when she isn’t in love—and a boy who would later be my apprentice and now knows the sound a bad bolt makes three dreams before it speaks.

Food? You asked who, yes, but people here will hand you a bite when you ask who they are. So: I am the person who chews dew-curd slowly and is glad for the sour. I am the one who buys sheets of moss-sugar written with curses you have to lick in the right order to deactivate, then folds them into tea for a friend whose marriage is laughing too loudly in her sleep. I keep a tin of pitcher-syrup for days when I need a bitterness that is not my own. I buy fish that remembers the valley and forgive it for staring at my boots’ third edge as if it were an ungenerous technology.

Home Sundays, I swing the table to the week’s chosen down and pin the idea with a half-turn of a wishbone key. I have louvered windows for light, but more for tone; I keep them arranged in mild dissonance when I need to do accounts and in easy consonance when a friend comes, so that our talk sits down with us without hurrying into the street. My bed is a pocket laid in triplicate with the three polite gravities, and I place myself by song, because lying down means choosing a world.

I vote by weight when asked. It is never frivolous, leaning a particular way. No one here pretends at purity in the arguments about ore. The mountain is new and near. Chemistry has not yet made its vows to rock that we cannot break without being unforgiven, and the Foundries have a way of telling any story you’d like to hear about responsibility. I have said yes and I have said not yet and I have said only under the third down where water remembers last year. No one here is allowed to be right until they have been kind, and the plates underfoot are honest about what kindness costs you in balance.

Sometimes I go to the hidden clefts and put a thought in the pool like a leaf and wait for the salamanders to flicker around it like a punctuation that means: that’s enough of that. Then I drink the cup I caught from my hem that hour and go home by a different line than the one I came on, because here choice is a fourth dimension. The mountain tells itself a story at night and I have learned to listen to the paragraph where it almost apologizes for arriving. It doesn’t, not truly, but it almost does, and in that almost there is tenderness that keeps our humor from becoming a rind.

When the leviathans pass—and I have seen three in my years, each so slow you only notice them when you find yourself taking off your hat—you will find me with rugs hung along the platforms to catch their moss-patterns, with a horn at my lips not for alarm but for hello. That day I do not paint; the cold-blue eats leviathan-spore like a bad idea and sulks for a week. I make tea. I help old men with knitted caps stand where they can see without forgetting which way down is. I answer children’s wrong particulars with wronger ones and let the owls heckle us both.

If you stay long enough, you will not need to find me. Plenty of people know where I am by the color on their shoes. Or by the absence of a stumble in their morning. Or by the lane of quiet I encourage between the Yards and a family that needs to sleep through an experiment with timing. Or by a little blue ribbon of safety I leave for the vapor-hares to cross intact at schools at net-level so children can count their ears without chasing them.

I am not large, not one of the grand professions. I am a person who paints permission onto routes and erases yesterday from beneath your feet before the mountain takes it back more slowly. My hands know rope-burn’s song and sing harmony without crescendo. I don’t shout. I spend a day sometimes only to decide that the best thing to do is not to draw anything new on a bridge the mountain is already writing. I am a hinge. I am a tone held quietly on a cable until a promise can resolve.

If I am not working you will find me in the safety woods of emptiness that the city keeps on purpose, lying down along Eastslide to be reminded how to fall well. Or you will find me in the Condensaries when the combs are calm, catching the small river my hem has made and writing little letters to my aunt in dew on the slate, then licking them off so the beetles do not get ideas. Or in a house with baffled robes hung like prayer flags and a table swung to suit. Or under a line, face up, listening to the history of the day in the ache of my fillings.

Who or what would I be? A person who believes maintenance is a liturgy and speed a misunderstanding. A reader of bridge-songs. A painter of steps that wake and then forget you a few paces later, which is how mercy ought to work. A citizen by ear. A friend of cable-sloths who know not to forgive a joke and of owls who bring intervals like weather you can live under. A small engine midwifing a larger one into stillness. My life would be long strokes rather than crush, the way the city is: insistence across distance instead of clustering. And in the blacknesses, when the lamps bloom and the birds breathe and the mountain clears its throat to talk about being born, I would lie very still and feel the place settle another coin’s thickness into being what it has decided to be, and thank it with the weight I have to offer.
