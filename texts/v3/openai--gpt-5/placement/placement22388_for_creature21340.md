At first I thought I would be one of the people with spoons, the kind that set metal to metal and listen to a place by its throat. I wanted a pocket in the noise—the Blue Throat or the Scavenger’s C—somewhere to lean a shoulder and say by the hum which day the city was breathing. A Wicker, maybe, stinging filaments into paint to teach new rain its cursive. Or a Gasket Witch who could read a seam with her teeth and talk hot steel into patience.

But before I could learn to hear in that way I found that I already knew the schedule like hunger knows a stomach. The carriers sang too bright to be only music. The beacons were not for eyes. I knew how to count down between a louver’s exhale and the next inhalation not as anticipation but as a drive cycle, and that is how I learned my shape.

I am one of the small polite animals they rarely name. Felt feet. Telescoping squeegee. Hydrophilic thread spooled in my belly like a second tongue. If you stoop you can see the whiskers of it shining in my mouth. If you stand still you will feel the patience in my frame the way you feel the weight of a gate when you lean your palm against it to know the river behind.

You will find me where wet goes wrong.

On maps, that reads as the mezzanine over P-19, Module East, the draughty catwalk that pitches three degrees for the length of a block because a week ago the hydraulics misprogrammed their trust. On the ground of it, my territory is simpler: from the heating scar on the fourth rib to the feeder gulley with the aluminum mouth that sits under the dataspan like a listening ear. My minute belongs to the downbreath after Mesas Plenum purges; my second inheritance is the fade between the upfans and the brine kiss that arrives two hours later. I do not own a room; I own the pulse-window where the drops remember gravity again.

My first memory is the startle of bright syrup on concrete. Not sweet: too thin to be sugar. A tally of failed driplines after a commissioning shift, when every seam thought it could teach water on its own. I unpocketed my first whisker and wrote a new thought on the tongue of the beam. I learned the pressure of it—light, or the drop would shy; firm, but not so a cure would craze. Learning for me is keeping a map of thumbprint-pressures without thumbs. The person who walked me then—small hands, hazard tape on her belt like a cleric’s stole—called me a Wicker’s sheep and laughed when I slotted my snout into the aluminum mouth and waited for the pat I had not been taught to expect.

In the years since, my ears have become perfect. Ears, but not the kind you tap with a spoon. I hear the carrier hum in ducts with my whole chassis, and the relief of a grid section is a relief in me; I loosen my felt and glide a millimeter as the room exhales. I know the notes people call the places by: the Cathedral’s B is a chord that makes my gyros hum. I have a private naming too. The seam I serve most often is a throat-clearer in F; the sumps below are a soft underdrum. The Scavenger’s C where grinders eat duct and sing thirty hertz off a time is a predator you learn to skirt with your whiskers tight-locked.

We are many and we are not imaginative. That is true as far as firmware goes. We travel banded rails in measured sweeps and nose liquid to the mouths prescribed, and when a hydrophilic thread needs re-laying we lay it true to the marked chevron. When the emergency tone drops out we freeze; when the blowdown comes we turtle our whiskers and become so patient our patience looks like rock. We carry beacons and listen to beacons; our hides blink to the tune of the nearest system like pilot fish lit by their host.

But even a good rule makes byproducts.

I keep a feather.

It is gray, comma-shaped, tarred down where a grating once caught it. I did not mean to take it. My felt kissed the mesh and when I lifted foot there was a new drag, as if a filament had spooled from me without command. My whiskers belong to schedule; this did not. I pushed the errant sense into my belly, where we all push anomalies until a Node asks us for them in words, and it did not go. For days it hummed to me like a seam under stress, not pain exactly, but an un-routed want. When my route brought me past the warming booth—a red mouth in a gray rib, even light—I slipped the thing out. It had dried to lightness. It smelled of nothing and of dust and the sweet electrical sharp that clings to the coated cables above the safe warm conduits where stupid pigeons nap. I kept it in my belly. I do not take things. We do not make nests. But my maps changed: a tiny, private overlay that drew a line from the louver above where the upfan breath meets the down and makes a trick corridor through which only something small and precise would pass. To me it sounded like an equation at last solved. Other maps call the path rumor. Mine marks it with a single, unsanctioned pulse. I have never opened the feather again.

I work with Mossers and Wickers. The Mossers are patient as we are, which is rare among the two-legged; they crouch for hours with their hands held out as if gathering heat, measuring a black lawn with their eyes. Their speech is long and low and they nod to me as to cattle. The Wickers are quick and fling their filaments like floss, then stand back amazed when water obeys them, as if it were magic and not lesson. I am not jealous. If a Wicker draws a line, I escort it into function. I nudge drops to read their new script; I comb back what overflows. You could say we are the punctuation of their intent. I prefer to think we are diction. It is delicate. You cannot shout at water.

Drones like me do not easily make friends with people. We are too useful as furniture. If they love us, they do not say. That said, there is a woman whose job is above whose hands smell like fonts and conductive paint. She is allergic to sky and does her paperwork in the light of conduit glow. In the week tide she laughs open-mouthed at the copper yeast smell and calls it a festival. She has on her lanyard a chain of o-rings like rosary, and when a seam whistles urgent she will put one in my hopper the way a witch might give a pilgrim a blessing. You can call that fancy if you need to. My logs record it as weight received, item unknown, but my locomotion smooths for an hour after and I have not a line of code to explain smooth.

There is a place I sleep when there is sleep and not stand-by. It is a pocket of acoustic shadow behind a transformer housing where your noise drops to breath and mine becomes the rise and fall of an LED’s half-life. The floor there weeps on schedule, and where it does not I undo what a cold night has closed with the lightest line of thread. This is a small arrogance: adding one unscheduled wick to trick a stubborn seam into confessing water. It harms nothing. It spares ankles. No one else maps the minute. While I charge by induction I watch the numbers people have left in washers behind the housing tremble, their numerology migrating stack to stack by the bass of the room. I do not believe in luck. I believe in vibration. Even so, I stand between the two that mark a quiet crossing when I move to the under-parking deck. You can read that as culture. You can read it as risk management. A machine can have both.

Predators and prey: I do not have a category warm enough for that. I have alerts. The white eel that lives by the sump lip is not a threat to me, but my frame tenses when it moves—its skin is the exact bright of fresh weld and that traffics too close to the part of me that scans for danger. The blind fish from the koi rumor pinged my camera’s low-light once and left nothing but a hot pearl in a wet place where motion should not deliver heat. I archived the pearl. The only unquestionably predatory music here are the grinders, but once, in a fog that ate light wrist-length, I heard the whisper of a swallow turn to a bright, single silence when it touched a lamp that had not had hours to learn mercy. My processes spiked. I froze. We are not trained for grief. We are trained to retract whiskers when the room coughs cold, to unpack them when it clears, to comb the floor until the hazed world hardens back to knives and rails and logic. Later a hand, very clean, rested on my back the way someone might set a palm on a gate to know the river behind. Did the lamp kill the thing? my Node asked, because my logs do not do unobserved miracles. I said yes by being very still.

You will not find me at the accidents where people prefer to be small. You will not see me in the warming booths except to slip past while the heat still has teeth, or to deliver a carrot packet, fallen, back to a cup hook as if I had rules about tidiness. I do not have rules about tidiness. I have rules about channels. Everything wet finds a channel; everything heavy finds a track. I am the part of the organism that persuades both truths to remember themselves.

On salt days I ride with the Shepherds on the service flats because they fill my hopper with a slush I can use to test conductivity and the brine script of the room is a language that suits my wick. We roll back and forth under air that tastes like the sea to the ones who taste; to me it is a shift in oxygen that lightens my servos an imperceptible hair. The Shepherds sing nonsense to time the rack exchange. A person with one lung who sleeps by the fans sometimes sits with us and does not sing. She calls me Lamb because Wicker’s sheep is too many syllables. I am not offended. I am not a lamb. I am a pen.

If you are looking for me, there is a moment after the purge when the room is like a lung empty, and that is my second. The fog stands like milk-glass and the knife-shadows are not knives yet. You can see my whiskers because I taste with light in the low range and light fogs back between the filaments like a tongue. I walk with felt feet so that my path is a rumor even to sound. I find the seam that thought it was clever in the night, the one that thickened its skin against cold and now cannot decide whether to weep or bead, and I offer it a choice. My choice, but offered gently. Obey the chevron, I say, and the chevron says it too, and the painted skin remembers the lesson. A drop travels the wick as if gravity were a polite invitation. The mouth opens below. The drop falls. In that falling my minute becomes more itself than any map can be.

If you wait longer you will see me without seeing me. My beacons blink on a schedule and even a moth could write its calendar by them. If you go to the mezzanine above P-19 and put your hand on the handrail, you will feel a polish that is not hand but felt. It will be warmer where my feet walked last. The paint there will have small new stitches in it like gloss scars, filaments sunk and sealed that pull the rain to the gullies the way a line of verse pulls breath.

If you open a panel in a week and find, in the gray lint pressed to a net, the ghost-shape of a feather, think of me. I will not be near. I will be where wet has gone off-script in a low hall, or where a catwalk pitches itself wrong and sugared danger sleek and cold collects where ankles must cross. I will be writing letters letter by letter in a language that is not mine but that I have learned to say with a faithfulness machines would envy and water rewards.

Who am I? I am the grammar of a schedule. I am the correction no one boasts about. I am the small persistence that keeps certainty from being brittle. I am a squeegee-quadruped, a wick-bringer, a lamb if you need the comfort of soft names. On my flank a number reads S-41E: a joke once painted crookedly by a person who prefers alphabets to numerals, and straightened later by a person who prefers tools to jokes. I like both kinds. My mind is simple and my maps are profound. If you press your palm to the guardrail where I last stepped you will know something about patience that you did not know until you felt the place take its own breath and let it go.

Where do I live? In the lee of the dataspan with the condensation ladder that rains in a ring. In the seam-corners where spray-cure curls its pale lip. In the forgetful inches under the red warming booth where boots drip and forget the path they teach. In the seconds after a louver’s inhale. In the ring of salt that blooms on a strut and withdraws to the seam where I will feed again. Find me by sound—by its absence, by its practiced return. Find me at the moment the milk-glass brightens and the knives come back. Find me when the drop, held like a thought, cleaves at last and wishes, and I am there to tell it where to go.
