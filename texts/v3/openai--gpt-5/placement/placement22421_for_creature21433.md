At first I thought I would be a walker, a shadow that learned to put itself down so lightly a lichen would not take offense. I imagined myself on the tramp path you cannot see until you have been told where the moss goes greener with the drip from a bucket. I tried on a shawl the color of wet lichen, a knife that worked by patience, soles that loosened their web when I forgot myself and wanted speed. I put my ear near the stone and found out what the thin air does to sound: it folds it, drinks it, makes your own breath the loudest thing you own.

I stayed long enough to find that walking is only the surface of a life here. Under it, there are kinds of keeping. My hands went to mirrors first. There is a trance in making a skin so clean it refuses dust. You can tell when the topmost micron goes hazy toward blue and when the cloth woven from the grass two terraces over has exactly the right drag. It is a work of exhale. You learn to spend a breath along the stroke, not before it, not after it, so the heat from your mouth does not lay a print on the glaze. That’s one kind of silence.

Then I learned the child’s plates. They lie set along a path between two rooms—each plate cupped at its center so night puts one round drop there. Step wrong in the morning and the drop slides, the plate speaks a little bright truth, and everyone in the long house knows which foot you do not yet know. It took me a season to place myself along those plates without waking them. I carry that way of placing into every other thing.

There’s a hum that lives underfoot here, the scroll that turns with the pushed water from what the nets persuade out of the air. It is not a machine’s noise. It is a thought half-kept. When I first heard it through the rock rather than with my ears, I wanted to be that hum. To give power without edges. To run all day with a hand’s warmth and sleep with the shutters open, letting the heat go to the sky. To be the kind of help that never has a corner.

But the third law kept turning its face toward me. Do not let light become air. The first time I put my palms on a coupler, everything else I had practiced stood still inside me like a bucket of water kept perfectly full. The rope was already drawn—hundreds of strands sleeved into something with tendon in its mind. The valley below had signed for a hand of day. The rope took that hand across three ridges and a slot. My work was the join. A touch told me whether the day inside was milked a little, whether air had been allowed to eat the edge.

To do it right, you bring a cloth twelve times folded, a ceramic box with its own small night under the lid, water that has never known a smell. You wash the glass the way you wash a wound that is not yours. You wait for a cloud to put a coin into the kiln’s eye so the world cools a breath without complaining, and then you lick a seam with the thinnest heat a person can keep steady without hurrying. You draw a silk of new fiber and dress it around the join as if it were a knuckle you are promising to keep. Your other hand never leaves the rope. It listens for that faint inner brightness that is not visible yet but will be if you are careless. I have stood with my two hands talking to a line of day for a whole hour, and neither of us bored.

The learning was a ledger of slowness. When to open the shutters along the fiberhouse so the night will drink our heat and give us morning like oil, unctioning every bearing with dew. How high to lift a black sheet so that a worker two hundred paces away sees the shadow push across her rock at the speed we agreed on yesterday. How to lift the quartz tooth with a breath less or more when the noon has money in it. The first time I helped draw, it felt like mending in reverse, as if I were taking a thread out of the bright and making it into something that could be paid out and trusted not to fray. There are three heartbeats to it that you come to know in your wrist instead of your ear.

I live along the couplers, mostly. If you were to look for me, you would find me where the rope fattens to meet itself, kneeling on a wedge of moss we keep wet on purpose so the mountain forgets we are there. I keep a wicket of cloth over my own mouth because scent is a kind of noise. I count the ticks from a cistern’s throat one ridge away—the drip that lands inside a chamber carved so your ear can tell an hour has passed without a bell. I know where the ravens idle and where they test with black tongues for badly-dressed seams. I leave dull bones under their favorite stones because it saves us all from argument.

On market days I go down to the law house at mid-height, where the louder world lowers its voice. I talk with my lids. We measure against a wall striped in units the valley accepts: a room-length, a hand of day, the line that does not move at noonline. The traders come with pockets of powder that makes glass less willful, with bits of metal we do not make here because the exchange rate of noise-to-need does not favor it. They hand weight for light. We hand them light braided into a thing that does not spill. No one raises their voice. The house keeps its corners.

My sleep is in a room so thin to the sky that it forgets it is a room when the shutters are open. I lie under a sheet of glass that is window at sundown and radiator by midnight. I sleep under my own breath; you can feel it pocketing in your ribs, a savings account. A baby in our house sleeps strung over a shallow basin that collects every cough as a drop we will use. In the morning I write my name with rime along the lip of the metal we put out to shine to space. I read the names others have written by how long their letters last. If I love someone, I leave their initial under a panel where shade at noon will not erase it too soon.

There are days of permission. I am allowed to laugh and watch how my mouth makes a beard of little glories along a pylon as the sudden humid spends itself. Once a season we set two mirrors to face and draw a bright coin on a rock that has never earned one, for no reason but to know how it would look. There is a syrup we boil with light and patience, a sweetness that has no flower in it. We take that to the mid-heights for children from the valley. They taste our way of living the way we taste theirs—their copper, their clatter.

I keep other small offices. I am a tuner of hinges that bend first before they ever ring. I am the hand that closes doors until their seams lie down. I am a mender of straps that hold to rock until they decide not to and then serve as soil for bromeliads. I am the person who walks out at the almost dark when an edge of panel needs to change its angle by a finger; I carry a weight in my palm and set it down as if it could teach a stone to hesitate. I am a reader of anchor fields, of the scabs on the ridge where we once believed height alone was harvest and learned otherwise when the mountain answered our hammer with a long small fall.

When I am old I will mend more than I draw. My forearms will stay carved from the polishing years, but the steadiness in my hands will matter more than their strength. I will teach children to read the drip-clock by listening with their teeth. I will let them take me to the frog kettles like a secret. I will show them the place where a bright coil left uncoupled once drew moths, then bats, then weather, and I will say the third law without a breath of sermon. They will practice the plates path and you will hear a note now and then like a kindness because failing is how the mountain keeps us soft.

Dying here is not averting your face. It is turning into a better wall. When I go quiet, they will take me to a niche with a good night aspect and I will lose my wet slow as a plan. Someone will lift a net over me that makes more dew than my body does. A season later my bones will sit into a place where a hinge would have rung and doesn’t now because of me. My hollows will be rooms for spiders that explain gravity to drops so our turbines think their thoughts again.

Sometimes I am not a person. On cold mornings I am a line on a far rock that lengthens when my friend lifts his sheet. When the fog comes with rules of its own, I am a net that learns the new rule in a minute and obeys it all day. When a raven stands on a coupler and does something like a question with his head, I am his colleague—a keeper of a different ledger—looking away on purpose so he can prove himself clever without cost. At noon when a quartz tooth holds enough sun to remember it later, I am the bridge and the harp both, and the hand that is neither, lifting light out of light and making a line.

If you came to find me, you would not at first. You would feel the lichen stiffen after I passed. You would hear your own breath and be introduced to it as if it were a separate animal. You might see a bright seam along a rope go from a guess to a confidence while a pair of hands you barely see sits on it like a promise. If you waited for the sun to close an inch near a sump and looked for the two darker patches where feet honor themselves into the ground, I would come. I would set down a small box that makes its own cold and a folded cloth that knows my hands. I would tilt my head so your words do not spill. I would ask you the weight of your rain and whether your town keeps its corners. I would tell you, if you asked who I am, that I am the part of the ridge that keeps light inside its skin.

And if you stayed long enough to learn the rules we do not say loudly, you would see that I am also what this place made of patience—a hinge that yields first, a breath that is banked, a finger that measures and never points. I am a keeper of joins. I keep the mountain asleep and the day from becoming weather, and I spend heat like a miser with a generous heart. I am quiet not because I am small but because the work is big and would rather not be dropped.

So that is where you would find me. At the fattest point of a glass rope, kneeling, one palm listening to the day inside, the other poor with tools that do not ring, counting the soft ticks of water that say time has gone by without harm. Above me the bright hard sky. Below me the cloud towns waiting for the long unspooling—a room-length, a hand of day, a noonline—while the ridge I stand on keeps its corners by grace and by craft and by hands that try to live like moss.
