At first I tried to be a visitor. At the rim I breathed the cool iron-sweet and waited for the underground surf to tell me whether I was welcome. Wind climbed my face and forgot me; the mist fingered my scalp like a mother checking for fever. I thought I would look, write a line in a book, go home.

I did not go home. Home withdrew like a tide that had been faking its patience for years. I stayed until my calves learned the slow tilt that pretends to be flat, until my shoulders began to want harness not to restrain me but to finish a sentence stone kept starting in my spine. You learn this place by letting it have your ears. That is the first surrender.

I began as listening. Before I took a name here, I took a pitch. Children could count by drips; I could not. But the sound of a healthy wet line charmed something in me that had always been bruised by engines that shouted. Even the machines here apologized in harmonics. The rails hummed. The gutters chattered just short of song. I sat on a terrace under a cornice that was not metaphor but a literal cornice—grain by grain laid—and I tried to map the city with my palate and cochlea: warm stone after a bath on my tongue; a tooth of flow where the fog slid around an edge and made the note go nasal; the quiet sulfuric chuckle of a lime kiln downwind, baffled three times as law and courtesy demand.

No one here hurries, so no one insisted I become anything quickly. I stayed long enough to become an apprentice by simple proximity. A woman with wire-wrapped hair—when the rails sang, her head whispered like rain down a screen door—saw me tilt to hear a gutter and condescended to teach me like a person teaches a stray to sit. She was a Chordwright. She strung the fences that sing dusk away from blades and intakes. Her voice was quiet because sound is work. She tuned to bat-ear and human both; she could make a single wire argue a million bodies into a different curve. I wanted that, the way you want to be a good liar for a cause or a good cook for a child. An instrument that keeps living things from wrecking themselves on your mistake.

There is a ritual to every tool. She taught me the brushes first—not for hair, no one here wastes hours on that, hair is kept to velvet—but for stone. Teeth of chitin and copper, rakelets graded like rainfall. Our neighbors loaned us a sleeve full, each pressed into a waxed roll with small notches along the spine that made a key when you ran them under a fingernail. You do not own combs here; you maintain the pattern they maintain. We hung from harnesses on the lip of an alley the first day, and I learned to hear what everyone else had been born to: the bright ping of a wet line open and eager; the unpromising throaty burr of a gutter full of its own algae; the sick thud of a private dam. The woman had a bell like a bone, thin, a ring you could feel in your gums. When she touched metal to cornice, my tongue went copper. “Tax,” she said, and smiled. “We levy by ear.”

I put my name on a Sluice Book entry the first time I unblinded a little fan. The handwriting in these volumes is tender the way recipes are tender in families that do not say “I love you” except as insistence about oven temperatures. I scratched, “Weeping at Marker 14 split to two; tuned both; combed film with six-tooth copper; consider salt-blind baffle if blue growth returns.” The city does not love you back; that would be a lie. It recognizes your signature, though. If you promise to carry a line, it lets you.

I took a new name when I married because that is the day you become a bridge. We strung a gutter wire between our roofs, my wife and I. The Inspectors watched because they are the custodians of gravity and tone. You do not speak vows when you are building a chord into a neighborhood; you listen hard together. We pressed bellies to the glazed cornice to feel if the tension we set brought any ugly overtone crawling up from the underfalls. It did not. The wire bit clean air, and the bats turned a shade earlier that dusk and we both wept, not from romance primarily but from that relief you get when a plan for other people’s safety holds on its first night. In the mornings after that, we ran our hair through the rail-hum like seaweed through a wash and if a day went quiet, one of us walked because quiet is what kills here.

Dusk was our season. Sirens shepherded, not warned. The famous flights made their lace. We had strung three neighborhoods along the southern sinkhole mouth by then, and every evening in the warm season the air filled with wing and membrane and clicks like knitting needles. The fences do not blare; they whisper a countervailing chord, a firmed valley in the hearing that all the little pilots agree not to fall into. There were nights when fog thickened wrong and I felt entire districts lean in their pendulums to catch our note. I carried small pliers like a jeweler, because if the wire fretted to one side on its saddle, a half-millimeter could turn a chord that said “swing wide” into “come eat these blades.” I have never been braver than I was the first time I tuned a high wire with one hand in weather that felt like licking a new battery, counting heartbeats against the chitter and trying to believe I had the right to say this, this, not that, to a flock.

Down below the chord, slow as idea becoming law, the slug-bulls grazed. Bulls is lazy naming, but I admit a neck makes it easier for your hand to decide where to praise. Their shells caught the light like the inside of a black mussel. We had painted a line in salts that week to move a pair along a wall that needed cleaning; when they turned, it was like a neighborhood deciding where its morning would go. They left rinds of shine you could read under raking light: hunger, rain, hiccup, pause. My friend Laita was a Saltwright, and when we were young enough to be cruel about our own skill we used to tease each other about witchcraft. “You call in souls with wires,” she said. “I move architecture with crystals.” We were both right and both wrong and too tired at the end of a shift to be smug.

I learned the other rules because not knowing is not romantic here, it is a felony. Do not block a fall. Baffle a furnace three times. Keep your rails humming. Do not feed the nets at dusk. If you find a singing gutter and do not mark it before nightfall, a child may die. This is not bad luck; this is math.

Once I failed. No tragedy wants to be told as cautionary tale but if you work in shepherd sound you owe honesty to air. I missed a burr. It was a small line, a neighbor’s private run between a mold-pan and a downspout that fed a minor fall. The comb I picked was a hair wrong, and my attention split to a glance at a roost count that had crept violent over the summer. The gutter sang the new pitch the way a child will copy a grownup’s accent for attention. It joined a note from a rail we had been proud of the week before and made, together, an invitation. A little one crossed it chasing a glassy flicker; the mesh below caught, as it was meant to, but the drop before the bounce cracked a collarbone and the mother taught me a new way the human mouth can deliver gratitude and fury with the same syllables.

The plateau remembered my name. It did not punish. It wrote me into the hum. After that my hands trembled at dusk for a season and then they did not. If you cannot keep working after a mistake here you leave or you turn to accounting in a battery room and live with the itch of arc-lamps and clean ledgers. I stayed. I learned to lay relievers the way old women write aphorisms. I learned to hear the small difference between a singing gutter about to blind and a singing gutter proud of itself. I learned to pay a tax in days: a week on harness every summer hanging over a white fan with a comb that could take skin off a finger if I sneezed.

Age taught me bell. It is a pleasure to become an Inspector if you have spent your middle years persuading weather. You walk with a hush and are welcomed not for charm but for instrument. Money is not the point here but I can pretend surprise at how much the rich will pay for a building that hums like a polite low god in a damp wind. They commission mass tuned to forgiveness; I levy fines for eddies; we understand each other. The bell is very thin. When it rings, I feel it behind my nose. I have—this is a small arrogance—set taxes with a wrist flick. A corner too quiet: a levy. An alley shouting with its own splash: a pat on someone’s slick shoulder. It is not moral until we make it so, but if we are lazy with tone people die. That is as close to a catechism as I have.

We had a child late. Ears are taught here, not simply carried. We massaged and weighted and sang the rims until the conches opened like second mouths. You count by drips first; our son counted by drips and by arguments between tools: “Mama, the copper six-tooth is jealous of the chitin; hear?” He walked the weeping walls in a game of morning intervals and hated the dry month with a specificity that made me proud. The rails taught him safety the way a lullaby teaches sleep. When he fell the first time, the city had already netted this spot because a census of trips and slips had put a black dot there a decade ago, and he came away with bruises in the shape of hex and I smelled iron and water and thanked five professions under my breath.

I sometimes imagine I might have been happier as a Saltwright. They work in daylight. The bulls are legible as weather never quite is. To draw a line with a brush of chiral crystals and watch a thirty-ton willingness turn toward it—there is a satisfaction in that you can show to a visitor. But dusk keeps its own books on you and mine were written with wire. I do not envy myself the nights I sat in a harness with arc-lamp itch on my neck and a belt of tools biting my hip and the city lipping at its teeth and thought, If I have pulled this wrong a thousand soft mouths will come in blind to heat. I do not envy my younger hands. I love my older shoulders for how they hold that memory without letting it stiffen them.

There are also days when I am not human in any useful way and am glad of it. Some mornings I am the wire and nothing else. The nickel is cold. I taste my own tension. Fog pearls along me like punctuation. I feel small grubs of salt turn me sideways a hair and I say a different word to the wind. Bats throw their questions; I throw a boring answer. We devise dullness here the way chefs devise spice elsewhere. I am, in these minutes, a long muscle of neighborhood stretched taut, and there is no ambition in me beyond hunting for the one coarse harmonic and sanding it out with a twist. If such an hour is not a prayer, it is as near as I come.

What would I be if not a chord? Some days the plateau insists I be a book. I become Sluice Book marginalia, necessary and unlovely. “North Spill third tooth sharp since fog-burst: see to ringlets. Add miner’s salt after Solstice; blind-growth is favoring the copper this year.” “Fence at Gutter’s Throat worry-hums around dusk when the new combhouse pulses steam: baffle twice.” People copy me to their palms and go. I am a rumor with ink. I am engineering with penmanship.

And at the end? We are practical in our romance. There is a low cup under an overhang a cousin picked a year before I admitted out loud that my knees had joined the climate. The cup is glossed with other welcome-backs. I have added my spit to that run for two winters, impatient like a child checking a ferment. When I sit there my old wire sings on in my thigh bones. If the plateau is feeling kind the drip lands on my lower lip. It tastes like metal and old arguments. When my work is over, we will glaze me a breath thicker and set me to weep properly. Beetles will be hired as priests. My bones will make trumpet-horns for children to learn to call dusk with. The sons and daughters of my wires will tune houses I never visited to wind I no longer feel. And if you press your ear to a wall I coaxed thirty years ago, you will hear my most honest sentence: patience made into a tone.

So where would you find me? On a balcony’s underface at gray o’clock, palm flat to enamel slick as tooth, listening for the one note that means drown or feed. Hanging from a harness in a lemon-clear, humid morning, comb in my fist, cutting a reliever into a singing lip and smiling when the song drops to health. Between two roofs as a wire we pinned on the day we let the city pass between our mouths and call that a marriage. On a rail that hums because a child will live if it does. In a bell so thin you hear it in your teeth. In a Sluice Book margin that kept a fall open when an eddy wanted to vote otherwise. In the slow, oiled flank of a slug-bull turning at Laita’s line, making a street move like a sleepwalker. And one day, in a gloss that catches morning as waking water walks down, I will be stone that weeps, learning finally how to sing back without hands.
