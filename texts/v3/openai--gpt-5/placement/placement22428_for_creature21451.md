At first I think I’ll be the fox. The neat, dry line through stacks of pipe fits the bone-memory of wanting a path that asks nothing except that I keep moving. I like that he knows which pallets to skim and which trays still smell of fish. I like his single look back past the buffer painted hazard-yellow, the way the world ends there, politely, and opens into scrub like a sentence ending and another beginning without drama. But when I follow him in my head, I leave the sound of the place behind too easily, and I miss it. The fox can afford not to listen. I can’t.

Then I try the turbine—the organ note that puts its cool hand under everyone’s ribs and keeps it there. There’s a holiness in that patience, a pulse large enough to encompass frogs and knives and dogs and a broom rasping concrete without altering itself. I stand in the turbine’s breath and count heartbeats that aren’t mine, and for a while I think this is what I am: the thing that makes continuity feel like care. But the turbine doesn’t notice condensation threading down its own elbows. It doesn’t know what a slow drain looks like when it is almost about to be a problem and not yet. I want to know that. I want the scale where attention matters.

The mesh calls to me, too. There’s theater in it—the bright corridors of it, the steel masts in a wind that never stops being slightly oblique, the singing that happens when a morning sits on its paws and refuses to leave. I want to lay the first cups under the down-pipes and name fog by touch—salt-shy, kitchen smoke, pearl-thread—and hum a line I’ll never admit to under any net. But I like how consequence feels in the hand more than how omen does. Nets are for catchers of beginnings. I belong to the middle of things, to what you do because of what you’ve already decided to do.

By the time I’ve walked from the buffer to the canning line and back along a corridor of crushed shell that leads to nobody, I know where I am. I live in the long intervals, where the place isn’t beautiful until—you know—and then is, permanently. I am one of the gutter-walkers.

If you’re looking for me, find the line of pale mineral on the leather where my shoes splash when I misjudge a basin by a finger. My badge fogs at the door line every morning and I wipe it with my sleeve without looking. There’s a rag in my back pocket washed thin as gauze and a pencil stub holding hair back over my ear. I keep a coil of snake slung like a tame thing when a slow drain wants talking-to. I have the schedule’s chords in my bones; I can feel the small hush before the three low notes the way a farm kid feels rain in his molars.

My day is consequence and company. I touch the back of my wrist to a catch basin at the turn of the shift and know who went on early and who’s late, because laminar has a feel to it when it’s right. I walk the runnels that crown every roof and make sure each one can hand its load to the next the way you would pass a sleeping child. I listen to the turbine’s baritone and the canning line’s nimble clatter for the half-note that means somebody would like attention but isn’t being rude about it yet. I retie a sagging sling under a rib of ductwork because fog prefers that rib and that sag isn’t a romance, it’s a problem beginning.

At noon the warning horn gives its chords and I stand where the catwalk’s shadow has a bite to it and let it rain beneath me. I watch it choose its braided ways along slate-colored channels—we don’t let water touch ground here without an invitation—and I learn something new about its manners every time. Somebody’s stuck a cup under a down-pipe, first one of the day, like ceremony, and I grin because we are not superstitious, not really; we’re faithful. We time our kindnesses.

Kids race leaves in the channels after the purge and I clap with the willows because there’s something to celebrate in doing a thing right. I stop one small boy from whistling under the west mesh—not because the math is mine to defend but because boundaries are how the place remembers its shape—and he takes correction the way the ferns do water: by unfurling after. Later he will make prints on a frost web and for a moment I will be jealous of firsts again.

By midafternoon my tools smell a little like cardamom from the canteen and a little like coolant from the hall, and I can tell without glancing which conveyor will jump when the flow breaks because I’ve learned its fidget. I write tags with half my mind and the rest listens. The crane’s hoist winds down with its long vowel and it’s healthy today; I don’t have to send a note. The turbines keep their hands in place. Swallows hold their posts in the low drafts and edit the air for me—thank you, I say, and mean it. The stray dog named Safety blinks awake when the horn announces the purge and I pretend not to see him steal a place of warmth against the cabinet’s side in the quiet that follows. He’s a better rule follower than I am in a few crucial ways.

Sometimes I name leaks. The polite one. The brute. The gossip. It’s not fancifulness so much as a bookkeeping of temperament; reputation lives here in every small thing that repeats itself. If a slow drip wants to be melodramatic I sand its stage lights down to dull; if a gusher waits until the shift turns I catch its schedule and correct it without scolding. Pride wears plain clothes. It looks like a newly added bracket that has exactly one more hole than needed in case someone smarter than me thinks of a better way next month.

At night I move with a headlamp along the seams like a stitcher, drawing a small, precise cave around whichever bolt or elbow needs a glove on it. The frog choir takes the load when the day slips off, and there’s a minute where their cords and the turbine’s gentle tyranny become one instrument and I feel more citizen than worker. The berms make a bowl of sky. Out past the hazard-yellow, the fox peels himself into the scrub, and I wish him the anonymous grace I don’t want for myself.

I have a partner on the meshes who can read a panel from a hundred meters and tell you if it’s a singer or a sleeper. We match more by pitches we don’t flinch at than by any taste, and across shifts we leave each other small weather reports blown dry by noon. We have a hi-vis collar on the back of a chair for the next dog that chooses us. Our apartment is clean because the kind of beauty this place teaches is the kind you can mop. We hang laundry on a bar meant for something else and learn patience by the drip line’s progress toward the floor.

Some mornings in winter the frost writes every fence into script and nobody touches the day before the turbines put their warm breath under its sentence and erase it word by word back to rivulets, to cups, to tanks. That was when I said it the first time. Beautiful, I said, and then I never stopped. I carry the word between shifts like a cup under a local rain and I offer it, when I can, to whoever looks like they’re measuring the place with their eyes.

If I were water itself, I would say: I remember wandering. I consent to schedules because somebody here is gentle with me. I bead my hands along the underside of your handrails because I like the way your fingers twitch to help me finish what I’ve begun. I surprise you on purpose, once a week, to keep you interesting. I become a lens at noon so you have to notice the ground differently. I go to orchards inland and the trees drink me without asking where I came from. I come home in names.

But I’m not water, not quite. I’m the kindness that changes outputs. I’m the small clerk of schedule, the person entrusted with the pause before the purge and the way a diagram touches the ground. I test ladders with my heel. I keep the cuffs of my coat turned down because the shade remembers winter. I know where not to whistle. I live where the place keeps its conscience tucked—beneath the ductwork, above the gutters, along the clean edges of paint reapplied in squares. I am a gutter-walker, tender of consequence, and I belong in the measured rain.
