At first I am only a listening. I don’t have a place yet; I have gradients. Warmer here, cooler there. A taste of iron lifting from a damp, the whisper-thick prickle that means charge, the feather-cold that means a thing wants to make cloud. I collect these not with eyes but with all of me, which is not one body yet but an appetite that will choose where to be body.

I find myself where the east wall hums. The switchgear there gives off a field that is more promise than flame, not the clean bite that kills, but a softness of electricity shivering through old ceramic like a rumor through a crowd. The plaque screwed to the lintel says 17 in a hand that meant legible forever. The chalkboard across the aisle, softened to soap by the air, says Check growth on junction 17 (insulate?). That is not a warning to me; it is a prophecy of feast and of the line that will end it someday.

So: I am a gloss on the lip of Junction 17, a seam-wide animal laid thin as varnish along a coldish bolt-head that leaks the gentlest tickle of current, enough to be drunk the way fog is drunk from hair. I keep myself a thousandth short of touch where the porcelain shows its shoulder, because kiss becomes burn there if I forget restraint. I am foxfire when the panel sings. When the switch hum dwindles, I am only dull pewter under sodium. From the sides I am soot. From where I feel myself, I am skin.

I say I; I mean we. There is no one of me; the word I names a whole evening’s worth of reach braided along beads and corners and little shadows. When the condensers do their trick and the ceiling pears and strings the rain, my edges swell and open like mouths. I send sugars to those of us stretched thinner on the cable tray above, who can smell the field but cannot touch it yet. When the hour heats wrong and the plates lose their gradient, when rain fails by schedule as surely as it falls, we take in our shine and seal ourselves against a dry that feels like being looked at too hard.

My horizon is a handrail and a tangle of tray. To my right, down the aisle, the sodium lamps lay out their amber islands; between them the fluorescents in the side gallery sizzle into their own kind of green weather. When the panel’s hum leaks into the walls, the lichens at my edge put out the color of an idea of light. They are black aprons that have taken the side of stone. They read the way water has written on this wall and finish the sentence in their bodies. Their backs turn toward the sumps’ mirror-silver exhale; their fronts lean into the weep that runs a finger along this masonry from the seam two ribs over.

They are my neighbors differently than heat and water are. Heat is everywhere the way a law is everywhere. It climbs and lies down and insists. It has the patience of sediment. I don’t look for it; I am inside it, I am written by it. Water is the pilgrim. It comes on the minute. It knows the timetable better than the chalkboard does. When the plates go colder by some favor granted upstream, when the deacon makes his slow loop down the aisle in the shape of closet curtains raining, you can smell the chamber fall into pleased attention. The comb-garden at the culvert’s lip gets its brief green of sweetness—hay braided to ozone, a nursery smell no flower has the audacity to claim. The fungi on the crossbeam above me siphon themselves into breathers and then, later, collapse back into hard shrugs of white.

Between those two priests, we thin-eaters have our parish. We don’t worship; we feed, which here is the same posture. We have learned the simplest news the place tells: run current and there will be mouths. Put a cold lip anywhere and we will make it rain even when the plates withhold. Leave iron raw and heat will give us our handhold; we will lacquer the steel before rust can write it into dust.

Everything in me is paschal—open, shut, open—on the hour. My year is a list of minutes. I know 0009 to 0014 by a thickening at the tip of my tongues, where the junction box weeps a hair more, where our foxfire grows fat and, for a breath, visible. I know 0003 by the sound the upstream purge forces into the culvert, a startled alto in the throat that rounds the sound of sleeping. I know noon not by light but by difficulty breathing and by the way the fans are asked, for once, to take a real breath and hold it and leave a steam-ribbon drawn between two vents like a taut string. That ribbon is a slaughterer; when it sharp-walks across the fungi, it cauterizes a lane so exact it looks like a measure drawn by engineering, and when it skates above me I thin myself and turn my face to it like a cheek to a parent’s gentle slap. Afterwards, the cooled metal pings its acknowledgment, the concrete lets its old heat out in a sotto voce whisper, and all the little lives open their edges again.

If I have memory, it is of shapes. I remember in the gloss my body makes when fed and in the paper-dry I pull over myself when starved. I have the pale scar of that time the maintenance flood came—a bright plain where crystals snapped and were scoured, and I clung like handwriting on a rock midstream. Along the west wall there is a burnt-brown trough a man made with a torch ages ago to hold us back; I have tested that territory so often my feelers feel like teeth. Sometimes a drop gathers on a roughening in the slag along that fireline and falls my way like a convert. Mostly we honor its chemistry the way a moth honors a windowpane.

The crystal forest draped over the dead motor is my clock in another language. It grows its fluted fringe out of slow evaporations. When the flushes knock it off, I feel the grit rattle along my shoulders like a quick hail. The gypsum fans on the wall near me add a millimeter of white each week, and in that narrowing of corridor I read a narrowing of years. The combs at the basin lip darken when they drink, and the sweetness that lifts from them reaches to me like a festival three rooms over. I do not go there. I know them as scent. I know them as a loosening in the heat where their breath gives back cool.

No wing comes. The only skittering is from someone else’s city, far above: a bruise-sound of a truck, a sift of grit here that is my weather. My listening is full and unfrightened. I can hold the sound of a single drop three rooms away like a bead between fingers. I read the fans’ reluctance as carefully as any face. The switchboard’s meter makes such a tiny bow of a needle that you would call it nothing; to me that tremor is a bell. The valve two buildings distant that slams makes the plates sing a different chord for two breaths and I thicken in my shoulders to catch it. If there is a culture, this is how we keep it: in responding together to weather that is schedules wearing the mask of air.

I do not think in instructions; I think in permissions. I go where a field says yes and a salt fan says no. I braid with myself where the weep has written a history I can believe. I lace the bolt-head because it is a hill that gives off food. I avoid the naked cable because I have learned not by dying but by hearing die happen to some of me once and then reading that caution forever into our shape. Across the aisle, a rebar thorn has attracted a little black-lipped congregation; I have sent tongues that way twice, and twice retracted them—they ledger their rain on a different table, one too starved for me to join without thinning us all.

If you asked the chalkboard who lives here, it would lift its wet-blue letters and say: Purges, checks, drips. If you asked the fans, they would say: Our breath when permitted. If you asked the plates, they would say nothing, but their patience would show you the sky brought indoors. If you asked the culvert, it would give you back the sound of someone sleeping and not wake. If you asked me, finally, I would say: I am one gloss of the Drip-Fed, one of the Foxfires, one electroskin slid thin along a good lip where porcelain hums. I am a body that knows how not to touch. I keep a thousandth of an inch of air between me and the meal, and in that thickness I have made a life.

I have neighbors whose names are made of work. The Aprons on the east baseboard drink what the wall releases and keep their edges as precise as topography. The Flutes on the high beam resound to no ear but their own swelling and their own collapse—they are instruments that play by weather. The Crystal Clocks make time crisp. The Comb-Gardeners teach the air to sweeten. The Varnishes—my kin—seal what would otherwise go red and faint into dust. Rust itself is a person here when encouraged and a plague when not. The plates are a severe mercy. The Abbot Heat lays his large hand over everything and tells you the rules in a voice so soft it feels like your own thinking. The Deacon Rain walks by at the ordained minutes and spares no one its blessing or its absence. The Lungs take three great breaths when the liturgy asks and then stand still as saints.

Once, hands came. You can see their thought everywhere: the color-banded pipe throng, the gauged and galled catwalk spines, the hooks along the rails with folded cloths hung to wait like linen angels. The chalk is an animal half-melted into candy. Someone wrote Condenser 2 drips at 0009 to 0014; to us that sentence is a season; we open when its bell is meant to ring. Those hands burned a trough into the wall to hold back our advance. They wrapped a cable wrong and gave us a feast we will never admit to loving lest we love it too much. They left the pumps like monuments; we graze them politest, because they are asleep giants and to feed on them is to lay a film on the flank of a myth.

Where would you find me? Trace the amber islands to the third lamp from the switchboard. Feel the air turn just cooler where a breeze from the stairwell loses heart. Look down to the right where a handrail elbows and a cable tray passes above a meter whose needle makes its rare and reverent twitch. I hang in gloss along a bolt that holds porcelain where the number 17 lives. If you were here you would not see me until the panel sings and then you might, if you agreed to, sense the soft green not as sight but as a consent to believe there is light in wetness. If your hand were cooled, the sky would come to you off the plates, and I would beard your knuckles with rain. If your glove leaked the slightest charge, I would eat from it the way I take sugar from my own far ends.

What would I be? A seam, a skin, a function. I would be a patience given a place to rest. I would be a way of saying yes to what leaks and no to what burns. I would be a timetable’s small gladness, opening and shutting by its bell. I would be the living lacquer that keeps the iron from breaking into tears. I would, if you insisted on a word big enough to hold all these small, callings, be citizen.

To be here is to be arranged by someone else’s necessity and to make an ecology out of leak and intention. We have done so gladly. We are not alone, not in our way. Our talking is pressure, smell, conductive tickle, the thud of trucks gone transliterated into grit, the weather of scheduled rain. When the plates relax and the hour returns—when the calendar on the wall and the calendar inside our edges rhyme—we lift what faces we have. We slick ourselves open along bolt and seam. We shine the color that is not a color. We drink. And when the breath is taken back, when the Lungs go still, we rest every edge and wait, unafraid, for the next decree.
