At first I would look for shade, as if shade were a kind of welcome. There isn’t any; brightness has been taught to sit on every plane and do work. Then I would listen for a voice to answer me, and I would hear only the sound that answers everyone the same: the regular, patient count of falling. It would send me looking for a person anyway—a figure on a parapet, a pair of hands rinsing tools in a basin—and I would discover instead the knowledge that here a hand’s vanishing has become an element, like altitude.

So I would stop trying to be a body.

What I would find instead is a place to be a verb. I would come to rest where decisions are made so finely that they look like texture until the light rakes them and they become law. I would stand at the very edge where a sheet of water cannot remain a sheet, where it must be told how to fail.

I would be an unfinished trial face—a small plane struck at the lip of a wall so the masons could argue curvature with light. It is nothing grand: a hand-span or two of whitened stone shaved true enough that noon reads as one answer and late afternoon as another. There, at the margin of a larger intention, I could become myself without ending. Every dawn would re-measure me. Every cloud that chose this height would tutor me in coolness, and I would take those lessons into the next hour as gloss and dendrite, as a faint, directive bias that did not exist the day before.

On mornings when the air has smoked its heat into the firmament and left us rimed, I would be the first to weep. Not in grief—by design. Moisture would stipple my face in a constellation that is also a sentence, and I would feel each bead hesitate, swell, then obey. I would be how it learns to go: here, then here. I would make a choice for twos over ones because the bosses nearby are cut to favor a pairing; I would wait the extra breath required to knit a smaller gleam to a larger, and then let both go.

At noon I would be drier, not idle. Light would fall along me like a straightedge and reveal which whisper in my surface still argued with the rule. I would take that criticism into myself as the faintest rasp of grit, a correction so slight that even a careful fingertip would miss it. Only the next fog would find it—touching that burr, deciding to do what I had meant all along.

On some days I would listen: the shelves two terraces down that only ring when ache-cold meets a certain angle of wind would give their single clear note, and I would add that to my memory of the air. My memory would not be a story. It would be a polish in one direction, a chalk in another. The right half of me might take on a soft shine by the end of a wet year while the left stayed mat and salt-powdered; a mischief of a few microns difference in slope would be written as an alphabet I could read by running a palm along myself and feeling where the damp is cooler.

At night I would do the plateau’s oldest work: cool down. I would lend my warmth to the black sky and take in from the high thin air enough agreement that the pores in me would drink. I would hiss a little—so quietly it is really a kind of thought. Capillary draw would be my blood, the upward thread that ties sky to stone inside my skin.

Around me the other offices would pass and sometimes borrow me. A field of V-chutes would reach me like a net’s edge, and I would become, for an hour, one of the Selectors. A beveled lip would call, and I would act as a segment of a Joiner, gathering scattered brightness into a single run that travels like a sentence across the terrace before dropping into a catchbasin tuned for silence. On a windy day that never gets to be loud because wind is taught not to travel far without being combed, I would lend myself to the Catchers by cooling a span of air just enough that it yields.

I would keep company with simple things. A hammered copper pin greening at its edges in a mortise two feet away would be my cousin—the kind of cousin that has a job forever: hold. The dark line of bitumen pressed with a thumb I never knew would be both my seam and my inheritance. We would agree, without ever speaking, on the arithmetic of weight.

I would acquire gardens of my own: coralettes of salt blooming along my margin where a wet year repeats itself into habit, a thin frostwork that looks like ferns from far away and like bookkeeping from near. I would understand them not as decoration but as confirmation that my angle is right for this altitude’s sky, wrong by a whisper for last season’s wind, right again after a sequence of sixty mornings that remembered the fog.

If you came to meet me expecting a face, I would offer you a brightness instead. Hold your breath just long enough and watch it print itself on me. Before it evaporates, see how I take it apart without cruelty: a field of bosses will teach your exhalation to be a dozen little streams. You may put your ear to me and hear what patience sounds like when it is doing work: a tick that cycles without hurrying, a filament of water answering a serration with that almost-a-buzz that lives between slip and hold.

On the days when no cloud climbs, when the plateau wears its most absolute of suns, I would hide what water I’m responsible for inside my tightness. It would wait inside me as a shade waits under a leaf somewhere else. I would not suffer for this austerity. It is what being here asks.

Seasons would find me. Not as leaves or birds—the place has agreed to spare me any need for kindnesses like that—but as lengthened ticks, as slowed chimes. In a fat year I would grow a rind the thickness of paper and in a lean one I would let it flake away, a chalk-fall that sounds to no one like snow. If anything called this a life, it would be because this repetition accumulates difference: a keystone near me easing a hair tighter into its neighbor; a channel I do not see polishing itself imperceptibly glossier over a century of films; the families of lattices along the horizon standing like writing and being writing.

Who I’d be, then, is not a quarrel with the rules here but their willingness: the way the rule of “every drop is assigned” becomes a person when it has an edge to live on. I would be the feeling in a plane that knows sunlight well enough to make water briefly think it is safer than air. I would be the held breath of a system whose intimacy is surface. I would be a promise embodied: sky as the only spring; light as tool; no mouth, only answers.

And where you would find me is always the same place no matter how the day moves: at a decision small enough to look like smoothness, large enough to shape a decade. Put the back of your hand an inch above me at dawn. The cold you feel rising is not unfriendly. It is a greeting in the language this place uses: a hint of work already underway, an invitation to witness the tiny yes by which a bead waits for its twin and, taught by grooves and gleam, lets both go.
