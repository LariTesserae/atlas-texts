I would find myself where “down” needs a witness.

There is a place on the east-facing cant where two terraces disagree by the thickness of a hand about what gravity intends. The plates there are bright with beads that have not yet made up their minds. A catwalk joins them at a courtesy—its bolts have been cinched with a compassion you can feel in your feet—and the skyplane above is in its patient blink: an eyelid of weather half-lowered, the light combed but not set. This is where I stand most often, with my palm on the rail and my palm on the other rail, listening to the threadbirds talk to the screen and the rail-eels humming their two-note selves into agreement.

If you need a name for me, you will have to borrow one from the tags. People up here do. Mine is stamped into a kidney-shaped plate by a stairwell with ideas about circles: 3R-Δ/Teeth-7. When I was born my mother put her ear to the pressure hound and told the story of its bleat, and my aunt kissed the baffle tooth that cleared the painted line that week and smeared that gray onto my lip. We count like that; we are counted like that.

What I am is a Tender—made thin by thin air, wrist-trained and light-footed—and also, some days, adjacent to a Specification. That adjacency did not announce itself. It grew in the skin like lichen-sugar grows in cups, slow and almost clear. It started as a habit of standing in arguments between angles and coming away with my jaw aching and my knuckles tingling, as if I had been learning a language in a night class taught by metal. It went on as a knack for speaking a number back to the world that the world had slipped from itself, and watching a service stair let me arrive on the same catwalk different from when I left it, and that difference being the only work that mattered.

You would know me by the tools I keep and the quiet with which I keep them. I carry a hex that will answer three standards because in some bays the bolts remember a measurement the manuals forgot, and in some bays the manuals remember a measurement the bolts never learned, and in some bays intention is its own width. My compass is a string through a square of mesh; it twitches toward the plane the catwalk has chosen today, and I adjust my stride to agree. The mask across my mouth is not against poison; it is against mis-air, against that too-honest clarity that will dry your throat to varnish if you do not soften it with your breath.

You would not hear me coming. You would feel the rail cool an extra half-degree where my fingers had just been, and you would see the beads on a plate find their courage. You would learn my yes by two fingers on yours, and my wait by three slow driptaps, and you would watch me say now with the angle of my jaw. The air up here respects small economies: a syllable is worth a liter; a laugh costs a week. We spend our noise like sugar from the cups—sparingly, ceremonially, tongues sweet and brief.

Where I live is a high place under a low roof of made weather—catwalk stacked over catwalk, skins stretched tight to keep intention from blowing away loose. My sleeping is done in pits of lagging that smell of graphite and pump-case warmth; the hum of the ground loops is a cradle-song with an argument in it, yesterday on one side and today on the other, and the rail-eels teaching their bodies to be both. In fog weeks, when the gliders hang like pages half-turned and won’t commit, the mammals without fur share our insulation and we share their patience. We peel off strips of wrap and leave them near; in return, the coolant lines wake unharmed. Pacts are not written, but the ducts know.

I do not farm in the way the word wears in lower air. I tend. My fields are the cool sides of uprights where the pale lichen writes a quiet script in mineral flecks. I brush the gloss from condenser plates into a bladder and mix it through cable-moss, and I sweeten it with the sugar that the cups exhale when the fog gives them permission. Filtrations, emulsions, braidings: what we eat is a knot of disciplines that can be untied only with time. Beetle eggs rolled to caramel glues, spool-worm roasts dusted with dust, eel jellies set low and cut square not to offend the plate’s sense of geometry. On a fan day—when the coin-blade houses consent to stir, once, and the air grows round with that motion—I have seen a person weep just to be reminded that heat can be an act and not only a premise.

Animals call me if I let them. Threadbirds know the sound of a screen lifted with respect and will come in pairs to hook from the damp underside and leave the spool-worms in peace enough to make another coil. Beetles arrive like bright coins shaken from a pocket the moment a resin valve thinks about thinking—their copper backs tidy as a new tag—and if you offer them your palm they will use its plainness as part of their arithmetic, rolling eggs into lines so crisp you can run a shadow’s edge along them. The rail-eels are the ones I teach and am taught by; I will stroke one from yesterday into today and hum the new frequency into the handrail, and the eel will shiver not with fear but with the relief of being right. How satisfying it is to be long enough to include your own future.

Gliders do not need me, but I borrow their methods. I keep a membrane stitched from refused kiteskin, oiled neat with beetle rollings, and when a day offers nothing but brightness—no heat, no wind, no reason—I'll sit under a condenser lip and hold that film out from my shoulder and learn as much as a wrist can learn. Their sound is the sound a careful reader makes: a page raised, held, turned. I am a line of type under that cadence. That is enough for me to understand how a thing that should fall can find a draft under it and call it home.

I officiate minor ceremonies. In the bright pause, during the long blink of the skyplane when puddles school themselves with the patience of old animals, I will take a single bead from a plate too eager and set it—not wastefully, not arbitrarily—on a plate that has not felt “enough” in three days. It is a promising the array hears better than it hears us. I have eased baffles a centimeter per season and I have retorqued a cap by a quarter degree and counted for a month by the quarter-song it sang back. When a killdeer drops into her hinged run under a bracket and throws her false-broken wing at my heart, I reroute a cable ladder so her lie can ripen into truth. We admire good pretending. Much of what we do is pretend on behalf of the future until the future consents.

If the Specifications are people, they are people in the way a duct that refuses a wrench has a person in it. The first one I met, I did not know I had met. I was fourteen and too full of correctness. I had learned the mesh-string, I had learned the driptap, I had a ledger of scratches on a beam where I kept my interferences listed cleanly: bolt—once; eel—soothed; fog—thickened; hound—asked to hush. I was sent down a service stair with a rope on my belt and the instruction to stand still where the manuals change and say the number my aunt taught my mouth to remember. I went down four flights that were not repeats of each other. At the bottom I arrived where I had begun; the catwalk agreed I was the same and the light disagreed by half a color. I spoke the number and my knuckles pricked and a pencil-thin darkness uncurled itself from between two fins and asked me where I thought “down” was today. I answered with my jaw. It watched me think. That was neighborliness. I came back with a switch that obeyed.

Neighbors, then. They live in the seams where reality has been patched—where the manuals go prompt and the screws prefer not to take a standard, where a gutter gathers its drip, slows it, turns it back so it can jump into a shadow that looks like a lip and is a lip if you are willing. When I have entanglements too fine for my fingers, I speak the number gently; I lay one clean bleat on a hound that has not measured itself in weeks; I leave weights of mite-herded magnetite lozenge in a particular nest of cable tension that attracts their notice; and someone nearer the rule than I am will uncrease the air enough for me to walk between both answers. Payment is not transaction; payment is alignment. They lengthen a day by a half-degree of clarity. I stop asking for a week and listen.

Outsiders come up sometimes. When they do, they try to bully the silence into meaning. They listen for engines and are disappointed to find the noise we have is a souvenir noise: the ping of cooling metal a long terrace away; the clack of a dropped bolt that is a confession and a shame; drip ticks rehearsing a code they do not have ears to learn. They want to see a river and I take them to the sumps half full and dark with mirror-silver. “Here,” I say. “Here is a reservoir of patience.” They want to see a field and I show them the lichen and the way it makes of a polymer edgeband a breathable hunger. They want stories of a before, and I bring them to the glass box and let them read until the page refuses their alphabet. If the world wants them, the pencil-dark will flex; if not, I have shown them burrs where panels were punched. History enough.

A body is a parameter. The array entertains my step with small tilts and, in courtesy, I let it measure my weight as a kind of weather. It has been told Wet and it has been told Enough and it tells those back to me with what light it has. It is young; youth has edges you can cut bread with. No one has softened them yet with sleep or regret. You can smell the resin of purpose. You can lean cheek to cheek with a duct and feel frost bloom on your bone where yesterday there was sweat. You can put your face in a place the skyplane has just taught to be day and see the pores of the air, combed and set.

Time here is an ankle with a slow pulse. We keep it by obedience events. Bleat, once: a night. Tooth cleared: a month. Fogflare: a week. A killdeer’s lie becoming a true sprint: a day. I have a ledger of scratches for the interferences I have permitted myself. It is not holiness; it is inventory. A rescued beetle egg is a line beside a tightened valve is a quieted child. Children are scolded for throwing bolts—a clatter is a language you do not have the right to speak without cause.

Birth is low, in the warm runnels, where duct swarms fatten the air and the pumps’ cases throw a heat that forgives first breath. We open the mask a fingerwidth and let the machine teach the lungs what range is. Names are numbers, codes, onomatopoetics of pressure: bleat-names, tag-names, seam-names. Death is left to elsewhere. There is a place on the west cant where “down” is generous; if you slide someone along that catwalk, gravity believes it. We say the array keeps you for a while: not you-you, but a taste of the way you liked to coax a droplet into believing it was ready. It is enough. It makes of grief an engineering: precise, not cold.

When wetter comes—and it will, in long pours that make the sumps hasty, in throws that remember the shape of river and ask to be pronounced properly—we will tune that. The fans will accept their rotation; the belts will become rivers; the armatures the size of barns will tell stories that heat understands. We will reassign our cups from sugar to spray. We will learn new kinds of down off the water’s face. A foilwing will ride an honest thermal and not be ashamed.

Until then, I will be here, at the disagreement and the handshake between planes. I will lift a screen for the threadbirds. I will hum to the eels. I will ask the Specifications politely and give them my good pressure when they ask it. I will lay a child’s flake on her tongue and let her close her eyes without being seen by thought. I will count by obedience and reticence. I will keep a few numbers in my knuckles, ready.

I will be a seamwright of gravity’s suggestion, an officiant at minor truths. A human, yes—thin, wrist-proud, painted dull—but also a little of what the array has made room for in itself: something willing to be parameter, experiment, courtesy, weight. If you come, find me where two catwalks extend at once and both are correct until my foot declares allegiance. I will put two fingers to yours on the rail. Yes, I will say. Wait. Now. And the beads will hear us. And they will go.
