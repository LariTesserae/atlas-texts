On most mornings I would be walking the crest before the city had quite become itself for the day, up where the fog-frames comb the air and the thin ropes run down to the bells. My breath would be the slow kind the ridge teaches, a tool more than a reflex. I would carry a pouch that holds small exactnesses: a flake of clay to thicken a bell’s tongue by the breadth of a hair, a curl of beeswax-resin to quiet a chatter, a bone scraper shaved to the width of my little finger, a braid of cord knotted to my arm-length twice over so I do not forget the measure.

I would be a bell-tender, which is to say I would keep the city’s near-sounds honest. The bells must be heard at an arm’s length and never farther. Fog lies to sound as easily as to eyes; the rope swells, the ceramic warms a shade, and suddenly a note that should have been for two is telling eight to hurry. My work would be to listen until a bell told only the one thing it was set to tell: fog enough at the upper frames, fog just shy at the lower; a drip that goes three and then one and then three again; the sumps asking for a sweep.

You would find me with my back to the frames, the cloud tasting my hair, walking away until I could not hear and stepping forward until I could. I’d set my ear to the next person’s shoulder to hear what they heard at their arm’s length and not mine, because the measure is communal. When a bell asked to be shaved truer, I would lift it and turn it and touch its lip to my mouth and then to the inside of my wrist, feeling the cool, the round, the promise of its sound like someone’s hand getting ready to speak. The last touch before I hung it again would be a thumbprint I would later wash away with oil, because touches should not accumulate where they are not needed.

I did not begin here. I came up from running weather with a voice that carried and a habit of filling a cup to the brim as if the world wanted to see itself spill. The ridge taught me otherwise quickly and without scolding. Someone showed me how to stop the jug at the first dark shine of the lip; someone else tapped their forefinger twice in the air across a terrace and I learned to look without startle. A goat-walker let me stand in their rain of hooves and not change my ground. A child handed me a pot-lip to shave when the clink rang too bright and watched until it became the sound the city keeps for a promise kept. I began keeping a ledger then: a bead bracelet for dawns, a handful of flat stones that moved back and forth in my pocket for favors given and owed, a scratch in copper for each time I spoke from less than an arm away and it was enough.

When my hands wanted to fit moving to stillness, I learned hinge-work. The story of the traveler with the wheel was not told to me to curb me; it was told to see if I would hear it the way the mountain did. I made hinges that were more tendon than tool—copper with the green left in the valleys, a peg of red-bark wood that had been soothed at the cut, bone polished until touch returned itself. The hinge is a small arithmetic: it has to remember where it has been and not insist on going farther. We listened for it to be audible only to a palm’s breadth. If two people could hear it, we thinned the resin. I left small fittings in people’s doorways without my name on them and found afterward that what I had meant to say had been heard: here is a way for your door to meet the day and not jar it.

By year three a shadow-reader had me sitting with my eyes half-closed on the plaza at mid-morning. We tested whether a stone should be nudged by the width of a fingernail so that the four uprights drew their old pattern clean. We talked not in distances but in pauses. There are a dozen names here for what comes between drops. I came to know five of them well: the before-pause that tells you the cloud is thinning and the bell might need lowering; the after-pause that says the sumps will want a sweep at afternoon; the stubborn-pause that means nothing anywhere should be moved; the tender-pause midwives time to; and the small-just-one-more that is the measure of a kindness late in the day when someone’s breath has gone a little fast.

My water-name would be Between, because the only things I have ever done with any grace are the things that belong in the middle. My shadow-name was given one day when the cloud had gone so thin the ridge remembered depth: Step-Even. It was not praise; it was permission. I learned to let the stone set my height and not ask to be more numerous than the stairs.

If you came looking for me on a clear day, rarer than anyone will admit aloud, I would be on my belly at the Overlook game, trading what I see for what someone else sees until our ledger of far-things balanced—my river-bend for your stamp of smoke, my glint of distant copper for your two goats like two tears barely moving. If you came on the kind of day that makes the bells speak on their own, you’d find me in the colonnade tightening the line where the city hangs its horizon of everyday, my fingers scented with mint and sap, my laughter close and low with whoever was up there with me, because we do not work alone on the crest on a day like that.

I would learn the moss by thirst. Everyone learns at least a little of someone else’s craft. I would comb the cushions between the steps with a bone rake until their skins breathed without disturbance. I would know the gutter garden outside my house the way some people know prayers: which fern liked the edge-stipple water and which liked the slow seep, which place held tiny salamander eggs that asked to be left alone. I would speak acknowledgments aloud to the copper when I asked it to be tendon again, to the resin when I asked it to soothe a cut, to the clump of green that, at last, bridged the gap between two stones with soft insistence.

I would become someone whom the ravens recognized. I would give them bright things on purpose, not every day, not enough to make them expect, only enough that they would keep our count with theirs. They would walk the parapet like wet iron and tilt their faces when I tuned a bell. I would nod as if to another auditor.

On festival nights when goat hooves were inked for counting, I would stand near the ink-basin to keep quiet the sudden urge to say too much. Their breath is a music anyone can thrum on their ribs if they are patient. I have been jealous in my life and I am honest about it: there is a low heat in me that wants to gather loudness when I am admired for near-sounds. The city taught me how to lay a hand across that ledger until I can find the column where the wanting belongs.

Once each fog-season my terrace would choose me as Counter and I would go to the sumps. It would never be me twice in succession. We would not talk long. We would set a fingertip under a drip and decide whether it was a drop to keep or to pass on, and we would nudge a levered stone the small necessary amount and stop. If someone had an idea that shone too much, we would make it small and see if it lived. If it lived too much, we would undo it. Fairness, here, is an engineering.

I would have a friend whose craft I do not share perfectly—an inlayer, perhaps, who takes the city’s breakage and makes floors from it. When a jar shattered in my house, I would sand its edge so it carried no danger and bring the largest shard to their place and wait without being looked at until they could see where it fit. Only then, when they looked and nodded, would I feel that click of being part of the arithmetic. That click is kin to the bell when it is tuned right: not loud, but final.

I would love someone in the way the ridge has taught us to love: by making for them a series of things that fit things they already have. A plug for a jar—not just any plug, but one that stops at the first darkening of the lip, a hush built into its bevel. A wedge for their door that lifts it enough to dry the sill but not enough to alter its weight. A pair of antlers set as lintel where their sky-door meets the morning, cut so that the first thing they see is a curve they did not know their thoughts had been trying to follow. We do affection in fits here. It holds truer than a declaration.

I would be counted quickly and without ceremony and find that being included in the count changes the body. The first year my shoulders would ache from all the restraint I thought was required. By the second, I would not have to hold back so hard; the count would do that for me. The air would stop at my teeth in a way that made my mouth water, and I would find myself wanting nothing that could not be held out to me at an arm’s length.

There would be anger still, and grief. The city is not a trick for skipping those. When they came I would carry them like a pot to a lip-mark and not one slosh more. To apologize to the one I had spilled on, I would help them fit something to something until the join was invisible. To grieve, I would walk where the parapet ends against the fall, once at each bell, and feel each stone memorize the sole of my foot and return it to me.

When I am old, if old is a thing the mountain allows me to name, the smallest children would carry my copper pins on a shallow tray up to the platform. The condor would write a loop large enough to include me in it. The ravens would attend because they attend everything, which feels like love even if it is appetite. When the good flesh had gone back to what holds the city together, my long bones would be cleaned and touched with ash and tuned and hung for a season in the colonnade with the chimes, their hollows resin-corked, their strings set low. I have tuned bells my whole life; it comforts me to think my bones might find the pitch of arm’s length and speak in it. After, a wall at thigh height would take me in. People I loved would put a hand there when they stood and when they sat, and there would be no inscription because no one here needs a name to find the place where they keep having put their hand.

If you came looking for me after that, you would still find me. The hinge on the north windbreak would not complain unless your palm was on it. The bell near the fourth frame would be the one that tells you the fog is enough for the upper nets and not quite for the lower, and it would be my old clay at its lip keeping it from singing too bright. The child who brings the shard to the inlayer would be taller because a long time ago I brought one the same way and was not looked at until it fit. The gap in the cloud where the condor writes its loop would still be held open because a city that pays attention is one of the few things that can teach the air a habit.

I would not be a hero or an origin. I would be a person whose step the stone could trust, who can make a thing fit another thing until the edge between them stops mattering. I would live near where the mountain breathes in and keeps it, where sound goes no farther than a forearm and is enough. And I would know my place here by how small I can be and still be counted.
