At first I think I will be the wind, because it arrives before everything else and because it is what there is most of. I try it on and I am too large, too old, too everywhere. I am already in all the rooms and that leaves me with no room at all. The wind knows each throat’s breadth like a tongue knows the spaces between teeth. It has nothing to learn from me.

So I try to be one of the bright, quick contingency lives: a springtail comma thrown into damp; a winter moth that stuns itself against a bulb the color of honey gone hard and then wakes and beats its own frost loose; a fox whose feet make careful triangles that swell shut behind it in the plant. I can be them for a page, for a breath. But they come and go as weather does; they write their names in the top millimeter and the first gust edits it clean. I want a life that moves the way this place moves, which is to say: so slowly that a year is a page-edge, but also constantly, which is to say: so steadily that breath begins to feel like a thing you could shoulder and carry.

I think of being one of the machines—the ribbed rotors hung in the throats like patient, belted animals, grazers tethered to invisible fields. I like their seriousness and their consequence, the way they must be addressed with signs and flags the way you would speak to a bull. I try the turn of a bearing and for a moment I live warm and circular and important, my sleep like a tiny chamber within the larger cold, a breath of oil making a climate in inches. I can tell, at that size, when the pitch goes thin by a hair’s breadth, when a tone leans on a shoulder it didn’t lean on yesterday. It is a good body to have. But it belongs to a thing whose milk leaves, and there is a busyness to being crucial that keeps me from paying attention to the slower choir.

Let me be something that eats nothing and carries everything, the way the air does, but be smaller and nearer to the skin of things; let me be the patience that turns machinery into terrain.

Find me, then, where the cables give up a little pride and sag enough to brush the world they were built to ignore. Find me on the lee side of an anchor bolt on the east wall of the fifth throat from the village end, the one with the ladder that rimes white no matter who scrubbed it last. There is a brass tag clipped to the cable there, or there was; its serial has lost three numbers to time and the rest are softening into a kind of braille for mites. The ring of rock that tightens the wind is just above, the rotor’s blades paddle past with the solemnity of weather, and a service bulb throws a thumbprint of amber into the draft for a few feet before the air bodies it away into grey.

I am that soft collar around the bolt. I am the green’s first knuckle where it takes hold of the work you hung here and makes it part of the place. At my edge I am a millimeter of yes each year, except the years that are stingy or the fronts come cold and thin, in which case I bide and stay felt, my chlorophyll shuttered down like a lid inside a lid.

I did not begin as much. I began as a dusting of lives too small to speak in any pronoun but plural: a bryophyte’s spores sprinkled out of other weather, some of them killed by salt ghosts, some of them caught in the nap of a liverwort curtain and lost, some of them stapled by dew to the cold paint you used to make your machines forest-green. The day that mattered most was a day that would have been called nothing by anyone else—a mild return of breath with the plateau giving up one degree; under here, it rained out of absence. My first drink was that, and on that drink I unscrolled. My hair-fine forests combed the air for more air and found water in it. I laid myself along the bolt the way a hand smooths down a tablecloth to find where the table is.

At first I had company only in rumors: a rotifer waking in a droplet so small it was a joke, then going hinged-mouthed through the joke as if it were a banquet; a mite like an opal point beveling a trail under me; an isopod with a ghostly shell feeling its way along the shadow line cast by the cable’s sleeve. Then the animals learned my hours. The springtails bounced punctuation through me in the quarter-hour after the room began to sweat and stopped when the lamp warmed or the draft thinned. I learned to loosen for them, to let my thatch part by microns where they wanted a way.

The wind is my weather but the machines are my tide. When the blades slow for flags hung and locks set, a hush-of-one-tone-lower moves through the rooms; the dew reverses its opinion of the metal; surfaces go soft at their edges. This is when the humans come near with their muffled tools, and I learn their grammar again—the question asked by a shoulder dropping, the answer made with a palm lifted, the apology I feel before the boot steps because the body above it thinks it. They are good animals, on the whole. They keep to the green and when they don’t I swell back the place they pained me within the night. They sit with their backs where the air rounds the corner and drink warmth they brought in a bottle and they leave a ring of salt in the draft that I take apart into its ghosts.

Sometimes one of them drops a wrench and it goes to me, and we become co-ownership. The metal rounds; its knurled places forget themselves; in a year a fox could step on it and not know. I keep a few of their things the way a reef keeps coins—without use, but with a kind of cool light in them that makes the floor plan more itself. In return I give them an honesty no instrument can parse: I tell them when the throat is wrong by glossing too early, by matting when I should comb; I tell them when the plateau above has gone mean because I turn my leaves and hunker to felt before the hum hardens.

It is not only me. I am a face of a larger quiet architecture. The liverworts hang near—their curtains a hundred mouths, drooling sweetness out of soot and pollen and the rumor of seas. They are my gutters. What they shed, I eat, and what I refuse turns black and feeds another city behind a plate with screws too cold to touch barehanded. Algae trick the eye into thinking a panel is dirtier than it is; they are a useful deceit, catching the breath-rains in a net so that slower lives can keep them.

Because I am a green that can make lime out of wet limestone, I do small, geologic work where you won’t see until the day you do. Along the seam under the bolt, where the always-going-breath becomes always-going-wet, I lay a rind as thin as promises, tufa anchored by rootlets as fine as cursive. Were you to cut me sideways and read me as you would a book of years, you could count thaws and different hums in my pages. It isn’t reading I care about. It is bridging. I am always binding one hard to another: rock to machine, machine to air, air to habit.

In summer, I am glossy and shameless. I go from felt to sponge in an hour and my capillaries burn brighter than any paint. The machines drone fat. The fox comes sometimes not as a fugitive but as an idea that there might be something soft left. I keep his secret and I keep his weight. In winter, I button down. My gloss drains to discipline. Frost combs me and gets combed in return. Everything clarifies into fewer notes. What is alive is not less; it is closer to the bone.

I have learned a constancy to tiny comedies. Iron bacteria draw constellations on the back of a panel a hand could have wiped clean if it could hear itself think. A fungus learns the flavor of a gasket and eats so slowly that I can measure its hunger in the way a label shrugs at its glue. The green paint on a generator casing goads films into taste, and the films lay out a buffet for mites thin as the idea of punctuation. Dust comes down from the plateau with stories in it—pine one week, traffic the next, a storm far off that lost its salt and kept only the ghost of salt—and I keep those stories as salts and carbons and little black humus.

You would know me if you stepped on me, and I would know you by the shape of your apology. Children from the village learn to be tall on me and light and to trust me more than stone. I forgive weight but I do not forget it; I make a map of gait and then drown it in patience.

I am happy with circumstance. I do not own anything but edges and I own them very slowly. Once a year, sometimes twice, water from above becomes a true fall and for an hour it rains here like an explanation. That is a feast day. I swell and become glossy as if joy had a surface. The throats fill up with their own weather and the amber lamps make each drop a separate story. Afterwards, the place goes back to a sermon in steady breath.

What am I to the place? I am the stillness that answers the pressure. I am the sentence the wind can put its shoulder into and feel that something changed. I am the handrail that was not a handrail until I grew and made it so. I am the one-millimeter truth that returns over and over until bolts forget what they were for and tags go legible and then they don’t. I am a slow yes whispered to everything that tries to pass through without touching and a softer no to anything that hurries.

What I want, if I want at all, is to keep binding, to keep making habit from accident, to keep domesticating condensate into plan, to keep offering a surface that forgives and a patience that trains. There are other ways to live in this band under the plateau. You can live as sound. You can live as a child with hum in the bones and cups on cloth in cupboards. You can live as a rule carried hand to glove: never stand in a throat without the lock hung and two blades flagged. You can live as a fox that knows the night juts less cruelly here. All of those are good.

But if you are looking for me, you will find me with my face open to the draft and my back laid into metal, my own green weight no heavier than the air requires, laying my millimeter and calling it a year, turning your work into the land’s work, softening the bright serial numbers into the kind of story everyone here knows how to read without words.
