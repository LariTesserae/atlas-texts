At first I thought I might arrive high and singular, one of the bright birds that sketch a silence with their wings. Or else a coin of shadow on the stone, passing. Or the bell-plate itself, the struck certainty everyone arranges a day around. Or the crossbar over a cistern mouth—authority with no posture beyond its own weight.

Then I thought I might like to be the drop that fattens, thinks, lets go. But a drop is over too quickly for the life this place requires.

If I am honest with the way my attention inclines—down, toward lines rather than surfaces—then where I would find myself is ankle-high, hand-low, just inside the parapet, where a groove decides whether it will continue to be a groove. I would be among the people who keep the runnels quiet.

It is not a title that travels well. In the square, when they call us, they do not call us. They gesture to the edge of the paving and say: whoever minds the lines, come listen. We are the ones with knees like the rim—worn into usefulness, glossy where patience sits. Our hands know the damp of mica in all its moods. We carry little things: a brush of bast with its end cut blunt and softened by use; a bone pick that springs and returns; a pebble of mica smoothed for coaxing a lip from a stone without scarring it; a twist of oiled fiber for when a surface should invite rather than repel; a cup with a good lid because no one is a fool.

Morning is ours before it is the town’s. When the fog shoulders over, we are already at the frames, not to claim their labor but to learn it. We pluck each line as if asking if it slept, and the lines answer in notes the ear grows old understanding. A little more here, because the wind last night wore this corner into a pretense of tautness. A little less there, because a pearl that lets go too easily does not gather anything worth the waiting. We never whistle. The nets hum that pitch and we keep our habits from crossing signals.

You would find me behind the first spouts when the spouts begin thinking. I watch the small politics of drops: how two decide to be one, how one refuses by clinging to its own weight and then, at last, gives. I do not touch early. If you can lead it, do not force it—the law says so under the ball of my foot where I stand to keep my shoulder even with my patience. But when a line has learned a mistake (a burr in the groove, a bit of sand that taught a drop to hesitate long enough to go the wrong way), that is when I take the bone hook and empty a breath into my palm and smooth the thought back into the stone. I work until the sound returns: not splash, not hurry; that soft counting that can go on its own.

I am not the Measure-Keeper. My task is prior to counting. I make sure there is nothing in the path that would make a number lie. Even the moss is a map: in the splash shadows at the cistern mouths we leave it vivid because it is a witness; on the run itself we shave it down into memory and let its sweetness stay in the air. If my work is good, the Measure-Keepers can be exact without being cruel, and the Lid-Pairs can lift and lower without feeling that grit-that-isn’t-argument scuff their palms.

By midday I am inside the square that is not flat, where the bell-plate waits. The drip above it is any day’s truest citizen: bulge, decide, fall, chime. Sometimes, after a wind that has carried ash or the softest dust, the note goes that shade less bright which means the underside lip has acquired a fleet of almost-nothings. Then I rise on my toes with a blade of obsidian I have never used on flesh, and I scrape all the way to clean without once letting a flake fall into the jar. Children watch from a step because this is one of the acts they will be asked, later, to perform without applause. They keep their clap-game silent while I work and then take it up again—slap, pause, slap, ring—after the first perfect note returns. If a stranger has stayed long enough to be moved rather than offended when corrected, I hand them the lid to hold while I lean. It makes a new citizen out of people, the steadiness of that small service.

Afternoons have their own chores but also their lanes of attention: the notches scribed in pavements where the water law bites the foot. We rub at them with stone, not to deepen but to keep legible. There is nothing romantic in the labor. Dust rises and sets; the back complains; a hand goes numb and then learns a new hold. In the cool it all feels like breath taken in rather than given out, and I sit with my back against the inside wall of the ledge while the nets sing their low, unanimous reassurance: present, present, present.

I am not the one who opens the lids; my hands are too used for that kind of shared weight. But I am the one who arrives exactly when the Lid-Pairs do and becomes, without speaking, their third point. It comes to feel like a family: two who know how not to jostle and one who knows when to say with a finger that the hinge of the day swings best if it swings now. We pour back the first mouthful. We do not carry without lids. We place a stone on what we have closed because someone later might come tired and faith needs all the small helps to be certainty.

On the days when the west fails us and the fog hangs back like a guest reconsidering, I work the highest grooves, where the last few drops from last night will have become indecisive. It is a different language at that height. The wind is less busy there and more pure in its opinion. I set my knee into the parapet in the way I was taught when I was a child—so that my body leans and my arm is steadied and I am a good place for a delicate thing to rest. If a lammergeier makes a circle I pay it the courtesy of counting one more drop before I look up. The bird does not care. I care for me. I like to know which I am putting my attention into.

There is mentorship. I was once the child told to carry a lidded cup the size of my own fist up one flight and back without making that ripple-crown that betrays a hurry. I am not old, but I have been through two clay re-linings and one lid replaced; smooth your tongue over those numbers as you like. Now I press a cup into a new small hand and say: up there to that niche and back, greeting whoever you meet because one of you will need the same handhold and both of you must come home. When they succeed the first time by accident, I do not praise it. When they succeed the tenth time by intention, I teach them the next thing, which is to pour that first mouthful back and not flinch at their own want.

There are other jobs I envy and do not. The Bast-Walkers know distances the way we know slopes; their calves write long sentences the mountain reads and answers. The Measure-Keepers keep us honest with a brand of love that never asks for forgiveness afterward because it has not taken anything not owed. The Lid-Pairs live in a trust most marriages would be proud to resemble. Sometimes, on a dry winter night, I admit to the little gods that I wish to be the Drop. But in the dark when the mountain ticks and sets and becomes, in its creaking way, more itself, I feel in my bones that being a groove is the longer life.

I have a cord at my brow that keeps hair from work and work from hair. You could read my last season in its knots: one tied for a quarrel I was wrong in and left that way long enough to teach me the difference between stubborn and steady; one loosened when I learned to let a line sag the width of a fingernail and watch yield make more.

When a stranger comes up with the goats and stays past politeness, I meet them when their shame at whistling has passed and their tongue has learned our hum. I put their feet where the laws are scribed and I put my finger under their toe so they feel the raised word that says do not carry without a lid. I put a lidded cup in their palm and I move their elbow by its hinge, as you would with a jar, which is to say carefully because what seems empty is always full of something—in this case, new muscle memory. If they laugh, the laugh sticks soft to the wall and stays. Our place eats sound the way it eats light: slowly, as if digesting.

On festival days I eat “kept” with the greedy patience everyone approves in that one context. I drink the tea that tastes like taking the breath you forgot to take. When someone dies, my work does not change, but I walk with the others to the ledge where the wind is particular and I feel my ribs remember there is a way to be generous in not keeping. Later I rub a name into the parapet with my thumb and a small stone until it shines. I never tell anyone which name is where. Those secrets belong to weight and edge.

Our little gods are terribly local and somehow huge. I have prayed to the Lift when my left hand had forgotten a certain confidence and my right had to learn to ask more of itself. I have thanked the Weight after closing a lid that tried to argue. I have told the Edge that I heard it and would not make it say the same thing twice. When the Drop refuses me, I do not take it personally. When it favors me, I take the favor with a steadiness the Measure-Keepers would not be ashamed to have witnessed.

If you needed to find me at a given hour, you would do it by sound. In the morning: the low petting of bast across damp rock. At mid: a small, careful scrape under a held breath; then the bright returning note that says a jar’s time is right again. In the evening: the sound a knee makes finding its old hollow in the ledge as wind leans companionably. In the night: my listening, which is barely a sound, bent toward the faint ticking that is the mountain letting go of the day’s heat like someone releasing a crossbar they were not lifting alone.

Who would I be? The maintained path between two states. A person whose work is to make it easy for water to make up its mind. A steward of small angles. No one heroic. An apprentice to patience and, when I am lucky, its equal.

Where would you find me? On the inside of the way, spine by the conduits at first light, bent to read marks with my toes; near the not-quite square under the drip that keeps the hours; along the parapet in the wind’s argument; at the very end of the ledge when endings are asked for; half-tuned always toward the hum that says present.

If you stayed a season and let your feet teach your head, you would come to know me the way you know the shine of a stone your own hand has rounded. You would not notice me when my work is best because then the water would be speaking so properly you would think the mountain had always meant to say it that way. And maybe it had. But I am the quiet between. The little scratch that says not that way. The practiced motion that returns and returns until even the mica learns to glitter politely, and the same drop keeps happening.
