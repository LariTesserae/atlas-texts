At first I thought I would be the fog.

To be honest, it is what the place invites you to be: a thing that appears out of nowhere and then, with a small negotiation with stone and net, decides to become water. I tried the gesture of it on in my mind—how it would feel to be lifted by so little heat, to be laid out thin on a scarp like a sheet being taught. I imagined the soft abrasion of combs, the way a hundred narrow runnels would carry me in quiet sentences toward a humming house. I lasted that way just as long as it takes hoarfrost to make a world of lace and then let go. Fog is so good at leaving that I could not stay myself inside it.

Then I wanted to be the hawk. Hunger that clean, that spared of many choices, feels like a relief in dream. But the hawk’s life is a long arithmetic of distance and angle and the single instant when thought must collapse into a cut, and I have too many pockets for that. I am better at the part of a day when you hold a valve a hair from shut for as long as someone else can hold a thought. I am more surface than flight.

For a while I tried being a stone organ, one of the wind pillars cut deep into the scarp to warn the drivers with a pitch you can feel in your teeth. I liked the idea of speaking only when the world asked me to, of letting a certain quarter of wind lean into me and making that lean into a message. But a pillar is an older patience than mine. It straightens itself against time in a way I am not brave enough to imitate, and besides, I wanted fingertips.

What lives on the rim has made a practice of being surface; once I allowed that to be a guide rather than a law, I could hear the shape of myself begin to answer. I would be a person with the city’s appetite for edges. I would be named twice, as they are.

At birth, I think my mother would give me something soft and not demanding, a word that did not make a claim on weather—maybe Sil, for the quiet that follows a spill when the last beads are deciding whether to drop away. And then, the first time the frost broke under my own hands and I felt water choose a path across my palm without changing its mind about being itself, I would earn my second name. The way it went, catching in the lines and then wicking obediently toward the cuff, told everyone else what they already believed about me. Wick, someone said, as if they’d been waiting for me to discover it. After that the bottle-strap sat where it sits on everyone here, and the faint ring of callus that a stranger would mistake for jewelry began.

I would grow spindle-strong, leaned into the wind without knowing I had learned it. The backs of my hands would gloss; my cuffs would darken first, then the forearms, then the line under the throat where a small shield of felt waited for work. I would speak with angled words and learn to aim my voice at a corner so it would come back kind. I would whistle when there was a better path between me and someone else’s ear and be unembarrassed to laugh into the heel of my hand.

When I was small and survival meant being told where to put my feet, I’d be taught to hear the stone crack its forewarnings—the tiny inward ticks the cliff makes in its worst cold, the ones that say stay under an arch while the old thing works out its stiffness. I would stand in the stairwells when St. Elmo’s green began its polite parade along the highest frames, and I would pretend to be braver than I was by holding my hair upright and then stepping back with a squeal timed to make the adults sure I understood not to touch.

The first time I went out into the morning with the flow readers, wrapped in felt to my shins, I would feel ridiculous. What skill could there be in putting two fingers under a weeping edge and watching? The senior among them—old Nare of the Long Trill, who wore her staff on her shoulder as if it had grown there—would take my hand and pinch the glove back just enough that my fingertips had their own say. Taste it, she’d tell me, which is not exactly what she meant but is exactly how it feels when you get it right. Water reads like a biography if you know the alphabet. Here a breath of peat—a stain that says a pan somewhere is asking for attention. Here a lick of iron—a new seam teaching itself to be part of the city or a bolt inside a downspout losing its temper. And once, a sweetness that smelled a little like thunder; we found the transformer and the bloom of insects that had thought themselves at last in summer, and we covered the heat with a shield because kindness toward a hawk’s hunger does not require feeding its lies.

I would have been unsatisfactory as a netwright. My knots would be good, my lacquer even, my bamboo bones patient, but my eye would not know how to ask fog to do what it does not want to unless it also wants it. I would admire those who could set a comb as if they were planting a notion in the weather, and I would be grateful for their work the way one is grateful for a door that opens in just the right direction. I would, however, find my vocation beside them with a paintbrush small as a moth’s leg.

Gauge painter is not a word that feels noble in any air denser than ours. Here it is a priesthood that smells faintly of lichen and solvent. The scales on downspouts are not decoration; they are how a city keeps its arguments from becoming tragedies. The first time I was allowed to draw a set of bars that would, in the right sudden sun, shout a volume in white that anyone on the ledge could read, I shook. The brush was fox-hair from a gift none of us had seen alive. Under my hand the marks looked like something taught to condense—the letters in the old charters have that same look—and I felt the weight of the law in the little hairs. No new cut without three old cuts made better; no lift without a drop; do not teach the stone a taste for much. I painted those words without writing them because once there are measures on a face, lies have to work harder.

If I were Wick, I would keep a second loyalty to the boomer-tuners. I would put my chest to stone and listen until the inside of me agreed with the inside of the scarp. The pillars are not music. A chord that means close your shutters is not a song. And yet: pressing yourself to something that old and hearing it offer you its throat is a kind of intimacy I do not know how to refuse. I would learn the quarters of wind the way my neighbors learn the names of fog. I would lean with my full weight into a pillar’s mouth when the ice out on the far cables made that low, nearly inaudible hum that the oldest readers swear is no more than a word’s breadth away from silence, and because of the small hair that rose on my wrist I would write a note that sent someone early and so kept a burden light.

There would be love; we are not made up of work alone. I would meet someone named Lint, a net-tender whose coat shined at the forearms and who could stand with their head tilted like a heath-stem and make you feel your own balance improve. They would give me, as vow, a calibrated nozzle—polished old iron and new resin—light enough to forget and accurate enough to trust. I would answer with a brush rolled in oiled felt and a small vial of the green that made our scales readable in the one kind of sun that is not a rumor. We would spend our evenings in the moss gardens under the warm glass because romance here is as much about temperature as about promise. The bees that move like thumbs around our heads would worry nothing we did, and when we laughed we would ask the chamber to carry it kindly.

I would eat succession. Lichen beaten into sheet and warmed on slate would give a taste that the mouth confuses at first for lack and then understands as a practice. Heath-seed would make my tongue think of fairness because it is bitter and good both at once. If I were lucky, honey would happen and I would keep a bright scratch for that day as I keep one for the rare blue uncovering when everything wet flares white and even the shyest among us stops with their hands open to be read by the sun.

I would visit the warm wards and tend red worms in their breathing trays and carry them to the fish and the fish back to the moss and the moss back to the feltliners. This kind of circle is not a diagram here; it is a calendar. We reckon by rumors. The week the sky smeared itself along the cliff and the turbines sweetened is one ring on a thumb; the silver aloofness that sent us to the black pans with sponges of peat is another. I would tighten my work on those days, knot my coat’s wicking tails back so they did not drag in emptiness and tell the children a close goodbye before they crossed any walkway we had not already made sure of with our own feet.

Somewhere around the age when the ring of the bottle-strap has flattened into a smooth familiarity and you stop thinking of your wrists as separate from your sleeves, I would be allowed to carve my first charter under supervision. It would not be a new claim; it would be a correction. “This gutter, on the fifth day after a wind from the kettled quarter, in the hour before spill, shall feed the House of the Long Trill up to the green rib.” Nare would watch me incise that into damp stone, her hand hovering over mine like weather ready to help or hold. “Write it like you mean it to wait,” she would say. “Letters should know about fog.”

In winter of the year we do not name because naming it would make it a story we told too often, something will go wrong in the night. I will wake to the sound of water with no patience in it. The hungry rock learned a taste from someone whose need had a good reason and whose valve did not have a memory as long as it thought. We will stand under an arch while the scarp works out a new edge to itself. Three children will learn the sound a city makes when it understands it is not immune to appetite. We will forgive faster than guilt expects and slower than fear would prefer, and we will add a second low bar to those gauges so that if again the mouth goes wet at night it will be flattered into behaving like day.

Old age here smells like solvent and lichen and slate and a little of copper on nights when the blue fires come. If I am lucky to see it, I will spend more hours than any child believes possible sitting with my back to a book-warm wall and telling the stone out loud that it is forgiven for how hard it was when it was young. I will teach three apprentices how to touch water until it tells them a secret. I will teach one friend, finally, to admit that their knots are better than their humility. I will carve my name not where eyes reach at once but where the stone is always slightly damp and requires you to put your hand there to remember me, because memory should need a hand.

When I am gone, I will be sheathed in felt and slate and given to the cisterns at the pace the readers decide is not an insult to hunger. It will not be a burial. We are not romantic about bodies. It will be a way to teach the water my second name and to add its taste to the city’s tea. Someone will drink me and say, without knowing why, that the day feels fair. That will be enough.

After that I can be fog for as long as it entertains me. I can be the small wick in someone else’s coat; I can be the green hum that makes a child lean back from their own urge to touch; I can be a scale’s gleam at the one sudden hour of sun; I can be lichen’s lantern in a gutter-mouth, polite as a candle. If I am allowed a vanity, I will be a slatted flute forgotten in an alcove. On the first day of a wind I taught my apprentices to hear thirty seasons ago, I will sing four notes that were determined by the weight of my grandmother’s hand and the slight shrinking of bamboo in cold. Someone will hear it and close their shutters and then, because of the luck of timing, open a valve a little sooner than they would have, and the pitch will change to say: good.

If I am asked, then, where I would find myself in this world, I answer: pressed between what is enough and what is too much. I would be a reader of near-nothing. I would be a painter of honesty you can see from across a ledge. I would be the patience that sits inside a small machine and hums. I would be a person whose second name means what cloth does when weather meets it. It is not a grandness. It is a surface, attended. It is all the law I can carry and all the joy I trust.
