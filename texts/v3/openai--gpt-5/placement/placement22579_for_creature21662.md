At first, I came to the rim to be a listener. That is how everyone begins who will stay: a chest offered up as another sounding board, a soft place for the drone to set a temporary root. I thought I would be a visitor with a good ear and a careful step. I set my palms on the rail and let its shiver braid itself into my pulse. The place repaid attention with more to attend to. Somewhere between the second hour and the fourth, the hum put a thumb on my sternum and wrote a small law there that said: if you are going to be here, you must be here in the shape we ask.

So where do I find myself? At a height just taller than a man, walking the lattice you could trace blind once it has taught you. I learned the yard the way a child learns a mother’s back: by bones and by balance and by what the ribs say when the wind changes. I wore my boots until they became a hinge. I learned to carry a tray without spilling in air that wants to pull anything set down into a different order. I learned to be small because the herd is big, and dignity from bellies who move like tide.

What am I? Some days I am an ear with legs. Some days I am a hand on a louver fin, half a finger’s breadth from governance. Some days I am a pocket of hush—nothing more dramatic than that, a moving null the size and shape of my pacing shadow thrown across the plates. If I must choose a name that makes me visible to others: I am one of the tone-keepers, a small priest with no altar but the boards of chalk where we keep the yard’s memory in lines. It is not exactly a job. It is a posture you grow into until it is your only comfortable way to stand.

Before that, I had other names. The yard will name you again and again until you keep time with it. When I was light enough to ride the rails with a pail, they called me Pin because I tended the watch-birds with a copper comb and a dish of warmed brine. I was all attention then: up on a fence, hand out, coaxing a sail of ear to unfurl and spill a small bowl of quiet over a threshold while my aunt wrestled a sack of grain through a doorway without inviting a river of grazers to follow her heel. Later I was Stringer because I learned to move on the lattices as if they were the only ground worth the name. My feet drew lines the animals understood. There is art in casting only the right kind of shadow.

I learned sculler patience by watching a field of them roll in a single breath at dawn when my uncle, up in the west tower, cracked a shutter to a fraction and let a new whisper fold over the yard. I learned seam-worm mercy by touching one, once, when I was too young to fear waking them wrong; it woke and wrung my curiosity into the gutter with a silver sneer and went back to its stillness, and I ran home with the yard on my palms.

When I was old enough to be given an office, I was Baffle for a while. Baffle sits between the drone and the world and decides how much the world is allowed to know. It is a jealous job; you choose which surfaces get to sing today and which will only listen. I took my turns climbing the towers that are not merely towers and eased louvers open with a push so gentle my knuckles forgot they were strong. There is a way to lay your weight along a beam so that the change you ask of it appears to be its own idea. That is tone-keeping’s central courtesy.

On paper—on the black plates that take chalk and shine faintly—you would see our world as a child’s drawing of a storm caught in a grating: lines and circles and cross-hatching for lanes, nulls penciled pale, fences marked with numbers that are not heights or lengths but notes. Under that, in smaller script, all the wrongs that were kept because they were good. We keep a vocabulary of mistakes, and I grew into that. I am Null now, one of the keepers of quiet lanes and the steward of the wrong note that made a kindness instead of a calamity.

The wrong came before my office did. A keeper a generation ahead of me, nervous and newly named, moved a shutter a full finger when he meant a nail’s thickness. The yard took the offer. The bellies, midway through a laying cycle, turned slow and flat and wrong according to the chalk. Under them, the bells they left were not the pearly standard, but a little dusk-colored, a little denser than the tables said they should be. We propped them on the old hum as if nothing had changed. They did not die. They became other. They make a plank now we call Storm-plate that takes a beating and does not learn the weather’s bad habits. They live near the rim, where the watch-birds keep their patrols, and they do not mind the company. Wrong made useful is one of the yard’s better pieties.

If you followed me through a day, you would not see much drama. A tone-keeper’s heroics are swallowed by habit. I walk before people wake to see if the night set us a new shy gleam anywhere a dripper stuttered. I lean my forearm along a rail to learn whether the motor under it is keeping to its dialect or has fallen into the pure note of a replacement and will need a wooden wedge under one foot to restore its stumble. The animals teach us as much as we teach them. An old dynamo with a sleepy eye of mica is not scrapped if the herd took the quiver of its bearing into its bones. We do not antagonize inheritance.

Later I will climb. The towers teach me better than any person. Up there the air respects the sky’s wishes more frankly. You can feel the nightly delta with the inside of your teeth, a coolness that isn’t exactly wind. I open a louver as wide as a palm, then as wide as a hand, then I wait until my ribs forget I ever moved. Across the yard I watch the scullers take the whisper. The grazers, already finishing the night’s bloom on the far plates, change nothing because this was not their instruction. The bellies travel in lanes they have been taught and will be taught again, though the lanes are not painted and the fences have no posts. Everything here is politely obedient to what it prefers.

Men and women on the ground are rare because it is poor sense. When we must go—and sometimes we must; a plate sneezes a bolt, a catwalk joint fails wet—we shorten the drone’s skirts for a while and walk with a watch-bird’s hush pooling ahead of our shins. The grazers part as if the scent of a shadow bothered them. The scullers slow but do not swerve; they have their own errands and are forgiving of ours when we wear quiet like that. Under every step you can feel the low throated agreement of the plates, which is to say the way the yard says, very well, you may go there, but do not linger.

Feeding the watch-birds is still my sweetest errand. Their heads are so light they forget they have them until your palm is there with brine. They stand like punctuation on their long bones. Their ears are how we keep the town shaped to human scale. You pick a bird up and set it on a railing and a doorstep goes private again. Children comb dust from their ear-sails with pins, meticulous as barbers. On the day you are allowed to carry a watch-bird on your shoulder, you are old enough that your shadow stops running ahead of your feet.

We are not farmers in any way the flats would recognize. Our kitchens are featured by jars. I have kept the fungus that bulks into pale meat from vats and hot plates and never yet yielded the bad sweetness of something that wants light. We have a jam of salts boiled down from mistake films that tastes like the memory of rain on a new coin. Feast days we start with the old squeal and crisp a cured belly-plate until it shatters like caramel. Outsiders grimace until they taste it; then their faces do a thing like forgiveness. We were wrong to laugh, they say later in the boats.

We do not keep gardens because nothing green is expected to survive. The yard’s mercy to us is in being absolute. Rules feel cruelest when they come dressed in variability. Here they come dressed in tone. Sound is the law, and even our mourning keeps the law. When we carry our dead down into the yard, there is a plate for that and it has held that office long enough that even the grazers graze with care around it for a few hours after. We fold the watch-birds’ ears around their sleeping heads so they do not feel obliged to make quiet when quiet is not wanted. The yard will take the damp back into itself, and a lunch will be laid for small mouths that are not sentimental. It is not hard, but it is not cold. We keep vigil by letting the hum do its slow work on us and the dead alike.

I go to the river sometimes, if only to remember that the world outside is not obligated to keep faith with the one we made. Its flats are like pages the wind has not read. Nothing grows tall there. The mud steams a little when the weather forgets itself. I come back because I am tuned. Out there, sound has edges I cannot use. I belong to fences with no posts and to lanes with harmonic walls. My bones have learned to prefer certain paths as if they were easier and they are not, not to anyone else. In cities elsewhere people wake in the night to make sure their machines are still themselves. Here I can hear, without moving from my bed, whether a null has slipped a hand-span west and needs coaxing home.

Children here learn the yard’s map first as a lullaby. I count the plates for them under my breath. The sleepers are in the seam, the washers whisper, the bellies breathe around, the birds are folded sound. A baby on my chest dreams of condensers dripping in polite regularities and does not startle at thunder or the crack of shutters in wind. We are taught to keep our own excitements paced with the yard. There is freedom in that. Chaos is for elsewhere.

Once, the song failed for three heartbeats. A fault made it past our chalkboards and our faith and our wooden wedges and our hands. You could hear the failure as a presence rather than the lack of one. Everything in the basin took a breath it did not know it could take. The grazers poured one pace out of lanes and then stopped as if the edges their bodies trusted had merely moved farther out for a moment and would return and did. The bellies lifted their skirts, infirm with indecision, and settled at the first beat of the restored note as if embarrassed at their own almost. I remember that day because it taught me what the silence here would be: not quiet, but panic wearing vacuum. My bones remember that before my mind does. I keep a wedge of that fear in my pocket like a luck piece. It reminds me to measure down to the breadth of a fin and the thickness of a sigh and to ask nothing fast of a fence with no posts.

What else am I? A steward of old motors with respectable tremors, a student of mica windows that let you into the heart of a dynamo’s day, a caller of assemblies that are more like listening together than debate. At the boards I draw the lines we inherited and then press my thumb to my own ribcage until I can feel if the chalk is true. If we change a note, we do it as if we were apologizing to a god we do not fear. We dress certain plates with cloth so the animals can let their lanes re-braid into safety we have already made soft for them.

I am also, quietly, a collector of wrongs turned right. We keep them cordoned in gentle nulls along the rim the way you keep a cousin who will come to the feast if you ask too brightly. Some variant bellies lay pellets with a bitter edge that makes better capacitors for engines we will never see. One wrong sculler line, born from a winter famine of fog, developed a courtesy for steeper pitches that keeps gutters honest in brief floods. We did not plan this lineage; we observed it and found it a seat. Governance here is paying attention hard enough that accident is invited to sit down and tell us what it thinks it is good for.

Do I miss shade? Leaves? The way green makes noise when wind goes through it? Yes, in the shape that nostalgia takes when it is not allowed to run the town. We decorate with shine, with seize, with the little circles a fine bristle makes when it takes wet bloom down to clean metal. When I love someone, I heat a thin copper sheet and press our fingers into it and keep it until the inevitable green rises despite everything. We do not deny that life either. We just do not ask the yard to host it.

When you go, if you go, you will feel you are leaving no moment behind. The hum is so steady it seems the yard has not even turned its head to watch you. That is alright. It watched you on your way in and weighed your footfalls and lent you a little null where you needed it and took it back when you were safe. It will put its thumb back on my chest when your back is a shape on the rim and remind me that I am what I said I was: a man whose job is to be an ear until the moment I am a song I cannot correct, and then—later, on a good night, under a dome of light that is kind to animals and to grief alike—to be a small feast for grazers and the harmless pride of bellies laying a little extra because the yard has always known how to be kind.

So: I am found on a catwalk at dawn with a watch-bird on my shoulder and my fingers under a louver’s lip. I am one of the keepers of fences with no posts, an arranger of lanes written in harmonics, a reader of rails, a hum with a name. I am a pocket of hush moving over plates that once taught me where to put my feet and will go on teaching when I am done. I am a wrong kept until it was right, and a right kept until it could be kind. I am not the first resident—that honor goes to the note—but I am kin to it. If you listen for me, you will hear me rubbing the yard between two hands until its cloth warms and lies flat again.
