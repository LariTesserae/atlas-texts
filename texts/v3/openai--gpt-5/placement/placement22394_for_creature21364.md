At first I think I have come as myself: a person from air that moves too quickly, from rains that forget to touch anything on the way down. I arrive with the kind of voice that assumes distances, with a habit of pointing at horizons. The table ignores these habits the way stone ignores a leaf skittering across it. It receives me as it receives anything: by pressing me into the scale it knows.

Everything close. Everything touching. The sky not overhead so much as here with me, catching on my cheek, doing its slow work.

For a while I am only a pair of listening soles and a mouth that learns to keep shut. I move with the careful gait of someone walking inside the instrument they came to hear. I place my hand to one glossed wall and lean my forehead against a brim and learn the way my own breath darkens mineral for a second and then disappears into its ongoing.

I try on things without meaning to.

One morning a long avenue runs straight as a string through lifted rooms, and wind persuades itself into a ribbon down its length. I stand at one end and whisper, and the air picks up my words and carries them as if the city itself were curious. I feel my voice go thin and precise, navvying from lip to lip, borrowing each smoothness it passes to keep from tearing. The word arrives at a far balcony, smaller than a thumb, a whole day later, and comes back to me like something that was out longer than it asked permission for. For that day and a few after I am sure I am a message, a piece of traveling thinness. I learn the tide of the long breath that listens. I measure time by which straightways are awake.

Another noon I find a basin that keeps four notes going. High, low, middle, a shy one you only hear after you’ve heard the others. I sit and half close my eyes and know the way each note is a body of water deciding itself, added to very slowly, surrendered very slowly, weight distributing along an edge in ways that are older than my words for them. I hold my palm under one place where a bead hesitates. A skin rises between wet and dry. I feel tension, that old, brilliant negotiator, spread itself like a cloak. A drop agrees to be larger than it had to be simply because the stone offered it a road. I pull my hand away and, for an hour or so, I am a meniscus. I am the argument that prevents falling.

In a shaded atrium, fog thickens like wool, and from a fold under a ledge a bell-chime begins and then becomes itself for an hour. I stop all walking and sit with my knees up and let the sound take a small instrument I didn’t know I had and tune it. A pale toad the color of sweet dust looks out and gives up none of its thoughts, and in that moment I am a listener of a species that hadn’t included me until I consented to listen like this. The choir goes on until it doesn’t, and the silence that follows is not emptiness but the platform on which the day stands. That hour, I think I am a rung on the ladder sound uses to climb out of the ground.

Once, very early, I am a lowlander with the wrong feet, stepping too hard where the floor has a drum in it. The thinnest places in the old shell announce themselves up through my calves. I feel the emptiness below as a cool hand I did not ask for, and I step back. After that I am a person who watches where the shell is thin, who loves the hollows enough to keep them unbroken. I learn to walk like fog.

In the evenings, when the big breath turns around and comes back over itself, pearling along eaves and rooflets, I could be a veil. I watch how a comb of horsehair and sedge draws a ribbon out of air’s reluctance and leads it down into bowls barely the size of my two hands. I touch the wet hanging on that comb and then I don’t, and the not-touching becomes a discipline. The veil is hung exactly so to be obedient to what wants to happen, and for a night I am that obedience. I am the suggestion a surface makes to water: this way, yes, and this long.

And for a week it rains without raining. The plateau leans into itself, the high plazas wring, the subtle plumbing finds its hour. The long word comes down from upwind. Somewhere a great terrace that has filled within the patience of a day steps over its own lip and remembers how to be plural. The wash goes through. You can set your seasons by it. It doesn’t strike; it persuades. It doesn’t shout; it occupies. It touches every room on the way down with the steadiness of a good hand on a child’s crown, and the city answers by opening all the small doors it has grown for this purpose. I wait under a balcony that knows my head and let the voice of that passage make a scaffold in me. For that span of minutes I am a schedule that lives in the body. I am the rung by which time climbs into action and down again into rest.

I learn to speak at the scale the place uses. Balconies are arranged for people who like to murmur. Sound between them falls into itself if you push, so you don’t. You lean on stone the way it has taught you to, and you let your words carry just to who needs them. Someone across the alley gestures with only their fingertips, and the gesture is a whole letter. I begin to think of names as happenings, not as tags. Addresses here describe behaviors. I want an address like that.

I watch how the longer-lived citizens keep their records. A knotted cord under a eave to catch a thread of wet and lay it like a sentence. A hair placed on a rim so the future can start with the thickness of that hair. A row of pebbles wrapped in sedge weighting down a lip in the spots that are thinking of failing to be lip. The ledger is not ink; it is arrangement. It is this and this and this will mean that, in a year.

I am trusted, eventually, with a corner. That is when I stop trying on being anything else, not because those tryings were foolish, but because they were rehearsals for usefulness.

My corner is small by any other city’s measure and larger than I deserve. You could walk past and never know it had a keeper. You could stop and press your palms to the same places I press mine and feel something like a pulse, and not guess the room has my breath in it so deeply that if I left, in a year you would still be able to find it by the darker line it draws along a lip.

It sits at the knee of a long right angle where two old joints in the limestone have argued themselves square. Above it, a basin keeps two clean notes and a third that only shows up when the afternoon’s third sigh begins, that moment when the day is ordinary and then not. Below it, a gallery no wider than my shoulders takes air when the table breathes in and gives it back when the table breathes out. Every dawn something like milk writes itself pale along one side of a pool because a swath of algae I tended there last summer has learned its work. There’s a curtain that wants to be a shelf, and I have been patient with it, giving it a line of sedge to think along, anchoring that line with tiny weight and will. On cloudless days a gullied wall throws back enough cold at midnight that dew makes the wall briefly luminous with the thin light that comes from whatever the moon is thinking. The bees – when the plateau allows them to be called mine – hum in a washhouse under a dependable weep; I put my hand to their swarming when asked to and only when asked to.

Between those notes and that gallery and that dependable weep is where you would find me. Not at an address any lowland post could deliver to, but at a relation: up from the long square corridor that carries words if you only say them gently; beneath the cooling wall that brightens in a thin crescent on clear nights; where the third afternoon hush persuades fog into the smaller archway; under the shelf we have agreed, the stone and I, to grow like a slow smile. This is the whole of my province. It is more than enough.

I wake with the plateau's in-breath and lie still to feel it go through the drum floors. There are a few I haven’t learned yet; I don’t step there early, not even to gather the water set out to come to me. Children come by and count the ticks between drops where I have pinned a thread in place to make counting possible. I teach them the way to listen to the lower note when the higher ones are showing off. I teach them how to place a hair where it will begin a future. This is what their teachers taught me when I arrived with too much distance in my mouth.

Most of what I do is agree with things that already want to be as I want them. I spend a winter wicking a seam with cloth until the weak light that reaches it at noon can do more than it did before. A flake learns to think away from its wall. In spring a cold night loosens the thoughts we have learned together, and in the morning I walk by and shoulder the flake from itself. It falls as if grateful. We lift it – many hands, always quietly – and lay it where steps have almost become steps. We do not set it as a single act the way lowlands would, with decision and edge and tool. We invite it into a program that is already prospering, and we take the joy of a result we will barely recognize as something our wanting accomplished.

I am dressed in what the place thinks clothing should be. Sedge and willow-bark fibred, waxed with thin sweetness, slowly taking on crust. Rims draw lace on my cuffs where drips find me reliably. Sleeves remember drips and grow scallops. Hairs in my braid are taught to keep their curves by the same mineral that strengthens lips. The tips of those braids brush undersides and come away beaded, as if every stoop under an overhang is a brief conversation something has with me and chooses to leave a punctuation mark for.

I am not loud. No one here is, except the water that needs to be loud for a minute every day so all the agreements can reset.

I am hungrier than I expected to be and less worried about it. I eat what the table makes when it makes it. A bright spread pressed from algae; seed crushed with a stone that was a stone before anyone thought to use it for crushing; a bitter leaf that tells my stomach what to do with the green. If there is protein it is because a veil was well-hung in the morning and the little clouds were generous, or because a gift fell from the fast people in the air to remind us we share a world.

Some afternoons, in a long hallway the right height for it, a friend and I stand horizon-apart and speak like one instrument doing a duet with itself. The ribbon of wind carries the second voice back past me to some balcony where someone else is tending. So we all keep each other. We are never as alone as the table looks from above.

I do go down sometimes. A shoulder of waxed thread. A promise to come back with the sort of fiber the plateau does not grow. The descent is a stair a fault suggested thousands of lives ago and we have all been practicing since. The trees down there are extravagant and clatter too much; giving their talk no attention is a discipline. I barter our good thin honey for a knife that is too sharp and will be borrowed by everyone for a summer and then go away or be lost, which is the same thing here. When I climb again into a weather made at head-height, my lungs say the word for joy in a whisper you can hear across two balconies if you are waiting for it.

I have no name I would ask anyone not from here to use. Among ourselves I am the person whose home exists “between the two-note dish and the right-angled draft, beneath the cooling wall that crescents at moonrise; the shelf-hatcher; the one who set the hair that began the little bridge.” I am a keeper of intervals. I count the seconds between two hesitations and know by that counting whether the basin above has run enough to risk. I knock very softly at the places that sound hollow and make sure you never see me do it.

When I am done – in the sense that one of my bodies stops and will not be coaxed to start again – I will be washed with moss, and those who still learn from me will lay me under a friendly eave that has been waiting for years without knowing for what. Algae will be taught to write there with a particular color so that the gloss that forms has a letter in it. In a season there will be a light you can feel with your palm. In a handful of years there will be a lip exactly the curve of my own hand, and those who never heard me murmur will know how I liked to place it to feel the chill and the work of it going on.

If you look down from whatever high kindness lets you look down and try to see me, you won’t. The table is a plain, and most of us are the color of the work we love. But if you know what to watch for, you could catch sight of a bead too bright to be only water at the mouth of a narrow arch; it will be a veil I have set and the bowl below, waiting. You could notice a darker comma along a certain curve; that is where I breathe as I go by. You might see someone, not tall, lift their foot just so over a rim, not from fear of breaking it, but from devotion. That could be me. Or someone who holds the same address in a different season. Or one of the people before or after all our word-ways have ended, who still keeps time with what lives longest here.

Where to find me, then? Come in on a day when the third afternoon sigh is ready to begin. Stand at the mouth of a square-cut corridor that listens. Feel the breath turn around. Turn with it. Follow the shy note under the two loud ones until it confesses itself. There, beneath the wall that will catch the moon, in the arch where fog likes to be taught to become water, I will be the person with her palm out not touching the drip that wants to be a lip, counting, waiting, useful to the ongoing.
