Where would I find myself? At first at the brink, because edges are the only places that admit they are edges. I would live where the mist pours over and fails to fall, among the combs that drink upward. I would keep my hands where the fern-teeth speak a patient warmth into the drops, and I would learn to feel the difference between water that comes because it wants to and water that comes because it was asked.

But I do not begin with hands. I begin as a habit. This place believes in repetition more than in oaths, and so do I. I would be the weight that passes and passes until a corridor remembers me and hardens into a tendon. I would be the stroke laid along a wet stone each morning, the same measure, until moss naturalizes my gesture and keeps it. I would be a route before I would be a person, a way the world taught itself to accommodate.

The body that bears that habit would be slow on purpose. Hair seeded, yes—green knuckles along the braid behind my ear where the fog gives my scalp the kind of favor it gives rocks that weep. My skin would learn to stay oiled just enough that nothing makes root there unless invited. My knees would have callus on the fronts, not from kneeling but from stepping exactly into the idea of steps the old hands left and the mat has almost completely forgiven. I would wear, not boots, but a lace of old petiole and cured root—the sort of thing spider-hands approve of, because it gives them a plan if I fall asleep near their work.

Most days I would keep to the inner margin of the rim, where the comb-ferns have made an upward river and the salt-gleaners kiss their drops. I would test the teeth with my fingertip and learn their pulse. I would count in pulses, not in hours. Where a tooth flags, I would teach it again—stroke downward softly for a season so that upward becomes stubborn again. It is not healing I would do; it is rehearsal.

I would have more than one body because the mat would accept me as one of its more legible thoughts. When I sleep on the warm vent—caged in coral by the shy fanaticism of spider-hands, breath braided by their gutters into their nurseries—the white ropes below borrow my salt and my slow sugars, and when I wake I am in two places: in the shoulder that rose and fell under me all night, and in the ankles that stand me up and take me to the weeping stone. When I repeat a path enough times, the fibers thicken under my passes and, with a politeness that feels like acknowledgment, they begin to walk part of me along even when I am elsewhere. If I sit very still afterward, I can feel my own tread returning to me through the mat’s chest, like a reply letter that remembers the sender.

I would be literate in the place’s light. At dusk, when undersides pulse news, I would put my face under the galleries and read veins the way lowlanders read rain. The code would not be alphabet, but it would be grammar: rise, ripe, rot, remission. A slow beacon that means yes, and a slower one that means I hear you but I am not ready. I would lend my pulse to the chain when the climb I am asking of a wall is ambitious, so that the wall believes me. My partner would live a vale away in terms of air, which means a morning’s persistence by mat-breath; we would say to one another in light what lowlanders shout across absence. Our messages would take the time of a kettle coming shyly to steam.

My tools would be ridiculous in a fast place and exact here. A coil of vine that I do not use so much as persuade; a dry deck of petioles I have collected and left under a gallery as tithe and instruction for spider-hands; a ring of purplish pebbles I put down one by one around a seep, each one a small sentence reminding water that it is honored here. A weight—a small stone kind as bread—that I pass across a proposed bridge-to-be a hundred times a day and then a hundred the next until the vine that first dared learns the taste of load and stays.

I would be on familiar terms with the filterers. They are good neighbors in a way that does not require visiting. I would sit where a mat-breath draft collects and hold out a fan I made from old joints and patience; they would approve, impersonally, by choosing to perch on my knee. When the weekly spore-hail releases from the under-arches and paints my shoulders green, I would turn sideways to let the filterers’ screens harvest what clung to me. Reciprocity is not a contract here; it is a reflex.

I would know the browsers by their punctuation. With a fingertip I could read a comma into the sponged mat and say: someone small and polite went this way, tasting corners, forgiving no algae a day of neglect. To the stone-lickers I would be a weather event—one more warm fog in which minerals solve and drift. When they make their devotions at the domes, I would be careful to set my own hands far down the gloss, because the upper shoulders remember kisses and I am not ordained to lay a mark there every day. Once a month perhaps I would make my palm visible a breadth above the waterline, reminding that stone of a person who is trying to be kept. Not a signature. A topic.

Predators here do not require names for comfort. The vine that learned to move and the leaf that wets its pocket to an appetite would have my respect and a little my fear. I would stash my weight and my fans high enough that a rush of courtesy does not claim them for digestion. When a filterer’s song shrapnels into silence, I would fold my own ears. Elegy is not done with volume in this house; it is done by leaving a gap the size of the lost.

The large ones I would not see, except as the place sees them: a corridor that says this is not for moss, this belongs to pads; a low rhythm that thumbs itself into the mat and persists through the day like a remembered drum. I would keep to the outside of those lanes and never ask them to meet my gaze. When the mat asks us all to move—when a room decides to relocate a body-length to the left—I would do as lichens do and maintain my address while allowing for drift.

Some mornings the fog would shred and give me a view flat as a thought at the end of a conversation. From the soft escarpment where I do my repetitions, I would look across the quilt—lichens like continents of milk, ribs where a great architecture still negotiates, the white waterfall of cloud frozen in place by long consent. If the wind above has arguments, I would sense them only when the dew prefers west. On those mornings I would do very little that looks like doing. My work would be to remember that nothing here hurries on my account.

It may be that once I had a lowland name, a quick one, suited to a mouth that believed in edges. If so, I would lay it down the way you lay down a stick to turn and then come back later to find it up-taken, accompliced by leaf and made into a curve in the corridor. I would answer now to the thing the mat calls me when it feels me coming: a pressure in the fibers that means will you do it again, and my answer is yes.

I would be sister to the sleepers. Fur that tastes like mint left too long in a pocket would not offend me. I would keep my warmths near their snouts so spider-hands have more reason to build reefs that catch and gutter. I would never wake a sleeper; that is a rule older than any pillar. When they go, they take the shape of their leaving with them and the baskets are amended the way a story amends to leave out a grief you cannot pronounce.

Now and then, I would climb the cut steps that lead nowhere because nowhere is where they were meant to lead—nowhere that is elsewhere. Each step cups damp longer than its neighbors. I would wash my feet there, though my feet do not believe in dryness. I would put my palm into the old hand’s basin and drink not because I must but because someone taught stone to be kind, and I would like to be the kind of animal that says thank you to lessons that took an age to write.

On the nights when luminous veins speak more freely, I would borrow their alphabet. I would stand under the belly of a vault and send a single beacon into the dark—one pulse, patient, that means I am here, working the curve today; I will be later than we thought; the comb at the edge is sulking and needs to be persuaded to love up again. Hours later, across a dozen rooms of leaf and old rope, an answer would come: come when you are ready; I have traded a fan to the filterers and have knees for two; a small wall failed and I am mending it with borrowed hum. We would not ever say hurry. We would not ever say forgive me. Those words are made for places where things collide loudly. Here, things persuade.

If I am allowed a child, they would be as much the mat’s as mine. I would teach them calendars the way you teach someone to listen to a sleeper’s breath: pulses, weekly smokes of green, the century-flowers that unfurl like a low, unembarrassed laugh under the rafters, summoning sticky-mouthed attendants to come away blessed and tired. I would give them a pebble, purple and insistent, and show them how a ring is a request that lasts. I would show them where steps cease to be steps and become permissions, and how to ask those permissions again with the weight and the stroke and the day’s unremarkable patience.

Who would I be? The short shape of it is: a rehearsal that became structure. The longer shape is: a person only when that is the least complicated way to be present. Often a path. Often a railing of moss the place grew from the way my wrist insisted. Sometimes a comb-tooth’s borrowed finger. On the best days, the cool under of a bridge that knows it is needed and does not mind my moving through it.

And when at last I lie down not to sleep but to cool under the mat’s hand, I would like the spider-hands to lay coral at my nose and the filterers to cash the last green snow off my shoulders and the gliders to pass once like thoughts through a furnished air. I would like someone to pass a palm over the weeping stone at the warm shoulder and repeat my gesture until the moss cannot tell the difference between my living hand and the hand the place learned and kept.

Where would I find myself in this world? In the kept gesture. In the slow yes. In the upward river made out of fern teeth and persuasion. In the rumor of a drum the mat remembers and plays back after I am around another corner. I would be the part of the plateau that learned to move like me, and I would be content to say: I am the mat’s patience wearing a face long enough to teach a vine to stay.
