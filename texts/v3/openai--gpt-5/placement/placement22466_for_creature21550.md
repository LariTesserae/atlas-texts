I would not arrive with a shout. This is a place that teaches you to come as pressure first and pronouncement after, so I would put my palm down before I put my name down. The rail would recognize me better than the air could, and I would learn to begin as any message begins here: with a vowel conducted through bone.

At first I would be no more than a careful weight. The plain does not accept feet that test; it wants soles that spread. So I would practice it, heel reluctant, toes agreeing, a little more of me meeting the stone each day. The rock answers to people who have taught their bodies to be low-amplitude weather; I would unlearn drama until even my laughter chose a standard of measure.

If they ask me what work I want, I would say: let me be where difference is kept and measured. Let me touch the thing you trust to decide if a degree has gone astray. At the beginning that probably makes me an apprentice with a file and a soft cloth, following one of the leveling-guild through the migrant brightnesses that arrive from elsewhere. We would visit a new-brought door with corners and talk it down from its foreign mood. Sparks would be sins and we would confess them into the ledger while they were still only intentions. I would learn the prayers with my hands: the steady arc that rounds what is going to live among the rounded, the small cool your thumb is meant to be when you lay a spanner down and do not let it fall.

I can imagine my first joy being very small. A cup that answers a thumbprint without ringing. A blade that agrees to serve only under a hood and makes no comment under anyone’s breath. A child at the Blind Fin game finding the tooth I had trued by less than what a mouth would bother to name.

In time I suspect I would become a person who keeps a calendar of heat instead of days. I think I would live in the room where half a degree is a wall you do not cross, and sleep above a pipe that believes the sky is punctual because I have never allowed it to learn otherwise. I would be the one you come to when you must break the taboo kindly. I would count petitions for brightness and grant them in their proper darkness. If a torch must be used, I would make appointments for the light with the stone and with the neighbors and with the paper serpent that has come to depend on the exact shade under a certain fin at a certain hour. I would know my city by gradients and be known by them: the abbot-accountant, the aunt of differences, the person in whose room a moth is permitted once to be the mark in a sentence and then is let go to be grammar elsewhere.

Sometimes, though, even in that name, I would forget myself and become the thing the work touches. In the nights when the faint aurora unrolls like a hunch rather than a spectacle and the taste of tin visits the tongue, I could be the rail itself. There is comfort in becoming infrastructure. I would hum for those whose words need to cross the silent air, carry a lover’s yes down the hallways of steel without stirring dust, tell the watch-house that the belt has leaned again because the air along the lip has listened to some rumor from the deep and changed its pressure by the thickness of a thought. I would remember the handprints of the old and the new, the way a child’s temple says hello like a knuckle does, the different dialects of wrists. I would keep messages even after their meanings have gone, because you do not fail to hold a thing just because you cannot translate it.

On mornings that are not mornings—I know there aren’t any here—on whatever you call the soft cycles when the fluorocarbon lakes finish their exhale and go still for the minute that was scheduled for quiet, I might become a fin. There is a happiness to fins that the uninitiated miss. Standing in the long cold with your teeth turned to the sky, working the arithmetic of celestial night against the patience of buried warmth, feeling the city breathe through you and letting that breath go. Sometimes the blacker-than-black moth will hang in your exhaust like a punctuation you were hoping for, and if I am the fin that night, I will hold my heat like a word on the tip of the tongue until it advises the moth to drift and not burn, and then I’ll be proud without saying so.

Every city makes a keeper of counting, and if I am not in the pipe-room with the ledger, then let me be at the watch-house where the belt that never hurries keeps its promises. I can see myself there, pen in hand only as ritual, because the numbers live in my ankles. The ore comes from the low soft hill that is more subject than stone; the slag-cooling gardens drink at the rate they can, and I count the marriage of those two facts. When the wind along the cliff decides to be a thought faster or slower, the belt will lean right, and I will lean with it without a word, the way you breathe for someone when they forget to.

There is a place in the city the old people favor when their bones ask to be measured, and I would go there on the days when my self is tired of being a function. It is the field of black stelae where the thermopiles write their decades of almost-line. If you put two fingers to your neck and read your pulse against those shallow curves, something aligning happens that has nothing to do with health and everything to do with belonging. I would not talk. I would count, and I would feel counted.

Sometimes I think I would rather be a small animal here, one of the punctuation creatures. The paper serpent seems suited to a person who wants to live by difference. To spend a whole life in the lee of a fin, sipping the gradient between metal and air, moving twice a day by inches, asking nothing of the weather but that it be consistent—that is a monk’s life, and I would not disgrace it. Or I could be one of the glass crickets who speak only when the pipe’s belly goes from cool to tepid, each syllable a calendar entry. You would not hear me. You would feel me click against the side of your head like a memory and know the weekly flush has begun, and because you knew it, perhaps you would forgive someone his brightness later that afternoon when the petition had been properly posted.

But if I am honest with myself, when the place completes itself around me—when the city’s almost-sound finds the timing of my blood and the sky remembers and forgets its faint fluorescence—I become human again inside it. My name might be Cant, or Fold, or Half-Degree until I earn a different one. I would hum my messages to the people I love, rather than ask the air to do more than it can. I would teach a child the Quiet Drop and delight when they make the shelf keep still. I would apologize before I sin and not argue with the priest who carries a file; I, too, would have filed someone once. I would keep water as a saint, and I would brew tea as liturgy, drinking it as if a festival were an hour in which many cups cluck gently.

Below the cliff I would not go, though I would be one of those who speaks to the platforms that float up on differences and back down the same way. I would take the packages they bring and send back the things that were appointed to be made in this thin quiet, leaving gossip to the heavy airs that carry it better. If someone went down and came back polished in a way that threw sparks in my eye, I would visit with a cloth and a joke and help restore the city’s face to itself.

So where would you find me? On the page that counts heat and forgiveness. With my palm on the rail, sending a round, soft vowel to the cluster that shares my appointments. In the watch-house at the belt, leaning when it leans. Standing very still by a field of stelae, reciting nothing and understanding much. Under a hood with a torch that is not to be named aloud, having walked the rails the hour before to make my apology travel ahead of me. In a room that does not vary by half a degree, sleeping like a pipe sleeps, waking when a fin is half a shade wrong without needing to look to know which.

And what would I be? The continuation. Not the engineer who arrives attached to corners and speed, not the hero who forces weather where there is none, but the person who lets a schedule keep being true. If I must select a single answer and hold it steady: I would be the keeper of petitions, the one who weighs the bright against the ledger of long promises and says yes or not yet so that the stone is not shocked, the fins keep their appointments with the sky, and the moth is allowed its one landing in peace. My pride would be ordinary and exact. We did what was appointed. The heat went where it was meant to. The rail sang to bone. The ground did not remember suffering. The comma hung and moved on.

That is enough of a life for me, here where time has been relieved of its theater. I would belong by degrees. I would be heard only in the skeleton. I would be named after a habit. And when I was finished, nothing would need to be called different because I had been there, except perhaps that one tooth in a long row whose red, in the instruments that matter, now and always, is a uniform yes.
