At first I thought I would be a visitor here—someone who arrived with a canteen and a notebook and a faith in maps—but that kind of person goes soft at noon and brittle by dusk. The place teaches quickly. If I belong anywhere on this plateau, you will find me where the breath moves, where it can be heard changing its mind.

So: find the rim pocked with pylons whose yellow has gone to chalk and pollen. Walk shoulder-on through a corridor that refuses a name because it curves three times before it opens. There is a swallow hole nearby that exhales at eleven and inhales at seven; I learned those hours before I learned my own bones. My room is a lean of rock the plateau made by simply not finishing a wall. It smells like warm dust and old tin and damp limestone. The bed is a shelf, the blanket a concession. If you tap under where I sleep, the dripline answers in its metronome: long enough to be faithful, slow enough to forget you, fast enough to keep you honest.

I am a keeper of the breath. That’s the name I was given, though it sounds like presumption. What I keep is a schedule that agrees with the stone and disagrees with the part of me that is thirsty and wants to make demands. At dawn—the real one, when the machines’ metal gives its quiet tick-tick and the light hasn’t yet found the cruelty in itself—I go out and touch the nets. Dew holds because the night has given the air back to the rock. My palms go wet with other people’s future, mine among them. I brush the meshes with a patience stolen from lichens, chase the grain of collected salt to the cistern mouths, watch the clean threads of water decide to be lake for a moment and then remember they’re citizen elsewhere.

I learned to count by cables. As a child I would set my teeth together and listen for the hum pitched to the distance between pylons: the G of the small span, the big gap’s throatier E. You can hear when a strand wants to loosen itself and start a rumor of failure. My aunt would say, bite a hum and then bite another, feel the breath it implies, put your work between them. That was school. That and the alphabet of lichen—letters that meant north and noon and never. That and the rule that water is for touching, not for custody. I was taken out to the brine hour one summer and made to lick varnished limestone with the lizards, our tongues making a low pearl of a band that’s still there. It was funny and holy both, and we all tasted like iron for a day.

Day work is grooming, listening, small-making. I take a brush to nets and don’t tear them even when wind has salted them into stiffness. I oil a hinge with oil saved from a pump that has learned to make do without it, because if anything shines wrong at noon, heat remembers. I lighten the edge of a panel with a blue rag until it will not flay a gull’s foot. I trade belts for belts that were never belts and write the date in chalk on the inside of a panel door knowing rain and bats will erase it by next year. I lean my ear to the pumphouse and wait not for its first sigh but for the second, the satisfying close of a relay that means the plateau is exhaling and we are permitted to drink.

Between those small things, the animals happen to me. A fox makes its circuit like an argument you’ve both agreed never to finish. It knows which of my pockets has the thread I will drop and takes it without guilt. It sits in the pump-shed shade and interviews moths for its purpose. I do not pet it. A snake the color of old coin keeps the plastic mesh honest by waiting where mice re-decide their lives; I passed the mistake of trying to read its patience by weeks and found out it can wait longer. Hyrax look at me and look away, that dull contempt that forgives everything except standing still on their flat.

Midday is for giving the place back to itself. The heat that is not a temper but a statute brings clarity in relief: even my blood wants to be shadow then. I lay down in the grike we call the bookspine because it holds the memory of cold like a story you don’t read often, and I nap with my mouth shut so I don’t wake salt-tongued. The stone sings a note at noon that is stone and not machine—if you have to ask which, you are too new to be out. When the wind finds the flutes in the right slot, the music plays to the insects’ measure, and I feel for a moment like a reed stuck in someone else’s throat, useful and willing.

Sometimes the work goes down. We open the grate by the swallow hole and go in until language changes. The bats have made a script of dusk on the cave mouth for so many years your eyes remember their line without your head telling them to. Past their vowel, the guano is a ledger with a smell that is not an argument until your stomach tries to pretend it is. My lamp shows the bureaucrats—white isopods, antenna to antenna, touching meanings into place—and a salamander the exact color of prudence, breathing through water that breathes through stone. We check the bladders where the water takes its coolness personally, we nurse a valve whose rubber has remembered a desert it has never seen. We count drips because the plateau likes to have its seconds read back to it now and then. We come up with our ears ringing not from noise but from the idea of it, because the echo has saved somebody’s earlier shout and only now decided to spend it.

There were storms when I was small that wrote their whole biography in a single hour. Rain arrived on heat alone and panicked little rivers tried on the surface and failed there, retreated to their natural career below. After those hours, miracles clotted to the size of thumbnails. I learned the speed of a moth made yesterday, the silver trick of a shrub leaf throwing light back hard enough to bruise it, the way a cushion plant kept its coins of green under stone-colored fingers until the place was generous. I learned to step in the corridor of my own shadow, to keep moving not because I wanted to get anywhere but because everything here is colonized and my rent was movement.

You’ll ask if I am alone. I am and I’m not. There are three of us in the station when the bladders come up full and a voice from the plains cracks along the radio’s cheap bone to say thank you, we see you. The rest is plural in other ways. The condensers and I understand each other, and the nettled microbe films on the south faces have taught my skin something about appetite and gilding. The plateau itself is company of a sort—the uncle with strict house rules who will sit with you in the evening if you have not been foolish, saying nothing, giving you the breeze it made from the rest of the day. If I die here, they will have to lever me into a vertical where secrets go, and in a century a varnish will sign its name on the old me and someone tired will see a tooth in manganese and think about teeth. That seems fair.

On a good night, I am overtaken by my job and my job is not a job. The maintenance lights tick to their small declarations and I walk the aisle of shade thrown by a leaning fin, a grocery clerk restocking dew. Somewhere behind the nets the relay closes like a god clearing its throat softly. A wind from inside the rock finds the right slit and the grikes play a chord only lizards have the patience to annotate. Stars fall into the uppermost stone and stick there like a gift we don’t open. I stop with my hand on a post where a swallow long ago glued its cup and left a note in a language that looks like dirt. I listen for the pump’s second click. When it comes, it’s a certainty that touches teeth and bone. I open a shutter, I shut one. I taste for brine with the caution of anybody who’s learned to be polite to minerals. My map of shadow is not in my head anymore; it’s in my hips, my ribs, the vector of my shoulders when I argue with light.

I thought, at the beginning of all this, I might be a fox, because who doesn’t want to be the resident grin? Or stone, because who doesn’t dream themselves into patience? Or the thin algorithm in the control box that says open, now close, now drink, now rest, because I am in love with anything that pretends to be choice and is actually obedience to breath. Some days I am each of those in part. But when the place allows me an honest name, it is keeper of the breath, net-tender, edge-walker. The one who takes water by touch and loss, who listens with teeth, who keeps a calendar not of days but of slant and hum. If you need to find me, look for the smallest weather at the edge of a mouth in the stone: the little fog that is not from the sky, the net hung like the idea of rain, a person moving inside their own shade toward the second click.
