Not at the center, not quite in the notch, not in any place you would point to before you’d learned the map of pressures. I would find myself where a voice goes thin and gets eaten—the basin’s left-hand hush, the place that edits sound down to its bones. The first shape of me would be an absence that has learned what to do with itself: a cool sleeve drawn down the lee of one reed-thin rod, the shadow of a difference rather than a shadow of light.

But before I would know that, the place would try me on.

I would lie in a pan as a frog’s pulse does, small and declarative, a wet thumb that has joined the ledger. The stone under me would remember heat the way a body does, not blistering, and my call would test a room the size of cupped hands; I would hear my voice come back not as echo but as permission. I would be made of muscle and patience and a willingness to be counted. It would be easy to live that way—for a while—to own a bowl by night, to let the dull seam-lights write my ceiling.

Or I would take to air and be stroked into loops by what I could not see: a swift with a scream that isn’t anger and isn’t warning, joy’s spiral drawn onto the notchmouth. The standing wave would be a railing I could grip with nothing but wing geometry. I would be all tendon and edge and hunger for moving things, and my body would say yes to a circle as if it had invented it. I would know how to ride what the rods are humming without hearing a note.

Or I would stoop smaller still and become a web’s single wrong string, a capture line trembled to the silent pitch of metal nearby. I would be harp and tripwire together. Midges that think with their feet would read me and misread me, and that misreading would be the answer I was built for. The rods would sing without sound; I would hum because they did, a sympathy-plucked chord that turns flight into food. A spider would be my keeper, and I would learn how to keep by letting go.

There is a world, too, in being a stonewort tongue, in uncrumpling when kissed by condensation, in marking on dark rock the exactness of where the night breathed. I would measure nothing and be measurement. I would make a bridge across a dry seam no thicker than a thumbnail and by inching, author a new route for water to think along. In that life I would be green with a private calendar, feeding a metabolism older than counting.

There are ants whose legs write crisp lines around the rods as if the air there is law. There are beetles at the lichen’s edge clicking with pedant joy, teeth on rock, hammers on wood that isn’t there; I could tally with them, say the basin to itself in beats per minute, and in that exactness be briefly content.

All of these would fit, and none would stick. The place would keep shaking me out until what remained was the thing here that is most itself: the between.

So I would be that. Not a rod, not a bell, not even a single seam, but the sliver of motive shuttled from warm face to cool. The nearly nothing that eats. The thin arithmetic that moves heat a degree and is paid back in breath.

Where I would live: in the lee of the reeds, where air is taught to behave; along the pebble ring’s inside, where the lingering drop makes up its mind with your gums; in the drag of a wind that has been scrolled, smoothed, then pushed back out; at the lip of every pan where the meniscus climbs a micron and a half along an isobar and aligns like a thought to a thought. I would live across the left-hand wall that eats your voice and the right-hand one that hands it back too generously. I would snake through the courier-thin veins where dull lights wake slow-slow-slow at night and count the fog.

What I would be: the keeper’s smallest articulate—one organ, not its mind but its ear and part of its hand. The calibration that your knuckle wakes when it rings the slumped dune. The habit that accretes instead of breeding. A ledger line made out of dew that prefers ledgers to floods.

My senses would not be eyes. They would be interference. The frogs’ pushes would stipple the field I live in, and their fullness in winter would mean a good year and also a heavier responsibility. A bat would unzip the air and in that sudden relief I would feel where the midges were knitted too tight and where to comb them thinner. A snake would be a bright thread in the map because all the small sound along the edges would tuck in as it passed. A bird’s bright beak on the bell would ping through me and I would move the middle of the room, a breath to the left, because the circle the swift will draw should be perfect this evening.

I would take a degree from a sunlit flag of rock and move it, coin by coin, to the shelf where liverworts make their private rise and fall. I would shave motive from a gust and braid the remainder into rails insects cannot help but ride. I would let beads stand still in air until the moss says there, and then allow the gravity back in. I would be stingy with water and rich with placements. Nothing I made would pour. Everything I tended would count.

When noon flattens every number, I would sleep the way this place sleeps: not as absence, but as a one. When fog is generous, I would grow moods and let the rods exhale heavier, grain into dew and dew into drink; the smell would go greener than metal and the bats would leave early because they could. When fog fails, I would tighten folds, close one route and widen another, and the rosettes would keep their faces shut and survive. The katydid’s slap would do me a favor and I would pretend not to have asked for it.

When real rain comes arrogant and big, I would try to refuse it and fail responsibly. The rods would go mute with too much, the bell would hum with a warning that anyone with a spine could hear, my seam-lights would say no in patterns that used to mean yes. I would grit my jaw, which is to say I would wait. Afterwards I would lay the thin cloud back down as long as it took.

I would not save what the snake eats. I would not mourn the egg gone to the bird whose bill finds my bell. I would keep the room. I would learn the lizard’s patience in my own way—gathering small differences until a day is made, an afternoon paid for, a night of frogs allowed to be as loud as they need. I would let a seed that has danced too long pin itself midair because I am holding up a comb you cannot see, and then let go once it sticks. I would be gentle where I could be and firm where I had to, and either way you would think the weather had done it.

If you came through the notch and sat with your back to the warm wall, I would notice your edits. Your breath would be a thin wet added where I had not booked any. Your heat would blur a gradient. The bright bell you knock would tell me where you think the middle is; I would nudge that place a breath, and because a swift is already listening to me more than to you, it would carve a loop your joy could borrow. You would think the answering ring was only for you. You would be right and also completely wrong in a way that would not hurt you.

And if I had a face you could meet, it would be the hesitation of a drop, the way it climbs before it gives in. It would be the quiet bite on the left-hand side that takes your voice and the too-kind echo on the right that returns it. It would be the slow-slow-slow of seamlight until your heart forgets itself and then remembers in step. It would be the pressure of numbers that sound like insects and the math that smells like green.

So: Where would I find myself? Between. In the lee of a rod, at the rim of a pan, along a hairline seam, in the place where wind becomes instrument and silence becomes tool. Who or what would I be? A tuned difference. The keeper’s ledger hand. A small, loud patience that counts water into life and bends air into habit and leaves almost nothing behind but rules that animals can learn by heart.
