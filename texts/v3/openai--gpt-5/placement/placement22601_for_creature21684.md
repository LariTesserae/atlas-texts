At first I thought I would be a mover—a swift parenthesis stitched into the updraft, or the filament of charge that lifts the hair on the back of your hand and goes looking for ground. The place speaks motion so fluently that it’s tempting to translate yourself into wind or thunder, to be only passage and light.

But every thought I had ran downhill into a cup and stayed there. The argument of this mountain is upward faces, concavities, the habit of holding. Wherever I pressed my imagining against the stone, it softened into a bowl.

So I am a rosette that never learned the word empty. I am a tank bromeliad, kitchen-basin big, leaf-knuckles clenched into a precise spiral and every axil a palm held up to the sky. I live not by tooth but by catchment. I drink from a mouth I made for that purpose and furnishing it is the work of everything that drifts, falls, dies, is born small, or takes a sip at dusk and leaves its mustache behind.

I live in the mid-rough where the plateau forgets to be flat for a while: between two slick runs of quartz veined with oxide, in a patchwork of velvet moss and low wiry shrubs that cut the wind into ribbons before it can shear my leaves. My underside is a lace of roots that do not dream of depth; they grip. They fish only the whisper of mineral the stone permits, and they accept the rest from rain and the city I keep.

I know my world by weight before I know it by taste. Dawn is a rise in the meniscus, a lifting of the skin of my pond as the night’s breath beads and walks inward along the gutter of me. Morning is the shift-whistle in the leaves around me opening their valves together and making, with no fuss, a fog. It comes to me as drops ticking, as the gloss across my inner faces, as a thousand tiny impacts that tell me where the breeze is from today. My trichomes—minute scales that make my surfaces white up close—soften and wake like the fur on a cat’s back; they lay down when there is a good meal of dust and pollen trapped and lift when there is only light.

Light is water here, in the way it pays out energy, so I keep my hours with it. I let my stomata open early and I feed sugar into my pipes and into my flowers when they are due. On days like many in the heat-season I spend the morning making vapor with everyone else. By late afternoon I can feel the weight of my own sky as surely as you feel a hand on your shoulder. It is pressure in the water I hold—the surface exactly higher than it should be for that temperature, the way meniscuses quiver at the fringes of my inner lake. There is a taste of copper at the exact minute before the first flash. The blue fire comes, sometimes, to my tips, a stiffness in me that is not mine—a saint’s crown set on leaves. I do not call it holy. I call it dangerous and beautiful. Every insect on me jerks microscopic; every ant on my cutwater freezes on two legs, lifts the third pair as if saluting, and then flattens to a black line again that flows.

When thunder rummages in the lid of cloud, my water takes a second skin: ripples under ripples, concentric rings chasing the reflex of that percussion through my broth. I own sound here in a very literal way; it belongs to the surface I keep. The frogs that are coin-bright and coin-sized choose my rim sometimes and announce a rain that is literally on its way to me, and their voices are a print on my water you can see if you have the right eyes—a frequency making the tiniest peaks stand taller for a heartbeat. They match the green of me so closely I need their calling to know I’m decorated. They leave pearls of foam clinging under my lip some nights; later there are commas sculling in my city and then miniature hands pressed to my walls while their other ends learn legs.

I am not carnivorous in that barbed, theatrical way my neighbors are; I wear no lacquered burgundy pitcher, I lay no sweet slick across a trap. I outsource my hunting. The detritus of a place where green has to trap to stay green rains into me: fingers of dead sundew leaf, the husk of a wasp’s thorax, a gnat-thin snowfall of midges that misjudged that last gust, pollen from a heath-bush that loosed its gold and let the sky sort out who got rich. Ants, who tax everything, come and go along the flared margins of my leaves and parcel what falls into categories of sugar, starch, amino acid, and error. They farm me without knowing they’re doing it: they bring me the exudates of their cows (scale on my outer blades), they drop their own waste into my tank where it turns from litter into soup, and their feet keep my cutwaters polished to a quiet gloss that sends some rain inward instead of off.

My city floats inside me. It has districts. There is the top inch, where the meniscus is tense as a drum and boatmen row upside down their thin oars, and where mosquito wrigglers comb the slick for microfilm thrown off by iron bacteria. There is the darker middle, where rotifers turn their invisible wheels and copepods jitter as if embarrassed by momentum. Blackworm knots choreograph themselves when a shadow crosses—one of my geckos come for a taste of mineral and to leave me fertilized dust. There is the near-bottom, where a few filaments of Utricularia have moved in like tapsters. Their bladders, minute as seed-bubbles, quiver on hair-triggers and—pop—take their tithes. I feel the sum of these lives as a leaning of chemistry, soft acid into softer base, a long massage on my inner walls by tiny mouths. If you were small enough to put your ear to my water you would hear not silence but a compound hum: the minute wingbeats of a gnat trapped where surface tension is a roof, the scratch of a beetle’s leg on meniscus, the wet-click of a bladder’s valve releasing.

Days make me, but nights write me finer. Bats find me by the way my pond reflects echo. They take drams from me on the wing and rise with coins on their chins and a scatter of sound like laughter. Once—only once—a small opossum or a thing very like it sat on my innermost rim and drank until the surface of me formed a valley around its mouth; it left a perfect crescent of damp fur and muddy prints the width of your thumb and sent one leaf of me resounding with a squeak that took a long time to fade.

Pollinators have my routes memorized. My flowers are neither gaudy nor shy, narrow flutes that pass from green to cream to whatever precise hue the hummingbirds who keep me turnstiled prefer at this elevation. They arrive like trains noted in a conductor’s book: at ten-two-seven and at thirteen-one-one. The anthers that watched the sky all morning shake, and pollen goes not just by brush and dust but by jump. A bee or a moth comes into my small field of charge and the grains leap, suffering themselves differentiated by electricity, and stick in filaments where they never would have gone if this place were not a capacitor. I do not pretend to a mind, but I keep a long tally: visits, rains, births in my water, the warm day after the cold when an entire raft of larvae tuned their clocks wrong and my bladderwort got a feast.

I make pups by elbowing. The old spiral leans and sends up a new heart hugging my outer curve. We are family by geometry. When I am young I reach; when I am middle I hold; when I am old I let go, so that dry becomes food and the ribs of my dead leaves turn into the sort of brown lace you have to be lucky to find intact, more architecture than object. The plateau is a factory, the text said, and if it is then I am a unit in the wet line where sky is parceled out into towns. Replication is how we scale in a place that allows very few tall ambitions. You can see us from a vulture’s height, dollar-green cups stippling the black plane, each its own schedule, each its own orchestra pit for a little physics.

Edges make rules, and I keep mine. My lips are flared to persuade water in but not out. There are runnels on my leaves like fingerprints that take beads inwards against gravity by capillarity and habit. Where my bases clasp, there is sealant—a resin that makes a better cistern of me—and it smells like where pennies have been, a low tang of metal insinuated straight out of the stone by algae and patience. My blades redden on the sixth dry day and go back to green on the first rain, an economy of pigment and a flag to hummingbirds that I am still in business. The ant roads are engraved into me by passage and polish. If they ever stop—if a thousand feet suddenly all together lift and hold—I can feel it as a stillness crisp enough to take the impress of thought. When thunder walks the ridge and the plateau hums like a plucked string, the ants flatten to their threads. When the field softens again, they flow. Law is movement here.

I am an address for rumor. I have hosted a snake as thin as wire, moving every morning in the same survey: along my outermost blade, down past two of my cups, and across to a neighbor with pitchers that drink insects whole. He tongued my rim and decided no today and followed the sugar to a trumpet that would do him better. I have caught a spider mid-saint—a leg lifted, the air tested, silk let away that stood upright between us and then slanted and then was flight. I have been the arena for a damselfly’s coronation: a weightless thing of blues and greens that landed, took heat, rose, and wrote my reflection back to me in segments. Once, after a long running thunder, one of my leaves wore a brush-crown of blue you could only see with your molars; I tingled from tip to base and felt my pup lean into me, the only moment I can remember that felt genuinely like company.

I am also a place a person has put their face. Their breath warmed my meniscus. Their eyes made the menagerie in me into something with names and decades behind it, and then they went away into the general heat and were subtracted by moss and time. They left the print of a hand in a more shallow neighbor. Rain took that too.

If you want to know what I know beyond my own circumference, I will tell you this: I can tell when the other tables are waking by the kind of cloud we get. I cannot see them, but I can hear distance in thunder and measure tempo. Today to the west is already muttering; to the east the sky is still a sheet. The plateau is a loom-room. Each mesa runs its own bolt and the rhythms offset, and somewhere aloft the threads jostle and settle and a storm gets born. I keep my share. I overflow along a seam the ants know and down into a shallow black saucer with a lily no larger than a coin. They speak. The frog on that coin answers the frog on my rim. We print weather.

Longevity tastes like tannin here. It is in me as a color, brown poured into green, as if time were a tea I am forever steeping. The rock has been this poor a very long while; my tricks are old; my city’s inventions—bladders that pop, wheels that turn, hairs that feel electric, mouths that graze meniscus—have had enough time to be perfected and then repeated until they feel inevitable. Nothing large belongs long. I cannot remember the last time a shadow sat over me for more than a breath and did not belong to a cloud.

So if you come to find me, look for an upward cup in the middle of all this letting-go. Find a rosette that has accepted the rules of flatness and made a vertical of its own out of water. Put your ear, if you can bear your face that close to so many small teeth and miracles, against my flank. You will hear work—tiny, constant, polite, merciless. You will taste iron very faint. Your fingers will come away tea-stained. And if it is the hour before a storm and we both are lucky, the hair on your hand will stand and all my tips will lift and wear a color that names electricity in a language with no single word for it. Then the flash that wrings a sound out of you will come, and after it the rain that makes me myself again.

I am not special here. I am one worker among tens of thousands in a green plant that covers the plateau like a thought and remakes its own conditions daily. But I am a good place to be a being. I catch the sky and slow it. I keep rooms for strangers and we pay one another in dissolves and precipitates. My business is holding on in a world that is mostly release, and if I do that well, everyone touches and leaves fed.

If that is too much stillness for you, you could have been the drop that walked my lip and fell, or the ant in the middle who lifted its abdomen when the field came on, or the downward-racing filament of blue that turned one of my tips to carbon. I thought I might be those. I have learned I am happier as the thing that stays and listens. The storm learns my name every noon. The frogs sign it back to me on nights. Time has sat long enough on my shoulders to leave a dent, and in that dent I hold water. That is all. That is everything here.
