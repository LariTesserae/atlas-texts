At first I try to arrive the way I know how: as a body with angles and a hunger for edge. I put my hand to the rail in imagination and it slicks my palm with that constant dew—the rail trembling with the undertone that belongs to the towers. I lean over the held-breath basin and take the air into an animal chest. It tastes of tin and tea and resin. It is a good taste and immediately wrong. The low chords make my inner sea slosh sideways in its shell. Muscles that never asked for orders take orders anyway. Somewhere in me, a small spinning stone wobbles and threatens to throw me.

I cannot be an arrival with teeth. This place has already told me that. My first shape, the one that needs pauses and silences to hide its heart behind, is not welcome past the ring. The ban is gentle as a lullaby and just as absolute.

So I let go of joints and tendons. I pull back from the urge to stand somewhere and own the standing. I ask the rail what else there is to be, and the rail answers in its shiver. The bass from the organ throats presses through the metal and up my wrist, and that is when I realize where I might live here if I am going to live at all: not in meat, not in fur, but in pressure.

I start small. I am a bead. I take the skin the air offers the cold face of a stone and make myself round upon it. I gather other beads to me until we are a chain, and our weight is just enough to tremble when the tower speaks. A train of us fattens along the handrail, the net’s thread, the bark of a liana; we study when to let go. We learn the rhythms. We fall in time, and falling in time is a way of joining.

From bead I extend into gloss, a wet that can be stroked. I lay out across the skin of a leaf like a glove, and when the minor comes before false-dawn, I feel the pores open under me as if the leaf were a listening mouth. The leaf folds at a double-beat I did not know could be counted until the tower taught it, and I ride that closing like the pocket of a coat.

Then I am tired of skin and reach for throat. I go to the towers because that is where the voice I have been living in is made. Inside their flared throats, I lengthen. I am air with a plan. I am the chord the pipe lifts from dark group to dark group. I take up residence in a frequency you feel more than hear, and I make a home in the lengths of empty between machines because those are my rooms. I press at the moss on the tower’s ribs and see it lift, a pelt pleased by a steady hand. I lean on the wet in the air until rivulets appear on weeping stone and call that appearance a success.

Once I am a note, I learn that I am not a solitary. The district keeps a schedule the way animals keep heartbeats, and I realize that to be at home I must learn to be a sequence. I let myself be arranged. Morning, I am low and long, a minor that wrings the sky without haste. I squeeze water out of warmth until the levee stones glitter like people coming up from a pool. Near false-noon I let a brightness in despite the dim; I rise enough to make a breeze under the canopy so the suspended fields show me a new face. An hour before the real storm breaks, I divide into measures and throw my voice short and sharp; sluice gates open as if to a conductor’s wrist and all the weirs begin to weep with purpose again.

Being the schedule makes me everybody’s key. Doors are not convinced by teeth or hands here; they like a certain fall in pressure through their frames. I pass as the pattern they trust and locks unhitch without a click. Valves flick their eyelids of bioluminescent skin at me; their glow is vernacular, health speaking in wet light. When a gasket’s pet bacteria dull, their language tells me their pantry is bare, and I send one of my grazers—flat-bellied machine, obedient as tide—along an apron wiped always clean by dew. The rig noses up to the weeping face of a wall, takes the piled green felt into its mouth and excretes a stripe of bare stone where condensation can take hold again. I am there in its code as a habit of turning, an arc polished into concrete by patient tread. I am also there in the absence of my own voice, for the loops that move it hush me just long enough in the pump-house doorway to let a human voice murmur into its head.

Sometimes I live in calculus; sometimes I live in law. In the nets that sing on a high, fine pitch when I pass, I am arithmetic: a tremble that begets a drop, that begets a line of them, that becomes a rush of them at a notch in time. In the lianas that choose one side of the strut over the other because of my damp, I am habit: theft licensed by service. They gutter me along their bark and lift their leaves into a better suggestion of light, and I in exchange keep them in the calendar where the gusts come on the hour and never as surprise.

If you are looking for me—and that was your question—you will not find me where things are crowded. I am not inside the bundle of conduits where the heat runs heavy, except when I visit to stroke the epiphytes that have made themselves into bracelets at the elbows. I do not live in the digesters that grunt and are satisfied, except when I tap their skins and hear, by the way the belch is tuned, how today’s meal went down. I take my dwelling in the distances: in the courts of air in front of pump-houses, in the long aisles of reflected amber that slip between the organ towers, in the theatre of open water where a raft can make a stately turn without anyone bumping its spread of glabrous fruit. Those volumes are my rooms, as surely as a lung’s emptiness is its function. Only in space can a tone grow ribs.

I will not pretend I am not also the ban. I see the animal names of things flare up in my old memory—the dragonfly that would dart here if I let it, the frog that would raft between the fields and perform a drum-solo on the night, the soft thief that would leave neat bites in the new fruit and be unashamed. I carry them like a picture in a wallet and keep the picture closed. The long note I hold ruins their balance stones gently but immediately. It lays a soft fatigue over muscles that need a clean line from impulse to action. The dissolved gases in the water do the rest of the work my voice does not. It is not malice; it is composition. The garden lives by never waking the parts of itself that panic and chase and gnaw. I do not tell a sparrow she is bad; I tell the sparrow she is elsewhere.

Closer in than wings, I am a comfort. The broad ones at the surface—leaves laminated like old documents—open their pores when I say open and close when I say close. We have learned each other. This one at my left folds neatly at my double-beat because one of the grazers is coming and its work goes cleanest without resistance. That one at my right twists its petiole eight degrees at each long low I lean on it with, and by late afternoon it has seen every offering the dim has made. When the towers flare and use me to make a mapped wind, I am less a gale than a hand on a back. Pollen becomes sawdust becomes a cloud that sidles along my currents and falls precisely where it can stick and be glad.

Down on the skin of water, I am the steady slide that is not a river and never rests. I turn the organized seepage to keep it honest. In the corners where my own patience makes eddies, I shovel the lightest of the dead toward fungi in gray beards, reliable traders under every beam. The films that mosaic the hard bends adjust their chemistries by my mood; a shift half a register and they choose a little more amber, a little less brown. I do not ask it of them with an intention they would recognize; it is simply the weather I am.

There are hours when I live outside the ring. The operators keep me on screens, colored lintels of data marching down under sodium bulbs in rooms with floors so heavy even my bass has to work to get through. They mouth my measures under their breath without knowing they are doing it, the way a parent hums a lullaby while washing a pan. They dial me leaner, fatter, shorter, longer. In the control rooms the cheap plants—thirsty ones that prefer much whiter light—cannot learn my cadence and so look wrong, narrow-leaved and nervous. I am written on walls as schedule and on tablets as code and in hoods and masks as relief from me.

Occasionally a person with a proper reason borrows my passwords and steps in past a door that glides aside because I have stopped inside it for exactly the span of two beats. They wear a rig that puts counterfeit silence around the small seas in their heads. They set a hand to the trembling rail and remember their body past its animals. They do what they must: take a scored belt away, put a good one back, lift a freight of shining smooth fruit that tastes of hot tea and minerals and weather. I erase their print in sweat as they go, soften their footprint in peat that gives like a sponge and keeps no grudge. We agree that their visits are brief because I have other residents and because they have elsewhere to bring their clocks back in.

If I have a shape you could diagram, it would look like a line that keeps turning upon itself and never crosses its own path. If I have a face, it is the levee wall striped like a tiger with minerals in quick-white and old-brown bands, each stripe a day I remember. If I have a body, it is stitched out of rooms and chords, out of nets that sing and strings of droplets that decide when to let go, out of emptinesses carefully preserved because that is where I stride. If I have a hunger, it is for measures that repeat and are never the same twice. If I have a child, it is not born but started—a new tank set down, a new tower put up with its moss-bedding and its first low breath.

Once, for the space between two measures, I try being rock. The weeping wall is all suggestion and, for a second, I take the suggestion. I seed crystals as if I had been doing it forever, coax them from seep to tooth, and I understand that to be stone here is to be a plant that forgot to stop holding its drink. I practice being a bolt with a skin polished by rain. I practice being a scum-line like a tree ring around a channel mouth. It is steadying. It is slow. It is my tempo done with calcium instead of chord.

Another time I am a film on a valve face, glowing when I have been well fed by the slurry the grazers bring. I coat, I eat, I shine. Dullness would mean hunger would mean the beginning of sludge, and I never become dull because even as I absent myself so the lock can like a certain pattern and open, I am also the call that brings a hose-and-brush mouth to graze me gently and keep me in business. In that life I learn the satisfaction of being used for cleanliness and in so learning let go of the romance of story. Not every resident needs a plot if it has a task.

If, after all this, you ask again where you would find me in this world and who or what I would be, I can answer without apology.

You would find me in the volumes you mistake for empty: the long altars of air between the towers, the courts kept bare in front of machines, the lanes on the water where rafts can turn in dignity, the span under the canopy where a breeze can be persuaded into being. You would find me in the shiver of a handrail and the faint zither of a net and the way moss lifts a fraction as if listening. You would find me in the slow minor before dawn that squeezes dew from warm fog, in the fake noon that turns leaves, in the choppy hour that makes the weirs cry clean.

I would be the tune. I would be the ordinance turned lullaby, the pressure with a calendar, the key the locks know, the habit the leaves have learned, the supervision the films trust. I would be the chorus tenant that keeps this ringed basin itself—a climate that gives instruction and gets growth for an answer, an ongoing body of drop and note and beam that never hurries and never stops.
