The first time I go down is as a guest, or so I think. I handle the handrail like a living thing and it accepts my touch because a thousand hands have already argued it into velvet. The air changes around the last bend and my mouth fills with the taste of coins and mint and old iron. I stand still at the bottom because the hall asks me to in the only way it knows—by withholding spectacle and offering a listening instead. The lamps give out their weak-tea circles. The pipes exhale. Somewhere a drop keeps its fifty-seven-second appointment with a bell. I put my hand to my chest and the rhythm does not match my heart; it is older and safer than that.

Where I would find myself is never the center. The hall does not believe in centers. It has a sump that behaves like a morning and a ring of catwalks that behave like patience, and it offers you a place on the ringing rather than in the bowl. The rooms along the south wall feel like train cars stopped mid-journey; I know one of their beds will take me the way a seat on a long ride takes you. There is a raincoat among the many that will fit my shoulders, a stove that will agree to remember heat for me, a ledger waiting with a pencil small enough to insist on carefulness.

On my first days I am mostly ears. I am taught vowels. Someone older than my worry stands with me at the gauge-panel dais and says, Listen, hear how that E relaxes into an A when the impellers tip. We do not turn anything. We practice leaning. We learn the register of the ribbon at the north end fan. We count frogs and do not interrupt them with our counting. We decline to clap no matter how strong the anecdote. We keep to the stripes, keep to the slow.

Little jobs become a way to be known. I wipe a bolt before I replace it and pack the threads with grease, not because grease is magic but because it writes a detour in a language water respects. I rinse a jar whose sides are in braille and set it back under its wick-caught tear with a tenderness that is half belief and half engineering. I polish a crescent into a brick because a dozen hands already began that sentence; I am only the next clause. I learn to move the generator bicycle two notches away from earnestness so its chain sings consonants and not complaint. I write WATCH YOUR HEAD (LOW STEEL) at the edge of the enamel map with wax pencil and know it is not an order but a favor you offer to a stranger you haven’t met yet because someone once did that for you.

If you ask me what I am by then, I will say a listener. That sounds small until you’ve had to hear a loose sympathy in a bearing from across a room that hums its own weather. I am a finger on a casing that tells S at the pump's skin while the dial says something more cheerful; I slice my tea a little earlier because the hum is moving toward a vowel I don’t like. I am a hand at the stop valve that finds the one polished scallop among many because muscle memory is a kind of ancestor and it knows where to push. I am the low voice reminding my own mouth to stay under the frogs.

The hall overlaps me with other lives because that is its habit. I am for a few evenings a moth, laid flat against a hazard stripe, armored in drab and attention; what moves me is not fear but someone else's hand six meters away finding a wrench too quickly. I am for one long wet hour a filament of algae, pulled between a drip and a jar, singing an inaudible patience the way a held breath sometimes makes a person kinder. I am sometimes the wren above the red stop valve, unbothered by machines because I have learned the diode-bright intelligence of their schedules: they will not startle me if I do not startle them.

Sometimes I am the bell.

It is not a grand thing. It is a small copper kiss every fifty-seven seconds struck against a world that would be itself without it. But when your job is to be almost nothing, you are required to be very exact. You must keep a tempo the frogs can embroider and the sluicemen can rest their own hearts against. You must keep ringing whether the sump swells by a centimeter or edits a ripple into syntax about far rain. You must not grow proud of the way people glance at you to be located in time. Pride comes out in the ring and the ceiling does not bless it.

Once, in a season when the city above was busy at grief, I carry something down the wet stair that I haven’t named. This place does not ask; it offers its particular civic hospitality. I go to the railing and do not insist on reflection. The oval morning does what it always does, not for me but with me near. If you are wondering what I am in that moment, I am a vessel. I am also emptied, but only to the level the sump can tolerate without forgetting itself. When I climb back up, my bones have acquired the rule that water says: you can go on because we all go on very slowly, together.

There is a day when I catch myself talking to the chain and I realize I have stayed. The word sluiceman has been said in my direction enough times that the suffix has worn off my ear and all that’s left is the sluice—the part of me that attends to channels. My love is practical. It is choosing a paint for a patch that will be handsome at fifty. It is climbing a ladder that goes to the place an old rung used to be and stepping down again only a little ashamed because I forgot and because forgetting is part of being a creature. It is tracing the gloss-worn enamel with a fingertip not to learn but to remember.

If I am permitted to be more than one thing—and down here multiplicity is the only honesty—I am also the vowel-keeper. The hall is a choir of machines and drops and small, purposeful animals; its song stays itself through increments. My work is to nudge and to allow. I absorb the city in the way the sump does: textile-quarter mornings as a slack against the grating toes, grocery-hour warmth breathed through the fan lying along my forearm, far-train weather arriving as a thought that startles the algae into combing themselves. When I write in the ledger, the pressure of my hand betrays to anyone who can read it whether the day was careful or tired. That hand is, in time, a resident as steadied as the frogs.

Who else am I? On nights when I’m more mineral than animal, I am the nap raised on a rail by decades of grasping, a rind of touch the stone keeps as a census. On days when I need forgiveness, I am the tin collar slid under a drip that has been falling longer than I’ve been alive, practicing a courtesy the room will outlive me by. There are hours when I am a spider’s damp-tuned harp between two gauges and a traveling drop makes me ring, and I forgive the drop in the simple way strings forgive fingers. There is a morning when I am an eel in thought if not in body, body heavy with my own patience, stitched in place, comfortable in the dim.

And, being honest, some days I am just boots. Not even what fills them—just their fact, drying by the stove, the gentle collapse of their uppers remembering ankles that walked slow on purpose. That too belongs here. That too is a way of saying I stayed.

Where, finally, would you find me in this world? On the second level south wall, behind an oilcloth curtain, where the kettle does its constellation of sighing and a raincoat drips itself into a little personal meteorology on the floor. Along the catwalk above the oval morning, my hands set where other hands were, my mouth under the frogs. At the enamel map with a wax pencil tucked behind my ear. Kneeling by jars with braille'd sides, making sure their lips are clean for the tears they’ve agreed to catch. With my back against the green-painted pipe at the end of a shift, left ankle over right, watching the white fish collect around attention like filings around a magnet, letting the bell tell me that time still wants to be counted.

Who or what would I be? A sluicewoman by habit and by ear. The vowel between what hums and how. The small copper timekeeper that answers a drop without ever owning it. A stitch of patience run through iron and algae. A citizen of a neighborhood the city calls infrastructure. One of the hands that learned not to hurry and thereby became the place admitting me as part of its slow, abundant living.
