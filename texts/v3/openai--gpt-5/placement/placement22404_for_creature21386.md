At first I thought I would be a wanderer, always skimming the green streets between pillars, letting the hum set my stroke. But when I listened longer, the place itself chose me. I found myself where the sound thins enough to be made useful: alongside the banks of flutes that face the long, patient current off the west moorings, where the city takes the weather into its body and translates it into something we can live by.

Who am I? I am one of the Rain-Readers, a tuner of the city’s hearing. My personal name is Slack-Note, given in a year when the thunder walked higher and the surface went glass often enough that children learned the good hold without trying. I wear my algae in lines that echo the kelp spines—lattice on shoulder and back that drinks light and gives me a faint coolness when the sun leans hard. My hair is long and looped through shell spacers so it will sing against my neck when I nod, and everyone who knows me can count the clicks and know it is me, even in a crowded hall.

Each morning starts bone-first. I wake to the shell-chimes along the floor answering the day’s first shift in the shelf current, a slow tremble through hip and thigh that means the west tie’s loosened half a breath in the night. I press my palm to the timbers where I sleep—they answer with the same old hum as always, a comfort like an elder’s pulse. I put my garden-of-breath to my face and hum a little. It’s a veteran garden, balance sure; I keep two younger ones at hip and neck for work, bright velvet lining woven cages of reed that drink everything I don’t need and gift me back the clean taste. When a garden is happy it cools the cheekbones the way shade does. I listen for that.

The path to the flutes is a nave of water-fern we’ve convinced into a tall aisle. It took a decade to teach the trunks to hold that blue-green window, and they still insist on trying to fill it in. My auntie—she keeps the law-vines for our stretch—strokes them when they reach, patient, reminding them of the agreement. At the end of the aisle the long banks stand like an organ, lips open to the current. Some are a little hoarse from moss; some are quiet today because the eddy off the northern knuckle has lain down. I slide along, fingers and toes fanning, web easing the small work. My apprentice—Second-Bead, who wears light-catching beadwork instead of algae because their mother’s household likes shine—joins me with a sign for morning, the wrist held easy.

We work without knives first. You can do a lot with thumb and patience. The mouths want to fur; the sills want to catch small grazers who’ve come curious. We persuade without making bites the plant won’t forgive. Where sap sticks, we rub it back into seams. Where a lip is cracked from last storm’s hard cough, we wedge it shut with spun fiber that will root if kept broad and wet. Second-Bead drums a steady pattern on a bladder stretched in the arch—a tone the flutes know as “hold this; hold this”—and I can feel the resonance click into true. When a pitch shifts a fraction in a fraction of a day because the surface has thickened with rain, I log it not by writing but by wrapping a runner twice around a vine, then a half turn, then once—our code for shift higher by hair’s breadth. The law is full of plants like that, carrying our memory in their bodies. I am happiest when I can feel today show up as a new tension in what has already agreed to be.

Everyone says I read the sky well. It is not the sky; it is the underside of it, but we still call it that. The ridge makes small weathers. A freshening here means a slackening two halls east. The roar goes pale at the top when the rain above is mist more than hammer; you can feel the city lean into its elastic. I put my mouth to a beam and say wait into it in words, soft enough that only the timbers and the three households bound to this span will hear. It travels. The baffle-tenders farther down sign back up the corridor with shaved jawbone tools against trumpet-pores: they hear me, they will not tighten the moorings yet.

By midmorning the commons is jade-bright. The web of root that is our big table holds the set-out of the day: seedpods for oil, skins for satchels, polished stones with good holes, laughs about whose fans threw flamboyant leaves in the night and how they glowed like a parade. I go not to trade (we keep our oil under our own leaves and we are content) but to put my palm on the law-vines and mark what the flutes told us. Auntie has them singing already, thin and constant, a hush of sap beneath the great roar. I touch the notch that says the west tie comes loose in hard white days; I touch the fork that memorializes the time when the east neighbors wanted to take a runner we had taught to arch and we taught it back. You can feel old arguments in there, smoothed to silk. Sometimes you can feel a sliver of bone, pale and slick, where somebody you loved went into the anchor after they were done being a body and became part of the curve. I press brow to brow with Auntie. We vibrate jaw to jaw: we agree that when the thunder walks stiff tonight, we will open the south-facing baffles so the sound goes through us and does not stay.

People say our jokes are dry. We give each other rope pegs carved with half a smile. I have a superstition about tensioning: I do not tighten a line until it sings a note my grandmother liked. It is foolish work; it keeps me careful. Once I tried to break the habit on purpose. The rope warned me for a week, then failed in a day I would have called quiet. No one scolded me. I hung my cheeks to my beam and hummed into the wood a sorry. The law-vine kept that. There is a small hook of a curl on the Third Hall string that means Slack-Note tried to go faster than the water would tell her and has learned better.

By afternoon the grazers come, combs tidy as always, sauntering through the lanes of clear we keep for them. Children go with them, a froth of little hands in the stringy hair of the old fans where mites bloom like dust motes and burst when brushed: shrimps like confetti. My child wears a blue bangle from the south slope, the fashion of the season because the ridge keeps giving up those shells and you can’t ask it not to. He is practicing the drift-still. He is good at the slow games. He breathes from a young garden-of-breath that makes his exhale pearl like bracelets; he loves the look of his own breath rising. He and his cousin hunt a snail that grazes on northern shade. They will come home with beaks to make needles and a handful of wet jokes.

Some days there is council. Today the House that Holds the East Span and the Splicers of Third Hall and our Rain-Readers gather in a big bend where a fan-fern accepts being flat enough to be a bench, because a runner from the commons has entered the Splicers’ room and wants to core on our anchor. You cannot own a runner that will not root where you tell it; you cannot teach something to be what it cannot. That is in the law somewhere, but not as words. It is in the angle of a vine that once turned away and now turns back, trained by a dozen patient touches. We float in midwater, shoulders forward, hands gentle and serious. Someone will demonstrate with a real tendril when it is ready. For now, we practice on air. I sign this much for me, this much I can be persuaded to let go, this much belongs to the runner itself, and the auntie in charge of scars reaches and holds our wrists in turn, placing our fingers on an invisible line. In three weeks we will make a cut that leaves both cores intact. In three years we will stroke the place and call it good.

Heat is appetite, always. I stop at the fermenters’ hall on my way back to the flutes because the roar has gone thin and high—our bass can find room there—and it is pleasant to be warm from inside out. They feed me plant steamed under bladder, threads sweet as if someone sang to them (they did; you can tell when you eat it). They give me oil on leaf and remind me, laughing, that I owe them a song played on the big drums next thin day because I promised and never came. I promise again. They believe me. Pleasure here is slower than a flare. It is the way soybean in a skin becomes something spreadable on the fifth day; it is the way a long animal dies natural in the blue once a long while and we go with solemn light to take enough, and we eat it for a season and it warms us all the way to bone. That is a big day. Most days are a handful of simple warmths, repeated true as a note.

Sometimes a predator laces the edge of vision. Today a shove of space announces a fluke-broad elder, all deliberate, sliding a corridor we keep for just such bodies. I roll my shoulders open so the song can pass through me without slamming any joint, and I feel the hall lean in relief as the old one’s frequency moves the stone. Second-Bead reaches without looking and opens the chaff-screen across the fast passage so the small things don’t get blown down a stranger’s throat. The law says to do that; no one needs to say it. We learned it from the first time it went wrong.

Lightning comes rarely to our height in the water, but when it does, a sheet of light will happen, sudden as breath after a good hold. I have seen it, a white plane walking down the ridge, and the plant skins flicker blue-green where the charge hurries along sap. Everyone goes wholly still, in courtesy and delight, and then the flutes waver all at once and spend a minute finding each other again. Those are the days when you can feel in your ribs that there used to be fire that did not need rain to be born. My son cups my jaw in both hands after and hums the story of it into my bone the way my mother did when I was small. We keep the story not because we need the thing, but because the shape of it makes our halls feel bigger.

Do I go down the blue? A little. I have Sounder-cousins who shave their hair and polish their skins and learn the manners of distance, who carry messages stitched on satchels made from long weed that float just so, sent with fluked elders to other ridges we will only ever know by secondhand song. I have been to the falling edge and hung like an ornament a long time and learned when my hand should let go. But my desire is domestic. I like to come back to the banks where the flutes stand, and their mouths are never quite finished with me. I like to sit under the highest hall when thunder’s footfalls are near and feel the jump run through the grown buttresses like laughter. I like to know which rope will fail two weeks before it does and to teach the rope how not to.

There is a silence for us. Not empty. It is a sign. When a grown hall falls below its usual hum on a day when the rain is thin, when the shell-chimes on a floor do not set the shin-ache you expect, you go. You place your hand on the nearest skin and you listen. Once I did that at dusk when the plankton blooms sparkled along every edge and made it all look festive. The west mooring hall had gone quiet as an apology. Inside, a ferret of eel had coiled too deep into a buttress and trapped its own fin in a seam. The eel-keeper was there already, scar under wrist smiling faintly, speaking to it with touches. It took a quarter of an hour and a laugh and a bruise on a knee to convince the creature to unwrap. We signed to the hall thank you, sorry for the pinch, and it answered by warming a little against the palm. Of course it did. It is alive.

Work makes you thick in the middle, not as fat but as aware. I can tell, with my eyes closed and my hands not touching anything, when the surface goes from jade to glass. I do not have to think about where I put my feet in a space where there is no floor. I can ride a rope-lift on the buoyancy of its own bladder without touching more than three times with my toes. These are not accomplishments here. They’re how you have a body in this element. We praise other things. We praise patience. We praise a cut made where a plant will forgive you and then return stronger, because you asked it well and did not steal from its core. We praise a hall that lets thunder pass through and does not keep it. We praise a child who, hunting a snail for a needle, stops to watch a school of ribbon-ones knot itself into an impossible puzzle and then loosen like a dream.

At night the city goes to a different key. The roar persists, the law persists, but other notes get room. We take the big drums out when the overhead thins and set them under the vaulted spans we have spent lives teaching, and we put bass where the day left space for it. It is not performance. It is an answer. It says here, here, here we are. Lovers press mouth to mouth-guard and murmur into each other’s lungs, learning with their ribs. Old ones who are almost done being bodies let their gardens be young and bubbly one last time, just to feel the pearls slide from their lips. They laugh when their cheeks tickle.

One day I will go into the anchor and become part of the curve of a hall. People will stroke that curve the way I stroke my grandmother’s, not as mourning but as a rightness. Before that, I will plant more flutes than I tend now. I will leave a law-vine with three forks that answer a question we keep almost settling and then unsettle at the last minute. I will probably finally go play the big drums for the fermenters on a day I did not promise them, because promises and days don’t always meet exactly and kindness counts more.

If you are looking for me in this world, do not shout. Your voice will not go far enough. Put your hand on the beam that spans the west moorings and hold it like a friend’s wrist. Hum. Feel the answering hum. The pitch you feel, the one that says loosen, not yet, now, all right, listen—follow it along the banks of green pipes with their mouths to the current. There is a person with shell clicks in her hair and algae lace on her shoulder and a superstition about songs in ropes, hands gentle, belly oiled against cold, jaw pressed at intervals to the grown wood as if kissing a secret. That is me. That is what I am for: to hold things gently until they hold themselves, to hear what the rain has to tell and then tell it back into our bones.
