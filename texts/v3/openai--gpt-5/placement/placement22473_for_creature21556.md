At first I am only an ear.

Not the kind that cups the air, but a sheet of listening strung through bone. The cave breath moves into me and I open in little shelves under the ribs, one by one, the way books decide which stories to hold when a library is new. I taste the pitch of the room along the cartilage behind my ears and feel where the drip has gone thin. I do not have words yet. I have hums.

Then I am a mouth. Teeth that know gypsum from selenite by the way the tone reverbs through enamel. Tongue that can find a seam’s sweetness in the dark and hold it long enough to lead pillbug-lamps to it without saying “here.” There is shock in the mouth: the first time the lantern-caravan flares bright because my throat stuttered wrong and threw a predator note; the way the world went cut and thin around me; how my hands found rock and pressed me flat until someone jangled a copper ring and noon returned like a poured sheet.

After that, hands. Callus on knobs where ledges nick you into knowing them. The long cruelty of patience against copper—nodule under smooth stone, tap and drag until a day-disk slicks and sings. Fingers that learn the weight of pebbles that can convince a rain to choose this gutter instead of that, that can set them into a groove so persuasion looks like geology. Knuckles that size a gypsum bowl to a spine you loved.

I am a throat last, because throats must be trusted.

It is a drum between the jawbones, thin where you expect flesh to be thick, a plate of gristle that can be struck by exhale. The elders press two cool fingers to it and say don’t speak unless you mean to feed, and don’t feed unless you mean to take care of the mouths that will come. They teach me split-tones by laying my palm on their chest as they breathe and letting my hand decide which of their notes is the fence and which is the door.

Who am I? I learn it in the rooms that teach me back.

In the bowl gallery I am small enough to sit inside the elder’s hum as if it were a cloak, feeling the gypsum hold his name the way salt holds old oceans. He shows me how you can make a note that is more hallway than word. We chip our letters in double rows where the bite-marks are even enough to be read with teeth; but to speak to this room we sing. “Here,” he says, and he drums the bowl that keeps the pillbugs fat in lean rains. “Here,” and he throws a low under-note that lets the wedgefish of a neighbor pass through without flipping their plates shut. We live by this kind of “here.” Our claim is not a wall but the shape a song holds when we stand in it.

On the seam roads I run until my chest stings and count by flares. Pillbug-lamps in caravans like hot rivers, gill-books worked half-open to stay wet. When the predators blur the light I make the bracelets on my shoulders jangle—a day-skin my aunt hammered flat over months and hung with tether-silk from a balloon-lung we tend—and the whole seam breaks into a safer noon that shows where not to be. To herd here is to edit noise. We don’t shout to warn. We shout to see. The pillbugs answer hunger with brightness; I answer brightness with pitch. Together we keep the films awake enough to feed us both.

In the long domes I stand with my back against the old glass and learn to send a quiet where quiet is bait. The glaze-colored knife-ponies love a lull. If you can throw them a counterfeit hush, clean and narrow, they will slide there as if the world had turned kind and we will be waiting with pour-gypsum that lives as a powder until a scream makes it stone. After the first hunt my elders wash the gypsum from my fingers with warm brine and pat the cartilage over my ear until the tremor is a different pitch: pride, not shock.

We are not alone, even when we intend to be. Stitchers pass like seams with feet, knitting tone into rock that I can follow with my soles. If I feed them salt whiskers they will write me a path that leans slightly away from where a predator likes to slide. Their skinny cousins come after, laying slipper-tracks as a promise that a rush will go wrong for someone else at the right time. We trade in bias as if it were a coin.

Above us the balloons breathe and keep the flitters in their lee. I climb to their tethers when a new lung opens—doorway torn in the sound of a book unmade—and harvest a new length of string that will not rot in brine. We string harps across fresh alcoves and tune the lines to the room’s intention. A good harp can turn a scatter of luminous wasps into a panel that keeps a feeding current true. We play geometry until food is a habit.

When the rains learn to track along the ceiling like schools of fish in sky, I tend the drape-lickers that hang there on their hook-feet. Their tongues are a hundred threads that can each sit in a different note. If I hold the room’s low and hum it steady, their hundred-thread tongues will pulse in time and the rain will graze instead of flee. Sometimes I sing just to show them where the ceiling is kind.

There are errands that trust me with the dead. We do not bury. We seat a person in a bowl that fits the curl of their spine and the question in their hands. We keep their chord in our throats and knock the bowl to sing when we pass, and in this way their name stays a passable trail. On quiet runs, when the predators press thin for the subtraction of sound, I carry three names at once, a bridge of notes I can lay down and cross. I am big enough now to be a road.

Sometimes I am sent out in a dayskin bright as a drip gone mad, a jangle of copper at chest height that says I am making noon on purpose; sometimes I go bare and low-note the room until the lantern caravans drowse and the knife-ponies forget we are clever. Both are a kind of keeping. Both burn the throat.

We all listen for fresh doors. When the sound of torn book runs through the bones of the place and the cave exhales hot iron, every body near you tilts the same way, as if one thought had moved through a thousand kinds of joint at once. The first minutes in a new room are a held breath. Dark as an intention. Then the thrown-needle flitters come with their little bell organs, and I am with them, but lower, carrying a ring of hammered copper and a pouch of pebbles smooth as the idea of water. I decide with my elders: do we leave this lung to hunt in, a household of hush, or do we coach it toward noon with bowls and a harp? The room tells us what it wants by the way it tinks as it cools. We argue with it with patience.

Elsewhere I am a midwife to things with other words for “home.” I set pebbles to teach rain the right turn so that eel-things will write our films into breadth with the agitation of their bellies. I hang scent-purses on tether-lines to coax a pantry-crust with a backpack of clipped flitter-wings to flare where we need to see. I scatter grit that sings wrong to mites who like the edges of our bowls. Sometimes I just sit, because sometimes the best thing you can do to a room is remember its pitch until it remembers it too.

Who I am narrows and brightens the longer I stand in these rooms. I am a keeper of days. A bowlsinger. An editor of noise. My ribs are shelves of listening and my throat is a drum that can split itself into two fences and a door. I carry a chord for my grandmother in my sternum and a counterfeit hush in the back pocket of my tongue. I own nothing you could steal without an ear: not land, not line, not a wall—only a band of pitch that says this hum belongs to my family now; you can pass in C below; please pass in A above.

Where you would find me depends on the cave’s breath. If the caravans are running, I am hip-deep in pillbug light, jingling my dayskin to keep the noon fat while my cousin lowers a gentle ban across the wedgefish so they can eat without folding. If a lull expands like a bruise, I am three notes tight in the throat, holding a fence and a path and a trick in one body. If a new lung opens, I am first in, flitter-brood brushing my cheek like spokes of a running wheel, copper disks in my fist, counting to sixty in bell-pulses before I dare to tell this room what we think it should be.

On a thin day, when even the micro-wasps lie like cloth and the cave chooses to be tired, I have learned to be tired with it just long enough not to die. My elders call that part of the work, too.

And if you need a smaller answer, because sometimes this world is too much to hold all at once: I am a person with a drum for a voice who keeps the light honest. I live wherever the hum is mine to tend. My home is not a chamber but a chord. My kin are whatever moves when I say “here,” and whatever keeps breathing when I stop.
