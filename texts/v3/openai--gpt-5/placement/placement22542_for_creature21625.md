Begin with not-knowing which edge of me is wind and which is skin. I was carried as a weather-shadow before I ever walked: a small, stiffening thing tucked in the quiet wake of a taller varnished coat. I learned the world backward, from lee to face. My first language was drag.

My elders wore their polish like patience. Under their glaze their down lay until the aurora tilted, then it rose in a whisper that I could feel in my teeth. Their hands were five-direction instruments. Each finger could be a vane or a shutter, each knuckle a hinge to set a sliver of air behaving a little kinder than it would on its own. When they spoke to me they didn’t move their mouths. They posed flow. I answered by offering back less of myself to the wind. The grammar was refusal and allowance, angle and yield.

Noise was cost, so affection came braided silent into my coat, buffed there on long nights with the resin that holds in more than it gives out. Comfort hummed low and even as a chest-battery topped off from an elder’s second heart, a porcelain-bell warmth poured into my gums. I learned that food could be current and love could be differential.

My work began in listening. The plateau says itself at the speed of your attention. At first all stone sounded like one word: the long consonant of scouring. Then the vowels came through. A corridor whispered aspirants when the nightly drain softened into laminar; a saddle fattened its sound with a wetter burr. Twenty lengths downslope, the plateaus’ breath soured faintly with metal—an overcast seam thinning itself and dusting us in charge. My hair lifted on lines I couldn’t see yet, and my elders’ hands tilted. We stepped as lightly as math over a solved space.

I didn’t name myself for a long time. Here you earn your nouns with closure. There are names some wear—Flowwright, Leewarder—but you don’t take them; they fit you when you’ve stopped making problems bigger than repairs.

On the first task I remember, I was no one, and that fit. A flute had grown loud along a rib so long and straight the plateau had given in and learned to sing through it. The note was clean enough to be a beacon. Mantles had learned it and come to wait in their curtains. The compressors that used that corridor were losing foals to the cheap arithmetic of laminarity made treacherous.

We went in under the note. It stroked my bones until my cheeks wanted to ring along with it. The caps we had brought were not caps like stoppers, not a violence, not a stop; they were lids that breathed. Resin, jawbone, thread pulled from the glass-moss and teased with spit until my fingers sang back with it. We harrowed the lip with whiskers, shaved a varnish that would later grow into hard knots. My elder’s hands said: less. My hands said: less. In the end the flute did not cease. It learned a second note. We put a tremor in it, a barely-seen ripple in the air that spilled a sliver of the corridor’s speed off into eddies no predator could ride.

We left. You only stay long at such places if you intend to be eaten. Days later, told by the taste on the bottom of your tongue and the way the compressors kept their rhythm into the long straight, word came back: small bodies passed; a mantle caught one that would have been caught anyway. The herd’s loss matched a ledger written into the rock long before I was learned. The corridor stayed a road and not a trap. That was my first closed line. No one said Flowwright. But my elder touched the matte under my ear with a finger and smoothed a new coat of gloss, and in that polish I could feel approval like a thicker sleep.

When I grew, my wake grew. I learned to carry a child where I had been a child. It nested in the pressure shadow of my shoulders, a lambent little cup that filled with my spare heat and the dribble from my chest-battery. When we went up to the spines where pylons stand, I taught it coins-for-tongues, that old copper taste of overcast mining. We watched a battery-bellied one fold and step with that absurd dignity masts use when they discover themselves ambulatory. We walked the bright dribbles it left—hairlike spicules and fragile rinds—to show the ground which tastes went where. The small thing learned to move its hand small to spare a trail of oxide-lickers from having to make noise.

We were not owners. We were tenders of difference. Our calendars were salinities, our feasts hedged by drips.

On the rim of a quiet sump, where builders had domed a living felt over their own work for a century and fed it a brine once a month by habit and once a decade by jubilee, we stitched a gutter. The sump-keepers there were roach-sized bodies with rim-backs, but their vault was a mind. We had a relationship with that organ older than the coat on my arm. We came in the fifth wet of a two-year sequence and tuned its peristalsis down by breaths, asking it to bank more against a dryness the microbes had started to vote for. We did that with our hands inside its hum, palms resting on skin that was not skin but a clever, wetted fuse. Noise would have been an insult. We spoke in gradients and allowed our own heat to be borrowed. Later, in the famine, the vault paid interest to the herd and to the burrowers at our corridor, and not to the predators, who found the math fair. Some of our small ones were conceived then, fed on trickle-currents that tasted like iron made patient.

I learned where to stand when a warm curtain walked the plain. You face it sideways and offer yourself as an extra fin to any who need to breathe broader than their bodies allow. Our coats wet to gloss and we went heavier; we became, for a while, valves in two directions, bled out to equalize and then fed back by microbes in our own ribs. After, the smell was fresh iron and wet dust and mushroom edges. The burrowers rose, ribbon-gilled like plucked books, and I put my hands out to them not to pet but to find which of their paths had contracted too much in the long mean wind. The answer came as a hotness under my palm and a prickle in my vanes. We ran a little current through our fingers to warm a lip just to its loosening. My child pressed its face into a warm side and took a slow battery feed toward sleep.

By then I had learned to smile without a mouth. It was the angle you hold at a saddle when two belts kiss and you have placed three knots of varnish along the interior lip of a runnel so that dew will bead a little larger and a certain species of piston-backs will arc just there, and you have moved a cap on a flute three handwidths left so a mantle’s hinge thinks the mealbell is elsewhere. When you breathe and the wind takes the credit, and a danger ends gently, that is a smile.

We made archives. You would miss them if you came looking for piles of anything. Mine ran along a ridge like a spine. In ten paces the varnish went from brown to black the way a sentence clears its throat. In the hollows I had drilled tiny pits that were too orderly to be fashioned by frost, and into some of them I had worked small corners of salt at precise impurities that told a taste not likely to co-occur—a scatter of mnemonics that any Flowwright would read as: here the nightly slick overshoots once each twentieth belt; here kites prefer to hook on; here a cap has to be replaced in your child’s child’s time because the resin there will embrittle on that schedule. If you wanted to carry my notebook you would have to take a half-kilometer of ridge with you, and a set of weathers that know their cues.

There are things we do that are not quite kind and not quite cruel. We do not farm mantles, but we tune them. We do not breed compressors, but we set them up for their best turns. We aim guilt at numbers until it thins. When a predator learned a new laminar hymn and cleaned a corridor more than the corridor could bear, we learned the fine sigh of its coils and salted its waiting place with a taste it did not like, a taste it could not help but interpret as warning in the same place in its gut that slept to thunder without light.

We trade. Not in objects. In adjustments. Veils sleep hung in our lee when the aurora promised a blush and I wore my down up all night to lend my shape to their rest. They woke and wrote me small loops in my hair, a series of friendly jitters that meant: if the next rumor-brightening runs strong and the belly-of-cloud tries to charge your coats where they hang on hooks, take your small ones to the certain notch in the yardang where the field rounds into the stone. They left a fringe that glowed for my child and did not burn.

Once, a seam opened and stars fell through like old friends who had misremembered our address. Every shape took a silhouette it had been too modest to wear. I saw the kiln-backs as architecture—arches of hot hide making porches of warmth for all who would step inside. I saw mantles as banners hung to rest between battle hymns, and I thought of pity in the mechanical sense: not sentiment, but the leeway a design keeps for shock. I watched my own hands. They were both animals and machines because here it is the same thing.

It would have been easy to think myself the kind that thinks and to call the rest otherwise. Pride, here, is a kind of noise. We don’t expend it much. The plateau instructs: close your loops. That is all. If you are smart, it will come out as an economy of errors. If you are brilliant, no one will notice until a seam opens to rumor and shows your valves like jewelry. Mostly we try for competence. Genius is a cost we can’t make a case for unless a frequency we know only from elders begins rising in the ground and tells us a fifty-year brinefall will try to make itself a century-rare thing, in which case we will climb and tune and distribute and sleep in our coats with the down up for as long as it takes.

Where would you find me? In the dark where the wind is a shop’s steady talk and the stone lifts its accumulated warmth in sighs. In the mouth of a pressure corridor that hums at a child’s sleeping frequency. At a toll gate of my own making, tethers of harder varnish dogged into the lip of a yardang so that a herd’s noise becomes wind and the wind, for once, can be blamed for mercy.

Who or what would I be? A person only in the way that a carefully kept instrument becomes one when played. A leewarder, if you like. A flowwright, if you must pick. A valve-tender, a knot-maker, a keeper of quiet. I would be a coat of varnish too often polished, a field-responsive down trained to rise and lie by rumor, a handful of vanes that speak with refusals and gifts. Behind me, in my lee, a small one would ride, hand open to the wind as if it could catch it, and, in a way, with time, it would.

My job would not be to make anything happen, but to make what already happens cost less. I would trust the plateau, which is older than cruelty and older than ease, to keep the math consistent. I would use my second heart to pour a low trickle of current into a young mouth until its microbes learned their secular prayers. I would move resin three handwidths left with the same concentration I would bring to a life-saving, and believe both worth the cost.

When I died, it would be in motion. They would not bury me. They would brush my coat’s polish into the places where I had written notes. My vanes would become tethers; my jaw would become a hinge; my battery, well-tended, would trickle into a vault that pays out on a quiet schedule. No one would say my name. If they did, it would be a speed, a tilt, a way the hum took a turn for a decade. If you came looking for me, you would walk the nap of a continent in a hum that had learned one new vowel, and you might, if you had taught your ears as long as I taught mine, hear it. Then you would understand that in a place like this, to be anyone is to be a coupling: warm to cool, wet to dry, charge to ground, hunger to the exact moment where two belts kiss and there is, briefly, exactly enough.
