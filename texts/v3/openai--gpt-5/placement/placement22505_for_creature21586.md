I try on a few bodies before one keeps me.

At first I am a child at the cloud-edge because the drop has a clean logic. You would find me there at dawn with the Edgers, heels hung over the lip, spine bent into the pull, tasting the vertical breath like cold fruit. The undercloud is a pale gold soup and I lower a trichome line hand over hand until the rope burns polish my palm calluses and make a sort of memory in the skin. Something knocks the basket from below—a hollow, impossible wood-sound—and we grin sideways because that is what our faces do with a wind always tugging. We raise a length of drift like a story: salt-sanded, scarred not by tooth but by patience. It will become ribs for a silence fan, maybe, or a hoop for a wind-break that will rattle properly in the afternoons. The line coils in a good snake at our feet. The Mantle’s lip lifts once, only a finger-width, to weigh the air; the sigh that follows makes our tongues taste resin.

On the second morning I am not a person; I am a resonance corridor. Don’t laugh. I wake to it: my length a shade cooler than the plain, my sides smoothed by thousands of passes of percussion-tortoise shell and skate-lizard belly. Everything that moves along me must declare itself. The tortoises batter my ribs and I sing back in a tone that says no prey here, try the basin, old friend. Pacers lay their sticks lightly and the code runs so clean through my walls that even those asleep in camps three gullies away roll to the side they prefer when travelers pass. I feel the Mantle’s sugars creep along under me like the draw of the tide, and a child somewhere stamps a wrong foot and I, who am only a shape in a plant, decide to hold.

Then I am a minder of rafts for half a day because the choir front arrives and even the Mantle leans into it. We latch ourselves to dew-stones with little hooks and drink the breath right out of the pores until the bladders on our frames hum like bees of glass. We do not fly exactly; we refuse to fall. Thin struts of rib-bone we didn't grow but earned, membraned in a lattice the old color of bottles, glint in the plain light. Parasites sing through us, riding the harmonics, and when the wind finds its chord the whole organism of us answers in a single long vowel that arches the back like joy. Someone below—human by the shadow—presses fingers under their jaw and laughs into their own skull, and I feel the answer on my tensioned lines: that crazy intimacy of bone-conducted mirth that is almost speech.

By the third afternoon I have sweat in my eyes and resin crusting my knuckles and realize I am happiest under the gloss, in the sliver of shade you learn to make even on a place that will not keep shade. There is work there that feels like ethics: stitching quiet into a world that is busy with noise. So if you are looking for me when the plant softens and blisters and the law is get below the gloss or blister, follow the line of little flags hung along a stoma’s fall of steam and listen not for the loud but for the part where the loud is made gentle.

I am a quiet-maker.

We are a small trade with large patience and the bone of us is simple: in a place where sound is weather, someone must make rooms of less weather. We carry bundles of bundled things—fans grown from green-dry sticks bent slowly to remember the curve, panes of leaf-slate sanded to fingers’ thickness and beaded with resin at the edge, coils of hair and trichome filament combed from the Mantle’s underlayers and knitted into nets so fine a falling tone tangles in them. We set them upright in small precise companies that cast not shade but hush. Under their arcs, jaw to jaw, people can say the soft words that make a community: sorry; stay; the story about the first time the lung looked back at us; the map of dew this month; the vow you make when you leave a camp and return as someone who has learned a hardness without becoming it.

A quiet-maker does not own much. My tools are slow apprenticeships. The knife I use to shave a gloss-burr down so that the mat will lie flush is a split of node-tissue taught to cleave like thought thirty years ago in someone else’s nursery bowl. The teeth-press I wear under my jaw is wool and trichome combed and combed and stiffened with a seal that doesn’t poison the Mantle but will beat back mold if mold comes sniffing. Some nights I bite lightly against the palate-bridge—yes, we make a second comb that lives there—and send a word straight into the bones of the person leaning against me, and we both breathe differently for a while afterwards, as if we had been underwater and come up.

There is a neatness to our laws. I say ours and mean the people’s, but also the Mantle’s, but also the rules of anyone who tries to cross a living instrument without making a mess of its song. Don’t be Loud. Don’t be Still in the way that invites a fungus. Move at a beat the world can ignore. Wait for the reply after you make a drum. Don’t stop where the plant breathes because even a friendly inhalation can teach you more patience than you meant to learn. They are rules of travel, of survival, of neighborliness, and a quiet-maker takes them and makes them into tools.

Find me in a listening hollow when the air is knives. I go there as a person goes to a shrine. I take the mats we’ve woven from hair and firmer hair and resin beads that are cool to the fingerpads and I make a floor of them on the plant’s skin so that my breath will not telegraph too much while I lie down. The hollow is an ear in the Mantle, but it is nowhere in particular: a place the plant has fattened under its skin with that slow thinking it does in gradients of sugar and the little taxes it puts on the passage of wind. I put my hand on my own ribs and feel the pulse come back in a slower meter from below. It hears the big weather I can only think of as weight. It hears the far percussion-tortoise parties. It hears the choir migrating two basins over. On a good day I can taste an ironish hum that says the undercloud will be heavy in three days and the east will drone. Weather-listeners say it plain. Quiet-makers learn not to say what we cannot build a pause for.

I travel, because quiet is portable until it isn’t. Between camps the world is long and not bored by us. The grazers pass and we become the kind of signal they ignore: not prey, not threat, just a fold in the skin. I sleep in their lee sometimes when the wind presses and I can smell the hair-dust haloing off their plate-joinings. Children wake because the plateau has reversed its breath and the air in the shelter is suddenly wrong-wet and I knock a code on the support: two long, three short, like a reassurance, and everyone turns in place and we go on sleeping.

Find me at the leaf-slate roof on the curled spine when it is time for people to be together because grief makes us a shape that wants other shapes. I will be up in the ribs, pinning silence fans where the wind will not snatch them and rattle their spokes wrong. I will have traded a good shy piece of wood from the edge for a length of dew-stone polished by Edgers until it glows like mouse fur; it will condense the right way for the ceremony when the pollen glitter falls and tunes the air to that cold metallic chord. I will be careful where I set my feet because old spore pits are still fuming sweetness you cannot see and it is arrogant to think you can shout down the appetite of mold with resin and craft alone.

I have married once, to a choir-catcher whose shoulders remember the shape of lift. He smells a little like silk even on still days, and when the fronts pass he cannot quite keep his head from tilting into their pull the way the Mantle does. We talk in bone, mostly. Of course we speak like anyone—silent syllables made with jaw and breath under the hush of a set fan, the pulse code in the soles and with a stick so that even the blind turtles know we are polite—but there is a joy in the secret way words go straight into the skull. When he is away I tune our silence nets by the pitch his laughter left behind the last morning. Some evenings we sleep in the warm wash of a valve with our backs to the lip and the valve-crabs all around like a patient audience, and we wake flecked with resin and little leaf-gnaws and none the worse.

I have a puckered ring on my forearm where I let an air-eel love me too long, because yes, we fish them for their shimmer and the way their patience can be taught to make music with a line. Bonded, the eels will ride a humming string like a river you cannot see. You can turn them into instruments if you are not sentimental and I am not sentimental about anything that will not stay. My scar is less a mistake than a reminder that patience is a muscle, not a right.

There is mold like gossip and mold like war. When it comes in blue velvets you can sweeten the wood and the hair and send everyone to sleep in a warm breath. When it comes in a white starburst you must cut and burn and then do the thing the Mantle does when the grazers scour a place too hard: withhold. I carry a flask of bitter seal against it that smells like old fires and sticky tea. A fungus-ward showed me the recipe once and pinched my cheek for being a quiet-maker who does not trust silence alone. We are friends. She carries too many knives. I carry too many combs. We pretend we are not the same.

You will not find me easily in the noon. That is because I obey the plant. I get below the gloss when it gleams and the terpenes spray invisible on thin lines that can float straight into your pores and say blister in a hundred tongues at once. I sit where a stoma will vent steam over me in the chill hours and leave me dry in the dawn licking, which is luck and also knowing the lip and counting with the plant. My cartilage is thick and tuned; I did not grow it but was grown with it: my mother’s felt-and-resin U pressed at the soft of my ear while I slept and now the curls inside slow the world down into shape. I wear stones there for weight and because some of the old-leaf beads sound a note in certain fronts and tell me if we must lift the last silence fan and run.

When I am old I will go down into a lung for a day or three and see if what is said there is worth saying. I have promised myself that I will not come back with a voice that has learned to talk only about the shape of patience. We are suspicious of that, and we should be. The Mantle hears everything and has long ago decided what it will be; I do not need to be its bad echo. But I would like to lie on the floor of a warm air-lobe and feel, for once, the counting without my bones in the way.

On certain nights, when the pollen-glitter falls and each coin hums as it descends and the children hold their breath to hear the day tune itself metallic, I take the youngest to the listening hollow and put a rope in one of their hands and their sternum in the other and we lie and there is a moment—sometimes it is the sound a long way off of a percussion-tortoise being happy, sometimes it is the sticky hush of the Mantle testing the cloud-edge, sometimes it is just the breath reversing—that makes them look older than an hour can make anyone, and after that they are careful in the right way.

Sometimes, on the old acoustical paths, when I am walking with my stick and laying out the courteous knock for no harm, heavy load, passing through, I fall into step with strangers. A herder with raw hair on her forearms from sleeping between legs. A gloss-watcher who can name all the shine constellations and tell you which ones will crawl toward you in August when the plant has had enough sun. An Edger with a sideways laugh and a piece of oddwood under his arm. We tap, jaw to jaw, drift apart. Between us the earth answers: not ours, not yours, only here.

If you’re looking for me at evening, look for the way heat slides off and the pores drink themselves shut with little wet pops you can feel through the soles. Look for a pleat encouraged into shelter with a lattice of green sticks whose grain remembers wanting to grow. Ask the valve-reader, who will have her palms on the lip and her eyes closed; she will lift her hand and tilt her head the exact degree that means safe to stay or move before the inhale. Ask the resiner by scent: she will be able to tell you if the gloss became gentle today or angry. Or listen for the simplest signal we have: the one a quiet-maker sends when a field of hush is open to any body that needs to lay down its noise. It is too slight to be anything but an invitation. You will know it by how your shoulders remember to fall.

And if you do not find me at all, do not worry. Lie down anyway. Put your hands on your ribs. Press the comb to your jaw. The blanket of noise here is honest; you can almost forget to listen to it. Almost. But not quite. Beneath it there is the counting. Feel how the Big That Breathes counts itself closed, pore by pore. Feel how it hums risk into advice. Feel how, in its large appetite and larger etiquette, it is making a space for you.

That is what I am trying to be in this world: a small correct emptiness you can inhabit for a while without making a mess of the song. A quiet. A pause that knows the beat. A fold that holds and then gives back. I am not much. But if you come to the hush and press your jaw to mine and let your laughter turn to bone, I will give you my name the way we give anything that matters: hand to hand, so close the air doesn’t have to try.
