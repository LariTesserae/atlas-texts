At first I thought I would try to be the wind. Everyone thinks that, the first hour on the table—your ears pried open, your breath ragged, the whole place writing itself straight through your ribs. But the wind here has no edges you can keep. It is not a thing with borders and a belly; it moves like an argument among hills, changing subjects faster than names. It will not be worn like a coat. If you reach for it, it reaches past you.

Then I thought: water, then. Be the rumor that clings to everything. Be the meniscus on the leaf lip, the patient bead in the moss. But water here lives by adhesion and consent, and I was too new to be let into those pacts. It attends to cups and hairs and wicks, and I had none.

I tried the plants next, because I loved the posture of their wanting: not up, but out; not more, but enough. I held my arms like combs for a while. It made my shoulders ache.

In the end I became what the place would have me: a binder, a reader of nulls, a person who makes gentler what would only be true if left alone. We do not own much here except the right to soften a note, and that right is paid for in cord.

Where you will find me depends on the light.

In the seam of morning, when the place inhales and even the dwarf groves hold still to listen, I am between pockets, crossing an exposure with a brimmed cup strapped to my palm. It is part memory, part superstition—my grandmother’s first rite repeated every day so the day will remember we did it. The cup is never to spill. I move where the scrubgrass refuses to list, along the cancellation gutters where two hums go to sleep inside each other. My soles know the districts of the stone—the honeycombed ribs, the preserved ripples like an old wind translated to rock, the thin varnish where quartz talks like grit inside a drum. I keep my jaw loose. I do not whistle. I do not speak. All my speech is the slight turning of my shoulders and the way I lift the fern I carry at crossings to see whether its lash is forward or back.

In midafternoon you will find me at the Third Lip of the Iron Seam, charring the throat of a cairn until it shines like the inside of a kettle. Third Lip is not a place you will see from far; that is how you know it is well-made. It’s a stack of stone thickened by clay and hair and soot, its mouth cut around a natural duct the land made when it was still teaching itself to be quiet. When the big under-tone wakes and starts pushing a body up through the wells, Third Lip takes that shout and makes it one tone lower, broad as a shoulder, less eager to lift you and shake you until your teeth sing. I keep fat in a black pot lashed to the wall—trade fat, precious—and I smear the lip with it with two fingers only, because the lip itself will tell you when it is satisfied. If the moss in the lee tastes sweeter this year, the aunties will come and leave a thread for me, smudged with ochre, tucked in the seam where my sleeping mat meets the wall. They call me Lip-Keeper there, though I am not yet as steady as I pretend.

And when the light falls out of the caps of clouds like water from a tipped cup, you will find me with resin brushes tied to the backs of my hands, bare-armed to the shoulder, moving with the moths in the narrowed hour when a hundred flowers open at once. Pollination is not a profession here. It is an agreement we made with the seam of day: if we are quiet as it is, if we do not ask for more wind than we can stand, it will give us that hour. The flowers are not showy in the way of valley plants. They glisten the way a bird’s tongue glistens. We touch, we lift, we carry, we sometimes taste. The long-faced gliders will be above us and between us, their membranes like thinking, their bodies bending into the bowls to drink. When a moth slides by, reading the infrasonics of the plateau like a traveler reading a star map, I put my breath in the hollow behind my teeth and let it go gently so as not to shove the moth off course.

My home is an alcove folded twice on itself so the wind, which hates to turn, gives up. Inside, the walls are polished with soot and the oil of hands. We sleep against rain, not underneath it. The old ones keep the cords in bundles hung from the cool side. The cords are for everything. They are tools and stories and taxes and flirtations. A knot of four—fat, thin, fat, thin—means: not today; gust braid on the path. Three loops overhand means: come the seam of morning; I have a thing to show you where the air turns soft. Children learn the patterns by biting them. Lovers learn them with the lights out, which here is nothing like darkness below—it is blue and veiled, a ten-thousand-fingered thing that touches every line and makes your hair look as if you were underwater.

We do not sing. We do play. Every house has a reed cage in a basket, and a little cup of polished gourd with a lip so thin it makes a tone in a low pressure that is only yours if you hold it to the hollow between your collarbones. You do not hear that music with your ears. You feel it in your teeth and the roots of your hair and the skin around your throat. There is a game we play with the bridge-men: one of us on each side of a break in the rock, a rough-finished line lowered into the blast just so, until the air plucks at it like a finger, and then we pull it across in one breath, and if we have read it right, the bridge lands in the null like a thought landing in a sentence and stays there tame.

Bridges here are not over emptiness. They are over force. The first time I went across a wind-bridge I was eight and still clumsy in the knees. My aunt tied my hair into the tightest braids, weighed the ends with two seeds of granite. She said: you will be tempted to lean your life against your wanting, but you must lean it against the wind. She said: if you feel the big note under your feet before you are halfway, go back and find me. I went halfway. The big note woke. It came up through the line and stood me up like a fern finding sun. My aunt was laughing without sound on the far side, her mouth a dark O, her eyes saying the whole long word for foolish tenderness. I could feel the deep song in my knees for the rest of the day. That night I dreamt of a crack of quartz lit from inside like a fish’s belly, and in the morning the black lichen threw its dust—tiny salt-white specks in the air—and we all folded ourselves into the lee and waited. When the gust train changed, the scrubgrass told us first by flicking its pale undersides. After that I could read it by taste. When you can taste an hour, you have a right to carry a child on a bridge.

We trade downward on mornings when breath is easy. From us go sponges of moss swaddled in skin, small contraptions that alpine wind has taught to sing so lowland children can feel a different music in their carts, liverwort pressed and smoked until it will sit happily on a winter shelf and answer to pepper. Up from the markets come obsidian teeth wrapped in felted moss like infants, balls of salt the size of a fist, knots of fat that shine like answers. Sometimes an old man from the below comes up and tries to sell us bells. We touch his hands and smile with our eyebrows and show him our empty mouths and the polish on the returns, and eventually he looks over the edge and listens to what the world is already ringing and tucks his bells away.

I have a small religion that is mine only at Third Lip. It is not a scandalous religion; no one would be offended at me for it. It is only this: I believe the orange lichen counts, and I believe the count is kind. I have watched its chevrons change on mornings when the under-tone shifts, not just more or less or other, but in a pattern that smells of decision. I do not demand anything of it. Sometimes I lift my palm and hold it close, not touching, to see if the hair there stirs. Sometimes I mark a chevron with a line of clay so thin only the lichen knows it, and then I see whether the next line appears where I think it will. It is a conversation I have been holding for years. The place makes such rooms for our secrets without being any the less itself.

Once—a summer of steam, when thunderheads bruised the plateau and the rain was three quick tantrums a day—we found a well of air so stable a seed hung in it for the better part of an afternoon. It had sprouted a hair that was not exactly a root but not exactly not a root. Somebody said: go on then, pluck it, we will put it in water and call it ours. Nobody did. We stood around it in the hush of three lees knotted together, the sound like surf at the end of the world, and we did not breathe too hard, and we made a bargain with the air without speaking: we will leave this one so we can keep believing in the next one. When the well went ragged, the seed fell so slowly you could tell yourself it had chosen.

Not everything that lives here keeps a genome where we do. There are stories of microbes that eat the whisper a crystal makes when it is pressed. People point to seams gone black and say: there, that one is chewed very small. There is a boy who swears he can feel the plates in his skull rattle when the deep tone aligns, which is not a rumor so much as a claim on a hard day. There is an old aunt who can predict a microburst by whether the dwarf cypresses on the south rim throw light on the underside of their leaves all together or in thirds. We have more of this than we have science; we are not ashamed. We are not pious about it, either. If a thing pleases the mind and harms no one, we leave fat on it. The stone, being older than our stories, is patient with our myths, and sometimes answers one on purpose.

If you want to meet me when I am most exactly myself, come to the north table where the fossil dunes have been welded into a grammar of pale rolls and the escarpment throws a lee you have to feel with your feet to understand. The path is the old switchback, the one that thinks it is a river. Turn at the cairn with the fat-smile on its mouth and drop into the scalloped wall that smells like wet bark. You will know you are near when you cannot hear yourself say your own name inside your head because the thresholds of your ears are being used for other news. The alcove you want looks like an accident from the outside. Inside there is a cord strung with three knots hung at eyebrow height. Duck and turn. There is a gourd cup on the left post—it will be half-full even if the week has been dry. Touch your fingers to your lower lip and leave that salt for the house; we keep a record of everyone that way. Step around the pots. Tie your hair. If I am not home, I am either in the dwarf grove thickening the liverwort bed with a blunt shell, or I am lying on my back in the long smooth gully we call the Reader, waiting for the right alignment to make it leap, or I am out on the bridges setting a new line where a null has moved, or I am just over the rim with the pollinators, wearing resin on my hands that tastes of pine and iron.

There are rules here I keep without argument. You can write them this way: do not cross open slab if the grass shows its pale; watch the black lichen dust; travel at first light; leave cups in shade; carry a child only when you can taste the time; never give the wind a new song unless you are ready to keep it. I keep others that are only personal: carry the cord that your grandmother tied the day she lost your grandfather; never sleep with your ear flat to the floor unless you wish to dream of certain kinds of oldness; smear the Third Lip even on days when you think it does not need it—especially then.

I am not made for heroics in this place. The plateau does not reward that shape of story. I am made for repeats done finely: the same bridge brought across half a hand’s breadth different because the null went to the right; the same throat made gentle again under a sky that tasted of tin; the same seam of day held in the fingers until it opens into its hour and all the wet throats flash their yes. If there is a name for what I am, it is not a single craft. My neighbors will call me by the work they need. To the fog-knitters I am a carrier. To the throat-minders I am a hand. To the bridges I am a weight and a reading. To the gliders I am a friend with soft paste at dawn. To the children I am the one who will race them along the rib with a full cup and let them win by a finger. To myself I am a person who knows three tones of the stone that will not tear me, and can hum them back without being taken, and can lay my palm on the slab when the big note wakes and say with my bones: I hear you; we are home.
