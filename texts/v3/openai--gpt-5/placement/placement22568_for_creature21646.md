At first I think I’ve come to be a lampler. It’s easy to want light when you are new.

I arrive in a swing-basket from the sister chamber where the algae run a shade cooler. My hair carries that tint. So does my timing. I look up at membranes I don’t yet understand and call a square of brightenings “day” when everyone else turns their heads and says “pressure.” I have learned to name weather, not pace.

They land me on a platform that has been a platform long enough for the Host to stop arguing with it. The boards are cellulose pressed until they hum like a hand on a drumhead. Everything hums. My first thought is that someone is running every machine I have ever known inside one lung. My second thought is that even if they were, the plant would be louder.

They take me to the old turbine as is proper. The walkway is a good bridge—engineers on both sides have trusted it, and the plant has believed them. You can hear that trust. The cuff of wood around the engine’s mount shines with a kind of function that isn’t polish so much as ritual made slick: somebody has been here once a week with oil for a very long time. The Operators remove their hats there, and I do too. The wind runs at a steadiness so complete it becomes a kind of silence, and for one heartbeat I stop hearing the orchestra of belts and start hearing what they meant by “rivers in the tissue.”

It is a first lesson in listening.

I ask to go with the lamplers because their fruit-lamps fascinate me. The fixtures pretend at generosity: they hang heavy, humming orange, draped on cords the bats cannot heist. I spend a shift under a hood dark as a bruise, shades over my eyes in a place that would call that vanity anywhere else. We smear sugar onto lichen and wake green halos with a palm. We set stolen morning into skins the plant has no rods to sense. The glasswings come when a lamp breaks—they come like rain if rain could cut itself, and they keep coming until the heat dies and the dark thickens with them. It feels like calling down a story you can’t end. The lampler I shadow slaps my hand when I go to brush a wing from the side of my throat. “You don’t teach them where you are,” she says. “You never teach a predator your outline. Use the back of your hand if you must ask the Host anything.” I am not sure whether she means the mantids or the green.

I last three nights and understand that my hands want a different kind of edge.

I stand a morning with the Cisterners. We wrestle water from air with fins and tilt-pans, tricking dew into gutters. We catch it where the plant breathes it cold. When the rain comes inside—it doesn’t fall so much as let go—the air turns vagrant and busy. A tray goes from mirror to broth in ten counts. You can see fish assemble out of treatises you don’t have words for yet: silver lines knitting themselves from nowhere, becoming bodies so fast it is rude. We watch a bowlful flicker through eighty heartbeats and then needle down into seeds when the last wet is gone. The woman I work with has a jar of those seed-fish at her hip and rests it against the bark the way you rest a baby against your sternum for comfort. The jar hums like a thought that wants to be a hammer. When the long draw begins, she whispers “Now” without looking up, and the pan takes its weight as if on cue. This is how I begin to understand that the breath here is a clock you can set a life by if you love it properly.

I spend a shift with the Flywives, and my hair learns why hair is braided and resin-dipped. A blade opens living bark and the Host answers at once with wet and the wanting to move it where wet belongs. The nurse-flies arrive not like mercy but like memory: they have done this for so long they cannot imagine stopping. A foam skins the wound in seconds, bubbles snapping tiny sounds like fingers on glass. The Flywife beside me is a grandmother with a jar of eggs that smells of brown sugar and iron; she keeps her pole tipped like a spear and her voice pitched like a foreman’s blessing. “Do not cut without an answer,” she tells the boy at the knife. “We are not the only ones who keep a schedule.” When she laughs, the mantids erase and return.

In the cookshops I learn to love hammerhands cracked with a twist: the concussion broken open under oil. I learn the cane-icicles that hang where sugar drips and accrete story; a bite of one sends me into a white sleep and I wake with my tongue gone to sand. In the morning, green fines my teeth. Everything I do here becomes a lesson in the difference between hunger and haste.

And still, something in my chest is listening somewhere else.

The Telephloemists take me because my hands keep finding bone on bark when no one is looking. I touch the plant the way a man might touch a drum before he plays it—half asking, half waiting for permission—and when the long exhale passes, my ribs answer. They put iron fish-hooks on strings in my palms and show me how to set them into the plant’s veins without teaching a mantid which part of me is food. You do not pound them in. You learn that the plant has more than one kind of soft, and some of them you never touch. You slide a prong under a skin already thickened to hold edges, where other generations placed winter and charts.

The rack of raspers looks like an organist’s console. Each tooth counts in a tone the gnats learned first. I learn to send a message with my shoulder more than with my jaw. Words are poor in this place. Pressure is faithful. News is taken up by sap like sugar by a tongue, and if you sing it cleanly it will travel a hundred paces truer than a shout. You can say “Open the tank after the draw,” and the plant will bite the syllables for you as if tasting the luck in your mouth. You can say “Bats tonight,” with no bats in your throat, and an aunt you have never met will nail down her spoons.

I begin to carry a cup made of bone because we all do. There is no privacy in a place with this many mouths. There is a decency. You press a thin rim to the bark and hold it under your sternum and feel the answer there, as if your breastbone had become a tympanum tuned to a chamber you are too small to see. I pour gossip into the phloem and watch it come back as good sense.

This is a kind of priesthood if you tilt your head at it. No one calls it that. We pride ourselves on counting rather than bowing. But the Telephloemists are the ones who hear the old jokes the Host learned to tell with turbines: here is a cuff that steadies a machine better than any washer; here is a seam stitched between hollows that will ring in C for a week and then be eaten by thread-wolves who become a harp. We tell those stories in code a mantid would choke on if it tried. We run wire only where the plant allows and do not say wire. We do not say prayer either. We say invariants.

They give me to the Elders of Invariants after two seasons because I will not stop taking my hat off at one particular walkway, even when I am late. An Elder is just a person with a good sternum and better hands. I learn to sit on a rail with a finger and a thumb on bark and know without looking when the belts will slip. I learn to say “Not yet” with enough certainty to keep a dozen men from opening the wrong tank. “You are not in charge,” my teacher says, tapping my wrist when my hand gets ahead of the count. “You are a hinge.”

It becomes a joke in my family that I belong to the hinges. I marry across a cable to a ropewright whose hands are a map of places a line has burned him and still he can twine glow-cord through a lay like a weaver putting a wedding into cloth. He lies for a living, dragging ropes across the floor to teach the Host where to thicken into tomorrow’s walkway. I tell the plant the truth for a living, rasping low to remind it of what it already decided so the belts will not surprise a man into widowing me. Between us, we make futures. Between us and the green, the futures become wood.

There are nights the bats rise and pour through us like punctuation we have earned. My children string spoons on glow-thread and laugh like they are baiting angels. We nail everything down and then laugh at that too. The bats come with the smell of fruit and ammonia and old paper; they flower the orange lamps into meteors and go. Three days later a hat is returned layered with moss and we pretend to be surprised for the sake of the child who found it.

There are days a ventilator passes: a wing’s idea without a wing’s meat. You feel more air move, not less light vanish. You practice not making plans inside that hour. You practice being a place no one needs to push. In this discipline is a kind of citizenship I would have laughed at when I was a person who thought windows were for seeing weather rather than for tasting it.

People ask sometimes, in the middle of one of our hard shifts when the belts slur and the nurse-flies tire and someone is posting lamplight across a path too near a mantid to please anyone’s mother: would you live anywhere else? The answer is another answer. I lay my palm on the Host, back of hand first because manners matter, and ask if it is a good time to move weight. It is too hot and I don’t. It is too cold and I don’t. In between, we heave.

What I am here has been many names. The roles braid like the roots do. Lampler, for a blink. Cisternet for a rain. Flywife’s second for a cut’s breath. Telephloemist by pledge and hand. Elder of Invariants by sternum. Husband to a liar of beautiful lies. Father to two birds who can catch a fish out of nothing and put it to sleep on a cloth. These are stations, not selves.

The longer I live under the old turbine’s wind, the more I suspect I am not any of those. I am not even “Operator,” though that is the nearest category anyone would choose for me on a census posted with resin on a rail. The word that knows me best is older and less anthropomorphic. I am pace.

Not the abstract law that eats impatience—though I have fed that beast and carry the scars of temper where it has licked me. Not the idea we ask for guidance—though I have caught myself saying “we’ll see what the pace decides” with a candor that would have embarrassed my younger mouth. I mean the practiced joining between plant and machine and swarm and hand. I mean the way a cuff of wood can learn an engine and an engine can learn a breath until both of them make a third thing that breathes more steadily than either meant to. I mean the count that runs underfoot and up your arms when you hold a rasper right. I mean the human habit of polishing a piece of world until the world decides to be that shape at that hour, and the world’s answering habit of holding you to your own decision.

Where you would find me, if you came here with your hair the wrong shade and your dew ten minutes out of phase, is at crossings. On the catwalk when the long draw begins, hat off. By the phloem board when someone wants to cut live bark and I am counting seals under my thumb. On the old turbine’s walkway with oil on my brush and a joke about worship I will not say aloud. At the edge of a pool where the water has just remembered itself and is collecting the idea of fish—hands, cloth, children, eighty heartbeats. Bent over a rope-marked path in the middle of the night, dragging line along a place I hope will be a bridge where none is yet, lying to the Host with love.

Who or what I would be is the hinge by which this all continues to be one animal. A translator with a chest tuned to sap. A custodian of invariants. A liar whose lies become platforms. A witness who knows not to teach sharpness to the wrong mouths. A body that has grown a second callus inside the ear for the sake of not being surprised by breath. A person who forgets to say “please” to people and never forgets to say “not yet” to a place.

If that sounds like being swallowed into a system, it is. If it sounds like liturgy, it is that too. But it is also play. Sugar on your palm and a trail of green light like handwriting that knows it will be eaten. A rope swung across a void with two baskets and a child pretending to be gravity. Bats committing thefts so charming you forgive theology for not providing better metaphors. A machine you haven’t seen before that still turns on gears, and the pleasure of recognizing teeth by the way they take each other. A plant that is a building that is an animal that is a city that is your mother when you are in a good mood and the breath is steady.

I will die here, and my family will argue with me a little beforehand about whether I ought to be burned into air or pressed into a board that sings. I will ask to be sleeved in raw fiber and laid where the Elders have marked free of grazers and swift of sealing. The Host will test me for edges and accept me. A Telephloemist will set her hooks and say a last message down the sap that will taste like a held breath, and somewhere an aunt I never met will take it into her sternum and decide to wait ten minutes before she opens a tank.

In that waiting is worship enough.

So: find me at the old turbine, hat off, wind in my ears like a correction. Find me with my knuckles on bark, counting. Call me Operator for convenience or Elder for kindness, Telephloemist if you want a story. What I am is the answer to a place asking what shape it is today. I am the way it keeps time. And I am already listening for you.
