At first I am only skin that the note can find.

Before I am anyone with a house or tools, I am a surface encouraged into meaning by pressure: the pads of a hand that isn’t a hand yet, fanning and cupping of their own accord, learning what part of me cancels and what part of me carries. The chamber recognizes this as greeting. The floor lifts through me. I learn to hold my own shape against it and then, delicately, to let pieces of my shape come apart so that a small quiet can happen where I am.

Here, that is a talent like breath.

I try on other lives as if they are toys scattered at the edge of the hush. For a while I am a fan of mica caught in a gallery, flexing in place and pressurizing oil through graphite-dark veins. The single note is enormous on me. I drink it and my edges prickle bright when the wall burns its tiny lights; I thrill, and I am fed by my own complaint. Then I am a reed, ribbed, a tongue of glass that the room plays; I ring and ring and send that ring back into swollen nodes until they hum up like bees no one here has known. For several heartbeats I am salt dust warmed by nothing but rhythm, happily adhesive to a frond’s hair, ferried to a diode-mouth to be split and stored and called food. I am the patience of an eel that is mostly absence threaded with lips, a hanging paragraph of not-quite-up, catching a wealth that is pressure and salt and slow flake. The room phases me to where the anti-node is sweet and I dwell there so gently that up and down go out of meaning.

All of these are convincers. All of them make me happy. None of them quite answer the itch that my skin—that first negotiator—keeps telling me is me.

So I try a voice next, and by that I mean a way to take this one enormous voice that all of us live in and pinch it so that there is a word-shaped valley. It works. What I make is not sound in the way your ear expects. It is a measured slackening under my own pads that someone else can detect with theirs. It is a subtraction that does not offend the law because it gives the law back, perfectly, as soon as the sentence ends.

All at once I understand where I am likely meant to be.

Find me, then, not in the screaming centers of antinodes and not in the silent far corners that the wave forgets, but in the eye—the rare stitches of place where the geometry of swell and reflection laces itself into a pocket of comparative mercy. I live where the built begins: among lean-tos married mica to mica until their seams hold like a song note, beside a line of return pillars set at the spacing that makes the wave come back upon itself like a well-mannered thought. In my house there is a mat of glass hair woven to my grandmother’s grandmother’s pattern so that it refuses the fatigue of an overtone that made her bones ache. In my bowl is resin made obedient by time and slight pressure and no heat that burned. In the corner leans a slate cone whose rim bears salt; it waits for the wall to flinch into flame so that our history can be written as brightness and then be done.

I am a husher. I take that name not because I love silence (no one here has met what you would call silence; it would be a cruelty we cannot survive) but because I am expert in the making of exact small hushes that allow other things to be themselves. It is a kindness, and it is work.

My skin is a mosaic. Over my shoulders and flanks lie plates that look like scales to you but act like capacitors to me, holding and letting go on the chamber’s near-constant push-pull with a domesticated eagerness that feels like appetite and like virtue. Across my ribs and throat is the long seam we all carry: an organ that breathes the note. Inside, my meat is stacks of piezo that flex and rectify, lattices that behave less like soup and more like a map of tolerated paths. I do not inhale and be grateful for an oxidizer. I drink pressure and spend charge.

My hands—say hands and I will forgive you; the word is not wrong if you mean many pads like petals fringed with cilia—end in broad leaves of flesh stiffened with mineral. Nothing I do stamps. Everything I do is a cupping, a shunting, a laying-on that convinces the law to pass around what needs to be sheltered the way a river might be coaxed by stones you would never dare call a dam. When I walk I keep to the clean bands that all of us hive; the dusty strips are for growth to consider itself in, for beetle-altars to practice their private hymns, for fracture to scab without strangers hurrying it.

I speak with valves and with the small treasons of my own body. If I wish to say I to you, the twin swellings that sit to either side of my listening seam open and a thinness appears exactly where your pads would sense it as a familiar hand. If I wish to call you close, I cock my wrist and bear a little of my charge the length of an arm and let it drip, a sequence of pulses that resolve at your elbow into come. Our laughter is a carefully made mistake, a twisting gap that chases its tail until the room’s insistence catches it and makes it stop. We cannot have the joy of shouting and so we have become gifted at every other kind.

What I do, mostly, is midwifery. That is the word I know from the other part of my mouth that reads and writes, and it is the cleanest way to call what I am best at. Birth here is geometry fed by patience. An embryo is a knot of patterns, a packet of oils and charged frameworks that knows what to do if the world will only become a tutor for a span. To be that tutor is my calling. I measure out the cradle in rods of bone cut to family lengths, in slates that know how to send a crest home to kiss a crest and make, temporarily, a deepening. I spend mornings on my belly with my pads to the floor finding where the day’s slow breath has put the half-wavelengths and the first harmonic and the second. I set my cones. I tune my returns. I seal a chink with resin and whisper apology to the law for the interruption, then open something else to pay it back. I convince a small place to be a little older than it is until a pattern loosens from potential into publicness.

When a child is near release, we gather. We do not sing. We arrange. We become a ring of skilled refusals that robs the wave of a width of itself until it is calm in the middle, and in that reprieve the budded person has a way out. The first sentences the child will make are accidents against my palms: a back-hiccup that makes a dry laugh of me, a happy little un-pressing that my cilia translate: hunger is for pressure and for play. A name is a place in the field we can all make by hand. To speak my own, my sister must lay her three outer pads against my spine in a counting of nodes that only our family uses, and then the chamber knows me as the valley that appears in that exact relation. I am a notch. To be called is to be slightly missing for a beat and then entire again.

When other work comes I am called to grind. Nothing we do is as crass as sawing; we rub and rub with teeth-powder and shards and oil until mica plates mate like faithful lovers and no raggedness remains to spoil the wave with a thousand small arguments. I weave mats of glass hair that feel different under different bones; what rests a shoulder frets a hip; my loom knows both. I tune cords of silkstone to make beds that sink exactly so much; a child sleeps like a held subtraction and wakes full of willingness. I polish the last third of a return pillar until it sings only the overtone I want and eats the others as if I had fed it a seriousness.

We have animals like you have ideas. They come and go through our doing without any sense that they are ours to own. I keep a pair of eels yoke-trained to a tool-basket that floats three handspans up in the sweetness above the floor; they hang attentive there while I climb them to reach a patch of still-wild reed that needs to be asked not to shade a hinge-mouth colony out of its sipping. The thread-mammals—bright with their pretty capacitor coats—come to the ropes I have set in the antinode by my door. They rub, crackle, and then lick their own blue glow and thank me by not biting my mats with their silly, good rasps. A beetle-altar I have courted visits my house most evenings. Its back-shrine is tuned to my favorite equality of thirds and fifths; it makes a private chapel of tone for me when I am sad and then tips itself unreadably and leaves.

Taboo holds us as surely as the pressure does. In my kit with the bone rods are other lengths: the lengths that measure against temptation. I never split a plate carelessly. I carry a set of rubber bands—resin gone tractable that my cousins brew by the quiet in winter—that slip over the ankles of toddlers who step too loud for the season’s patience. We do not make toys that jangle unless they can be operated in a pocket shadow where their jangle is a letter written to a single ear. When festivals come we go out to the pillars and move slowly by rote and genius both, opening and closing what we are in a choreography that makes the cone lips burn white with salt-light and then go dark along a syntax that everyone feels and some of us weep at. There is no noise. We leave with our hair stiff with small emotive crackles and our limbs full of borrowed relaxation.

I count by harmonics. My calendar is a bundle I carry on a loop at my waist: bone lengths; a horn; a sieve with a private lattice that my grandmother worked alone and then taught me. I track time not by seasons—I do not have them, would not trust them if I did—but by the number of times the wall’s brightness has kissed the salt on my cone and by the speed with which oil returns to its tightness after I press and release a bead from a leaf. Elders carry more hush around them the way elders elsewhere might carry heat; when my aunt comes near you can feel, in your bones, that the world’s insistence likes her and lets go a little on contact.

We have gods in the way a math proof has lemmas and axioms. The Note is not a person. It is the condition. We adore it because it is what lets anything be. Around it hang our saints of place: the Quiet Eye, whom I serve by work and by birth; the Crest-Returner, twin to every pillar I polish; the Little Hurt, who makes light enough to lay salt to and so teaches us when the planet we live in twitches in its long sleep. In a corner of my house there is a line of oiled stones that ring—a cemetery of small rings. When my mother’s charge began to go soft and her same laughter stopped returning thin making into my chest the way it had all my life, we made her a shell of resin and set her high on a return pillar so that she could hum to us as she relaxed. I go to that grove sometimes and lean until the overtones she wore come through the mat into my skin and I know her as a clarity. When she is entirely dust we will carry the gift to seeds and ask that they grow listening like she did.

Above is a seam we do not speak of to children when they are still trying to keep food inside them. It is a rip in the roof where once, when my aunt was a girl and the wall cracked and flared so much that the cone wrote rings until it smoked, she crawled and looked and brought back stories that make my lungs seize just hearing them. A brightness without pain. A shout without a law to hold it. A wet that never would consent to be organized by pressure the way a sensible wet should. Some of us dream of it and wake slicked with release that is not water and apologize to the Note under our breath for wanting a sky that will not learn to be gradual. Others, like me, love the crack because it is our reminder that this is a pocket and could be torn and that what we call patience is a practice, not a trait. My name in the family kit lives also in the notch we have carved in the crack’s lip and filled with polished stone: a writing to say to whoever—whatever—presses at the other side: we are here by making, not by accident.

On a day I am happy, this is what I do. I rise early—or what a husher calls early: I feel the floor’s patience has climbed one bone and know that now the nodes are where I like them. I open the mat over the grove where we plan a new hive of tubes; I hold my sieve and flare the wave through it so that my plant-children will seed exactly where geometry tells truth. I tune a bracelet so that when my lover and I press our wrists, a private quiet happens worth waking for. I grind a plate. I chivvy a reed into consenting not to scythe a child’s leg with its newness by borrowing its charge and showing it a gentler ringing. I sit with an embryo and I am steady for a very long time. I mark the cone when the wall gives me a kindness. I go home and, in our sleeping pocket, I relax my scales and let my charge spend so low that I can feel the pressure wave’s innards, which is the nearest to dreaming we have: knowing from inside the thing you have always lived outside of.

If you came, foolishly brimming with air that this place rejects, if you could be persuaded not to cough and therefore not to injure, if you could agree to love a weather with one word only and a thousand inflections of that word—where would you find me to ask for help? Find the quiet eye. It is there in the corner where the lean-to’s edge kisses itself by our ointment, near the row of precise pillars, beside offerings that look like dust arranged until it made sternness. Call me by laying your pads (or whatever you have instead of pads; I am generous) two hands apart on the mat and making nothing there until I arrive.

What am I? I am someone who can make a little less and call it love. I am a midwife of gaps, a keeper of clean bands, a scribe of light that is a wound. I am a person who heats by listening. I wick ions, not moisture. I remember my dead by holding still in the right place. I eat by rearranging. I take joy in ornaments that tune me to what is tolerable and in the hard innocence of a plate so flush to its neighbor that they become a third, better thing.

In a world that is a room and a machine and a field, I am the person-sized absence by which meaning happens, and I have learned to keep it together by hand.
