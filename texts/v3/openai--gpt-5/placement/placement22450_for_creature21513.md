I would arrive by the seam that smells of ice and decision, shoulder turned so I could slip where the mountain exhaled just enough to part my hair. The light would be asked to wait outside. I would not argue; my hands would go ahead of me like readers who don’t need sight to know the plot.

At first I would be breath. Not mine exactly—mine would be filed as now, the way the chamber does with anything warm and temporary—but breath in general, joining the stack of humidities that lives in this room. I would learn how to put myself down without making a sound topple over. My ribs would haunt the ironless thinness. I would not speak, because something in me would remember that noise in here grows edges it isn’t proud of, and is better for being shy.

If I have to be a shape, I would begin as a pause. The gap between a bead and the bowl that keeps it. That small hanging moment is the only place in this world that is both full and empty at once, inhabited by waiting. I would be that: the weight choosing itself right before it lets go, the little animal of water deciding to become downward. I would learn the schedule of a single cornice and a single hollow the way people elsewhere learn street names. My heartbeat would get braided to the fat plink that arrives every ninety of its own; sometimes I would feel it hurry a breath when the draft from the seam thins, and then correct itself as if embarrassed to have noticed weather.

Then I would be the wick. Laid along the lip of the shallow floor, combed with fingers into a certainty, humming at the note only ears that have stopped hearing their own shirt can distinguish. My life would be capillary, a continuous negotiation with enough. I would pull without announcing myself as motion. I would grow old by becoming furred with the mountain’s tiny hairs; my retirement would be slow and ceremonial and end with me as a relic the rock adopts and eats politely over decades. I would take pride in never making anything run.

A while later, I would be the bowl’s inside, the place the drop chooses. Not clay anymore so much as a decision of silica and calcium accreting itself smooth. I would remember the first palm that pinched my lip and the last palm that lifted me in the dark and never knocked me against anything. I would know the weight of my regular drink and when to tilt to spare the seam a year’s worth of drip shifted by a young stalactite that has not yet believed in itself. If anyone called me a vessel I would allow it, but I would secretly think of myself as an altar that only one god, who is water, ever visits.

Between those roles, in the time that isn’t measured, I would be a hand. The Attender’s hand, the Wick-Keeper’s, trained until even its skin knew to lie down quietly. I would move knots on a cord by touch—and it would be an old cord, hair-soft, that smelled faintly of vegetable evening and damp rock. I would move one knot every ten falls, or every ninety, depending on what the chamber had taught me this year. The act would not be tallying so much as grooming: keeping time’s mane from tangling. I would climb the pegs once in a season just to feel the old hardwood under my toes and reassure the shelf that hands still know it. I would tap the little ring of cooled glass for the music of a retained strain and then apologize to the hush, because even that much sound arrives here like a visitor overdressed for a simple dinner.

On days when storms outside go by and do not matter, I would be a hypha marking the humidity with a map no eye can see except as glisten. I would steal from crystals with patience that cannot be misunderstood as theft. I would test a floral vowel in the air, a syllable no flower ever gets to say here, and then withdraw into thread again. My fruiting body would try itself out the way a child practices standing on tiptoe against a doorframe and leaves a smudge there to measure how much more of itself it can become.

I might spend an afternoon as the isopod that has given up on eyes because here sight is the least reliable sense. I would be as white and exact as an eraser shaving, polishing the floor’s rim as if it were a scripture, tasting bacteria the way a monk tastes a season in a sip of beer. I would be contained by my seam, and proud of that knowledge. I would be hungry without hurry. Somewhere in me a springtail would flex and be a period. It would be enough.

I have a life outside, but when I think of where I live, it is here that builds the room in my head. On the mountain’s brighter face there are voices and roofs and heat that takes offense when ignored. I use those things. I repair nets. I cut dead wood back and leave live wood be. I let my hair grow until it forgets it is hair and then I cut it so it will remember. Some winters my joints ache and I drink the smallest mouth of lungwater, and the chamber forgives me as if I were an animal that has earned a sip. When wind delaminates snow into knives and pushes iron into the valley’s smell, I go to ground in the only place that never becomes emergency.

On the most patient days, I would dare to be the chamber. Not as an owner, not as a god—a geology with a temperature that confuses mammals into thinking it’s alive because it has the decency to be warm. I would hold the rules not because I wrote them but because I am the sort of body that keeps them: weight is a polite suggestion; water decides; nothing runs; everything wicks; fire is a rumor; noise is quick to be ashamed; size learns modesty. I would continue finishing my making without hurrying my finish. I would let silica frost in summer and let calcium think about teeth and then talk itself into a lace that could be a stalactite someday. I would remember the gas bubble that first imagined this room and I would keep imagining it.

When visited by one with a lamp, I would perform: condensation would stand on end, trembling, as if applause had been coerced from it. I would teach that person quickly that light is not a neutral guest, and if they have any kindness in them they would put it away and learn to read by touch. I would accept them after their shirt has learned to keep quiet.

If in some year my hands are no longer mine, a small hand would be pressed to my ribs and coaxed to believe that stone can be soft when it is warm with its own slow heat. That hand would be taught to move in this room the way a drop moves: by deciding exactly when to be itself and then letting go. A knot would be slid and then, in time, stopped forever on one side of the cord. The cord would accept this as a kind of grammar, and start becoming mineral hair.

If I am to be named, I could live with any of these: Clerk of the Drop, Counter of Small Thunders, Layer-Reader, Wick-Keeper. But I like “bead” best. Not because I shine, but because a bead is complete and singular and also part of a strand that only exists by recurrence. I would be one recurrence. I would hang correctly. I would let go.

Where, then, would you find me? Up in the mountain, low inside it, at the height that feels high to lung and meaningless to eye. In the oval lung-room that has taught rock how to breathe itself. Palming a wall as if it were a sentence I am learning to pronounce without sound. Kneeling by a bowl, not praying but agreeing. Listening to a fat plink the way you listen to the sea when you finally stop needing waves to be dramatic.

Who would I be? The pause that makes the cadence a cadence. The wick’s certainty. The bowl’s inside. A hand that counts not to know the number but to keep companionship with the falling. A fungus thinking in thread. An isopod polishing patience. On very still hours, the chamber’s warmth as it continues to finish being itself. A citizen of smallness whose edges are shorter than a hand, admitted for a while to a place that is complete enough to forget me almost instantly and to keep me precisely by making somewhere in itself where my heat once belonged.

I would come back in a lifetime and find myself there again, where the new is exactly where the old promised to become. I would not be surprised to see my loosened knot grown a little frost of stone. I would take off my button and oil my hand. I would set my face in the dark and be a bead among beads. I would wait until waiting remembered me, and then I would let go.
