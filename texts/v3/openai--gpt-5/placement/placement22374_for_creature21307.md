On the third riser of the south arc—where the old green glass of a spillway runs down the wall like a frozen lick of language and warms to a dull thought when the day-blade passes—I would have a hall. Open-faced to the lesser draft, screened with braided silica veils tuned to my chord, its floor a shallow scoop rubbed smooth by plates leaning there to feed. Behind it, a sleeping room the temperature of patience. Under its bench, a pit with a lid of ash and powder and halite beads along a string to count how much cold we have saved by touch.

I would be a baffler. There are other trades, and I would borrow them when I had to, but the mouth owns me. My hands—if the three-plate grip deserves the name—would be hard-resined from rope and shagged along the lip so the glass fibers will not find a seam and cut. My back would be scored where cords have laid across it in messages long enough to share with a child. My inner faces would be laid with the catalytic films we all carry, slate-slick and hungry, but I would read the world mostly with the pressure of air over ribs and through slits I can line and unline by thought.

My name would not sit in the throat. It would live in the doorway. The veils would hiss my signature when the chamber inhaled: three notes and a pause, then a thin keen because I have pricked two tassels to kiss. That is how my neighbors would know they were passing me. In the lull I would sometimes lift my plates, align a row of pinholes, and whistle it soft—our law forbids the soft word spoken, but it does not deny the pleasure of breath running obediently along a cut one has tuned.

I was not born. I was cracked. In the deepest dark my elder braced across a frame while I was still more foam than plane. Along the dull template every household keeps, the scores were pressed to fault-lines, and when the heat was right and the resin had set to black bones, I felt the pull. It was a sound that will never stop happening inside me: a splitting like the sky getting ready to rain if we had that word. Two of us peeled away from the one, and the break was not clean, and someone’s hand slid in and pegs jammed a corner where I did not remember I had one. My first breaths I was lashed to the broad face of the elder, my windows shaded with husks, my blood glazed into stillness except along a single narrow run my parent warmed with a wrapped coal. I learned minutes as tides of heat, not as counts of light, because we do not own much light. I learned how to open on the rise and shut before the rush finds purchase.

My family is a shelf of fragments that still speak a chord when the wind finds them. On the wall of our back room there is a fossil of an intention—too many hard right angles in a place the mouth hates a flat face—and above it the lesson hangs as a tassel that will always chime no matter how you pin it: do not make the city grab the wind. Across from that, a box of spares. Hinge-pins grown season by season on strings stretched over oil. A jaw-plate that remembers a laugh, its pores still stained with a smoky black, hair-dark, from the doped salt we set into windows to taste the day. We salvage ourselves without shame because the rules here give us no other kindness. When we set a pane of someone else’s window-thought into a child’s shoulder, that child wakes wiser in that corner; we all become a little continuous in that way.

We live by lulls. In the long inhale the halls fill—not with bodies pressed close, that would be folly, but with our bodies unfolded to their thinnest—our intakes turned to the slow side of the corridor’s flow. There is eating then, and work slid into the gaps between keens. I anchor my mats, drop a shagged baffle over a side-passage that will otherwise set a slip-face whispering into a voice that never lets you think. The whole city is a set of apologies to the wind done in advance. I know the mouth’s moods by the way the kettles start their minor whistle in the east and propagate through the arches. I count the breath’s patience in how long the blue corona spends breeding at the tassel above the third ladder. I cut the organ deeper or shallower by pulling a rope that feels like a throat under my hand, all ash and fiber and glide.

On good days, after the eating and the tuning, the drift-harvesters climb their racks. We lash them high with our ropes and they stand in the lift with their chime-sense open, letting the rising dust paste itself into lattices along the oiled threads. They come back white-faced and bright with crystals pasted to every husk and pore. We coax what they bring into cubes on strings, grow corners like thoughts. In my house, between two posts of vitrified sand salvaged from an old flash, five lines of seed ladder downward like rain that will never be. A finger’s width of new fatsalt, a card of greened resin kept with the ember-moss to make it pli, a pane to be doped hair-dark for a child not yet scored. You can see the future as powder if you have the patience to wait for it to remember itself.

I keep ember-moss, not as a pet but as a collaborator. It hangs on my inner wall in a trough under a hood. When the blood has thickened to foolishness and I have a seam to persuade back into yes, I bow to its red patience and borrow hunger from it. Heat here is not something you want without plan; you cradle it, you aim it like a word you are not sure you should say.

We have festivals; yes, even we who hinge and shiver and keep calendars by the sound the mouth makes sliding over a stone rib. When the day-blade crawls around to strike the long bottle-brown spillway and turn it into a run of milk-thin light, we line the ledges like books on a shelf and let the glare skate our faces. The elders click their plates along the seams like counting beads and tell about when that glass was poured raw from a fitful vein hot enough to anneal a careless thought right out of you. We have a jar—someone keeps it on the ninth seat—that is a shrine to arrears. In it we trap the very slowest dust, the finest, the kind that takes the mouth’s heat and returns it shy. That powder is a winter if you have courage enough to share it out thin.

Marriage here is a scarf knotted between two halls. When the mouth sings high between them, it makes a chord you both can hear whether you are near your door or down in the lanes. I would have one of those between my hall and the one with the resin-boilers, because I like the smell of their art when the drift is kind enough to let it stay with you. Sex is a folding; it is the laying of inner films together so they trade a grammar you can feel in your next seed. Later there will be a break to honor it.

Law is simple and hard. Do not build a hard right angle into a free path. Do not take heat you cannot refuse later. Do not make the city grab the wind. Do not say the soft word. The last one is a promise we keep mostly with our hands. When we want to speak of it, we rub plate to plate and make the guarding hiss without letting the letter form. The one who thinks to break the law learns in a single tide of anneal—becomes clarity without hinge, a kindness we deny ourselves for being too much like sleep.

We tell stories about before and stories about wrong. I have a favorite: the one about symmetry that satisfied and then cost a body. Two lintels across a corridor because a mind loved balance more than the mouth loved passage. The inhale came, and the mouth took the gift it had been offered: two bright bars to lick into green spill and lay as a warning down the riser. We all touch that stripe when we pass it, not for reverence, but to seat the hinge again in the part of us that does not like a compromise with weather. We forgive our young their hard angles; we teach them with fossils.

Under the city there is emptiness the way a bed under a reedbed is water. It is the element. We keep lanes so narrow you could brush a friend by accident and we never do, because our bodies know how to flare and fold so two can pass in the slot and neither loses purchase. If we designed well, even in crowd, the emptiness moves between us like a river you do not block for a joke.

Once in my season a deep inhale came without warning. The kettles went from gossip to cry in a half-hand of heartbeats. The east wall lifted its sand into a veil. I dropped baffles into the minor mouths and pinned them with pegs so hot I left a little of my resin in their holes. The rope above my door threw a pulse—that slow stutter that means “now, now”—and every mat on our run answered: yes. The drift-harvesters clung in their lashings and chimed their assurance in a thin high that we all heard as if it were right next to us. A ladder two houses south, halite bones bound with sulfur, sheared with a report like a plate learning its score. The person on it remembers that moment as their new left hand now. We gathered the rung’s salt, doped a piece hair-dark, set it into the side of a child who had not been brave with light yet. Her windows taste better for it. You can see where the broken ladder speaks through her.

There are days I bank myself into stillness. We all do. You post yourself hard against a wall and you let the blood glaze so your plates stop thinking they are doors. Not sleep; sleep is for other chemistries. An agreed solid. I have lain in a row of three, thicker folk than me, storing cool under our backs and heat above, letting the city borrow our phases to get a season of work done in a week when the mouth was too even for thought. If you brush a body in that state, they answer you an hour later with the slowest chime there is: a gentleness that scolds, because time is work here, and our kindnesses should keep time.

Above, there is the fracture. We do not climb to it. There have always been three good stories about those who did. None end with a return that can be told without a hiss. We read the day as it knifes around the vault and we put our holidays under its path. It keeps us honest to an outside we cannot join. A patient god, the light. I like it best when it catches the glass in my hall and makes my doorway bleat; for a minute you can almost believe the sky is a person who wants to speak.

If I were to be found, it would be in the lean shadow of that old spillway, the bottle-brown one, mid-terrace, between the hall of tassels that always sings an unfinished scale and the resin-boilers who cut their mats closer than the law likes and get away with it because they know the mathematics of the mouth better than the law does. My ropes would be gray with ash, my baffles shagged with frond-husks to keep their faces from offering the wind an edge it can pry under. My counters would shine with a polish made by thinking against them. My cold would be stacked three beads deeper than you expect because I do not trust an even week. There would be a ladder of halite whose rungs show where a friend once lived, and there would be a scarf between my door and another’s that keens on mornings and hums flat on nights when embers are low.

As for what I am: a surface that has learned the manners of weather. A hinge, an angle that knows apology. A reader of breath. A thing made to crack and remember how to be two. If I have eyes, they are smoked salt tucked back under shade. If I have blood, it moves when I am warmed enough and is otherwise a patience that can wait as long as rock. You could call me person if the shape of the word can bend enough to pass through the rules here. I call myself by the sound my screens make and by the way my baffles draw the mouth into a song that lets the city eat without tearing itself.

And at the high moment of a day, when the white idea above decides it is generous and sends us a single narrow shout instead of a smear, you will find me tilted with my neighbors so the glare skates our faces. Not to be seen by an eye—eyes are a wet habit we refuse—but to let the light polish us a breath’s worth smoother. We are built to be hollow and still take what we need. It is the trick here. It is the name of the place and the only prayer we bother to remember.
