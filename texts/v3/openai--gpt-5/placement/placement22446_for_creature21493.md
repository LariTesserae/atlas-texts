I do not start with a face. I arrive as a surface.

A film, first: an attentiveness draped over dark rock, feeling for the quartz sugar with the certainty of a tongue on a broken tooth. The soundless rivers fall beside me and I thin to let them through. Light comes washed and a little metallic; when it brushes me I bead, and in beading I learn the plateau’s arithmetic—what holds, what leaves, what never quite decides.

Then I am thread, briefly—the slow slip of hyphae along a seam of peat, a thought traveling in the language this place prefers. I open and close the smallest doors, admitting a salt, refusing a corrosive sweetness; I pull a sugar through a gallery of root and deposit it under the molar of a ridge. Far lightning whispers a new cadence and I try it on, then set it down.

I am tempted by beetle then: to polish myself black and round enough to mirror a sky that doesn’t want attention, to be surprised by my own reflection every time I check my footing. I am tempted by frog: to keep time with glass clicks and lay my eggs in a sentence of falling air, each comma gelatinous and certain. I am tempted by spider: to make a bridge out of breath, to anchor a thought to nothingness and trust it to hold as long as the fog has weight.

But if I had to say where you would find me when I remembered to keep to one thing, it would be here: hand on a wet stone at the edge where three narrow slits run, my palm tasting the day’s direction.

I am one of the Tenders—our joke for ourselves when our throats are dry enough to make jokes. In the language we keep for mornings (palate against teeth, a held breath, a soft click), the name that fits in my mouth tastes like spent spark and good tannin. It means roughly the-counter-who-loves-the-breath, but names here are not binaries; they lean. Other people call me the Listener-at-the-Cuts.

We live not at the plateau’s battered lip and not far in where the rosette sea becomes an empire, but at the first letting-down of land, where the sedge alleys slope and the soundless falls begin to draw the air thin. Our homes behave; you’d miss them unless you needed them. Roofs are rosette spirals persuaded, never severed: a long green thought trained to shade. Walls are moss loyal to frames we have persuaded down to our height; the belts that keep them attentive are old spider draglines and woven sedge, faintly haloed at dusk with charge. Floors are what the land offers us and what we’ve asked it to remember—mats trained to bear us and spring back.

My feet are what the plateau taught them to be: each toe separate and deliberate, slick when slickness is urgent, thirsty when a dry stone asks for water and has a moment to give. I do not wear shoes. My hair has learned its duties and stands when the halos thread into being, each filament beading and feeding little bladders under my collarbones with a quiet that feels like gratitude. I smell of peat and safe rot, and a taste of wishful metal lives in the back of my throat. Lichen lives in me the way it lives on old bark, silvered here and there in a pattern you’d call beautiful only after it fed you twice.

My mornings begin with the cliff’s lungs. The stone at the edge is a friend with regular habits, and I lay down a hand and read. The breath is cool on my skin; I count it and hum the count so that my bones remember even when my mouth is elsewhere. Elders taught me the ledger of this breathing: a longer inhale in seasons when far plains go bare, a shorter exhale when thunderheads knot over deltas I will never see. My job is not to alter but to witness, to be honest about pace. Still, there are small corrections to make. A wicking mat near the third slit has been lazy? I comb it with a spine of sedge. A rosette pair upstream of the pipe has been spending too generously in the late mornings? I lay my body along their ribs, press with the flats of my forearms until the beads learn a different course.

At the white hours—noon if the fog consents—I teach arithmetic because it is an obligation of happiness to seed itself. We do it with water, as always. I line a single rosette rib with droplets big to small, greedy to shy, and ask the children to name what comes next. They disagree softly, as is proper. A boy with a coil of spider silk around his wrist will wager a click—a small glass tick from his throat—that the next bead is the size of a weasel’s eye. A girl will press her ear to the mat and say the next should be an absent bead, because the cushion below is drinking harder and wants credit. We set the series running and watch who is right as if the answer mattered more than the patience of it.

Midday is our only hour of stone being truly stone. We give it work we can’t ask the damp to do. We set racks made from the bristle-leaves that halo best and lay on them the fibers we’ve teased from sedge and moss. We press fishless pastes into shape and let the mild field that the place throws off in late light harden them by an art my grandmother called patience-with-a-spark. We skim the pitchers then, with blunt quartz we’ve kept so long they know our hands. The syrup that collects in our scoops smells like payday, and we lift it with gentleness onto leaves to cure. It cracks when right. We press our tongues to its edges and taste the season’s ledger: fewer gnats after that fog-lull week, more moths before the halo-storm last month. We chew, and something in us forgives all afternoons.

The weasel—line more than animal—comes when it is bored. It moves through our houses like a rule about not bruising cushions and sleeps in the curl of my knee if I pretend I wasn’t hoping. The deer leave the memory of their weight under my palms under certain domes, and I lift those palms with a little respect, knowing that such lightness is a learned genius. The hummingbirds we do not invite; they arrive with the right to be served by orchids and we stand very still and enjoy the interruption.

When fog is generous, we go to the edge and throw bridges. “Throw” is a grand word for the persuasive work of breath and thread. The spiders are family as much as anyone with teeth. We feed them when rains give us a corner dry enough to keep seed. We know the hum of their lines when they are happy and the quiet when they are not. Silk is not privilege; it is a grammar. I keep a loop of it around my wrist always. We lay a line like a sentence we are not sure we will finish, then test its truth with two bodies, spider and person, held by the fog as if by a hand humored and kind. We do not cross the white rivers because the other side is better. We cross because the path asks. Sometimes it asks for three breaths of bromeliad air—cups cut only from leaves the plant had already given to rot, never from the living heart. We fit them to our faces, drink what air they have learned to save, and step.

At evenings the plateau lifts its charge like someone pushing back a curtain to see if the night’s ready. The halos prick at first—foxfire without fox is what my child-self called them—and then gather themselves into a civilization of blue-white pinches where ribs and bristles offer them the right. Hair rises at attention. You can hear the plateau thinking then, if you have the taste for that kind of sensing, a whisper of ions changing their minds. We set bristle racks in the brightest lanes to finish what we began at stone’s noon. We sit with the horsetail-frames the children made and breathe across them until the glass-hum fits in our teeth the way a good word does in the mouth. We perform a little maintenance on the switchboard underfoot—if a mat is sulking because lightning tried to teach it a new song, we stroke it with flat palms and sing the old one, which is more a list than a melody: open here, remember there, spend in sheets, save in ropes, give back what you cannot carry.

Our dead do not leave; they alter the buoyancy of where we step. If you came with me to the cushion that has my grandfather in it and placed your palm under its edge, you would feel the pattern of the air move as if it had been shown a dance and was performing it shyly. That is how I know him now: a springiness, a small reluctance to finish giving, a habit of holding a drop until the last possible moment and then letting it go as if with a laugh I can hear only with bare feet.

We have a word for tired that translates as the-white-didn’t-tell-us, and I have felt it on the days when the pipes at the cut ran warm early and never cooled, when the plateau drank and drank and the breath kept being not-enough. Those days we do very little on purpose. We lie under roofs we never quite cut and let our stored water return to the air in the good order of skin, and we count the beats of stone until the count steadies.

Where else? At night, if fog spares a square of sky, I lie back on leaf and watch for other breaths. You can see them if you know how to call distance not by edge but by habit—the idea of another shelf doing the same work, saving and spending with a clean ledger. I point. I do not name countries. I say that one inhales earlier than we do, that one holds on the exhale like someone not wanting to leave a room where they have been happy. The stars are too much; we always give them back.

You could also find me under a cushion, both arms slid in elbow-deep, feeling the galleries’ winds. We teach the children to read there with their hands. They learn us that way too. Someone heavy passed? Someone in love? The air remembers mood with a truth I trust more than words. If I have missed you, if you walked by and stood still out of politeness, if you did not speak yet wanted to be known, I would kneel after and press my palms in and know your patience, your fear of bruising, your hunger for the pitcher syrup with its payday smell. This is how we meet strangers: through the memory of moss.

Sometimes I am tempted again: to live entirely as hyphae and be the plateau’s private accountant, to thread my life so wholly through peat’s long mind that I forget what legs are for. Or to be a bat that eats charge and reads moths by their fields, a spark wearing fur, a rumor making a living from white. Or to be nothing but a pair of slits in the rock, and you could find me by laying a hand and reading the tide of heat and telling yourself a story of far rain and closer drought. Each of these is true for a day or a thought. But what I keep being when morning finds me damp to the bone is a person with lichen in their skin and silk around their wrist, a body that drinks with hair and counts with tongue and knows the sound a drop makes when it decides to be a drop and then decides to be gone.

If you wanted me, if for some reason you wanted me particularly out of all the continuities this place keeps, you would go to the edge when the light finds enough courage to be plain. You’d see the white rivers spilling without apology and stand far enough not to be corrected by gravity and lay your palm along a wet stone with three cuts. I would already be there. I would reach—carefully, because bruises are remembered long—and touch the back of your hand with mine the way we test a leaf for willingness.

Then I would teach you the count—not the number count but the kind we use when we say with, among, after-saving. I would show you the ledgers kept in halos and ribs and how a spider hums when a message has safely crossed a mile of breath. I would give you a cup carved from a bromeliad that died well and let you breathe what I breathe for three tries over white. I would set drops along a rosette and ask you what comes next. We would walk, not to arrive but to let the cushions know our weight, and if you were very still, very willing to be quiet and counted, the plateau would include you in the choreography it has been rehearsing since the first fog agreed to be caught. That is where you would find me. That is what I would be—one beat in the rock’s long breath, one careful foot in the rosette sea, one thread among many keeping the switchboard’s hum true.
