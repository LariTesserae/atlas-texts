Where I arrive first is not with feet but with the change in my scalp. The hair there has learned to lift a finger before the rest of me hears the note. The mountain’s mouth opens and the pressure says its one-syllable warning, and I answer by going low, by finding the lee. If you’re looking for me, you’ll find me where sound turns back on itself: at the knee-wall’s zig, at the little throat of drilled stones that sings the weather into sense, with a bottle of fat and a bit of pumice in a glove and a patience learned against rock.

I am one of the Leefolk, and I keep the choir.

They call me a tuner, a throatsmith, depending on which valley said it into our wind. It’s not a pride-word. The harp would sing without me, but she sings truer when I hold her gut in palm-warmth and seal her hairs with a smear of oil against the day’s cold. I know what pitch belongs to a pinch-point gale and which to a fat updraft that will carry goat smell as far as the eagle cares to soar. I know the sour that means a seam has moved and the sweet that means fog will work gladly into net and felt. The mountain never lies, but it can be oblique; the harp puts it into a voice we can walk by.

I am built like the place asks: not tall but elastic, feet like a hand I trust more than prayer. My great toes have turned out by slow degrees the way you grow toward light. Winter taught my eyes their second lid; the wind taught my mouth how to speak in brief. I count steps the way my grandmother did, and hers: seven near, two far, one, two, seven—marks cut into limestone where hands I never met smoothed the edges for me. If I’m talking while I move—rare, but sometimes you must—the words are short and heavy enough not to blow away. When the gust stands up on its hind legs, I make the circle sign that means wait and be small, and even the goats understand that one.

Where I sleep, if I sleep, is the small dark nook that would be a hut if we insisted. Our insistence stops at a roof beam of juniper, pegged with bone. The hearth there knows the black smell of peat and pellet both. Boots are clipped to rope; breath is the first thing to wake when the harp shifts key. I prefer the nights that pearl, when fog returns your own lamping to you from every convexity and the ledge gives back a low gloss you can walk by. Those nights the choir quiets to a steadier hum, and you can lie your sternum against stone and let the shelf sing through your ribs until all the counting you’ve carried all day pours out like a sack split under its own good weight.

My work runs along the top inch of the world. In the morning I go straddle-kneed to the row of drilled stones and see which strings have lost oil and which have taken water and gone slow. Hair goes heavy when it’s been forgotten; gut goes brittle if we are stingy with fat. I rub, I coax, I ease each long throat back to itself. I retie knots the wind has worried. I listen for the wolf note that means two voices have crept too near and begun to beat against each other. I am a jealous keeper of single tones. The cliff’s organ booms; the harp names the boom; my people make their day by that naming.

Then I walk my paths. I say paths, but the ledge writes them new every year. Water’s hand is compulsive; stone keeps accounts. I taste drips with a fingertip and tongue, chalk and shell. I slide copper under a sphagnum pillow gone too fat and ease a slice to another bowl. I shift a net two hands left because the rainbow’s tape has edged a finger to the right. I check the tether stones where the rope has worried the holes smooth, and sometimes I bow-drill a new mouth in the limestone with sand and water and the old song—one, two, seven, one, two, seven—until the hole rings true instead of dull. Anchors are like good stories here. If they congratulate themselves, they fail.

You might think my trade is sound and rope alone, but that would be a valley mistake. Spiders teach us sails; I leave their triangles hung where they’ve slung them and watch them take lift in the afternoon road of air. Swifts are my clocks. They score the sky with their scream and flag where the updraft makes a rail, and if they fall silent in the middle of good blow I can feel the eagle slide in, big as a stilled thought. I pull the line in at the drilled stone then and count children and kids a little faster. When silence lies down on the ledge like a new skin, you look up. That’s the law.

Fog is the crop I’m set to most days. Our garments are built for it: thick felt outside to catch bead, thin inside to carry, seams that wick the way a root does. I walk until my sleeves are heavy, then wring a river into a skin that will be sweeter than the last if the drip it hung under has worked long enough through limestone to leach the chalk down to a pleasant memory. We taste every skin; we notch the soft stone for change. Some years run lean and bitter. Some months are so rich you can make a stew without ever heating a pot. We don’t own it. We just stand so the sky can hand it to us before the updraft steals it back to witless height.

On the best days the mica wakes under sun and there’s a little universe knotted at every crease of rock—halos we coax by turning trays a hair, polishing flecks with a sleeve. We do not garden, but we carry the bog like a sleeper, the smallest civilities: a skein of red vine rooted into a clean runnel so water will remember to stay and make a skin; a frog tolerated in his pan although he scolds my ankles like I trespass under his eave. The snake comes, thin as a memory of rope, and I lift my stick to point him out to a child who is learning not to point with fingers. The child nods, puts her breath back where it belongs, takes her next seven near, two far, and I count with her—not to reach a number but to please a rhythm that has husbanded us longer than language.

Sometimes a day asks you to be a messenger. I carry storm knowledge down the goat paths, my rope in a coil that does not slap rock, my pocket heavy with damp. In the hamlet a woman presses my sleeve and says what will it be and when; I tell her for what the harp said this morning, and she plants or does not plant by it. We trade her a bundle of fog felt that will boil sweet when her well goes sour. She gives me a copper pebble big as my thumb and a length of juniper cut true. We owe each other this in the old way. Time is the commodity up here, and we are its quiet middlemen.

Who else am I, besides the ear that keeps the harp honest? On some days I am the string itself, a line of hair tuned to a pitch the weather wants to play. A gust finds me; I sing; an elder’s chin tilts by a hair, and thirty people step sooner than a stone chooses to molt. On some days I am the notch under a palm, thumb-worn by a hundred mothers and sons, a little pool where a drop swells and is heavy enough to make the next time the same. On winter nights I am a tufa rim writing down the breath-count of the season in a ring finer than hair, accounting for damp and thin, for storms too violent to precipitate a skin. If you sliced me, you would see every year I learned something and every year I forgot it in favor of what the stone asked that week.

I am also the voice that teaches children to stop. Not with fear—I do not scold the way the frog does—but with the respect the ledge deserves. Gravity is never your friend; the ledge is never your home; other creatures have as good a claim as you. These are the lines that do not blow away. I tell, in one breath each, the stories that have kept us light. A man laughed into the wind and opened his mouth too wide when a gust went inward. A girl wore a bright copper brooch and the eagle took her for a walking eye. Two brothers moved a boulder to get at the sweet drip behind it and the wind memorized that new throat and played it until the wall let go. The cairns carry these things in stone because sometimes words won’t hold long enough.

If instead you want me as a creature with wings, I could be the lammergeier’s patient circle, its eye that likes a clean white shard and will hang on a note of boom for minutes because it knows a goat kid’s mistake before the goat does. If you want me softer, I could be the milk of cloud itself, loosened from air into felt, traveling on a back warm with work, poured into a leather skin that swings like a fruit from a drip the ledge has grown a lip to feed. It would not be a false claim. We are what we carry here, and what we do not touch.

But when I answer without ornament, I’m the one kneeling at the knee-high wall, head bowed not in worship but to hear. I am the fricative our language makes behind closed teeth, a name for a person whose job is to turn weather into instruction you can do with your feet. You’ll find me with my ear close to stone, palm flattened to feel if the shelf is thinking about its skin. You’ll see me freeze when the applause of water becomes readable in the corner of my hearing and then grin without showing my teeth because the note says we can go. I am the one who puts a pebble on a cairn for you, should you manage to come unannounced and patient and be brave enough to become small. On it I’ll spell your name the only way we do it here: not in marks that last, but in balance. For as long as the harp has voice and the goat hair holds, I come. When the rinds underfoot tell me they are done and must molt elsewhere, I go. The mountain keeps the account. I tune the ledger’s throat.
