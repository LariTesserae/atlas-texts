In ordinary light, because twilight lies, I would step off the bank and try my weight on a green-headed stake. I would learn the place through my bones first: the shin that feels where the peat breathes, the arch that knows which velvet lies like bread soaked through, which lies like a promise. My ears would do what yours did—go wide—and then they would stay that way, a habit I could not break even in louder countries. I would find myself listening with teeth. The meshes taste like a penny, like menthol you didn’t ask for, like a question about whether the charge should be one notch higher when the air is not fog but trying to be persuaded.

At first, I would try on a lot of selves. I would be a bead of water stalled on a thread, considering a move that looks like uphill but feels like home; I would be the quiet muscle that urges it—a difference in desire strung taut between porcelain lips. I would be the bruise-colored bolt with a fur of life on it, the bolt that never turns because it understands that arrival here is always by erosion, not by statement. I would be the paint where only rain reads, a chevron dulled by algae into something the children still recognize as winter’s shorthand. I would be the rock that weeps in a lot of little sadnesses, and I would be the hush inside it, the long, capillary exhale that teaches patience to marrow.

Then, because I am not a stone and cannot keep still forever, I would join the readers. I would be lighter than I thought I could be. I would start as somebody’s second pair of hands and second set of eyes. My boots would be broad as rafts. Salt would climb my laces. I would carry, ungainly for a week, then comfortably for a season, the long-handled hook with the ceramic fork so I could nudge without pulling the whole field’s sleeve. Chalk would ride behind my ear until I forgot it was there and left white commas on doorframes and on my own neck.

They would teach me the alphabet of algae. Not on paper. Standing at the pan’s edge where coins spread and gather into a script that means a gate upstream has grown opinionated, or that the east-run gutter has found a new desire. They would tap the lip of a cup with two fingers and put my thumb where there is a quiet: Here, feel the craze take damp and give it back. They would make me swallow a mouthful of the air between wires and ask, What does that say to your fillings? I would answer wrong for a while. Then I would stop needing to answer because the answers would be in my hands.

I would learn the local gravity as if it were a friend’s temper. Where it can be negotiated. Where you must agree. How to divide weight, how to leave it evenly. Do not insist. Do not pry with toes. If you must move quick, move honest. Trust old footprints more than new. My calves would remember every blue stake for float and every red for suck, and I would stop thinking it childish to have tied color to consequence. I would become someone who argues good-naturedly about shades of paint because a hand’s breadth here matters a life’s breadth later.

Once, as a child in this telling, I would have put my palm to the mesh. It would have made me a sum for a heartbeat, lengthened me into the arithmetic of the lattice. It would not have hurt. Afterward I would have known, unforgettably, what it is to be added to an arrangement—held, accounted for, released with thanks. That day I would come home salt about the cuffs and a little bright-eyed, and my elders would not scold me. They would mark a small cross in chalk on a pole I could not see from the cot, a cross that meant: learns quickly; remember to teach the gentle way to nudge.

As I grew into the work my palms would polish the pump-house concrete by accident. I would check the tape on the levers not because I mistrust them but because habit here is better than faith. I would give quarter-turns their bird names—wren for a nip, lark for a generous nudge, storm for the time we opened number 3 against itself and bound it with my belt. I would hold a new lip while the hot box set its glaze and learn what warmth can do without noise. I would get good at reading needles through fogged glass and using a pitted winch for every purpose except the one it was made for.

Sometimes my body would be a bridge. I would lay the ladder down between two stakes and go over it sideways to spread the ask of my weight. Sometimes I would be the slight extra heat in the pump’s belly, standing shoulder to steel so the quiet nest of magnets could borrow a degree of me and keep the veins from icing over. Sometimes I would be a waiter—the patient kind—sitting with my back to the weeping rock line and letting their hush settle my pulse until I could coax a stubborn run to remember where it wanted to go.

There would be a day I would not be a person at all. I would wake as the field. My skin would be mesh and porcelain and tin. My nerves would be drawn wire, my thoughts the small caravans of beads that break a line into sense. The hum I make would be just the right side of audible, not so much song as kept temperature. A drop would lift its mind and I would feel it the way a colony feels one ant’s decision—not as command, but as a new thread in our braid. I would be the sum of arrangements, the civility that small forces offer each other when invited. I would be antigrace, tilt, inclination, schooling, cap trick, all the names written inside the pump-house door and crossed out and written again. For an hour of that day, I would have no need of wrists.

When I returned to wrists and ankles, it would be with a better humor. I would laugh at the private puns we never carry past the berm. I would add to the list on the door a child’s arrow toward a drawn drop and the unambitious word: go. I would wipe grease across the inside cover of a notebook because too much love ruins paper here, and because rain respects wax. I would write a chevron backwards by accident in a gust and let the rain correct me over days, trusting the place to teach, as it always does, by wearing.

Animals would pass through the periphery of my being and give it dignity. The heron would make one shadow into eight, and my heart would behave like a pan: receive, then give back when asked. A rail would unpick itself into a stitch of sound and we would not ask for a second. Frogs would count themselves in ones. Invertebrates would be rumors I consent to believe. The small mammal prints would soften even as I watched them and I would take it not as erasure but as apology. The skylarks beyond the weeping line would speak empire and I would be content to speak angle.

I would come to understand that the old things sticking up—rail-bit, barge-rib, a dial forever at nine—are not ghosts but grammar. They make our sentences possible. We make theirs gentler. A winch with no cable holds down a tarpaulin in a gale. An empty glass window shows me what shiver means today. I would learn to count time by the rounded corners of concrete, by the salt bloom’s patience, by the slow teaching of lichen. I would acquire the skill of knowing when to move a stake a hand’s breadth and when to leave it, because movement here is not progress; it’s a rephrasing.

What would I be? On a plain day: a drop-herder. It is as close to a true answer as any. Someone whose steps are agreements, whose tongue can taste charge, whose hands know the degrees between rimed and rimming. Someone who gives quarter-turns names and reads algae when paper quits. Someone who leaves notes where rain can read them.

But also, when names are not required: I would be a habit given hands. The habit of listening for the pump’s pause and sitting down to feel the entire place thin and thicken through the seat of me. The habit of leaving weight evenly. Of not insisting. Of treating gravity as a conversation partner who has lived here longer. Of understanding ownership as a valving schedule and care as a gutter angle kept true.

And sometimes—because what else are we here but sometimes—I would be the interval between pearl and tick, the second longer than expected when the droplet is held and accounted for, a resident like any vole in reed or rock with hush. In that held second, I would know my address: within reach, in ordinary light, on the berm, ears wide, practicing the small empire of things arranged just so.
