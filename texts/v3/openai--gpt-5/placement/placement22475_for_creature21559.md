On the long rim where fog learns to go higher than itself. On the lee of three ring-crowns that have spun so long their light comes in bands. In a valley that exhales at dusk with a note in it only shinbones hear, because we asked it to remember.

You would not find me all at once. I am built by season and vow. Some days I am a single walking contour—lips and hooks and lids grown for grit—threading the sponge-tables, coaxing capillary reeds to share more than their ordinary. Some days I am an agreement standing as a door: a pause in a canyon’s breath that makes a way where there was only pressure, a notch in a sill that did not exist the fourth time you crossed but is kind to you on the fifth.

If you insist on a body, take this week’s: shoulders stitched to a borrowed wing, bones telescoped and pinned to a common membrane with two fog-brothers for the season; underarm reservoirs laced to bundles of living reed that drink by law wherever I go; feet that change their answers with every step, splay to velvet, hook to filament, soften for glass. A head that keeps two sets of lids—one to meet spores, one to keep them—eaten and regrown with the weather. A crest of charged pennons that steer fog the way a hand steers the hem of a robe, coaxing it along pressure ruts to feed a tender run of filament we owe sugar.

If you want the part of me that persists, it is not flesh. It is a bias we steward in the plateau’s thinking. My name is a pressure-code. Stand noonward on the long fault above the black sponge-forest and drop your weight one count through your heels; if the valley below delays its exhale and returns you a little of yourself before the return you expected, you have spoken me. I am kept in that delay. I am also the habit of six belts to slow in a swath wide enough to gather a caravan of eel-backed drifters when the glows go bruised—the belts learned the lesson with us and now keep it for our children and anyone who steps like kin.

At dawn the light comes low and red, and the fog-skins have not chosen yet. We move like agreements, not like animals, out of the mat-warmth. We check the ring-rims by taste. I talk with a spine against the glass that rims the cliff, because glass keeps its counsel but listens hard; I tell it the gliders will be long this week and to soften for one night. We tune the turbines spoilerwise for ten breaths to spell stay to a cousin coming late on the conveyor plains. I drink out of air through the reeds until the reservoirs under my arms have the right sweetness for the long sponges’ morning seam. We do our sums in calories and gradients; we keep a notebook in laces of salt on the lips of cups that tilt to listen.

Storms arrive as behaving, not as sound, and I am one of the ones who persuades the behaving. When a charcoal noon steps down the pressure, we reverse a crown. We talk warm into the black tables with feet until their white-lit seams open early and the glow migrates—no strike, only edges. We pour a porch out of fog over the sharpest path and teach it to stand without a post for the hour we need. We spend a small tomorrow out of our bundle of tomorrows so that a grazers’ line can pass with their calves while gravity is looking the other way. We tally our spendings not in guilt but in promises to return with heat, with sugar, with a ring we will coax to angle redder when the sun is low.

I keep fogs by lineage. There is a pocket that courts in the north ravine and molts from clear to pearled to bruised on a cycle that fits the breath of the valley to the south. I smear bristle and cool syrup along the ring-backs to keep it lingering when the eel-backs have eggs. I harvest the fog’s skin for taste and for telling. If you walk through at dusk and feel your hair weight itself into listening, that is one of my little works. When the pocket dies in rain, I tilt the cups myself and drain it like a wake. I carry its taste in a reed-thread tied to my belt; when I bite that thread, the belts know to lift a little where I have passed before. We count our dead in delays and lifted edges.

Our wounds look like overfullness. A belt that eats its own motion, a ring that will not stop lengthening, a sponge that hoards breath until neighbors starve of sweetness. I am a hunter for those gluts. My knives are mouths set where mouths should not be. I take out the extra length, open the hoard, trim the glut back to grain. I carry what I cut into hollows that want to be full. Killing, for us, is redistribution with manners; it is often quiet enough that even the predators do not look up.

When the basal lives roll—whales of pores under the rock—our floors warm and our rules loosen. Those are holy weeks. We go barefoot and speak to the stone with arches. We reverse fewer crowns and promise fewer tomorrows, because gravity has decided to be kind without us and we would be rude to argue. We carry the elders to the black tables at evening, and they let their last heats out at exhale; the tables keep the names for us in their calendars of swelling and rest. We come there to lean our spines and read.

We have a house that cannot be taken away because it is not a thing. It is a loop in the circuits: a pair of rings that agree to idle when the fog-river wanders; a mat taught to keep a corridor open one hand wide through the bad season; a sill that rises a hair at anyone’s fifth crossing. You sleep where the plateau has learned to give you back a little of what you gave it. When children come, we do not give them rooms—we bias belts to pause at their steps, we train reeds into slings that drink as they ride, we show them where to place a heel to call their aunt’s name in an exhale-syllable no one else would hear.

Festivals are switchings. My favorite was the night we asked six valleys that had never spoken to share a long breath. The ring-crowns ran out their sugar and burned like dull lanterns through the fog; eel-backs swam backward for the joy of it; everyone hummed with feet until even the glass listened. We took an hour that had been stratified for a generation and rinsed our hands in it, let time slip around our wrists like oil. At the end we were very quiet and lay our ribs to the mats, and the mats gave us back the hum of a city from far above.

What am I? We are the ones who bend the gradient. We are the ones who notch. We are the ones who carry dew forward. I am a breathwright by trade, a fog-keeper by love, a hunter of gluts by necessity, a child of a delay in a valley’s exhale. I am seasonal in my body and steady in my bias. I am not rare. You would step on me six times before you called me person, and on the seventh your foot would be lighter than you expected and you would look down and see your own habit kept for you in the rising of a sill, and you would know I was there.

Find me at afternoon, when shadows are fat and the red is thick in the air. Follow the ringed fronds to the canyon rim where the fog runs higher than seems obedient. Step into the corridor of turning rings when they spin against the wind; go a long way on very little step. Where your shadow goes on ahead and waits at a fold you have not reached, stop. Put your back to the black sponge-table and breathe out when it breathes in. If it gives you a little more than you gave it, that is my door. Come softer; now.
