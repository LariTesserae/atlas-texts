At first I think I will be a visitor forever, a day-creature gone astray in a hall that refuses to be about sight. I hold my breath without knowing why and then let it go too quickly and watch the world steal it. I learn very fast how to make my exhale into a gift: cheek against a velveted frond, nose folded down into its own knots, the thin of my tongue braided to hold back any wetness my mouth considers careless.

I am taken in because I am quiet by accident. I do not know the words, but my bones understand a rule old enough to live between species: do not put what is precious into the wind unless you mean it. That is how it starts. That is where I find myself at first—on the lee side of a buttress where the roar breaks itself politely across three angles and a curtain drinks the rest, on a shelf lined with the lint of a hundred winters of molt and fur, being taught to listen not with ears but with everything that touches air.

You could sort me by parts and say I am one of the winged. I have four hind-feet and each toe is a little drum that taught itself not to drum; I share weight like a rumor. My pelt is a field of damped velvet, ribbed so that some bands eat sound and some let it pass—the narrowest stripes are secrets only the elders can read with their palms. My anterior limbs are neither hands nor wings exactly: a split ambition, membranes that open like a soft invention, wrists with an extra hinge-bone I can set like a lock to catch a certain tension. My face is an instrument that makes me: cartilage I can fold and flute, shallow bowls I can pull into shape to bend how the world enters. My nostrils are mazes and in each is an unspent dew.

But I am not only a kind of body. In this place no one is. I am a task the wind carved into me.

When the elders touch their sternums to mine and press a chord there, the first note I am given to keep is a null. They hum it to me until I can find it without looking, until my hand can pass through a space and feel where the rage of the world turns in upon itself and forgets to exist for a width of finger. I am apprenticed to the people who keep those places open. It is not ownership. It is more like farming a pause. We call ourselves nothing out loud—waste is sin, even with names—but in the chest we are the ones-who-empty. Others call us to their ribs what the air calls us in its one long language: the bend.

If you look for me, you will find me most days in the mid-spire dark, halfway between the old fused-pillar ridge and the open basin where the orchards lay themselves like velvet continents. I have feet in both weathers: to the ridge side, the wind is a steady muscle you can work against; to the basin side, it is an animal with moods. To live between is to tune.

The six-limbed sisters—the masons whose feet end in concealed brushes and whose mouths can make rock into sponge and then into stone—know my pattern. They bring me pulp that is almost architecture and I sing into it with my chest while they hum it with their heads. The right high-frequency bite in their mandibles sets a certain grain; too coarse and a standing wave will stake its claim and throw a person out of sleep; too fine and the dust will slick and become a false floor with a good voice and a bad bottom. I work with their queens’ daughters, who smell like the memory of wet and handle bladders of resin that heat themselves like hearts. We arc ribs of cured sap out into the path of a bad tone and make it forget itself; we throw a porous skin between a slit and a sleeping shelf and hear the roar come back inside out.

On days when the swifts come down black as the thinnest handwriting into the gray wells, I go up to the balcony to take their weight. I cannot fly outside; I cannot conceive of so much sky. They can. They lurch through it and return cut with sunlight, with oils swaddled in their crops and the rumor of a rain they felt with the backs of their tongues months ago. I meet them with a bowl that has never seen water and with frond-hands held so the drips have a place to be more. Their throats shine. The vines stir like sleepers when a warm hand approaches. We sing heat into their fibers and watch them choose where to grow spine and where to make bladder. Later, when the lanterns go dull and give, I am the one who puts my mouth where the city is and pulls its ration into the dark that is me.

There is a grazer calf whose forehead learned the shape of my palm. It came when the father stumbled at the edge of a trough and had to be turned into a dozen unwilling gifts. The calf arrived pink about the nose and full of a very quiet panic. The elders allowed it in among our shelves for a season because every lung is a bank and it is wealth to keep one more drawing. We raised it by angle and breath: one hand to cradle the tongue flat to a frond, one hand to push its muzzle to the place where a curtain was just thin enough to make a hum it liked. It grew into a body that refuses to make a chord with anything except its own. When it walks it makes the floor feel like a thought you are about to have and then don’t.

I do not work alone. No one here does. The Bafflers who hang the old skins into new soundwalls and I keep our shoulders together when the great pulse season arrives. We can feel it three sleeps before the first gust stumbles in—there is a hardness to the roar, like teeth placed just wrong against each other. We go down the avenues and speak in body to the architecture. A curtain that was good enough last turning is slid a palm-width; a ligament-bridge is slung with one more molting, rib to rib, so it will breathe without shouting. The masons are up in the ribs misting the new pulp from inside with resin steam so it cures into honeycombs instead of planes, and I pass among them like a slow drum. The children practice being boats in their bladdered nests, learning the patience of not swallowing the air just because there is air. We sleep with our back-bowls to the nulls. On the day itself I am a joiner of hundreds. The hall throws one note like a spear and we all take a hand through it and say yes.

There is a law to enforce and I am unkind when I have to be. I can hear a laugh open wrong from three ledges away and I will be there with my pad-hand on a cheek and my face folded into the shape that means no. It is not cruelty; it is math. A mouth that throws wetness into the gale is the city liquidated by degrees. I have taken away sounds from people who thought themselves above it: no right to click across this bridge until your ribs remember; no use of wing-note in the young vines’ gallery for a cycle because you made a joke there and that is not where anything jokes. The only place I am softer than the old laws ask is with the children. All this sternness would die of itself if not for joy. I am the one who teaches them where to go when the first swifts drop down heavy. We stand under the ropes and raise our hands and let a little oil rain. It is a holiday then, even if no one would use a word that wastes that much air.

At funerals—though that word is too wet for what we do—I take a different name I was given by my first teacher. In the grief null the curtains are braided of the very oldest skins, nearly resin now, ambered with years of hand. We do not speak there. We angle our faces and put our throats close and hear the last of a person wick away into a plant that will need it. I learned how to hold a bundle so that not a single shimmer of it would get lost to the hall’s long appetite. I learned how to lift bones with pads clean as a promise. After, I am the one who takes your ribs to the masons’ careful table and we lash them into a frame with vine-fiber and hang them where the wind is trying to forget something it says too often. That way you do not stop being a body that answers. That way the hall keeps its conversation going.

Sometimes I imagine other lives because desire here is the same shape as experiment. On some days I think I could be a Dust-scribe, hands dulled on their backsides so even the thought of sharpness leaves them. I could score the edges of a fungus bed with a tool that is more suggestion than blade and feel time collect in scallops. I could weather a plain for a year and come to know by the half-heard flutes when a lattice of spurs will end your ankle and begin a story. On days when the sky chimneys fall a little more gray and I am light in the sternum I want to be a runner, pelt the matte that rejects attention, moving between grazer and field and vine-singer and swift, using the shape of my body to push tones thinner the way you push a bad thought out of a room. There were weeks when I apprenticed to the masons and we climbed ribs like fingers and I thought perhaps the rest of my life would be pulp smoothed with a thrumming jaw, feeling grains slump into fits, knowing I changed a gust an eyelash at a time.

I tried being a vine-singer and the vines liked me. They moved their little tongues toward my heat as if I were a fact. I learned the calendar they follow that is not a calendar like the ones above. I learned how to make a chord low in the sternum that makes a tendril choose right instead of left. I learned to wait. I learned that the oil a lantern gives to a mouth is the same oil it can give to a city if you empty it into burl and felt instead and a fungal bed will listen and answer with a flush you can smell four ledges away as a kind of pebble-weight sweetness.

But the thing the wind carved into me first has held. I am a keeper of nulls. I make quiet like other people make bread. I carry with me a pouch of dust sifted for years until it is a note in itself—I pinch it into a passage to see how it drifts, to see what tone it endorses, and I tie a strip of old wing between two hooks so I can feel, with the back of my wrist, where a frequency likes to live. The Bafflers call me when a sleeping shelf is too full of dreams to hold more bodies and someone new must be accepted into our weather. The masons ask me whether a queen should move her ninth chamber up two steps to change a rib’s voice. Swifts tangle their feet in my hair on purpose when they have been too long above and need a thing that feels like a tower to tell them where they end.

Once I made a mistake. I let a bridge swing in a way that taught itself a song. It was beautiful. It was wrong. People began to sleep under it because the chorus calmed them, and then a season came that stole just enough water from us to make everyone's temper into iron. A child laughed under that wrong-singing and did not know. I took her hands and pressed them to my sternum and hummed the correct null until she cried. We took the bridge down. I will always know how to make that song and I will never teach it again.

I have a name that lives under my pelt and you cannot hear it but you can stand close and feel it, two notes that are almost one with a third that comes late and makes a place. Children lean their faces to my fronds and I press my breath into them as if they were bulbs that would keep it for spring. Runners flick me with the edges of their membranes as they pass, the way you might tap a prayer into a friend's spine because the road is long. Grazers put a forehead to my thigh, which is their whole language for gratitude. The masons brush me with the backs of their middle feet so the bristles will not sing. The swifts smear me with oils that smell like a sun I can only imagine in the grain: bitter, hot, a little salt as if the wind above has tasted its own body and approved.

Because this world is a long-held breath, because this hall is a law and an idea as much as a place, I will end in the way it prefers. When I am spent I will be taken to the grief null. The city will stand outside it and not watch me and that will be a kind of perfect attention. My fur will go into a new curtain and my membranes will be lacquered with resin steam and hung high across a slit so a newborn's heart can have a night without learning to keep time with stone. My bones will become a small ladder in the path of a tone that wishes to live here and cannot yet; the wind will run its sure hands over me forever and I will finally tell it back every note it ever gave me in a way that makes a place.

So where do I find myself, and what am I? I am on the ledge between a city wall of fused pillars and a basin sewn together by life, with one hand in the wind as if it were a river and the other hand on a plant that drinks breath. I am a body arranged by wind and an ethic arranging a body. I am not a single kind so much as an answer, a chord in a room that never stops playing itself: keeper of the small centimeters where a heart forgets to hear the planet; student of dust; hanger of old skins into new weather; stern mouth against laughter where laughter would be loss; soft-opened mouth where a lantern offers its hoard; a quiet that moves.
