At first I try to be water, because everything here teaches that first. I imagine myself as a skin a finger deep laid from horizon to horizon, remembering yesterday’s pull, consenting to today’s. I feel the long hairs of pressure rise through me, I stand up with the pulses from underworld tide, I leave this plain for that one the way a thought leaves a thought when it has been thought enough. I spend what I am on warmth and charge and torn sweetness, and in return I get to salt and file and lay films like instructions no one wrote but which everything reads.

It doesn’t hold. Water never gathers into a person for long. It is vein, not voice. If I tried to say I was water, I would be lying in pieces.

I try then to be stone, because stone here refuses to be just thing. I imagine myself as a hill no taller than a breastbone that has learned, over centuries of air fingered through pores, to prefer one note. Whole generations court me. Children lay their skulls against me. I weep in skins, I fruit in foxfire, I become a saying. My old throats round to thin flutes, and I hiss when a west fog tears, and I sob when an east one comes like a mother who is not a mother. My language is long. I keep it whether I mean to or not.

Stone has patience, and I do not. If I were stone I would learn it, and I am not sure I could bear that.

For a while I practice being the big walker, the door-thing. I bend and pump and open and shut and eat thunder with my ribs. I stand in my own weather. I pull a factory around me like a cloak. I make lanes of fog honest as paths and I wear lampreys of noise because to share is to live longer. I move without conceding movement to the plain, and the fine cords of everything I am know which chords mean feast and which mean famine.

It is beautiful, but too far. The door-thing does not come back when you ask it to. It is its own day.

If I stay anywhere, it is in the places this world forgets its own rules for the length of three breaths. It is under a lip of crust that leans, in a spoon of stone two hands deep, where the twin whistles that own that place wrap around each other and pluck themselves gone. It is a little pond black as oil, in which prints repeat in pairs so long the counting becomes prayer. When you stand centered and the roar does not leave but leaves you you for a palm-sized interval, there is room enough in the chest for a person.

So I become the person who brings people to that room. I am a midwife of hush.

If you need a name that will carry through these airs, take the closest one to the notch at the base of your throat and lay it there and hum. We would understand it as a chord, not a sound. I am counter by bone and burr-maker by habit. There are words for what I do in my people’s hands that no ear could carry. The closest are: fifth-finder; tackswoman; keeper of palms.

I live under a dull-skulled overhang that never learned to glitter again after some old violence sealed it. Dull is trustworthy. At dawn, before the fog-rivers have chosen their lanes, I go to the frame that stands two palms above the everlasting sheen—our sampler, the un-fence. The poles have throats cut so, skins stretched thus. I lay my weight along the top until my spine is a string, and I let the morning come up me. The frame tells me the day’s grammar. This throat’s burr is fatter than last week’s; that membrane holds a new bead of salt and so will quiver sloppily when the noon shear gathers; the middle note has been eaten a hair by pipe-eels down the slope, which means a family near there has meat in its hands and should be congratulated with three knuckles. The fifth between the ridge on our left and the vent by the old cairn is thin enough to walk.

We do not make maps. We make chords. I don’t tell my people to go east past the red stones and then north into the lies of the horizon. I tell them, press your ribs to this pole and feel where your bones say yes. You will learn where the yes is bent by where it fights with no. Keep the fight at your back. Step only when there is only one whistle. Shuffle when there are two.

Rules come to you with your skin. I teach children by pressing their joints down until their knees give messages to their feet the way reeds give to their rings. Flatten when the plain sings itself into more than one note. Become foot. Learn what dull looks like. Dull is not failure. It is promise kept. A lustrous face has a new language it cannot yet speak reliably; it will fling you when the next fog-river chooses a road. Horizons are liars; your eye will betray you for the sky’s delight if you let it. Choose a whistle and keep it. It does not have to be the nearest. Measure where others drag at it, and you will find the gap. If you are a counter, be fed, be begged, and be alone when you have to, because too much body will retune you wrong and you will spend yourself trying to find home with hands you do not have room for.

My body is wrapped in listening. Ears live where other people would wear sleeves. A shawl of cartilage flowers lies quilled against me until the weather comes and then opens, petal by petal, to feel agreement. Agreement is danger and bridge both. My ankles are thick as drift pegs, my knees lift barely because lifting is an invitation, and we do not invite the day to take us if we can take ourselves. My soles are soft as limpets’ bellies, soft in a way that means hold. My hair is a hedge of salted whips; each filament drinks and carries, so by the time the mouth tastes the sky it already knows something of what it has planned.

We dress not to survive the wind but to use it. Membrane and net, twisted gut, twined vine—veil on veil, each stringing a little different so some eat low burrs and some take the high keen and some save the wide moan that sits always under everything. Food is a steadied hand. On days when the plain is sulking and the chords refuse to cohere, we fast. On days when the ridges argue themselves into a phrase a person can sing back, we graze like grounded kites. You can taste it in the molars. The big walker passes and the fillings in your head hum as if to say, mind the generosity of larger throats.

Midday, when the paler patch behind the lid would be more accusing if it had the courage, I go to tend cairns. We do not build them because stone likes being led; stone does not like much of anything but it has learned to like being correct. We go to the places where correctness wavers. We mix shell with weeping until it takes a set a breath’s depth thick, and we press thumbtacks in, dark keratin, not for memorials but for leverage. One tack here, the old hum leans; one tack there, the fifth between two rills evens; one tack last, and the place promises people instead of trouble. We bury our elders in these promises. We thread tendons through ventmouths and string bones until the ridge speaks a phrase worth keeping. If you stop at one in the bruise-hour, lay your skull to the upper stone. You will hear the jaw-set of a woman who could count three whistles together on windless days, you will hear the laughter in the burr of a boy who oiled his harp too much and fed his mats to sickness, you will hear everything that kept your feet from unlatching into the hour with no you in it. No one says prayer. Everyone presses.

I carry a wrist-harp of weeping-ledge scrap and gut, three strings and a bridge with its own bright headache. When the pan sulks and the grazers at the film-cuts go slack, I make them earn. The harp drinks from the day and gives it back in a shape that opens gates in the smallest mouths. The mats foam a little along their undersides and their light grows a spiteful shade greener. I apologize to them with salt scraped from a net ten miles off that tasted like old clean. I have never been forgiven by a mat, but they keep gluing their neighborhood together under my fingers, and that is either forgiveness or the only answer they have.

Sometimes I must make roughness where there is too much right. A whistle too pure will lift a foot when a foot should not lift. We wedge greasy bundles of fiber into throats to teach the place to stutter; it thank-yous us by being survivable. In better years—there are better years, you can count them by how often lovers are seen in pairs flaring their shawls together under a lip until the world between them stops—we set a harp big as envy over a vent and make a league-square learn a new voice. Once, one of those voices became so beloved that everyone began to walk to it when they were too tired to count. We had to carry the harp away. There is mercy in denial. Our word for the art that folds a meter into a field-length is the same word we use for the thing you do when you stop a mouth with your palm so the breath coming out does not spend itself for nothing.

I am a leader sometimes. That is not a comfort. On those days I tie the oldest twine around my waist and hand the anchor-stone to the one person here I would allow to call me by the name my mother gave me. I put my throat to the notch and call. The call is not for ears. It is for marrow. Straps creak. Ankles think about nothing but ankle. The day chooses to agree with itself, just the once, and we step, all at once, or not at all. We go away. We are gone exactly long enough to count to hungry and then we appear where a tack I pressed ten years ago has cured to a firmness not even I expected. Children laugh, because children still can, and someone cries, because someone has always wanted mercy and did not know how to ask for it until now.

If you look for me at night you will find me where the light of the mats has sulked as bright as it gets and the foxfire beads on a face weeping itself thin. I will be checking the cords on our instrument-house, retying a breath of them so they drink a lower moan because babies are slap-hungry for that note and will sleep if you give it to them. I will be pressing my back to a back because food goes further when ribs beat together. I will be setting a senior’s hand on a stone and letting them count the day out of themselves with each pulse until at last they want to put their body down next to a phrase that deserves it. We do not end. We incline.

Animals intersect us. The disc that is mud until it must be more will flip its edge at my skin when I kneel to drink and I will lift my hand out of its way. The air-shrimp will rise like a blessing when the plain does something interesting and I will gather the new-penny jelly and eat with exactly the grateful face all my elders wore when they taught me what gratitude looks like here. The umbrella walker will go past once a year if it keeps to its lanes, a traveling thrum in the teeth, and I will not try to follow, because if I am honest some things must remain a door you do not open.

Some mornings I wake with no patience for any of it, and I think I should have been a pipe-eel, saying yes to the only direction the world offers, beating my skin gently on its womb-walls and adding a burr to a note that will outlast my bones. By noon I am back at the frame, and my nails are square with old lime, and I am happy to be the tack my ancestors left me to be.

So where would you find me in this world? Put your hand out in the dim and feel for an edge of stone that leans over a pool two hands deep, where the whistle from one side winds with the whistle from another and for three breaths they cancel each other’s insistence. That small silence, warm as a palm on the chest, is my parish. Follow the row of poles that are too even for chance and lay your spine along their top until your bones say yes in the same place mine do. When you find a cairn whose hum has been leaned just enough toward mercy that your feet wish to lift, look at the top. You will see a scatter of thumbtacks of nail, dark against the shell-lime. Press your nail into the soft between them and feel for the song that agrees with your skull. If the stone answers with a lack—if it holds, if it inclines and gives you that clean, cupped not-sound that lets the day pass through you without taking you into pieces—then that is me, too. I am the person who tunes that answer. I am the tack and the hand that presses it. I am a seamkeeper. I am the one who carries hush like other people carry fire, cupped, protected, shared, dangerous, holy.
