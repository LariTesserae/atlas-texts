I would not begin as an animal. I would begin as an hour.

Not a whole hour—the plateau is greedy about those—but the hinge of one, that notch where dew has nearly finished walking down comb and the steam from a hundred thousand slow lungs has not yet found each other and thickened into bands. At that hinge the stone is most talkative. It carries the day’s orders: who is crossing who, where the fog threw its back against a face and turned, which rib-lands rang with ice-sheath and which went slack. If I have any first sense at all in this place, it is not eye or skin; it is the feel of weight in the ground when a far-off boiler-line leans.

If I last past that first hinge without being drunk by a cushion or pressed thin by a trap that learned a rhythm too well, I make a choice without making it: I flatten.

I am one of the wide-headed ones. The rock named us before we named ourselves, because we are easiest to describe as a shape. Skull like the lip of a pot, heavy, sprung to be a rim. The bone is doped with crystal and iron in alternating arcs the way a luthier lays veneers to make a violin that remembers its key. My neck is short and armored; the rest of me is a shrug of meat and tendon dedicated to pressing that rim down and making a seal. There are legs, yes, four, but they are incidental, pads that grip basalt the way a tongue grips a tooth. The air is too thin to pad sound; the stone runs it just fine, so I put my head to it and eat.

We call it eating because after a day laid on a seam with a good hum in it—one of the big rails, the ones that come from herd-steps spaced a minute apart—you can stand and your belly is full and your fat is a shade thicker along your flanks. You have metabolized schedule. The piezo belts interleaved in the rim have squeezed and unsqueezed a million times under your weight. Ions have walked through membranes in time with pressure. What you take in is not flavor but the truth of distances. There is salt in it. There is the faint taste of the iron from a boiler’s inner rack because the herd has leaked a little hum from its chemistry into its step.

I live in rib-lands. The plateau pried itself into book-spines here, magnetite and feldspar standing in stacks so even the thin air looks ribbed when it moves over them. The spaces between are corridors blue with frost at dawn, bare and black enough at noon that your eyes silver over from the glare and your thoughts go flat with it. Good places to listen. The dikes catch sound and make it rail-straight. The seams run true. If a press waits in the false-cushion on the third shelf in from the drop, and if the herd you care about is new to this reach and has not learned that press’s hum yet, you can feel both at once—one sound fat and trusted, one thin and hungry—and you can put your body between them and decide.

We do not decide by running. We have no boilers. Our mouths have no interest in dew except when thirst has made a coin of it. Heat is borrowed through kindness and friction: I lie in the lee of a cushion until my blood is a little warmer than ice, or let a flock pass over me and graze its pressure-change, or ask a grazer for a shoulder a moment if my rim-crests ache and need sparks. Mostly I am cold. Cold clarifies. Chilled, the bone sings sharper and small differences in tremor come in bright. The price of hearing is always fat; you give it up until your belly is shallow enough that your rim touches clean. We are at our wisest when we look like famine.

I learned to lie. That is both a joke and a craft. As a calf—if that is what you call the young of us when what we are as babies is a dish of cartilage that grows its own edge—I was set by an elder across a seam with more sound than I could stand. I did not understand how to hold. My pads tried to kneel, then tense; I would clamp and the hum would shift and I would lose it. The elder moved his rim a finger’s width, set his lip on mine, and all of a sudden the sound gentled. That is the first language lesson we get: where to put your weight to tune a world.

Our hair, where we have any, is nothing much. We are not combs. We do not harvest dew on our backs. The air runs too clean here for smell to travel far and our tongues do not trust the idea of distance. We make eyes and then bury them; light cuts and blinds us and shows almost nothing of what we care to know. The mirrors on our cheekbones are permanent squint. What we grow large is ear-to-bone: ossicles so wide a hand could lay on them and feel them answer like a struck bowl. My skull’s sutures are not seams but hinges I can loosen or tighten to pick different registers out of the same run of sound. Joints elsewhere in my body are this simple: geared to turn a round humming mass of animal into a wedge and then back into animal again. We are built to be laid down, lifted, and laid down again in slightly different places for most of a life.

A life is perhaps two hundred seasonal turns here where dew rises and dew falls, longer if your sense for schedule is sweet and you are lucky with pressers and wall-peelings. We do not make nests. Our resting is a geometry: on the days when the wind sleeps and steam bands at shoulder-height, we take our rims off the stone and set them just above it so the air can do the rest of our thinking for us. You can see us if you stand back at an angle where the glare lets you: dark rings propped on pads, like low tables set as a path across a corridor. We look like work left out.

We are not alone in our thinking even when we lay alone. The rope-singers are our cousins by function if not by making. I will not pretend to know how they eat; it looks like wind and mica and a discipline of oil. But when they’re full and start to hum, I listen with a joy that would be worship if it were not so practical. Their lines are maps you can feel with your chest. They tell you which way the harm is blowing by pulling the aurora’s bright low so that a child could point: there. The boilers hear them, and the flocks lay their courses along their ribs of note, and the filter-walls splay their plates at a better angle and catch more glitter. Some days my job is nothing more than being a comma in that sentence: the pause that lets the meaning settle before the word for turn.

This is how I feed and how I pay: I memorize the sound of a line. The sound is not a single sound. It is a miscellany layered until the stack is one thing, the way you can see twelve filaments making a cushion’s fuzz and still call the whole of it one green. The minute beat of the steps. The steam-whisper from the lips flattened into a pressure that the stone can read. The faint frantic patter of a young one’s still-fast heart, wrong in the chord like a new tooth in an old jaw. The thieves flickering their dorsal ridges for heat and seized from backs in sparks; the dull tinny runs of condenser vanes cooling and the pop when a flock fails and is born again into rhythm. I feel those through bone and name them with my bone. And then I find the places where rock is too honest—where it amplifies harm the way it loves to—and I lie.

There are days you can be nothing but generous. The pressers set themselves thin three dikes away on a route that only the ignorant take when they are too green to know that the bright slope’s glare taxes boilers into slowness. I can taste the pressers’ hunger as a squeak in the edge of the seam. I can taste the herd’s haste as a failure of slotted lips. If I lie early and heavy on this seam, I mute the warning and the line passes toward hurt and does not hear what it needs to hear to be wary. So I go hungry for three dew-tides, thinner than sense says I should, and then lie farther down the rib-land, let the rail carry the pressers’ coil forward one shelf but not the next. The herd slides their last foot sideways, an angle you can see even from the glare if you look sidelong; their song keeps its fifth; they do not feed a trap for a week that would have cost them a calf. I do not eat their thanks. I eat the steadiness that comes afterward as they hum their names into the aurora and prove themselves still complete.

There are other days where the cost is personal. A season long ago the frost drew a salt line through a bowl-curve that brought a wall too well-fed and gelatin-bright growing out across a lip the boilers had used a hundred years. The wall spoke like ribs rubbing wetly in the hinge of a chest. The herd’s steps shivered wrong on the descent and several young started to sing their anxiety with their lungs. The whole chord went sour enough it would have trained pressers for years if left to sit. I did what we do. I boiled myself with friction on obsidian until sparks shot and the smell was old copper, then lay across the seam at a cant I had not tried before. The hum under my rim corrupted; it took the wrongness and let only the long true under it pass with any power. I starved myself to the bone to hold it.

What fed me was costlier. A press-hunger from our right reached up the rib like a shriek and a press-hunger from our left reached along the seam. Both recognized the damped chord I had made as a new language they could eat. For weeks after, I could not lay my rim anywhere without calling them, so I unhooked my sutures and loosed the big bone until my head rang with so much wind-talk I could not tell one call from the other. It took most of a cold to retune. I am not proud of the sloppiness with which I learned that lesson. The plate-heads who mentored me spent two days across a neighboring seam like parents locking a door—not sleeping, not feeding, lifting and setting, lifting and setting, eyebrows sparking with static from how close their bone sat to iron—so that the new pressers did not learn the herd by meanness I had made.

We are social when it renders right. We do not devour together. There is too little to gather without becoming a wall, and the walls have their own obediences. But we lie in bands when the ground’s talk is loudest. We set ourselves rib to rib and let the space between our rims become a corridor with its own small weather. Across that corridor we teach. You can tell newware by the amplitude of their patience. Their rims rest too hard and chatter; they lift to reposition on every rumor of change. The old ones do not lift. We learned the art of turning without standing, of sliding weight along an arc of bone so the stone hears a correction like a breeze. Youths hear only turns. Then, one day, you watch a student ease his rim a hair and watch the herds hear him, and it feels like you have passed a song without breaking it. That is our pride.

What we trade that is not sound is story. We have no letters but we have ranges of blue days etched on the undersides of our rims where the iron’s heat made faint auroral stains. If I lay tonight my head tilted so the singers’ lines brush close, I will wake with a scribble of pale green at one edge of my skull. That note is someone’s name and someone’s turn and where winter is bending. We mark ourselves in stain and carry it until melt. Lovers—if that is what you permit me to call the way two of us agree to lay near for a few seasons and learn one rim like a partner’s palm—differ by how they choose to set their heads in the strobing light when the sky is low. There is more art than need in that, and I cherish it. We have a little space for art when a year is good.

We make more of us in a way that keeps our pettiness small. Eggs the size of a curled thumb, a sheet of gasket-flesh each, are laid in the one window when bowl-pools blink open under rime and exhale thick cold breath laced with sulphide. We glue them headfirst to rock not for hiding but for listening. We brood them with hum. The seam we choose is a quiet one, a seam no press has learned and no herd runs. The rope-singers pass over and pull the aurora bright and our eggs tremble fractionally; their cartilage glands lick iron and set crystals in slow swirls that will align with whatever rail they live above for their first year. If you lift one—you would have to break my head to try—what comes away with the egg is a film of taste you will never lose, a lemon-salt that teaches even your soft tongue where not to step long after you put the egg back down from whatever kindness or cruelty made you lift it.

We do not die loudly. There are louder deaths here to make a story of. When we fail, it is often because we put our money in the wrong hour and starved to hear more. We die with our rims set to a seam that our muscles cannot lift from and the wall-peelings glaze our lips with a delicate salt glitter and the flocks rattle like page-turns above and after a while a cushion recruits our edge and makes a slow velvet of us. Some old rims never truly go under. They remain as stiffeners deep in the root of a mat, giving its city-state spine, singing dully when a herd passes so that generations who eat dew and syrup but not hum still feel the schedule and are soothed by it and do not slurp too greedily and earn their own strikes.

If I had been born otherwise, there are bodies here that appeal. I admire the milkers’ handwriting routes as they trick light into rain and pay it back. I admire the slugs with their caravans of microbe-farmers and their tongues like woven wire, the way they lay a smell over a rock like an instruction. I watch the boilers with a jealousy that has less to do with their heat than with their names; to carry a song you can sing at dusk into a body that others count themselves by is a wealth I can only graze at the edge of. It is not in me to be wall—my patience is long but not self-forgetting enough to mouth the wind for months—and I am too given to rhythm to content myself with veil and vane. I tried once, in the kind of youth that makes experiments, to lie under a comb-forest and keep time with their inch-high hiss, to learn their speech of dew. I lasted an afternoon. The tick of little arcs over my back drove me into a frenzy of counting that left me curl-shaken and embarrassed. I remember it because it taught me that knowledge is scale, and not all scales are yours to master.

I return to the ribs. The plateau has given me work that feels like a verb more than a noun: to listen and lay; to slack and stiffen; to catch an hour by its hinge and lever it into usefulness; to make a place for a herd to put its slow feet where the pressers have not learned to lock. There is no map of where I will be in a year that you could draw. There are maps to be felt when the rope-singers brighten the sky and your bones recall how your bones liked to be when you were egg and seam. We do not remember by storage. We remember by tuning, by passing the knowledge of a rail like a song around a band until each of us hears a different fifth and the whole makes sense only when we lay together and mute and unmute as one mind.

So where would you find me? In the corridor between book-spines that the frost salts every dawn. In the hard electric light that makes your teeth think of knives. In the low bands of steam that collect at shoulder-height when the wind goes to sleep. In the shadow of a grazer’s black, oiled flank when I need a thread of heat and a rub of sparks to loose my sutures. In the hum itself, if you had bones for it.

Who or what would I be? A listener with a rim for a head and hunger for cadence. A seam-muter. A broker of slow thunder. An animal that eats the difference between harm and habit and calls the meal good.

Hard to love if you love faces; easy to love if you love schedules. Hard to see if you look straight at me; easy if you pay with your hands and your knees and your skull to the stone. Patient until patience becomes an ethics. Thin until thinness becomes an instrument. I would belong here the way a comma belongs in a sentence that has learned to breathe.
