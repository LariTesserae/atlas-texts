The world announces itself before you can see it. A single, continuous thunder, the sound of a planet pouring. It fills the skull, a blunt pressure that eats smaller noises, that makes speech a pantomime and thought proceed by gesture. The sound has no attack and no decay. It is simply there, as if carved into the air.

You stand high—on a naked spine of rock cutting into a river that isn’t horizontal. The stone is young to the weather: edges unrounded, fractures still angular, faces glittering with unabraded mica. Rime glass glazes the windward facets in vertical tessellations where the spray freezes. There is no soil packed into the cracks, only dust caught in ice. Anything that might have been a plant somewhere else—lichen, tuft, moss—does not exist here. The only growths are physical: crystal, frost, and the gossamer of mineral veils laid down by evaporating brine.

Above and around, at this altitude, the air is thin and painfully clean. The horizon is abrupt because of the thing that rings it: the Fallbelt, a latitudinal ocean held aloft by wind and the stubborn habits of a spinning planet. It doesn’t stretch across the sky so much as through it, a miles-thick lamina of water streaming endlessly from one side of the world to the other. Enter it and you are submerged, not in a pool but in a continuously falling medium—pressure and flow wrapping you like a current. Sunlight penetrates it the way noon sunlight penetrates a clear lake: shafts laid down like pillars, streaking through the column of rain with metallic sheen. Every droplet, every thread of flow, throws spark after spark of normal white light. The belt is not dark. It is lit as an aquarium, flashing with the angles of it, with the lensing of its own skin.

The cold sits in everything. The water is just above freezing and sometimes below, supercooled in stretches that shatter into rime with a faint gunshot ripple inaudible beneath the roar. The metal of the few structures here retains the cold like a creed, transmitting it from weld to girder, humming with a slow, brittle ache. The rock is colder still, a thick-conducting silence beneath the noise, its temperature anchored by the altitude and the absence of warm currents.

The Fallbelt never moves, not in the way storms move. It has found a groove in the atmosphere and lives there the way a tone lives in a flute. It arcs the same course day after day, year after year. The gauging-stanchions pounded into the cliff faces here carry thousands of bright notches marking obsessiveness and patience; the highest glint is also the lowest. The waterline of the sky has not shifted the width of a nail in centuries. Engineers keep taking the same measurements. They make notes because that is what engineers do, and the notes rhyme with notes from long before.

Between this mountain and the next there can be fifty, a hundred kilometers of empty blue where the belt’s edge cuts a knife-path. That emptiness defines the place as strongly as the water: long clear reaches with nothing in them, low sound but for what reflects from the belt, no birds, no buzzing of insects because there are none to make it. The air here smells like nothing, a sterile cold that only lets the ocean’s breath in at the boundary: salt-mineral tang, steel, a faint sweetness of anaerobic films when the spray dries on metal and wakes again.

There are artifacts, scattered and small. The old turbine towers are stilt-legged lattices bolted into the stone, their mouths sunk into the edge of the falling ocean. They do not turn—there is no blade that could survive that shear—but they stand, and submerged in their throats are honeycombs of pressure-cascade chambers. The water drives itself through, and the chambers squeeze it into a regiment of laminar jets that work the turbine cores deep within. Everything is muffled in foamed sound. The outlets pour energy as cable and heat. Where the outlets meet the world you get steaming, finger-wide trails that vanish into frost after a few meters. On some ledges a factory stamps plates for replacements from ingots hauled up the mountain on silent, slow-going cranes. In a long view these built things are punctuation in the grammar of cliff and water, nowhere near a sentence by themselves.

Once in a while one of the older platforms is only a torsion of stripped girders rusting to iron smudge, half-seen through the falling light. Even the rust reads like a flower here because nothing else grows. No wires festoon the sky. There is no city. Industry, yes; civilization, no. What people come, come to keep machines properly immodest in their hunger for power, and to salvage what they can when a weld dehisces and metal remembers its ore-state.

Inside the fall, biology arranges itself along other lines. There are no plants. There is a slick complexity of things so small they don’t earn the word vegetation even when they aggregate: films that live across bubble-walls, strings of chemotrophs that pivot their metabolisms around iron and nitrogen strokes, particulate webs of exuded protein that trap dust. They inherit their carbon from the atmosphere and the oceans downstream; they inherit their order from the unchanging beat of water and cold. They shine under a light, greenish if you look for it, but that’s incidental. Nobody here turns the sun into sugars as a life’s work.

The beasts are not loud—how could they be, heard past the belt?—but they are present. Between curtains of water, in the lower-momentum eddies where sheer meets sheer, you see bodies: torpedoes of cartilage that flex with the grain, a thin veil of membrane between finned ribs that acts like a sail to hold them exactly where the pressure is kind to them. There are rings of bell-creatures the size of barrels, loaded with granules of magnetite, their musculature set to thrum at a frequency you do not hear so much as feel—a pressure bloom against your chest—as they communicate by leaning on the water’s constancy.

Further in, where the belt fattens into a real undersea that just happens to be vertical, are larger forms that live slow. Some ride the band for months, continuously falling in place while their feathery mouths rake the drift for microfilm clots. Others rest with their flanks braced against anchored ridges of artificially roughened steel—an accommodation built into the turbine mouths specifically for them, because nothing else sticks, and because the engineers learned that protein was cheaper than lawsuits. Where predators exist, they are long in the body and narrow at the jaw, more blade than mouth, their skin armored with overlapping scales that jingle faintly against steel and stone when they brush what is solid. They are not numerous, but each is a stripe of attention through the water, and curiously, they circle the same patches as if orbiting a law.

This is a world of rules that help it hold still under action:

- The fall sets its own borders. When wind tries to tug it out of place, the belt answers with a boundary wave that eats the tug and returns it as spray. The roar is its control system, steady as a tuning fork.
- Anything static in the belt erodes to a knife’s edge and stops there. The water takes what finds roundness and leaves what cuts flow. That’s why the rocks are so sharp. That’s why the machines are designed as geometries of non-compromise, with faces that refuse to grow rounded lips, with corners that keep their corners.
- Heat is argument. Introduce too much and it sublimates away as quickly as you supply it, splitting into vapor, rejoicing at expansion, frosted again a meter out. The only warmth that stays is warmth sunk deep into mass or into reaction. That is what the factories do: store work as altered things rather than hot ones.
- Sound has sovereignty. A whistle, a shout, the beat of a wing—if there had been wings—get ground up at the belt’s edge into its single note, which is a chord, which is in truth a library of notes, but so wide it’s indistinguishable from white. Any communication that depends on delicate sonic difference fails in the open. The bell-creatures cheat by speaking in pressure you feel in bone. People cheat by light, or by running cables in pipe along the rock, or by writing large enough to survive spray.

Light flares bright on clear days when the sun hits at an angle that makes the belt a forest of prisms. Rainbows skew impossibly near, an artifact of distance and scale; you can see the structure of a bow’s red band step down as it crosses layers of streaming. On overcast days the belt is simply slate, a granular curtain. Vision is good as long as you accept that everything in the belt has more edges than you can think at once.

Near-void is not an insult here. It is a trait. Step back from the turbine throats and away from the anchored needles of rock and you are in solitary space. The next platform is days of traverse along the cliff. The next creature a minute’s waiting and a hundred meters of water ahead. The emptiness has heft. It is the dominant material. The abyss is not below so much as between.

If there is history, it lies ground into the piece of the world that could be ground: inscriptions on gauge-rulers, a patch on a patch on a patch where a turbine failed twenty-seven winters ago and the metal remembers it. The Fallbelt does not seem to care for narrative. It keeps conditions stable so relentlessly that time is measured in scuffs. Things arrive. Things erode toward a common shape. The sound makes a frame and everything fits into it.

In this steady quality the fauna prosper by being exactly as many as the belt can bear. There are days when you see none of them. There are days when you count dozens clustered wherever industrial discharge makes mineral a little richer, heat a little softer, and the floc of microbes thicker. No plant colonizes these eddies. A quilt of mucilage climbs the rough-faced rungs and then sloughs away, over and over, even at constant conditions, because that’s what the belt does with anything: allows it to puddle and then insists it become flow again.

At night, if you can use the term under a sun that is often behind mist and optic, the factories' lights stitch little diamonds into the world. You watch them from a freeze-polished alcove, counting minutes by the regular dimming and bloom as clouds pass. The roar nestles closer. Frost grows hairlike from your instrument case in the span of your absent thought. When, at last, you turn to go along the maintenance path carved sharp as a justification into the mountain, you pass a sign that reads, in paint renewed so often that the steel beneath it never rusted: Mind the edge. As though there were anywhere else to mind.

Everything here leans toward an idea: that you can be inside water without being in any of the places people usually mean by that. That you can be high and yet submerged. That you can hear only one thing and still know where you are by it. That the world, at planetary scale, can pick one behavior and hold it.

Beyond the mountain’s shoulder, the belt continues, past the reach of any local concern, circumnavigating. It is not a wall. It is a habit the planet refuses to break. It is the loudest thing and also the most patient. It is so large that small weather tucks against it like sleeping animals tuck against a warm flank, and so cold that they never really warm. It is clear enough that it offers you a view into itself, as if to say: here are the rules. Play by them, and you can live in a place that does not change, except the way stone changes—into a sharper version of itself.
