The plateau gathers the sky and squeezes it. From the travelling lowlands it looks like any long, pale escarpment on the horizon, but up on the table itself the air is close with a fine, perpetual damp, as if the stone is an old sponge the world forgot to wring out. Even on bright days a sheen lies over everything. A hand dragged across a wall comes back beaded. Footprints fill as you watch.

It is high—already above the timberline of any ordinary range—and cool in that high-oxygen way that wakes the skin and stiffens it. The sun is untroubled at this altitude, yet the rock leaks. Clouds passing at arm’s length leave their undersides snagged on ridges and balconies; even when the sky is clear, the ever-running breath of the plateau draws moisture from airflows and sets it loose in tears. The stone has learned a patient habit: condense, carry, lay down. Over ages, that habit has turned topography into architecture.

The bedrock here is pale, chalk and honey, limed with centuries of thin films. Water does the slow work of a mason. It beads on seeding mats of algae and moss, takes on mineral, and drips. Every drop that pauses at an edge leaves a rim. Every rim becomes a lip. Lips thicken into shelves. Shelves merge into steps and parapets thick with the fossil signatures of centuries of falls and overflows. From above it reads like a city: not a scatter of huts, not an earthwork, but a network of terraces and runnels and rooms so tightly scaled to one another that walking the place feels like navigating a district more than a hillside.

There are streets. They are narrow gullies where flowstone has paved the floor into satin and the walls billow like draped cloth. They meet and split according to the jointing patterns of the old limestone below, so right angles happen more often than you would think, and occasional long straightways run to the horizon carrying a ribbon of sound. There are plazas—basins shallow as a palm and others broad enough that mist has time to wriggle into fog before crossing them—each with their crown of scums and filigree, a memory of the months when the water overtopped the lip and sought another way. There are houses, if you let your gaze accept hollows, grottoes, drip-sculpted chambers as rooms. Some open on alleys barely a shoulder wide; others lift in stacks three or four levels high, balconies above balconies facing into little canyons where the water descends in bead curtains.

The material of everything is softened and rounded by patient handling. Edges are not sharp; nothing here feels abrupt. Even fractures—the occasional crack where a frost once forced a flake away—get taken back into the program in a season or two, their broken tongues soon coated in the same low gloss as everything else. In places the floor cups under your step with a thin, hard drum—hollow layers, bubbled and lipped like the inside of a shell. In others the stone is full of pores, tufa light enough to hold roots and warm enough under sun that you can smell wet mineral and a green sweetness when the air stills.

Vegetation is present and used with care by the place. Mats of liverwort and moss fur the seeping lines and tell you, if you learn to read it, where water will run at dusk. Lichens scribble black arithmetic across bright walls. Cushion sedge grows like low pillows wherever a basin holds ten millimeters of soil for a summer. Dwarf willow and prostrate juniper, if they appear at all, come wedge-small and ancient, pressed so close to stone they look like stains rather than plants until the wind ruffles them and their resilience shows. Their roots do some work in shaping the city, too, trapping silt, stacking it into little curbs at the edges of pools, catching vanishing rivulets and making them stay long enough to add a thread of lime.

Fauna are rare, and their appearances seem to come with the weather. A dark bird rides an updraft once in a morning—chough or raven, the sound proving it only by a single, far cough. A stoat prints a story across damp silt, loping into a culvert and not showing again for days. Here and there a high-country newt lets go of the shadow of a ledge and moves like poured ink. In one shaded atrium a knot of small, damp amphibians pulse the air with a thin, bell-like chorus only when the mist thickens and the world feels even more wrapped. Midges dance by the thousands in the morning and are gone by noon; a single falcon comes over like the tip of a blade once in a week and you feel the plateau lean toward it and then forget again. You meet more footprints than animals.

The sound is mostly an absence. No trees here to clatter, no machinery in earshot, no herds. Wind grinds softly along corridors and makes a low letterbox hum in some of the tighter places. Drips locate themselves on stone by pitch; a bigger basin might keep three or four notes going at once, patient as a water clock. Once a day, usually at the same time and always with more kinship to tide than to storm, one of the larger terraces somewhere upwind overtops and a wash goes through: a wide, slow voice that hushes again as the city takes it back into channels and sumps. Distant thunder passes sometimes across the level places and never comes to strike.

Despite the hush, the place is busy if you watch long enough. Water glazes a rim here and leaves a brighter sawtooth the next week. A channel migrates a palm-width in a season, undercut by ice filming overnight and thawing at noon. A little pool gains a pale milk along the side where a tuft of green began to change the chemistry last summer. When the plateau breathes out at dusk and the cool climbs into the stone, vapor collects again out of wind that had seemed dry, and it pearls along the undersides of eaves, drawing lines of silver punctuation across the deepening honey. A rooflet somewhere collapses with a crisp, unimportant pop; a new lintel begins the years it will take to grow.

Everything is dense not because everything is crowded, but because everything touches. Air meets stone meets water meets mineral film meets life, in layer after layer. You can stand on a sill and put one hand to a wall slick with a trickle, and the other to rock rough with a dry lichened stripe, and your forehead to a lip above while the damp of your breath trails along an edge and leaves a darker gloss. Overhead, a second level’s ceiling hangs just low enough you feel watched by it; beneath your step, a sump breathes up coolth you sense through the thinness of your boot sole. There are hardly any empty distances: the horizon is so far away you forget it between chambers, and then one terrace opens and the city thins to a ledge looking over ledges and the impression of vastness returns like a lungful of cold.

The rules here are old and simple. Water will not fall freely if it can walk along a surface. The shortest path is often the one with the most little agreements to be made at the interface of droplet and stone. Growth is a ledger kept in microns and centuries. Geology provided joints like the faint dotted lines on a piece of paper, suggesting squares and rectangles to the work of dissolution; now the finished rooms and corridors feel right-angled enough that a mind can map them. The plateau keeps its own calendar, not of months but of patterns in the day where the breath goes one way then the other. The place, if you live inside it for a season, can be navigated by these: by knowing that the third whisper of the afternoon wind will coax fog through a particular arcade; by counting the seconds between two drips on a sill to guess how full the basin above has become. Addresses here are not numbers but relationships between flows.

It is easy to think no one ever meant for it to be walked, and yet it gathers a person in. Terraces often present themselves as stairs, their risers low enough to take without thought. Lips make rails where they are needed. Balconies look across at one another at a human scale, a few paces apart, and carry conversation only when someone is close enough to murmur. Any tools that ever served this place are biological: a palm spreading moss to pin a trickle, a shoulder leaning into a slab to uncurl it from ice, bees weaving a hive tight into the lee of a weepy wall, swifts cementing a bowl-shaped nest under a protected overhang with threads of plant down and spit. The hive’s gold is not loud. The swifts are not many.

From far above—a bird’s height or the rare cliff edge where the fractures stack in a sudden clean line—the city stretches to the limits of air, an even tone of pale mineral only faintly tinted by its thin life. The whole table is a continent, really, with its own weather riding close over it, spinning small fogs and quiet rains out of the passage of the big winds. It is old enough that all the lively damage of birth and uplift has passed; what is left is maintenance, patient adjustment, the long interplay between sky and stone. You can leave it for a year and return to find your corner subtly improved, a lip truer, a sill widened to the exact curve of your palm—the work of a thousand small meetings.

At night the light goes clean. Stars crowd closer at this elevation, sharp, their spill stopped where it hits fog that has gathered into horizontal rivers winding down the biggest alleys. The city shines in places with its own faint reflectance; a gullied wall will hold enough chill to condense a dew that throws back a hint of whatever moon there is. The lights are not many and do not move. You listen. You hear a drip. You measure an interval. You learn the place in that way: not by conquering it all with a gaze—no one ever does—but by settling into one saturated knot and letting its habits, its long-tempered balances, and its generosity of edges teach you what the whole is like.
