The light here is unsparing and everywhere. It lies on the world like a hard white sheet pulled tight, bright enough that edges ring with it, bright enough that you scowl by reflex even with your eyes closed. Heat presses up from the ground as much as it falls from above; breath comes wet, with the taste of lime and damp straw. From knee height down, everything sweats.

There is no earth to stand on—only work. Floors are a quilt of handmade plates: pale fired clay, chalky lime, salt-white terrazzo studded with shells ground flat. Each tile shows the press of a hand, the tooth of a rough paddle, finger-width gutters drawn through their surfaces as if someone combed the wet clay the length of a valley. Water moves in those hairline channels, not fast, never in a rush—just persistent beads that crawl toward one another, merge, find the next slope, and begin again. Between the plated floors rise low walls of porous brick, a kind of tufa made by seeding lime into old reed molds. The walls weep. Not just at dawn: always. Their faces are bearded with tears that gather along sharp edges and fall in slow, regular ticks into catch-basins cut to the exact footprint of the drops.

The land—if you can call a made plane “land”—is almost level, marked by long causeways that keep you a hand’s breadth above the pans and pools on either side. The causeways are narrow and straight, pegged timber wearing a skin of sanded plaster. They cross and cross again at right angles, a field of lines that stretch out until they stiffen and blur in the heat. No hills break the view. A low ring of something like a levee sits at the horizon in every direction, white like all else, defining the edge of this made plain. Beyond it, the light swells and goes blank. You suspect a sea, or a mirror, or both.

Everything green grows out of urns, baskets, troughs—never from the ground itself. Planters are hung from frames with knotted cord, or set in square holes cut neatly into the tiled floor, their rims flush with the surrounding glaze. In each vessel the soil is a bright black loaf, as if baked, threaded with shredded bark and fishbone and bits of char. The plants are lush and exacting. Vines sit heavy on lattices of willow strips and braided reed. Plantain and taro crowd broad shallow trays where the water stays four fingers deep, their stems lacquered with a continual skin of dew. Papyrus surges out of long sluices like green hair from a comb. In the shade of these stands, ferns and fattish mint throw their cool up against your ankles, and the undersides of leaves are furred with condensed sky.

Between the islands of growth there is distance, an emptiness that is not vacant so much as aired-out. Each cluster of trellis and trough is set off from the next by courts of blank white tile, fifteen paces here, fifty there, long runs of nothing but glare and the low whisper of dripping water. However much life seethes in its containers, there is always room around it. The emptiness keeps the heat moving and gives room for creatures to come and go without entanglement. Or perhaps the spacing is something else: a ratio that the place keeps to remain itself, distances measured with a cord known only to the builders.

The built extends upward, too. Tall frames of peeled poles—spliced, mortised, held with wedges and rawhide—stand in a wide grid across the plain. From them hang nets and veils: long curtains of fiber roped with shells and thorn, square sails of woven grass stiffened with silt, white cloth in stitched panels, their seams like bright scars. All of these catch the breath of the air and make it let go of its water. The veils darken and drip along their lower hems. Ropes pull those drips into channels in the timbers and down through the frames to jars set carefully below, where they tinkle and slide, tinkle and slide, and never rise or fall more than a finger in a day. High up there are wind-baffles and taut cords strung with smudged mirrors. They flash, yes, but they also take the white glare and throw it back onto the veils, heating them until the air within and without is forced to argue. The place drinks the argument.

No machine works here. There is no wheel whose axle bites the shoulders, no pump to chatter. Water goes where it is entreated to go, by wick and throat and pebble-lined fall. Gates are slabs that tilt, levers that you can lean on with your hip to ease a current through a bed of young leaves. Ladders take water up, not by rungs but by capillary and weave: cords of coir twisted and split along their length to comb a film of moisture upward to feed the trays hung beneath the eaves of the nets. Amphora towel off the air. Joints are pegged. Knots hold weight that a nail could not. Everything is made by hand, with hands in mind, and because of that the scale of even the vast seems domestic. From a distance the frames are a town’s skeleton; at your side, a knot the size of your thumb keeps a bucket steady.

Despite water everywhere, there is no slime on a step, no rot fattening in a corner. The place is too young for that, or too loyal to its angles. Edges are knife-clean, radii crisp where a craftsman scraped a curve with the inside of a cow horn. In the trays, silt has not built; when a bit of leaf decays, it becomes stripe and speck in the exact weave of the basket it fell into, darkening the pattern rather than making paste. Salt grows under the drip-lines like frost newly named: thin scales that regularly shed themselves as if bored with their own forms, revealing fresh planes of growth too clean for dust to stick. The channels cut through the clay floors are shallow as the line of a fingertip and sharp as if cut this morning. They do not feather at the edges. Even where water has run a hundred thousand pooled inches, it has not smoothed the world into a memory of itself. Things here keep their first intent.

And animals, many of them, make their way along that intent. The air is scribbled with dragonflies, their glass wings clicking gently against one another when they pass too close. Geckos sleep on the hot sides of jars, lids the color of pale bread, eyes like gold seeds. Toads the size of saucers settle into the damp smudges beneath the weeping walls, heads pointed toward the drip as if listening. A heron stands on a post and does not move for an afternoon; it does not need to. Eel-like fish travel the wooden flumes that run from jar to jar, making the same slow angle around the same knot over and over as if each knot were a bend in a river they had known since hatching. Snails with shells bright as a limewash brush the seams between tiles and leave wet signatures that last three minutes and end in the exact place where they began.

From time to time something larger steps through: a water-ox with its ribs traced in light, bells of fired clay on its neck that do not ring; a fox with black paws that slaloms through the courts of glare; a line of long-legged birds that could be storks or be the idea of storks, arranged at precise distances one from the next as if taught geometry. They do not cry. The only voice of any of them is breath, soft and regular, and an occasional single plop when a frog misjudges a leap and drops into a tray.

Sound does not seem to carry far. The bright world eats it. The frames are wrapped in cords and reeds that break echoes into threads and rub them away. You can hear the drop of water three tiles away, but if you clap, your own hands sound muffled, as if under a sleeping child. If someone speaks at a distance, the sounds come filtered through leaves and wire into a cipher of s, f, th. When the cloth veils shudder, they do so with the voice of a book-pages turned far down a hall. The quiet is not empty; it is built and tended and, as much as the frames and tiles, part of the work.

Light does not move. Shadows fall with military accuracy from north to south (or from some decision of the builders), defining long stripes where nothing ever sees direct sun and long stripes where nothing ever sees shade except what leaves and veils grant. Noon is perpetual and does not infer one o’clock. If you look for the sun you do not find a disc, only the pressure of brilliance in the firmament of woven cloth and stretched sail, a clavicle of light resting on the world. The heat has settled into all of this and is absorbed and given back in honest amounts. The air itself does not shiver. Sweat does not cool by wind; it cools because the nets insist on it.

And there are rules, obvious as the grooves in the tiles, if you pause long enough to feel them:

- The course of water is a loop without consequence. It climbs by rope and falls by veil, tastes clay and lime and chalk and stone, and ends where it began with neither gain nor loss, yet all green things drink. It is harvest without river.
- Distance is obedient. Walk straight for an hour and you come, not to an edge, but to a place you recognize and cannot swear you’ve visited. The levee on the horizon is an agreement between the world and your need for an end.
- Edges are truthful. What is cut stays cut. Joints never slur into one another. Time does not rub the corners down; hands would have to.
- Animals do not multiply or die here in your sight. There are always as many of each kind as there need be. You can follow a particular heron for days and discover at the end that you saw six birds taking turns in the exact same stations.
- Tools remember. A paddle left leaning against a wall keeps the shadow of the hand that last used it and will fit the same fingers when they return.

Because of those rules, the place feels at once newborn and ageless. You sense that it was finished this morning—fresh sharps on every corner, no scrapings of heel or hoof in the dust because there is no dust—and also that it has been like this for as long as the white light has burned. People sometimes pass, but they pass in the manner of glossed-over script the scribe has already memorized: a woman with a coil of rope as thick as her arm; a boy carrying a jar that sweats down his belly; three figures walking the causeway with poles and bundles of reed. They do not hurry. Their feet keep time with the drips. They barely speak. When they adjust a veil or tap a wedged joint deeper or sweep away a leaf that fell wrong into a channel, it is with the certainty of a ritual so old that to change it would be to ask the tiles to flow.

You might expect rot, but there is none. You might expect sediment, but the basins are as clean as thought. You might expect a storm to rip this polite architecture apart, but storms, if they occur, occur within the measure of the netting and come to nothing more than a faster run on the ropes. If rain falls—does it?—it is always the rain that the cloth and cord have asked for.

In the far middle—the place that is always middle no matter where you approach from—there is a tower only a little higher than the other frames. It is made of the same timber and pegged in the same pattern, but on its upper half hangs a skin of white clay plates like scales, each drilled with a pinhole. The plates exude a fine mist that turns to pearls on attaching cords, a permanent condensed breath, and the cords feed into a cistern ringed with a bench of lime. The bench is cool. If you sit, someone will put a shallow cup in your hand and it will drink the air for you, and you will wonder how this place, which never changes, grew the exact shape of your thirst before you even knew its measure.

Outside the levee, whatever it is that isn’t here—sea or blankness or a roaring field of identical—does not ask entry. The levee itself is not a barrier but a pronunciation mark. Everything inside has already been uttered.

As afternoon never comes, neither does evening. Leaves do not fold, and flowers that open to brightness stay open forever, fat and mild and never quite spent. In a trough the moss is always new between the papyrus rootlets, as if a scribe had paused after writing the first letter. A snail climbs a basket and is, when you look an hour later, the exact or identical snail, the same wet path bright behind it, unextended or re-laid to the same result. A heron lifts one foot, puts it down in the same place, and the sound is a hollow tap that does not vary.

If you look for wilderness, you will not find it; even the weeds here have been taught. If you look for city, you will not find it; the geometry opens itself and keeps opening, and the frames and troughs never crowd together enough to make alley or court. You are in something like a town that forgot to be many and chose instead to be one long act of care.

Standing in that care, your skin salted by the air’s hand, the brightness testing every nerve behind your eyes, you begin to believe in the promises made by cord and tile: that a thing can be enough if you stop asking it to become. That water can fall and rise and not be bored. That the world can be hand-made and still grow its own green thick as thought. That sharpness can be gentle. That quiet can be the loudest engine, turning everything without a gear.
