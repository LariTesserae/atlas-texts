The basin is low and warm, a shallow dish where the air never quite stills. You’re aware of its breadth before you see it—the sound hits first, like standing at the lip of a living engine. It is not the sound of animals. There are no wingbeats or throat-sounds in this place. The roar is vegetative, hydraulic, botanical: millions of stomata dumping and drawing, leaf-plates rasping against each other, xylem columns cavitating with gunshot pops, tension cracking in living timber like muffled lightning. The whole valley is a resonance chamber made of green.

From the edge, it seems like a city, but there is no masonry. The ground steps down in sharp terraces of root and rhizome, newly thrust up from a plain of soft silt and the occasional strip of damp black loam. There’s a raw, unfinished look to everything at your feet: peeled cambium curling like shavings, pale roots still beading with sap, leaf-edges sharp as fresh-cut paper. What passes for streets are phloem boulevards—broad, flexible ribbons of pressure-stiffened tissue that loop and spiral, slightly springy underfoot, carrying sugars in visible pulses. Where they fork, the forks are clean and sudden, like the angle of a new fracture in glass.

Water lives in pockets, not everywhere at once. Above you, megaleaves tilt and correct and tilt again as the day moves, presenting their slick undersides to the warm air. Every so often a fog banks along their veins as transpired moisture condenses and rolls to the tips. With an almost polite patience, each leaf empties itself in a measured sheet into cisterns waiting below—broad rosettes cupped like amphorae, their rims armored against spilling. Between those catchments, braided rivulets run through bright-edged canyons of buttress root, cold to the ankle and tannin-bitter. After a sudden rain, the whole place rings with water—leafdrum thunder, guttering vines gurgling like panpipes, seed pods clattering—but twenty minutes later it has retreated into the belly of the place, hidden in reservoirs of spongy tissue and in the long columns of its vascular highways.

There is nothing with a face here, nothing that flits from branch to branch, and the absence is loud once you notice it. Pollination is done by wind and by music. The city generates both. Towering inflorescences stand like organs along the major flows, each with chambers and slots that sing when gusts barrel through, tuned to call and answer each other. Their drones and overtones shape growth. Where bass notes hum deep, the architecture is heavy-lobed and hunkered; where high voices keen, the tendrils are hair-fine and twitchy, netted curtains knitting and unknitting with a nerve-like speed. New leaves orient to nodal lines on the standing wave-field forming in the valley’s bowl, and so what should be a chaotic riot of leaf and vine coheres into districts with distinct acoustics and habits, all without a single tool.

Moving inward, the density rises to claustrophobia. It’s not just that everything is alive—it is everything. You brush a wall and it flexes with the slow politeness of sap under pressure. Ground and building are the same organism in long places. Buttresses fold themselves into galleries; the galleries are porous, bored through by dull light. Some corridors squeeze shut behind you with a soft sigh of closing stomata and then gently swell open again, the whole place breathing. Others accelerate you with cilia-like rows of tiny plant scales catching at your soles, pulling you along with the insistence of a river. Keep to the old wood if you do not want to be carried: it is darker, lignified, veined with mineral crust where evaporated sap has left salts, scars from seasons of flow and drought.

The city has neighborhoods in the way a coral has: differences of form and rhythm rather than line and law.

- The Well-Districts are deep, ribbed funnels of living wood where humidity is highest. Dew rains upward here as much as down—pulled from the air and handed leaf-to-leaf along capillary lips until it vanishes into the dark lick of root. The noise at the low point is a continuous animal-less surf, the combined hiss of a billion minute mouths.

- The Stackfields rise where phloem flows fastest. They’re sheets upon sheets of leaves fused into laminar towers, their edges aligned to the pitch that runs through that space. You can feel the chord under your bones. The air here tastes of resin and cut grass and green pepper; touch anything too long and your palms go tinctured with terpenes.

- Along the Basin Arches—the only agoraphic gesture this place allows—great lianas span open chasms, knotted and retensioned daily by nothing visible, holding arcs of sky. The light pools here, strong and ordinary, and your mind almost relaxes until a trellis unfurls across that brightness in seconds, throwing shade like a thought.

At the scale of a hand, things are always new. Petioles snap into being, the green of them so pale it’s almost white, and then deepen to bottle-glass in the span of a conversation. At the scale of a district, change is stormlike. You can go to sleep (if you can, with that noise) in a cloister of lobed leaves and wake to find yourself under a bristling thatch of spines and bells because a gust two valleys away altered the tone and so the plan here took up a sharper edge. If the weather shifts, whole avenues vanish into a joyous, unrepentant growth spasm and reappear hours later reshuffled, with the confidence of a dealer cutting a deck.

There is, famously, the Slippage. Call it reality, call it growth habit: in some places the city remembers forms so intensely they reoccur as structure. A helical stair you just left continues under your feet three turnings later, though you could swear you never circled back. The echo of a leaf you brushed earlier is embossed into the wall ahead, already with veins, already toughening. Sometimes light behaves as if it were a fluid moving within plant rather than air—there is brightness under a mat of leaves where no beam should reach, and where that brightness stands longest, a paleness grows, a leaf that seems to thin the world behind it. Stand in a tone-field long enough and your shadow roots; it will persist a ghostly beat longer than your body, a softer, second you. Don’t tug it. The plants will tug back, kindly but firm.

Everyone knows the rules, or learns them quickly. They are not laws so much as courtesies observed with living things that do not negotiate.

- Walk when the valley is full of breath. Midday is safest, when the heat humbles the place and sap flow settles. At dawn and dusk the city performs; it is not paying attention to your path.

- Do not cross lines of newborn tissue—the ones that look mica-pale, wet as tongues. They are bridges carried to tension by a will that is not yours. If you must, step where something has already stepped; if no one has, wait until the leaf darkens and decides what it is.

- Sound is both map and weather. Listen for courts of pure tone: those are stable. White roar means turbulence; everything near you is about to be other.

- Never trust straight distances. They are temporary conveniences. Trust rhythms: the regular thrum of a phloem trunk, the periodic rasp of a leaf-louver opening, the granite steadiness of the low lignin-bass that belongs to things that will not move.

- When the plants ask (and they do, simple as closing a door), allow yourself to be delayed. Resist and you will be held more gently but more firmly, wrapped in tendrils soft as light, until the reason—some rushing growth, some rainfall pulled down the throat of a district—passes.

The ecology is absolute. Everything that grows is growing for plant reasons. There are no grazers; no one prunes this city but itself. Seeds do not expect to be carried on fur or feather. They detonate, or sift, or slide along damp channels secreted for them. Some build themselves into the places their parents vacate; self-replacement is a choreography of untethered grafts and husked-out spaces. Pollen is a weather forecast, a calendar page releasing: a sky suddenly sown with yellow that drifts and later hardens into a mustard film on water skins. There are motile forms, but plant-motile: thickset corms that plow slowly through the loam seeking friendlier chemistry, mats that go raft-stiff in sudden rain and set off down a rattling gut of channel and, hours later, sponge back into the wall of some new district.

Materials are everything you’d list under “green” and also things green has invented here. You can tell the age of a beam by taste; newwork is sweet and brassy with sugars, oldwork is salted with evapored minerals, and there are veins of something like glass where silica has been coaxed through phloem until it lays down in a thin, shining sheet you can see your own heat shimmering in. The city sometimes turns its scars into ornament: a split in a buttress rimed with crystallized sap like frost-work, hard and amber. It is tempting to pocket it; you cannot. The place will take it back, patiently, by growing around your hand and asking you to be sensible.

The sky is the only unchanged material, and even ituates. The light is ordinary sun but the city uses it as if it were a tool—shredding it with oscillating leaves into strobe, pooling it in cup-plazas until the brightness itself seems thick enough to spill. On the outer rims, the horizon is a ribbon of marsh and water-shine, where the big bodies of the place lower their voices and flatten into reed and mud-catcher, making pockets of fresh water cradled in living hands. Step onto those marsh edges and the sound dims for a blessed minute; it is only the trickle of rills and the fizz of tiny bubbles rising from the breath of root. Step back into the bowl and the organ starts up again, overwhelming as surf.

Erosion here is new-formed and altogether botanical. There are landslides of leaf, avalanches of petal. A tower sheds its outer skin in one long peel with a noise like canvas tearing and exposes a tender interior already hardening. Where water has cut, it has done so recently: bright cross-sections of living structure like diagrams, nothing darkened yet, every vessel clean. After storms—the real sky-kind or the sonic kind that run on pressure—debris fields lace the low ground: snapped tendrils, dehisced pods, the browned-out net of a canopy that chose to die cleanly rather than tangle. Within a day, sprouts purl through it all, and nothing looks raw for long.

For all of this constant tumult, there is a sense of organization deep under the skin. The city is not a mess. It is tuned. It is in tune with itself across distances that would baffle a topo map. What is “built” here is a habit of coherence. Bridges grow where crossings are needed because the pressure signals demand closure. Shelters occur where the bass drops and the green decides to be thick. “District” is just the sound a form holds. If you stand on a high arched liana and look across the basin—as much of it as you can see before leaf and dust and geometry misinform your retina—you can feel, in the soles of your feet, the way the whole thing thinks. It speaks only in waves and fibers. There is no tool other than growth. It is enough.

And when wind comes off the plain, the valley wakes to full voice. For a beating minute, it is deafening to the edge of danger, the air serrated by whistles, tongue-clicks of pods, the basso continuo of sap under strain. The plant city leans into that storm and makes it more. Leaves torque until the sky becomes striated sound. The streets thrum like taut strings. The noise holds you still. Then, by some ungraspable consensus, the tone cools, carries downward into a calmer chord, and the forms settle into their adjusted selves: different, sharper, newly made. In the sudden comparative quiet, every drop left in the laminae is audible, and you could convince yourself the place has an afterimage of silence. Then the daily engines wind again: breath in, breath out, a city built of plant and pressure, unable to be still and uninterested in being anything else.
