It is warm and low here, a shallow basin cupped by taller lands that never quite show themselves—just a slight upturn at all horizons, a reminder that you have walked down into something. The air is thick with the idea of moisture rather than moisture itself. Every surface sweats; droplets glass the undersides of leaves, gather in the axils of fronds, pearl along hairs. When a breeze threads a gap, the beads ask permission and slide. Sometimes the whole place decides to rain for a few minutes—no cloud, just a shared release as the plants push vapor from their pores and knock it loose with a single shudder. Then it is dry again, or at least not wet, the way a tongue is not wet though it is water.

Everything that is not sky is plant. There is no ground as in soil; underfoot is a flexing lattice of living roots, bark smoothed by years of feet and pads and clawtips and the slow polish of migrating spores. Walls, if you can call them that, are conjoined trunks whose fused cambiums spiral like braided muscles. Roofs are leaves widened into vaults, green ribs fanning overhead to admit shafts of normal daylight. It’s a city, but not a ruin and not a garden—an organized place with avenues and quieted corners and repeated forms. The avenues are buttress-walks, corridor-stems, hollow petioles broad enough to carry entire herds of something from one chamber to the next. The quieted corners are galls become rooms, seed-chambers that never opened, resin-clad hollows with one glossy slit like an eye. The repeated forms are whorls: spiraled stairs grown by a vine that learned to measure its turn; nested arches where branches learned to exchange weight minute by minute.

The whole structure has found its shape and settled into it. Moss grows in the same depressions year after year because the depressions are always there. Fluted columns—root-buttresses turned architectural—bear the permanent ripples of old wet seasons and old silences. Exposed surfaces take on the lean look of long work; you can see where the friction of years of passing hides has burnished the sapwood, where wind-driven grit has carved a polish into everything else. There is an ease to the place: forms that have been in conversation with the climate so long they no longer argue. You can pass a carved-looking threshold and know that no hand carved it, but the continuous rubbing of leaf against leaf, spur against bark, day against day.

And then there is the sound.

You feel the voice of this basin in your ribs before you understand its words. The place is impossibly loud, enlivened by a system of living pipes and drums that take every step, every lash of air, every insect wing and multiply it into thunder. Openings in trunks meet at right angles to become colossal flutes. Leaves arranged in opposite pairs slap, slap, slap a humid clap against themselves in a wind barely equal to a sigh. Somewhere two membranes the size of sails—laminae drawn drum-tight over hollow boles—are being beaten by no hand at all, just the collapse of a hot updraft and the sudden rush of its cooler ghost. The metropolis hums and hammers and rattles. Even the stillnesses are packed tight, hissing like a hot stone dropped into sap.

Sound is not only heard here; it is a substance that presses. When a bass tone swells, the air seems to thicken to a syrup and you wade. High trills lift you as if some part of you is buoyant in them. Loudness has weathered the place, too: decades of resonance have smoothed internal corners to horns, lipped apertures into accurate reeds. There are rooms where you cannot hear your own voice because the chamber has chosen a note and eats everything else—doors of silence in a city of noise. Step into one of these and the deafness has weight, a pressure that nervously mimics absence. Plants have grown absorbers into these nodes: dangling mats of velvet leaves, pale and hair-ruffed, that feed on quiet like shade on shade.

Water lives in recesses. It hides in little organs the plants have made for keeping it: cups at the bases of leaves that refill with nothing but the neighbor’s breath; long, narrow sumps running within the roots, where tannin-dark fluid sips along like tea through a straw; fist-sized pools where bracts overlap and hold a day’s fog, a clutch of mosquito larvae doing their tight circles. Thin meandering channels run between the root-ridges—old river memories in miniature—briefly catching the run-off of a communal drizzle before it goes to ground or to green. The city is damp, not because rain owns it, but because everything that lives here is its own weather.

The plan of the place is legible once your eyes adjust to growth's grammar. There is a ring of towering cambium, a circle of thickened growth that reads like a wall—but there are no walls, only laced supports tuned to catch wind at a certain speed and turn it into a tone that warns of predators. From that ring, radial arbors run inward, broad arcades of wood nettled with nodules that can tighten or relax to allow more or less traffic. Between the arcs are open volumes as tall as churches, nothing in them but air and light and the orchestrated storm. Vastness inside the tightness: in these hollows a hawk can fly for minutes without turning, and a glider-lizard can set her membranes and sail on sighs. The architecture organizes movement without requiring furniture. If there were benches here, they would have been photosynthesizing all along.

The community is mostly plants, but plants that have annexed the idea of a city. They lay down avenue after avenue not in asphalt but in root; they lift terraces not in stone but in layered laminate of living tissue. They have grown ladder-spurs for climbing, constrictors in the shape of handrails that soften when pressure is applied, repeating the idea of “grip” in green. They have tuned the chambers—this part matters—so that certain frequencies bounce three times and blur, certain notes travel an exact distance and stop. The place is a score etched in lignin.

Animals have learned it too far to call it learning anymore. There is nothing rare about fauna here, only the distances they have chosen to keep. Antlered grazers with ear-sails the size of fans stand in the hollows, tilted toward the wind-pipes, reading the next hour from timbre. Beetles with sonorous abdomens knock messages down corridors and get answers back as changes in temperature. Bands of small bright things—fur like leaves, ears like petals—flow along buttresses, catching the shower of insects that tumble from beating leaves when the plant decides to cull the air. Every plant-surface has a lizard or a moth pretending to be it, and sometimes the plants pretend to be the moths in return, unfurling a petal that looks like a wing and vibrating it just wrong enough to attract a mate who is here for a different kind of union.

Predators hunt with ears. A catlike thing waits not in shadow but in the standing waves near a pillar; when the right note slams across its whiskers it lunges through what looks like nothing and arrives at a shock of wing. A pack of bark-wolves run in coordinated silence—a held breath the size of a family—dodging the city’s alarms to drive down a corridor at the single time it takes the architecture to exhale. A long-bodied serpent-kite trails a fin along a leaf-edge to pluck a string the size of a limb, stunning the frogs that breed in the axils.

You do not see tools. There are no carved posts, no kiln-shards. The art here is built by growth, not made. Infrastructure is the arrangement of fluids more than the ringing of bells. Saplines—capillary highways inside buttresses—carry starch from the sun-bright canopy to the shadows below. Pollen lifts rise and fall through gaps as the plants tense and relax yokes, sending gold in well-timed balloons through layers of city without ever knowing what a pulley is. Pruning happens, but it is performed by termites that chew accents into a beam to change its note, by monkeys that nip tendrils because the taste of iron comes with a harmonic they dislike. Nothing is dead here that has not chosen to be furniture. When a main trunk fails, it fails into use, cracking into an arch that bridges two hollows, exposing a honey-colored membrane that swells with each pulse of the chorus.

Light is ordinary and perfect: no color cast, no trick. It slants in clear, mapping the ribs of the vaults onto the airy emptiness like the lattice shadow of a basket on a wall. Butter-dusted at dawn, hard and exact at noon, tawny at evening as if sap had tinted it. The plants choreograph it; leaf-doors swing to spill brightness into cisterns when the chorus cools them too much, then close again to keep a hummingbird-troubled patch of flowers in temperate twilight. Nights are no quieter, but they are purpler.

The rules of the place are not the rules you brought with you. Here, sound has weight. In the heavy octave around the heart’s pace, motion becomes work; the city uses this to slow predators in certain halls and accelerate pollinators in others. Echo is long-lived: a shouted note can be still bouncing faintly an hour later, and sometimes yesterday’s weather can be heard pacing the perimeter after sunrise, a ghost-wind of tone that never touched you. Silence can be stored and poured. Plants extrude silence in lengths of velvet and hang it where noise would scare a brood; break one by mistake and the hush runs out like cool water and the chamber screams again. Gravity feels like a suggestion tuned by rhythm; where the bass is thick you are more solid, where treble flickers your steps turn springy. Wet and dry do not alternate with the sky here so much as with song; drizzle is a chord progression and drought is a key.

What you can rely on: the basin is warm, and it stays that way; even the rain that suddenly happens is body-temperature and brief. The edifice is old enough to be easy with itself. It will not trap you unless you decide to stand where a note crosses and be held there by a promise of bone-deep music. Between presences—between the hung gardens, the bole-rooms, the perches—there is honest emptiness, hollow as a breath taken too far. You can walk for long minutes with only resonance for company, nothing to touch but air, nothing to see but arc and aperture and shafts of daylight stippling a cloud of gnats that look like flung seeds.

And yet you are never alone. You feel the press of a million lives, each busy with their own law—the slug that drinks condensed fog from a fern’s throat and leaves a trail like a smeared note; the hummingbirds that duel in refrains, beak to beak like tuning forks; the hyphae that web from heartwood to heartwood and carry somebody else’s news about a storm you could mistake for a drumroll. Things stalk and flee and breed in time. The city itself eats and breathes. At the edge of the ring, a slow tide of fern-fronds lifts and falls, lifting dew and letting it go. In the central hollow, every so often, the leaves tense and a sigh becomes a downpour for forty heartbeats; in that window alone, the frogs sing like coins shaken in a cup, so loud the deer half-step mid-gait and remember where they meant to be.

Stay long enough and you may learn to fetch water by walking through a certain bar of noise, to cool yourself by sitting in the negative of a tone, to follow a path that only exists when the third harmonic blooms. You will find places where the sound is bearable because the plants have made it that way for their infants, and you will find alleys where even the birds fold themselves and wait until the big, slow drums pass. You will come to understand that the city is not new and will not end; it is a way of arranging growth to keep itself alive, using the only forces available—sunlight, breath, appetite, and the ability to hum at the shape of its own rooms.
