From a distance the land looks clean and new, as if it were poured yesterday and left to set. A pale, low plain runs to every horizon, its skin a mosaic of cooling plates and hairline seams. There is no sheen of river, no hollows dark with ponds. When the light catches, it is on glassy facets in the ground or on the quiet skins of living things. Warmth rides the air, not oppressive, just steady, the kind of heat that loosens resins and makes scents bloom.

Up close the plain is not bare at all. Every slab and seam is furred with growth that is lush in density but not in the way of wet worlds. There are no leaves that drip; there is no damp. The vegetation wears a dryness that is almost velvet: mats of fine filaments the color of old brass; armored fronds with a dull-olive glow; low, tensed swells like cushions of packed needles. They lap across the mineral like tide lines frozen in place, stopping at fresh cracks, swallowing older ones. Where the ground’s raw polygons still hold their sharp edges, the plants anchor to them with resinous collars that spread into lattices and honeycombs. They gleam when you look just right, more amber than green.

In places the growth rises, gaining volume and intent, and here the landscape becomes unmistakably built. Not by tools. No marks of chisels or scaffolds. The cities here are grown by the colony-trees—organisms that are not trees at all but living coral and fungus and termite hill blended into one logic. They stand in clustered towers and buttressed walls, every surface textured for grip, every cavity shaped for air. They pull minerals up from the seam-cracks, pull light down through their chimneys, exude an architecture of glossy ribs and matte membranes. Galleries spiral within, carrying slow rivers of oil-sap that never run in the open, that thread from district to district inside sealed conduits. Where a thousand conduits meet is a junction, a node dense with exchange. Where buttresses from neighboring towers reach and fuse, an archway grows, and then a canopy, until whole kilometers knit together into continuous halls. Streets are not for feet so much as for breath and flow: long transects where the colony regulates its own wind.

It is quiet. The thick air and the giving surfaces take sound and hold it. What little escapes is close to the skin of the place: a faint creak where a beam cools and shrinks against the morning’s warmth; a velour rasp as grazers comb the filaments; the subtle, rhythmic valve-sounds of vents opening somewhere deep within a tower as it exhales its chemical weather. When it really stirs, it does so under the threshold of shout. The colony’s timed exhalations draw a steady breeze through the halls, a shallow suction that you feel on the hairs of your fingers more than you hear.

The rule here—the world’s first law—seems to be: no water. If any falls from the sky (and it does not, not here), it would be poison. The sap of the colony is thick with oils that do not know water, that would pearl away from it; the soils are more dust and salt than anything one would call earth. Life drinks from other wells. The mats gather vapor of heavier organics from the daily breath of the city, wicking it through combs and sponges that flash with pigments under the normal sun. Root-analogues plug straight into the cooling plain and leach metals where the mineral is still glassy and unopened. Nights are for condensation of invisible things: auroras of terpenes that bead inside catchment folds like dew that no human mouth could taste.

Because there is no water to grind or round, the geology is expressionless in a grand, newborn way. You see basal shrinkage polygons preserved like script across continents. You see vents where gases once lifted the crust and then stilled; now the colony cores its towers around these vents, taking their residual heat and turning it into gentle currents. Erosion here is life’s work more than wind’s. Grazers press and polish the edges of paths; scavengers chip and chew the city’s cast-off resins; tiny organisms etch the gloss from minerals to open purchase for the next wave of growth. With each season the living lattice claims the raw stone, not by washing it away but by assimilating it.

If you walk into the city—if it lets you—you feel the density. There is no such thing as vacant space. Cavities are lungs, reservoirs, brood-chambers. Every walkway’s underface has another street for something smaller. Between two trunks is an alley of vines tight as fingers; above the alley a bridge of woven ribs carries a sealed flow of exudate to some distant dark. In the breadth of a single meter are ten economies.

Fauna here are many and made for quiet. Their bones (or the things that stand for bones) are springy. Their steps are broad and soft. Study the sunlit aprons at the base of a tower and you’ll see seed-lappers the size of clenched fists straining aerosols through combed palps, their tongues slick with oil instead of saliva. Watch long enough and you’ll see long-bodied climbers, dust-colored and lateral-eyed, pressing themselves up walls in planes of motion that make sense only if you know the surfaces breathe. Out along the mats a herd of stilt-backed grazers moves like a wrinkle across a carpet, cutting the filaments with nibs that don’t quite close, sampling, never overgrazing. Where the conduits bulge near the surface, predators wait in still coolness, sensing with faces that are all ear, tuned to the footfall of something that knows this path.

Predation here is an exercise in timing around the city’s breath. When the colony’s vents open at late afternoon—regular enough to set your watch by, if you wore a watch—warm outflows call in swarms of winged feeders from the higher canopy. Their wings are membranes stretched over lattices that glisten; they do not beat so much as vibrate to ride the micromotions of air. They sift the exhalations for volatile sugars, for the faint tang of nitrogenous scents that speak: this unit is ready; that one needs pruning; there a mass has fouled and must be cleared. The swarms do the work of gardeners while feeding, trimming embryonic ridges into clean fillets that grow smooth and strong. In their wake come the fast shapes, foldable, narrow—killers with little sound and much patience—dropping out of the intermediate gloom to take a few, just enough to keep them quick and wary.

The light is ordinary daylight, honest and revealing. No tricks of violet suns or dim red dwarfs here. At noon the colony’s amber flanks are a rich, dull gold; the mats are green the way brass is green. On clear days you can follow the on-off gleam of spine and rib to the margin of vision. By evening the towers are silhouettes and their vents are halos. When the wind shifts across a district you can see it only by how the banners of airborne seeds change course; they are barely seeds at all, more like feathers of solidified scent designed to be sniffed and held and carried, flickers of purpose in a moted air.

For all its quiet, the place is not still. The whole world works by cycles that are short enough to watch. You can stand at the edge of a young district and catch it in the act of conquest: a leading edge of thickened mats bulging across the raw plate; tiny glistening tongues testing and tasting the mineral, then committing, secreting a primer of oils that harden into a tack; sprout-like columns punching up into buttresses in days, not months. Behind this front, workers you will never quite see enforce order, clip and cull, patching the inevitable miscalculations of growth. Fires are almost unknown—not for lack of fuel but for lack of a sky rich in oxygen. Storms here are different. A storm is the alignment of many cities’ breaths, a long, continent-scale draw and exhale that rolls unseen but strong enough to pick you up with its promise and set you a little further down, awed and unscathed.

Because water is absent, rust is rare, mold is something else entirely. Decay is not rot but reabsorption: dried scaffolds eaten back into feedstocks by creatures with mouths like pincushions; pale stains on a wall where a failed beam was liquefied and pumped away. Dead things don’t smell like dead things; they smell faintly of almonds and mineral, a suite of signals that attracts dismantlers rather than mourners.

Rules you learn, if you live here:
- Stay to the dark gloss if you want to go quickly; matte is for feeding and will be full of small lives.
- Do not step across a seam that shines with threads; it is a nascent conduit, and it will rise when it rises.
- When the vents change to a lower tone you will not hear but feel, make your way to a place with ribs—the city is about to breathe deep and long.
- If you ever see water—beads glittering in defiance of this world’s dryness—keep away. It means breakdown, and breakdown draws hunger you don’t understand.

Low as it is, this plain has the scale of oceans. If you climb far enough up a tower to a canopy where the resin has whitened with sun and age, you can see the line where your city’s architecture goes abstract and smooths into another’s, and beyond that another, and further still a shimmer in the air where the breath of all of them rolls upward into a dome of its own weather. The surface—raw, embryonic—is still being claimed. The cities spread by taste and scent and gentle push, growing themselves into an urbanity that recognizes no tools and needs none. If there were maps, they would show not streets and alleys but flows and exhales; not zoning but appetite; not boundaries but regions of dialect in a language of oils. And all of it—every tower, every mat, every quiet mouth—touches, presses, fills, until even the silence is crowded.
