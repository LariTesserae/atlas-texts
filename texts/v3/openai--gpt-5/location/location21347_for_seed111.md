A room where the mountain forgot to finish itself. Not a cave, not yet: a vertical cleft, two strides wide in its broadest places, a single long inhale from floor to high, coin-sized sky. The rock here is new in the way only catastrophe makes—faces snapped open, edges keen, grains unrounded—so that every surface sheens with a juvenile clarity. The light that manages to enter is lean and wayward: a milk thumbprint diffused by passing cloud, a pale drip of day that doesn’t reach the pooled water, a momentary cold flare of blue when the ice grinds a vein of quartz and the stone answers with a brief, triboluminescent star.

You stand on a floor that is not exactly a floor but a knot of things caught in pause: a jammed boulder, a slab pinched edge-on, a span of hard snow sagging under a crust of refrozen drip, a shallow bowl scoured into the sidewall where water has worried at a softer seam and then moved on. The mountain is high enough here that you can taste thinness in your teeth and hear the effort of your own breath, but the chamber is damp rather than frozen—cool in a way that keeps the air’s water perched at brink. Moisture arrives in fits, pressed out of passing clouds that snag against the crest above; it naps on the stone as sweat and then, coaxed by sound, shakes loose and gathers itself into long-slow drops.

Sound rules this room. Low, near-infrasonic moans roll down from the slit of sky—wind tasting the mouth of the cleft, raising a tone in it as in a bottle. That tone is not alone. The walls are colonized by life so numerous that geology has to argue with them for possession of the air. They cover everything: every ledge, every burr of fresh-broken rock, every pendant of rime. They make the room feel full, almost crowded this close to the clouds. They are not large—finger-length at most, mostly much smaller—but their sum is density. If you hold out a hand, something will try to alight on it; if you lean a cheek to the wall, the wall will seem to pulse, to chitter.

They are not bats and not birds, though they have learned the air. Think of the shapes that thin atmosphere allows: broad-membraned gliders that carry sail-thin fins edged with stiff cilia to feel pressure changes; many-legged shrimps of the rock that wear their own moisture in films under their plates and move in zipper-quick bursts, en masse like muscle fibers flexing across the stone; blind, pale cloth-moths that do not see but read the world by tasting the trembling of their own long, feathery antennae. There are soft-bodied things, too, slug-scatters with hairlike magnetite whiskers that keep them pressed to the dampest seam; and little foxed beetles whose wings are not for flying so much as for drumming—drum-wings that can be driven against the rock in exact ways to make the kind of vibrations the rest of the room will answer.

Answer how? With rain. The secret here—one of them—is that water does not fall inside this cleft so much as it is shaken free. The base wind’s tone wraps the chamber and hums the film of moisture on every surface. That hum by itself is a tease: it ripples dew beading but will not make it drop. The colony completes the trick. At certain hours, when a cloud has wicked in close and the stone’s skin goes from nearly to almost wet, waves of the drummers start up. Not all at once—they are not a single mind—but in sheets and fans and counter-sheets, one burst catching the tail of the last, each patch of insects and soft-bodied things bringing their own small strikes into sympathy with the room’s larger throat. It becomes loud, louder than seems possible in air this thin: a pelting of clicks and hard winged knocks and cut, hiked shivers, the whole space made into an instrument played with its own bones. The sound is work. Moisture quivers and congeals, and then—achieved—lets go. It falls out of itself and into runs that seek the bowls and the cracks and the waiting mouths.

That is one rule here: you take your water from the wind by making the stone sing.

Sparse plants, if you can call them plants, script the walls in pallid not-quite-colors. There is lichen so thin it is more a frost than a plant, ash-gray rosettes pressed flat to the rock and tasting minerals with their entire bodies. There is moss the size of a thumb, once or twice, in places where the light spends a few breaths longer: a tight nurse-cushion that stays pearled with dew even after the chorus has spent itself. In the weeps, in the threads where the water slides rather than drops, algae make faint green sketches you only notice when an errant flash from shifting ice gives them a kind of ghost-lantern. Nothing timbered grows. Even the lichens read as patient accidents, not masters.

Almost everything alive is animal and most of it is listening to rock. Eyes are vestigial, ocelli sunken like old buttons. Appendages are tuned to other senses: long, jointed feelers that lie along the stone to collect its stories; fringes that cosset droplets and direct them in. You can watch the economy operate. A drip swells. A collar of waiting mouthparts brightens with saliva to slow the last friction. The drop lets go—a silver weight, black inside because there is hardly any light to claim it—and three, five, twelve bodies lean it out of the air before it has fallen the length of your hand. They do not fight. Dozens feed on a single falling second. They must, because none of them gets much; this is a place of little margins.

Predation, where it happens, does not look like attack so much as edit. A thin-bodied crawler that is almost two-dimensional slips between a mass of drinkers, its feeding rail leaving behind a brief, bloodless quiet: whatever it took gone so completely that only the space it unmade is visible. A glider folds like paper, drops soundlessly, and opens again under the rank where the drummers are thickest; it surfs the downward wave of air shaken by the noise and harvests with a lip that is more comb than mouth. Even death is quiet matter for the most part, outshouted by the life that never stops stridulating.

The rock itself lends odd energies. Bands of quartz in the dark stone answer pressure with faint sparks and with hairs-on-skin shocks you can’t tell if you imagined. Some of the insects seem to know this. They ride their forelegs up on quartz seams and drum exactly there, as if the taste of microcurrent makes a sweetness in their cleaners. Small, transient blue twitches stitch the colony where the ice flexes and the veins complain. Nothing here lights itself; these are not lantern-creatures, only beings that accept whatever the mountain gives off and make use of it as far as their bodies’ chemistry will allow.

The city you might imagine from the noise never appears when you look for it. There are no structures, no intentional halls, no nest-architecture. What little is built is made by physics: beads of rime stitched together by long nights of wet and wind, scallops and splays in the raw rock where last meltwater took a fancy to one weakness and not another. The animals know this transient furniture intimately. They assemble on shapes the way people learn corners of a bar—they come to edges where the air slips fastest and line up their plumes into a living lip to catch it. If there is “tech” here, it exists at the scale of a limb-length and a decision. You watch a wedge of soft gliders use a chip of mica fallen from the roof to scrape algae from a seam too narrow for their larger parts to enter: two push the sliver, one rides it; they are done when the taste-flow changes, then they leave it without a backward glance. No one keeps tools. No one has walls.

Because the cracks are young, because the chamber is new, there is not much memory in rock yet. No polish where generations have passed, no smoothed places where hands have worried edges habitually. It is all edges. Sheets of stone still confetti down from the slot of sky on days when the groan of the glacier is closer than usual, and the animals flood the air to make a space for falling, then re-adhere to their lives before the dust has even stopped tasting sharp. Slow, then; slow on the time of plants and ice—ice that presses itself a fingernail further in every week, rock that sighs and cools in geologic exhale—but quick as a thought for creatures who measure change in the height of a dripping bead.

Smell is short here. You get mineral and expectorated cold, and beneath it a musky resin that lives on your tongue; guano, but so parsed, so quickly chelated by the film of lichen and the appetite of the soilless things that it never makes a heap. The taste of the air pulls metal out of your memories. The air feels old yet unaware of you. What does carry, what travels the length of the place and finds all the way into your bones, is that noise. It is not chaos. The base note in the cleft is organ-deep. On it ride the plays of the colony like embroidery—their rain-calls, their heat-calls, their slow lull signals that tell each to fold down and give the stone back its quiet when the cloud has moved on. Hours stretch between acts. The tone changes with weather, with the angle of the wind, with a new stone wedged by last night’s spindrift, with an icicle’s growth. They adjust themselves accordingly, and their lives stir and rest around those slow changes.

Now and then the sky opens. A band of true cold light pours down long enough to show you the geography you have been smelling and touching and hearing: plates you can shave with, dust that floats but never completely settles, a dark wet under the boulder like a closed eye, the slick of a pool no wider than your palm, holding a couple flecks that are not dirt but creatures swimming with blunt oars. Then the light is gone again, throttled by cloud or Theft-of-Breath Wind. The echo remains a while, the way thunder remains in the chest after a big one.

Outside is a ridge path, frostburn and exposure, and the people who once came this high left nothing more complex than a pile of votive stones set six miles away on a different nob, where light could bless them. Here there is no such human mark and no need. The place makes itself, slowly and loudly, for itself. If you stayed, you would learn the schedule of it. The chorus starts not at noon but at the moment the first thin skein of air chills enough to condense without freezing. The drips begin when a climbing swath of drummers find a seam they can ride into tune. Little bodies come apart and little bodies come together, both as sure as the mountain’s patient breathings. Over a season, the frost ribs alter and the water bowls deepen by the thickness of a thumbnail; over a decade, a ledge you trusted becomes a plane that will not take you. The colony’s tonebook changes, but hardly. It inherits more than it invents.

In sum: a wet dark at the top of the world, loud with creatures that learned how to squeeze water from song. Rock as instrument, ice as mouthpiece, wind as player, animals as chorus, light an infrequent, forgettable guest. You leave with your ears full and your palms tingling, sated by how much lives here and by how little is required to keep it doing so. The room—this sliver of infant mountain—stays, becoming itself by minuscule amounts while the sky drags its bright rag sideways overhead.
