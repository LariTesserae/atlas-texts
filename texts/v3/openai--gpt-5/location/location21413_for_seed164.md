Begin with the edge, because edges explain the rest. A continent lifts its rim into the thin, cold part of the world, a dark shelf of young rock whose fractures still look like handwriting not yet smudged by time. The drop is incomprehensibly far, a clean black line bitten into pale cloud. From below, warm air climbs and is wrung out along this edge; from above, the ceiling streams past in a low river of sky. The result is perpetual wetness without true rain: a fine mist that beads on every face and fiber; water that seems to come from stone itself; ridges that weep steady as veins.

The plateau behind the rim is not a single surface but a procession of high, fresh features: glassy flows solidified mid-surge, hexagonal columns sheared like organ pipes, ash plains frozen smooth as lacquer, and block fields where frost has already begun the slow business of prying. The youth of the land is in its edges. The flexed planes gleam where fracture faces remain unoxidized; ice needles stand like hair at dawn; rills have cut only the first letters of a language into the slopes. Farther back, the black rock is stippled with lichened patina—turquoise where copper bleeds, lemon where sulfur flares—and patterned ground has stitched the topmost inches into polygons by the heave and settle of seasonal ice that never succumbs to full thaw.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It makes skins instead of pools. It trickles audible but unseen inside the stone. It threads through the root-fabrics of sphagnum and sedge so completely that, when you press your palm to the ground, the world gives slightly, and when you lift your hand, an imprint gleams dark before it fills again. Low hollows gleam with black water under loose veils of scum-green and iron sheen; every outcrop leaves a little trail of shine. The marshes here form without aid of river or lake: perched above impervious beds of glass and dense basalt, fed by the ceaseless breath of cloud, their edges march in ragged bands that cling to the breaks of slope. It is always the hour after rain. There is the smell of cold rock and vegetal saturation, of metal that has sat too long under a dripping eave, of peat alive and slow.

It is cold in a way that anneals thinking. Exhale, and the air takes your heat without drama. The saturation robs evaporation of its theft; sweat is rare because the world has already done it for you. Frost does not glitter so much as it grows, fuzzing steel and stem alike with feathers in the night and slouching off mid-morning to fall as soft noise. The light is ordinary and untrustworthy. On a clear day—there are clear days—sunlight has a hard, honest bite to it, the high-altitude blue beating down parses every shadow’s edge. More commonly, the ceiling hangs a few tens of meters overhead, gray and luminous, tossed by standing waves that keep the lumens steady even as they move. Halos appear around pole-tips and tower masts; sun-pillars come and remain as if nailed to the horizon. Shadows are rare and surprising. You find yourself believing that the brightness is part of the land itself.

The plants have learned to be horizontal. Lushness here rolls low and thick and spreads its weight carefully. Mats of moss swell and decline like the breathing of something enormous, their capillary forests catching every droplet and lifting it into green architecture. Dwarf willows creep more than grow: the idea of a tree repurposed as a carpet, red twigs pressed flat to the world, leaves like coins buried in the nap. Cushion plants hold their secrets under domes so tight you could drum your fingers on them as on taut hide. Sedge-crowns bristle in pewter fans; liverworts write gloss across the shaded stones; lichens whorled and leafy feed on rock and weather and the odors of machinery. There is a glister to everything—a seal of water, a varnish of algal film, a rime of fine ice at certain hours that gem the leaves without weight.

There is no quickness here except what wind lends. Nothing darts. Nothing watches. The air above the bogs is empty of tiny lives that would usually hang there, complaints and whispers on wings; the pools hold no ripple from a fin; the ground keeps no sudden scurry. Lushness without appetite is a particular feeling. The sedges die of their own weight and fungal art, not of teeth. Spores go where the wind signs them, seeds travel in streamlets or are shaken loose by the same unending vibrations that set the condensing plates humming. A world that does not flee: it is unnerving and very calm.

Sound is the second ground. It is the plate tectonics of the air here. Along the rim, where the upwash hits the edge and must decide, a permanent roar lays down its base note. It is not a gale; it is something slower and larger, a river in which every eddy has been agreed upon. The basalt organ columns, born of cooling fire and broken to open mouths, take that flow and voice it. You hear tones that are too big to be called notes, bands of hoarse singing that line the drop for kilometers, modulated elsewhere by long guy-wires that connect towers to rock and buzz like harp strings under a wet thumb. Walk inland, and the roar relaxes, but does not disappear; the plateau is an instrument sounding itself, the masts and buildings and railings drawn into a weather that always hums against the things that would defy it. Water adds its language: a million drips, a million quick patters into shallow cups; the continuous hiss of falls that are only a few centimeters high but miles wide as they creep across stone. Steam speaks softly from the mouths where heat remains in the new crust, sighs that make ghosts out of fog and salt the air with iron.

Into this are set the works.

They were sited for the certainty of it all: wind that can be budgeted to the fourth decimal; air at its dew point, every hour, every day; young rock that holds heat and nowhere to put water except where you decide. The cities—if the word fits a thing that stretches, repeats, and leaves so much between—occupy the rim in modules. Each module is a condensary and a sound-yard, a low stack of matte surfaces and open ribs that takes the climbing air, cools it through labyrinths of fins and plates, and drinks what falls. It drinks more by blowing: great, slow sirens that do not ring but pull, tuned to the frequencies that the basalt now gives the sky. The water runs into gutters and from gutters to channels and down into cisterns cut into the basalt, lined in greenish glass that sweats as much as it holds. The condensaries are hooked to one another by viaducts that look like the bones of a great fish laid out just below the lip—arches that catch cloud and turn it to beads that drop through grilles into tongues of runnels.

Behind them, engine halls breathe with regular chuffs. The technology is not delicate. It celebrates mass and steel and heat moved by blades. Turbines turn on the bloom of the wind; other turbines sit on falls that run no more than a body’s height and yet never stop, every rill and gutter a harness. In sheds that glow faintly with sodium light, electrolyzers split the captured water, bottling pressure the way others bottle summer. Pipes run like ribbed serpents wall-to-wall, singing softly with gas; iron-banded tanks squat in rows and wear white beards of rime for half the day, slick bellies for the rest. A rail runs a few tracks back from the edge, single line for the scale of it, and when a train goes it is one articulated body of many dozens of low hoppers, bearing mesh-floors full of cut peat, iron nodules raked from fresh seeps, sheets of moss peeled in controlled patches to dry under warm air and become insulation. The locomotives blow rich, throat-sung notes that meet the wind’s and tie a knot you can feel in your teeth.

There are quarters, too—rows of buildings with eyes shut. Their doors are maintained against the wet; their windows are waxed and clean, but they are mostly without the sound of voices or footfalls. If there are minds here, they work in walls and mats and metal. Automation, yes; and more than that, a kind of industry that has grown used to a world with no surprises and has written itself accordingly. Machines tick and inspect and squeegee and oil and calibrate. Drones do make appearances, though they are not creatures in any sense the marsh would recognize: little sleds with shag-rubber feet for the mats, articulated booms for grooming the condensers, quiet fans for hovering at guy-lines. There is an etiquette to their movement that comes from the place—no sudden sharp flows of vibration that might shock a saturated structure, no heat dumped where the moss is richest, no shadows thrown onto favored lichens for longer than the protocols allow. On maps, the modules have names: Standard Drop 81; East Organ Span; Basalt Yard L-3. In the fog, they have presences: shapes that sing certain chords, silhouettes that always darken at the same hour.

There are sites where heat still answers. Not volcanoes in the old sense, but vents laced through the uplift where fluids sigh up through the new architecture of the crust. Around these, the vegetation fattened early—the heat lets the mats be careless about season—and the industry took notice. The vents are capped with valved domes that breathe like sleeping animals, and steam-lines go from there to houses where rotaries turn with a patient, loud grace. The smell there is different: metal and water and a sourness of minerals, but also bread—the smell of a fungus grown for insulation and for sponges and for shapes that must be spongy and unrottable, a technology knitted and kiln-dried rather than machined. Whoever built this made partnership with those that do not move quickly and do not require heat in great measure.

Weirdness here is not in tricks, but in rules that feel like they were voted upon. The plateau holds pitch. The air shudders always in a band deep below music; you almost come to think things here are chosen because they agree with that band. Moss-wires carry current better when they hum at certain frequencies; the condensers drink more fervently when the organ-columns sing certain ratios to one another. The very mats show faint alignment: not north-south, not to any pole you can name, but to the lines of tone laid down by the edge. In the fog at night (for there is a night; the world has a day, the light is ordinary, but the plateau remains articulate even in dark), you can see faint, steady coronas around the masts and along the edges of gutters where flow abrades against air. Sparks are not rare here, but they are slow as feathers and as harmless. The marsh gases do not flame; the cold denies them that; they pour instead into wet, luminous clots, microbe drift that makes the hollows glimmer as if full of tired stars when the dark is deep. Static builds and releases; metal tastes of it; you find yourself running a hand along railings and feeling tone instead of shock.

Dynamically, this is a place that has made a treaty with monotony. Weather is not absent, but it is appointed. The upwash is constant; the cloud river is reliable; the vents do not stop supposing heat; the condensers fill at rated rates; the mats exhale a fixed damp. Seasonal differences are so slight as to be charming when noticed—paler greens for a while, slightly slower drip in the midhours after noon, a sharper taste of iron towards the period when ceiling sits lower. The engines do not run on shifts; they run on regulations. There is a calendar here, but it is a legal one, an arrangement of maintenance and moss harvests and inspections whose dates are chosen so that nothing ever surprises an organism that has never had to learn what surprise is. Clocks are accurate because the air itself tells them what time it is almost without torsion.

The scale of it allows for emptiness between everything that is not the sky. You can travel an hour along the rim and the residue of industry is a low murmur and a repeating silhouette, chimed together by the long, taut wires that run from module to module. Between them, the plateau rolls away into its own horizon as if trying on the idea of a sea. Close at your boots, the ground is intricate and busy with pores and stems and water-skins; at a hundred meters, it is a sheet; at a kilometer, one idea repeating—dark, polished patches where the glass still wins, pale fuzzy areas where lichens have gone ahead, pewter green and peat black and a shallow, reflective gray from the multitudinous little channels. The emptiness is not lack; it is room given to the few presences that matter here—edge, tone, wet, plant, machine—each allowed to spread to its proper measure.

Rules of the world, as they declare themselves:

- Nothing alive moves fast. The loudness belongs to air and metal and falling water.
- Water arrives by agreement with the sky and departs where design allows. Streams do not wander.
- Edges are fed. People—if there are any somewhere below the noise, watching dials, answering to programs—build where gradients are permanent and tap where physics is in the mood to continue without oversight.
- Everything that is built is tuned. Masts are not placed to be seen but to sound; plates are set at angles discovered by listening to drip.
- Lushness is not height but thickness. The best farms are seven centimeters deep and spread for hectares.
- There are no eyes, so there are no shows; color here is for chemistry and for the benefit of machines that read in wavelengths more obedient than sight.

Walk it, and the place keeps becoming itself. At first you notice that there are no wingbeats and you think that is the strange thing. Then you become aware that the roar is a living map and could tell you where you stand if you could learn its language. Then the wet begins to read as structure instead of slime; the richness in the low cushions stuns you with its practicalities, its perfect use of a world that will not allow gravity the indulgence of a true fall. Then you meet a module and see a city that did not need bodies to make it human: gutters that are loved like good roads, masts that are named like hills, engine-halls that heave their breath into the same key as the cliffs.

And it never quite changes. Day follows night follows day, and you can set your pulse by the fact that the manometer in the condensary’s third bay will be at the same needle-width above forty kPa each noon. The edge hums at the same pitch you woke to. The mats shine. The rock weeps, more slowly in the coldest hours, more quickly in the dampest, always. If you leave and return in a year, the place will greet you by tone and taste and light exactly as before, and the only differences will be the ones the world made while you were learning to hear it—new words you have for it, new folds in the ground where your steps have taught you to look.
