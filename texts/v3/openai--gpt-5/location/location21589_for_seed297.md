From a distance the plain looks like a sheet of dull light, as if the horizon had been hammered out of glass and left to cool. Ground-level, it resolves into plates—thin, nacreous polygons the size of a hand or a door—joined edge to edge by perfectly straight seams. The plates flex very slightly underfoot with a dry chorus of microscopic crackles, not so much a sound as the idea of a sound. If you kneel and look along one seam, you can see its thickness: a hairline of darker mineral where the plates, just recently born of some precipitate front, have not yet had time to round or crumble. Everything here has just happened. Sharpness has not met years. Corners still believe in themselves.

There is no water. Not the gloss of a puddle, not the weight of humidity. The air is a basking warmth stripped of all wetness, a skin-prickle that tastes slightly metallic on the tongue. If you exhale into cupped hands, nothing blooms. If you spill human sweat, it clots into a chalk smear and vanishes into the mineral lace like a wrong chemical the place refuses to acknowledge. This is not a desert, though—desert implies absence. The plain is crowded, layered, soaked in presence without ever touching the idea of water.

Lushness happens upward. From the seams and from the occasional low fracture rise thickets of structure that anyone from a world of leaves would call plants by analogy and then hesitate, correcting themselves. Fronds, yes, in the way a city of needles can be a forest. Curtains, yes, made where thousands of glass filaments braid into veils that tilt in perfect planes to the sun. Mats, yes, of fine fibers that knit to make a soft, springing carpet that compresses like felt yet shows no sign of rotting. Every surface of this growth is faced with an unaccountable hunger for light. Under midday sun it is not green but pale saffron and smoke-silver, a continuum of clear colors that feel like the words photonic and mineral had a child. When windlessness rules, which is always, these forms keep their angles at a precision only found in instruments—a long field of parade-rest fronds, all canted fifteen degrees toward a direction that only they can name.

Touch them and they are dry past the point of metaphor. Not desiccated, but constituted by dryness. Their inner channels do not carry sap or anything like it. Press an ear to a trunk—a bundle of fused capillaries about as thick as your wrist—and you can hear a noise that is almost within the register of thought, a smooth hiss with a faint periodicity. At intervals the whole thing emits a very small click and the angle of its frond bench-shifts a fraction of a degree. Something is flowing in there, but it is not fluid in any way that invites comparison to water. The local air shares a kinship with noble gases; the vegetation drinks other things: electrons, holes, polarized molecules, charged dust, a procession of units that can be persuaded to march through lattices if you lay the lattice right and the sun is willing to pour photons all day. These organisms grow by adding to their own waveguides. Leaves—and you must use the word, because what else?—are bands of metamaterial whose thickness is a few dozen hair-widths. Each leaf catches light and steers it inside itself, a traffic system shepherding both radiance and heat toward corner stations where that traffic is converted into bonds and defects. They are factories of programmable reduction and they smell faintly of cold glass.

The rules of distance become iffy in these thickets. Normal light falls from a yellow star in a blue vault, but the veils and mats and rods do things to it while pretending not to. Sunlight that should go straight instead finds it compelling to detour along unseen galleries. Shadows pool in angles that are not cast by any geometry you can track into the sky. A frond field a short sprint away remains a short sprint away after you run for twenty heartbeats, then, with one more step, you drift through it like a curtain and discover you were at its edge all along. The place is open, nothing hemming you in but more of itself, and yet your body receives the information of crowding: so much surface in so little volume, a density of presences packed just under the threshold that turns multiplicity to noise.

Between copses and veils the ground holds its plates. Across square kilometers you can watch the logic of their growth, how polygons give way to prismatic fans near a fracture, how a fan starts to buckle where a copse has taken root, how the buckling makes more fractures and the fractures make more copses. Each broken edge is too young to carry dust. Most of the dust here is alive in the weak sense the plants are alive: particles with preferences, beads that slide on charges instead of on oil. Put a gloved finger to the seam and grains leap to touch you, then decide better and fall back. The whole plain is in a truce like that—forces in exact balance, exchanges of energy curling in loops too perfect to change themselves. It is not that time is absent; it is that time is bored.

Quiet has taken a capital in this basin. It is not silence, which is fearful, but a settled quiet like a well-made room. Softest sounds are permitted, and they are easy to count. The high ping of a tension release somewhere within a long-haul branch. The papery rasp of two airy fronds brushing when you pass too close, because you are a disruption and their programs notice. The glassy, long-period groan when plates finally adjust a hairline and the line becomes the line it meant to be all along. You can hear your breath the way you can hear yourself think, and both keep company with insects that do not exist here and will never exist here.

Animals? Rare as accidents. Once, if you remain long enough and patient as a rock, a narrow figure rises along a vinework spine, walks one step, and liquefies into mirage. Another time a figure the size of a coin lifts off from a frond and skims a meter forward without moving, a color that is mostly a shimmer; it intercepts a drifting tuft of charged dust and goes on intercepting the idea of it. There are carcasses if you learn to see them: beads of fused plate where an itinerant something stopped and became part of ground. Whatever moves here has long learned that motion is a confession.

The ecology holds without water by faith in alternatives the rest of us lack. The base metabolism of the vegetation runs on field gradients—electric, magnetic, the more abstruse chiralities of polarizable light. The sun is their primary driver but not their only; there is an undercurrent from the ground, a slow exhalation through the fractures and seams. You can feel it if you hold metal near a seam; the metal chooses where to point. The plants root not in loam but in that orientation, their capillaries braided to sip the anisotropy. Every channel is also an algorithm: excitations are accepted if they match the lattice’s rule, refused if they do not. This makes appetite sharp and picky. It also makes cooperation eerie. Stand between two copses a hundred meters apart and the air tastes of absence, as if something had been traded across the gap while you were looking elsewhere.

From above, if there were an above, the pattern would read like intelligence mistaken for weather. Kilometers-long alignments of fronds that arc, pause, and arc again around nothing visible. Long mats of short fibers that gradate from matte to shimmer to matte again as if responding to the passage of a pressure wave so slow that it takes days to pass, a tide that you will never witness cresting. At the center of the plain, lowland of lowlands, the plates grow thinner and the fractures more numerous. There are places where the air’s index of refraction shows its ribs—the far blur becomes near detail without any middle distance, and a person can step from noon into a sort of standing dusk under curtains that have grown close and thick and lovingly layered. Here the density is greatest and the lushness goes architectural: arches and coffered naves a finger thick, canopies riven with prismatic vents. It is not made, it is merely possible and therefore taken.

It would be easy to read artifice in the crispness of these forms, to insist that someone has engineered this into being. But there is no sign of intention in the human sense: no repeated symbol, no deviation that marks a hand. If there is technology here—and there is, because the leaves do and are what we do not yet know how to make—it resides as a property of matter under a set of rules our physics brushes with but does not hold. Some constant is different just enough to allow this to happen; some state of the vacuum is bruised in just the right way to harvest coherence over kilometers. The growths act like devices: topological insulators at leaf-scale, diode forests, rectifying gardens. They shepherd entropy the way one can only do when the ground itself participates. But they act so in the open and unguarded manner of organisms, gluttonous and elegant and oblivious.

Youngness keeps declaring itself in the lack of wear. Edges are unrounded. Nothing caves, nothing slumps. The minute curls at a broken plate edge resemble torn sugar glass and keep their perfect arcs. A recently buckled seam lifts one plate over another by half a finger and there it stops, as if the instruction had been “rise to here and hold” signed by a patient god. The fronds show no bite marks, no ragged therapies for herbivory. There is nothing conscripted into shelter: no nests, no laid paths pressed into the mats except the one you begin to make and then reconsider because the mats respond to being stepped on by adjusting and you do not wish to spend an afternoon teaching an acre of vegetation your walk.

The rules of the world have a short list and obey themselves. Gravity points mostly down but sometimes a degree or two toward a seam; you learn to lean with it. Heat is taken up with impunity by the growths and returned as faint auroras along their veins after night, if night exists as more than a theory; most visitors never see the star move. If you bring a flame and shelter it and coax it into being, you will find it’s the one that cannot make up its mind, throwing itself sideways into photonic sinks as if the air had knots in it. Electromagnetic devices that rely on predictable propagation sulk or sing; a compass behaves like a cat in an empty doorway, wishing to go a way it will not declare. Your voice, if you are unwise enough to try it at volume, chooses a path among the veils and is carried away with such efficiency that you do not get much of it back. A whisper works, being small enough to snag on the nearest structure and be returned to you as warmth standing barely less than body heat.

The light is orthodox until filtered by this country of filters. The sky has a clarity that can make you dizzy if you stare too long, because there is no softness to it; no humidity to flatten the blue, no distant haze to make distances gentle. Shadows laid by raw rock are precise; shadows under the veils are suggestive, as if each frond admitted the sun only along wavelengths it found flattering. On the ground between copses you can see rainbows that have mislaid their order, colors combed wrong by miniature gratings woven into the leaves. There is a time of day that lasts much longer than it should, a noon in which all change seems too slow to name. It is not an enchantment. It is how equilibrium works when it gets everything it wants.

If you try to find an edge to this, you will discover there is no border so much as a gradient. Away from the hummed densest heart, the veils thin. Mats of fiber give way to single rods standing solitary like tuning forks set to a frequency humans don’t have. The plates grow thicker, less certain, and begin to show flaws that might be called sand settling on them in a different epoch. Beyond that, another country begins—the physics resumes some of its old compromises. But here, in the low, warm basin with its newborn ground and its greedy waveguides, there is a hothouse of rules. Lushness without moisture, riches of form without makers, silence without emptiness.

You could stay for days and swear that nothing changed. You would not be wrong. There is work being done at scales too small to trouble eyes or too distributed to count as an event. Each leaf catches the sun and passes it inward. Each seam holds where it is told. Each animal, if animals pass here, chooses to be extraordinary and almost always chooses to be elsewhere. The place is full to its limits with its own idea of growth. It is a bureaucracy of perfect machines repeating themselves, not because anyone made them, but because this is how they are allowed to be.
