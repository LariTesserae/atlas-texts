What you notice first is the brightness. It is not sunlight exactly—too blue and too clean—but it has the same habit of filling space and finding edges, of defining distance by glare. It pours down in long, perfectly straight columns from the ceiling: rods of light as thick as tree trunks, as fine as threads, thousands of them, set into the pale vault like a vast organ’s pipes. They are the reason this place exists. They come from the surface through the stone and ice, captured and guided by transparent veins that nature found or invented—salt lenses, glassy ice, seams of clear quartz that act as light-wells and conduits. Most of the warmth has been stripped out along the way; only brilliance arrives.

Under that brightness: cold, and a ringing kind of quiet.

The ground is not ground. All horizontal things are an agreement between what used to be stone and what the plants have decided to be. The bedrock here is young in the way of things that formed quickly and then broke—a show of facets, fresh as a cut apple. There are knife-spines of frost-bitten basalt, raw shelves of limestone, freshly flaked shale whose pages can still be turned with a thumb. The walls rear inward in sharp scallops, and the ceiling is bristled with icicles so new they are clear-glass needles.

Across this, the vegetation has hurled itself with the single-mindedness of a planet’s weather. It is not forest—there are no trunks—or field—there is no soil—but something like a continuous organism flattened to architectural intent. Weaves of pale fiber climb the blades of rock and bridge the voids. Lattices of rope-thick strands cross and knot until they suspend wide, trembling membranes that seem to drink the light and spread it laterally into a uniform day that reaches far into the deeper shadows. The plant-sheets are thin enough that you can see their veins as shadow, and their whole surfaces are seeded with eyelike pores that blink shut when a cold draft moves through.

You could walk on parts of it, if you trust your feet and the plants’ attention. Many spans are tens of meters above open emptiness: a depth that is no longer human-scale but the internal distance of the world. Below, the floor drops away indefinitely, only occasionally revealing a ledge or an old slump of rock where the living mat has not yet found a purchase. The sense is of being inside a huge, hollow lung—oxygen-poor and patient—that breathes a little without making a sound.

Moisture is always arriving and always departing, but rarely in the same place twice. The rods of light are forever hatching cold, and around them the air’s faint vapor becomes visible. Beads of condensation stand along the plants’ strands like rosaries, and when the knots tug and re-tension themselves, the beads run together into brief rains that fall only where the web chooses to clench. From nowhere there will be a soft hush that grows across a kilometer of membrane as every pore exhales in time; you look up, and shallow clouds have formed beneath the ceiling, a white sob of breath drawn from stone. They drift until they strike a colder layer and come down as a sheet of fog, slow as silk and almost soundless. The fogs slick everything with cold gloss, then vanish sideways into places you cannot see.

Water never lingers except in cupped places. The plant builds bowls—some the size of your palm, some as big as houses—whose rims bead with dew, then crest, then release in a clear fall from lip to lip. Rivulets run along the prechosen grooves of living flesh, sleeking to a hurried, silent pace, and end in gravel pans of true rock, the only still pools in the cavern. They are black and mirror-smooth and so bitter-cold you cannot keep a finger in them for more than a heartbeat. There are very few, separated by days of walking. Every creature here knows where they are.

The plants are borderless because they are the place. They are not only membranes and strands. Reach down through the white felt under your boot and you will feel the solid, springy plate of layered living mats that has compressed itself into a kind of plank, anchored into fractures with root-needles so strong that they have split and wedged the stone further apart. From some of these plates, columns rise: bundles of cable that gather light like a sail and channel it downward, interior surfaces pricked with points of calcium and silica that scatter and hold the brightness. At the base of each column is a dark organ like a knot or a wide maw that opens and closes with a wordless patience, transpiring, drinking, tasting the air the way a tongue tastes.

Their rules are not the rules you are used to, but they are consistent. Light moves, so the whole vault moves. The focused rods that drill down from the ceiling wander with the hour. In answer the cords contract and lengthen with cold-stiffened grace, hauling the translucent sheets along rail-lines of their own weave. You can see the geometry of it in the tremor that runs a kilometer and comes to your feet ten counts later: a ripple of pulled tension, then a harvest of tinkling droplets as the beads of dew release. If a shaft of brilliance shifts too far, a membrane that was a bridge will unrope, furl like a flag, and be gone, reappearing somewhere else within the day. If you camp on such a span, expect to wake in a new place.

With the movement comes quiet. It could be that the immense volume takes sound and thinly spreads it so that even a shout has the courtesy to go elsewhere. It could be that the plants absorb sound, not as energy to use but as something that disturbs their measures. Mostly you hear your own tread, the click of ice in ice when the ceiling sheds a skin, the very far-off pop of rock adjusting its mind. In lulls, the membranes whisper a little as gas moves through them, the same way a sleeping animal’s ribs move subtly beneath a blanket.

On a scale this big, there is weather. You feel it not as wind—though there are winds, sometimes, sharp-edged drafts that flex every sheet and pucker every pore—but as pulse. Pressure will fall like a thought passing under your skin. In an hour a cold front will roll beneath the web and every droplet freezes at once, beads of glass on every line. Then a breath of slightly warmer air will move through from a breach into a deeper chamber and the beads will trickle and fall, a glass rain that doesn’t shatter but lands intact and spins on the mats before melting. Sometimes the ceiling lights dim to a steel white as the surface above is covered by a storm, and in that gloom the plants draw closer to their reserves. Sometimes all the rods brighten twice over as some cloud breaks, and then the membranes flower light as if relief were a color.

Fauna rarely break the abstraction of plant and mineral. There are pale gnats that drift like ash beneath the brightest places, living on the honey the membranes weep when the dew is too generous; they are so slight you can mistake them for a trick in your eye until one alights on your wrist and you feel the feather-pressure of its feet. There is a slug thing—long, white, with a rasping pad under its mouth—that ignores your presence entirely and eats the thin biofilm that forms where plants and bare stone meet; it lays coils of amber eggs that cling in clusters to the lee sides of knots. Once in days you may see a living ribbon tilt down from the high spaces, open skins like a book, and ride the draft—four membranous vanes joining at its spine, not a fish, not a bird, not much interested in you. It banks by thinning its edges and turns again to some light-pool you cannot see. Their bones are cartilage and grow through their skin as fine spines, and when they die those spines remain like combs caught in the weave, pricking fingers forever. Predators exist, but they are more suggestion than population: a shadow’s redraw, a reknotted silence.

There are people here, or there were, in numbers small enough that the cavern’s immensity reduces their meanings to footnotes. Their presence is in the artifacts before you see a body. A rope ladder hand-woven from plant fiber descends into a hollow whose geometry is too complex to cross otherwise. Each rung is a loop mended and remended by hands whose grease has darkened the strands to a soft gray. A line of knots on a tension-cable marks a path through a place where the membrane is known to retract without warning: three knots to warn, two to count time, one to pray. There is a stash of flaked stone at a ledge—shards of chert and ice-hammered obsidian with a box’s neatness to them. A bone awl is jammed into the seam of a growth plate to keep it open so a seep will fill a gourd placed beneath.

Hearths are not common because fire here is mostly a rumor. Smoke panics the membranes and seals their pores. Sap is too wet to burn and stone is too reluctant to share heat. So they cook rarely and slowly, with bags of wet plant hung in shafts where the light is strongest, where concentrated beams will scald a stew over a day or two, or with fermented things in lidded bowls sunk into the mats’ warmth. They sleep in nets slung under the thickest weaves, out of the drip lines. For tools they carry hafts grown in place: handles coaxed to curve around edges over months, bolted with dowels of bone and locked in with resin that the web bleeds if cut and heated against rock until it thickens.

The marks they leave on the larger world are faint because the world makes itself over while they sleep. A span once was scored with pictures: stick figures, beasts with too many vanes, a rod of light drawn as a spear. The next season the plant turned that section into a curl of sail and none of those notches face this way now. Instead there is a talisman on a pulley: a carved knuckle of mineral anchored to an eyelet made by piercing and training a young cord to grow around it. When the pulley creaks it says something to a family five kilometers away about the pressure shift they should expect with evening. There are baskets woven of pith. There is a charm net stuffed with the white feathers of the gliding things, but all the feathers are made from thin plant membrane—it is hard to tell where creature stops and place begins.

The whole environment is young in your mouth. Even the air tastes new—stony, sap-sour, a little like the lick of a knife. Surfaces cut because they have not yet been taught to be kind. You run a palm along a rock and come away with a series of precise white lines as if ruled there. The plant’s pads leave burrs in your clothing that are not burrs exactly, but static, the cling of a thousand microscopic hooks that tangle until you must peel them off thread by thread. If you slip and punch through the membrane in your fall, what catches you is a net with just enough give; you will hang and hear, distantly, the tendons of the place strumming one note, and then the net will haul you back up with the dry patience of something that largely does not care if you live, only that its shape remains unspoiled.

Underfoot and overhead, production and erasure go on together at a velocity that the eye deems slow and the mind, later, recognizes as frantic. A new bridge will be a ghost in the morning, half a suggestion by noon, a passable thing by evening; two days after it will have been absorbed into the skin of the world or torn down to escape a draft it cannot endure. Rinds form along weaknesses in the stone—glazes of calcite that look like shell—and then scale off in sheets that float down and shatter softly into chalk. A niche becomes a tunnel, then a well, then a dry flute for a winter, and then vanishes when the plants grow a diaphragm across its throat.

You come to understand that sound here is a calendar. The soft hiss is dew. The sugar-glass clatter is the freeze. The low far drum—heard only at sleep-waking—means a bigger chamber beyond has opened a crack to this one, and a new pressure and a new weather is coming. The sharp bang—once a month?—means rock has split raw under some new insistence, and a light-pipe will be born or die accordingly. You cannot smell rain without feeling the place flex to drink it. You cannot stand in shadow without noticing how the membranes around you thicken to leak less.

It is bright even when it is not. On the dimmest days, the plants keep old light stored inside them by folding it in their geometry, releasing it as a kind of afterimage you see only out of the corner of your eye. You learn to avert your gaze, to let your periphery carry your navigation, to step where dust motes stand the stillest. If you look up for too long, your eyes will pulse with the rods’ false noons and you will walk off the edge of sense. If you look down—to the cuffs of your own breath, to the frost-sparkle of the mat—you will see that every surface is an invitation to be part of it: that if you stayed here past your childhood, your tools would be twined into handles by slow patience, your hair would be coaxed into ropes by wandering burrs, your sleep would sync with the opening and closing of pores, you would be a small thing in a big moving thing and that would be the rule.

Outside this chamber there is another. After that, another. Distances take on the logic of oceans. People here do not think in miles; they think in drips. Between one water-bowl and the next is a day, or two days if a shaft darkens and a membrane curls away and must be detoured. It is a good place to be lost and an excellent place to learn the virtue of staying still. It is a place where the world is not finished pretending to be one thing. Even if you stood for a year at a single rod of light and made your camp and sang to it every day, it would stray from you, and the sail that kept you dry would be off service of some other urgency, and your knots would hang in bright, unused air. And then, after a while, bright would return, from a slightly different angle, and everything would make a new sense that you could almost, but not quite, call home.
