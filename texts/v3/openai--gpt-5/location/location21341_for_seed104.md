At this altitude the ocean has learned the shape of forgetting. It has worn the world to a low, rounded grammar and then settled into it, warm and steady, a dusk-tinted flood that never quite lifts, never quite grows dark. You are high—higher than storms used to climb, higher than the ghosts of mountain roots that sleep far below—but you are in water all the same, enveloped in a medium that flows like weather and holds like stone.

The plateau is broad and old. It hums with quiet pressure, a tableland of basalt and once-sharp granite long since eased into benches, ledges, rest-curves. Every edge is softened, every ridge is a spine under blankets of silt and gardens of tethered green. In places, the bedrock is polished to a dull sheen by the caress of a million years of silt-winds; in others, it is mantled with stromatolite domes and leafed with calcite, like frost that never melts. Faults once broke this place, but the breaks have filled with life and time, and now read as braided valleys lined with mats and forests of slow fronds.

Light comes thin and slanted, the way it does beneath a thousand meters of water or under a sky of permanent rain. It has weight to it, a hushed quality that flattens distance. Colors occur in short-lived intensities—an emerald when a pellicle of algae flares as it turns, a soft cobalt where the world opens onto deeper volumes, brown-golds in the liminal layers where silt is suspended like breath. The sun is not an object here; it is an influence, a pressure from one side that flickers as curtains of denser fluid drift.

Vegetation owns the plateau. It does not spike or tower in heroic columns, because time has negotiated all ambitions down to forms that carry, nuzzle, and endure. Kelp analogs—flat, ribbed sheets with edges frilled like lungs—rise from anchor-knots in the stone, bundling themselves in slanted galleries. They have learned the current’s calendar and unfurl at regular tempos, opening in the warm pulses that move from the world’s equator to its poles like the breaths of a sleeping animal. Between them, bushier beds of fernlike filaments grow, each frond tipped with a faint bioluminescent eye that blinks on a cycle out of step with its neighbors, so the mat becomes a granular constellation whose points seem to drift when you don’t watch and lock when you do.

Larger thickets are built by carbonate exhalations—the labor of microbes and time. They form wall-like shelves and arches, not quite architecture, but suggestive of it: corridors where the water feels cooler, alcoves with a dust of shed spores like pollen in a chapel. In their shadows sprawl leathery rosettes that catch silt and small drift-things, digesting both through slow chemistry. Every surface that can host life does, and every lifeform has learned to share the same inch of space by carving out a slightly offbeat clock.

Even here, in this dense and active quiet, there are ruinous marks—the kind that make it hard to tell which shapes are grown and which are left over. A ring of uprights on a rise, eaten through with borings, carries no lintel now but holds a circle of paler water inside its circumference. A low terrace, stair-stepped in ways that make gravity itch, braces the foot of a fossilized cascade. The uprights and the steps have been colonized to anonymity, written over in mosses of the sea and mantled with the lacework of worm cities. Their intention, if they once had one, is indistinguishable now from the plateau’s own.

The water here is warm as skin and possessed of layers like memory—thermoclines that you can’t see until you pass them, lines that seem to store an older season and then release it onto you with a sigh. Sometimes the layering is vertical, a tidy cake of discrete sheets destined to mix; more often it is slumped and folded, laid down on the contours of the ridge and valley, running along them like fog along a borrow ditch. Movement through such a medium is effortful and also easy; buoyancy feels like it knows your intentions and argues with them gently, turning a straightforward push into a curve, a curve into a drift.

Silence hangs everywhere, pressed and held by the ocean’s mass. There is sound too, but it is the minimum needed for continuing: the soft tearing of bubbles where mats off-gas, the glissando of grit across stone that only an animal the size of a hill would hear, a low creak when a forest of bladders releases its hold and reclines an inch closer to the bed. Far off, at intervals that do not repeat, there is a groan that could be the plateau itself adjusting in its sleep, or the long throat of a basin tidally emptying through a pinch far away. Thunder exists here as a memory you can put your hand through—contained within rain that does not strike anything hard.

Weather happens in water the way it happens in air, given enough scale and time. Internal waves the height of cities run across this drowned table, slowed by drag into stout slow shoulders of dense fluid that lift forests as they intend to pass under them. The plants have learned to bow; their masses hinge in unison, a neighborhood negotiating with a wind. Sometimes an atmospheric river brushes the boundaries of this stratum, a canal of condensed sky folding down into even warmer flow. Then a wall of rain occurs within the ocean itself: a ribbon like clear glass pouring through tea, but laden with silver microbubbles, colder by a degree, freighted with dust and salt. It falls exactly downward only at its heart; near its skin it bleeds into spirals that wrap rock outcrops and wreath the scattered uprights, making halos that stay when the wall has drifted on.

Fauna are present as omissions. You notice them because something that should move does not, or because the mat holds its breath. A slate shape as long as a canal lock passes under you and is gone, a vague absence decorated with hitchhiking tendrils. A glass-shelled turtle the size of a room, its carapace a lens that is not quite convex in any sane way, grows from one curve to another across the span of an afternoon and then folds itself into a hollow in the rock. In their quiet, the few animals that are here have The Look: old, single-minded, polite with the space around them the way one is only in cathedrals and ruins. They are not afraid of you. They are not particularly interested either. Everything that needs to eat does so out of the water itself or from the mechanical shedding of the vegetation. Predation is an argument staged rarely and at low volume—the slow draping of one form over another, the unflowering of a rosette to encompass a drifting sack and close again.

The rules waver here, if rules are the ways you expect movement and change to behave. In some lanes of the plateau the current is a loop; you can ride it under a familiar arch and come out under the same arch from a different decade. Distances are much as they appear until you try to cross them, at which point they arrange themselves to be longer or shorter than you packed for. A boulder will be in two places on some days: where it is, and where it has been most often noticed to be. Sound loses its edge around corners as if rounded by the same ancient erosion that humbles the stone, and your own wake trails you when you stop. It is common here for bubbles to stop in place and consider. Some of them take on rings—perfect, thin toruses that rotate not around a single axis but around all the possible axes implied by their curves. Plants sometimes reach with their tips into volumes where they are no longer plants and return with mineral gloves. If you stare too long at the faintly brighter places they will stand up and become structures long enough to be recognizable, and then lie down again.

The highness of this place expresses itself by the bottoms that are missing. Edges of the plateau do not fall away into slopes; they simply withdraw into cobalt, and then space opens. There is the sensation of great weather below you, not above: streaked lightning that is slower than lightning, continents of cloud that roll without getting anywhere. From some capes in the rock, if you back into a cooler lens and steady yourself, you can see down through the water’s gradations to where rain is rain, not sea—denser sheets falling through less-dense, the above-world raining past the ocean’s sunless ceiling like a reversed waterfall. The abyss here is air made heavy with its own water, a sluice that drains forever across a sphere you will never travel.

Erosion’s old patience has arranged everything into its final mood, and yet the place is busier than its composure admits. Pollen-like spores rise in plumes at certain angles to certain kinds of dawn. Pads the size of doors detach, fill their bladders with a lighter gas pulled from the microbubbles in the rain-walls, and float sideways by the thousands to seed distant benches, guided by faint gradients of salinity and memory. When the internal tide turns, there is a long interval in which everything loosens without falling—a suspension of intent—followed by a collective sigh as each tether reconnects to a new alignment. Shell-hives hang their doors ajar to strain the passing silt and then shut in increments as the particles thin; the score is all diminuendo.

On the rock that reads like a terrace, there are scratchings in a spiral. The spiral is too perfect for geology and too worn for craft. Things like that are scattered here, the residue of a time when the planet was not afloat. Some are only absences. One slope feels like a ramp not because it is smoother than the rest but because your bones argue with it, recognizing a measurement that stones do not usually honor. A promontory is too flat at the top, a flatness that collecting life is careful not to spoil; the mats here make a point of arranging themselves to frame a view over the blue nothingness, unrolling and rerolling like respectful flags in a wind. If there were once voices, they are made now of calcium and the sensible business of small mouths. It doesn’t feel sad. It feels concluded.

Time has mass in this water. It pools, it sloshes. The old bedforms—the long, low ripples in sand that the whole plateau signals it still remembers—are set not by any wind you can feel but by a conviction. You can sit a while (if “sit” means to adopt a relationship with buoyancy and stony shelf) and watch a ridge unroll itself one grain at a time as though it were rubbing its back and pulling up a blanket, a ninety-thousand-year motion practiced again with care. It is common to hear an ache deep in the body of the place on certain lunar intervals that no moon causes anymore.

If you stay long, the plateau adopts you into its layering. You will find that you are easier in the thick places, that you prefer close-grown coves where light filters through three different greens and the water has a little weight on it—a constant, almost-embrace. Quiet becomes good company. The rare animals will allow you to become a landmark they pace by, the way they pace by the ring of uprights and the terrace and the long low runnels cut in a time when flow had more to do. When the great internal wave comes through, you will tip with the forest and feel the benches take your shape and give it back to you slightly softened. On days when the rain-walls move fast, you will watch them throw scrolls of cold through the warm layers, and realize that the scrolls touch only what wishes to be touched.

The laws here are not strict, only cumulative. Buoyancy chooses allegiances. Memory is most buoyant of all—yours, the plateau’s, the water’s, the light’s—rising to collect like scum in corners and then being skimmed off by the walls of rain. Gravity is less a direction than a persuasion, and erosion less a reduction than an editing to essence. There is no technology, because there is no hurry left to solve. There are structures, because everything that is old enough will wear itself toward a structure, and the living here have had time to cooperate.

You can go to the lip of the place where the floor withdraws and look out over the unplace below. The sound there is of your own pulse reflected from a long, hollow nothing. It is possible to imagine skies, to recall what it was to have weather above you and earth beneath. The ocean does not forbid you this. It moves past, stately, warm. The plateau gives you its broad back and its furniture of mats and relics. You can wait as long as you like. The sea has time. The plants keep at their work. The rain falls through water, because that is what it learned to do. And in the dim, at this high, warm, drowned table, everything is very close together, very busy and very quiet, laying itself down into the last good shape.
