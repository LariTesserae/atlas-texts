Darkness has a climate here. It is not the absence left behind by day; it is a warm, inhabited medium, heavy on the skin, tasting of metal and wet leaves. The highland is so vast it makes a sky of its own, a continent lifted into the belly of a saturated atmosphere. Whatever sun the world offers does not reach the plateau. Clouds stall on the escarpments and unspool, rain condenses out of air as if coaxed by the stone itself, and the night thickens with a slow, steady breath.

Terrain reveals itself by touch and sound first. The stone has been working on one idea for a very long time: to be wet and full of holes. Entire cliff faces weep in braided veils, their surfaces pitted into honeycomb, stiffened with sinter shelves that grow like frozen curtains and drip forever. Valleys are not cut so much as dissolved. Wide sinkfields pock the upland, each a saucer of blackwater framed in fluted rock. The interior is a lattice of buttresses and corridors, immense ridges with knife-backs softened by decades of moss and slick algae, long trenches excavated by vanished rivers but still inhabited by the sound of flow. Stalagmites, freed from caves, stand in the open air, capped in choking orchids and ferns that never saw a sun but learned to love a drip.

Water is never still even when it is ponded. It skins the world, beads on everything, strings itself from ledge to ledge and grows into ropes that hum with the weight of their beads falling. There are rills so thin they move like oil sheen and are heard rather than seen: a whispering line along a rock seam that gives a place its boundary. Black lakes look like absence; their surfaces are perfect until something breathes. Whole slopes sweat, and the sweat finds paths that have names in the local speech—lace-falls, threadlines, silver-sinks—even if silver is only a way to say “the sound here is braided and bright.” In some bowls the air rises as rain: the cave-breath meets colder mist and a soft, pattering storm climbs up out of the mouths of the earth.

Vegetation has filled the dark with structure instead of color. Everything that can grow grows on something else. Rootmats colonize rock and then are themselves colonized, building sponges that drink the passing air. Leaves are broad, not to catch light but to gather water, guttered and lipped, with hinged tips that knock a rhythm when the drops are right; whole groves have their own metronomes. Pale fronds slough water down into cisterns where roots feed, and there they tend their symbionts: films of hungry bacteria, slow firefly lichen, furnaces so dim it would take the better part of a minute for your pupils to notice them. There are vines that can feel your breath and will turn towards it gently, not for the warmth, but for the carbon they lick from exhale. Mushrooms rise like books left open in a flood. Some of the trees are not trees but two centuries of woven cane and moss wrapped around a scaffold of dripping calcite, alive only because the water never stops coming and warm.

The sounds never pause. Gallons upon gallons glide and shatter and sift. Frogs thrum in chords. There is the wing-noise of something soft and large and numerous that passes far overhead, turning the mist with each stroke into a muscle you can feel in your jaw. Insects write a constant arcade of clicks and small saws, swung to and fro as populations climb and fall in the niches of the wet days. Stone booms sometimes when the big underground rivers turn in their bed and press at their roofs. The plateau runs on acoustics; flowers time their openings to rain-on-leaf rhythms, predators queue on the edges of waterways listening for the specific swallow that means an ungulate is off balance, the factories speak to one another with bass pulses you feel through your shins before you hear them.

In the absence of sunlight, life uses what the plateau gives. Roots farm heat; lichens couple to the tiny sparks that appear when fog rubs tense leaves against each other and build sugars in microwaves of chemistry. There is hunger for glow here, but nobody wastes it on distance. Bioluminescence is held close: a petiole’s warning tone in faint green when bruised, a throat-patch on a courtship singer, a line down a grazers’ flank to keep the herd together in a current of fog. A few things are brighter—the trap-lamps of certain pitcher colonies that breed a light-bacterium, the thousand-points of a fungus city under a rotten fallen ridge—but even these are swallowed a meter away, turned to wool by the black.

Fauna come as density, as moving textures. There are herds of slow, humid animals the size of wardrobes, their backs matted with moss, grazing on mats of bacterial pastry baked by warm rocks. They are nearly silent except for their wet tearing and the small chimes of the orchids that ride their shoulders. There are long-limbed, cartilage-light hunters that tether to overhangs by their elbows and swing, feeling for pressure waves off a misstep. There are wall-striders that eat the skin of water on vertical stone, combing it with mouths like squeegees and leaving clean streaks that are slicker than polished bone. Whole flights of blind birds—if bird is the word—move with coordinated hushing, each wing-beat shaped like the hand of a swimmer, bending the heavy, damp air to stay aloft. Amphibians whistle through their skin; even the worms fizz if you disturb their chowder.

Scale confounds. A single escarpment will run for a week of walking with the same weeping lip of rock, the same unbroken wind sing. The plateau throws weather back at the world that made it; it auto-generates its own circulations. You can map a year not by sun position but by which set of sinkholes are producing upward rain, by the timing of the fog tide that rolls down from the far basalt cap, by when the great migrations of the cloud-swimmers are deflected by thermal plumes from factories. On some cycles, the entire western rim goes to voice: a low, long moan as subducted rivers draw down through new dissolution, and nearly every vine orientates its leaves to it, a continent of green ears listening to the bedrock.

There are structures, but they do not sprawl. You meet their presences singly, or in lonely chains. A fall-plant hangs midway on a cliff, bolted to places where the rock is oldest, intercepting a portion of the endless drop with turbines fat as houses. Their throats are caged, because anything warm here draws life, and life gums machinery with surprising speed. Power lines—stiff, humming umbilicals—take the harvest across the night to other isolated throbs of activity. A condensation tower rises in a die-straight cylinder, jacketed with mesh and choked with ice-belts even in this warmth as air is stripped for something rare and precious you can’t see. Maintenance walkways, gritted and grooved so the foot can argue with slime, connect pumping stations that lift blackwater into digesters and back out again as clear, mineral-rich liquor. Every edge that pretends to be an edge is turned soft by growth within a year; engineers here build by accepting that nothing stays hard or dry.

On a mid-plain the remains of an old refiner lies almost indistinguishable from the hummocks it birthed by interrupting the drip: ribbed concrete cured by a century of rain, knitting with calcite, all its ladders sponge-wrapped, its floors hip-deep in root-bread. A single beacon still burns somewhere inside, a patient diode under a pellicle of algae, casting no more than a thumb-length of weak green. A cable slumps away into the trees as if in sleep. Newer builds learn from it; they speak in deeply pitched pings you will feel in your teeth, call-and-response with the foghorns that mark the safe clearings. The factories never shine. Their windows are covered with skin that lets the air in and the light not out.

If there are people, they move like cautious amphibians, tethered to handlines, their lamps hoods-down, their maps tactile. Their engines carry their own climates: warm exhale, a wet-metal breath you know even with eyes sealed. You find their traces in the small: braille raised on railing posts; a ridge of gritty paint worn smooth where many hands have made the same argument with gravity; a caution gong strung over a sudden shaft where water roars and picks at the edges. Tracks rarely last; water glosses scandal and moss edits memory.

Rules of the world:
- Dryness is a provocation. Anything not beaded with water becomes an attractor. Heat draws life the way sugar draws ants elsewhere.
- Light is not a tool for distance. Glows are intimate. If you make a bright light you will call a congregation you did not plan for, because almost nothing here is indifferent to light.
- Sound is a public act. Everything that can hear, hears. Machines speak low to stay below certain choruses; cliff-whales turn aside at specific notes; if you listen long enough you can hear a factory negotiate with its neighbors to avoid the frequency that excites the slimes to bloom.
- Straight lines are temporary; sharp corners are those things currently being sharpened by water.

Despite the noise there is a cool balance to it all. Erosion is not catastrophe but practice. The plateau has been rehearsing itself for epochs, water rehearsing its threads through the same channels, stone rehearsing its answers in ridges and bowls and tears. The upheavals are on the scale of singing cliff and river reroute, not landslide; the network of life and machine flexes and tunnels and puts itself back. Predator meets prey like storms meet seasons, and neither side seems surprised.

Stand for long enough by one of the weeping walls and the place describes itself to your bones. The rock asks the fog for water and the fog gives it. Plants make surfaces and surfaces make plants. Animals walk through and wear their passing into the maps of others. Factories push power into the dark and receive a chord back that tells them whether their bassline is acceptable today. In the world below perhaps there is color and horizon. Up here there is depth you can taste, and volume you can hear, and a darkness so complete and so busy that the idea of day starts to feel like an extravagance.
