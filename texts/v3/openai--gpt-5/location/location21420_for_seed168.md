High country without a river to name it. A shelf of planet lifted to a quiet brightness, where the air is clean of water and almost clean of everything else. The horizon is a patient line. Between here and there, almost nothing: a hard field of pale mineral glass and black fracture, a sky with no cloud to slow the light, a warmth that settles on surfaces and never steams.

The ground looks young. Edges haven’t learned to soften. Blocks break with shell-like curves and knife-true faces, as if the stone cooled yesterday and simply stood up into sun. Erosion hasn’t found its rhythm; there are no gullies yet, only suggestion-lines, hairline faults that catch dust. Where the crust tears, it shows interior colors—smokes of copper-green, sulfuric straw, a faint bruise of violet nitride. Underfoot, grit is not sand but the shavings of first attempts: flakes of glass-ceramic, granules of halide salts, powders of carbon that take prints and hold them forever because nothing licks them away.

The air is a thin machine: bright, warm, and shy. A breeze crosses with such gentleness that it seems imagined when it touches the wrist hairs and lifts a ghost-spark of static. If you speak, your voice comes back small. There is no animal to answer, no insect to embroider the silence. Sound is the occasional tick of cooling stone, the hush of a vaned surface flexing, a grit-sneeze when something far off sheds a flake.

And then, appearing at intervals like accidents, the groves. They rise from bare plateaus and from the sharp shoulders of faults—stands of growth that are complex enough to read as lush even though each grove sits alone in oceans of emptiness. From a distance, they look like refineries out of dream: trunks of dark ceramic, ribbed columns with bands and flanges; lattices of narrow pipes braided up into crowns; crowns flat as plates or folded like concertinas; long, drooping coils that suggest hose or cable. Up close, it is all living matter, if life can be said to be a habit of chemistry rather than a kindness to animals.

Leaves here are not leaves. They are heat exchangers: stacked, mica-thin scales of conductive polymer, pebbled with microscopic pores, each pore a mouth that wants no water. Sap here is not water. It is a slow, viscous ionic liquid that slips through capillaries of ceramic and carbon, never boiling, never freezing in this range of warmth, never inviting the rot that water would. Roots do not find aquifers but fissures of gas—threads of hydrocarbons and nitrogen exhaled from deep, compressed seams below. The grove pulls those threads, straightens them, breaks them, recombines them. It smelts air.

In the morning, as the light warms the plates, the entire stand answers with a subtle mechanics. Plates tilt on flexural hinges, opening to a precise angle where heat flow balances uptake. Ribs expand microscopically; bioceramic girders take on tension. That tension drives peristaltic pulses through the trunks, and the pulses move charge and solvent together. Where leaves meet the light, catalysts whisper long molecules into short, short into branch, branch into lattice. What grows is a lacquer, a glaze, a film—a thickness you can scrape with a fingernail and get a curl of iridescent resin. Growth looks like manufacturing because it is: scaled, repeated, precise. A grove is a factory that roots itself.

There is no pollen carried by wings. There are no wings. Exchange and reproduction belong to the weather of fields and to the long patience of voltage. As the day moves, plates collect a little charge from the sun, from the tribology of dust across surfaces, from the piezoelectric strain of their own scaffolds. Filaments extend upward—spidery, mineral whiskers—until tips meet a threshold, and a narrow, silent arc stitches between two crowns. In that violet stitch is the swap: packets of instructions encoded in conductive polymers, a thin mist of nanoscale seeds driven along the field line to plate and stick on a neighbor’s inner face. When the rare wind stirs, charged spores go farther, settling by arithmetic rather than luck.

The rules here are clean and few.

- Water is a toxin. A drop would seize the sap and lock the machinery, swelling structures never meant to hold it. All surfaces repel it. Life is built to scorn it.
- Warmth is gentle, not a season but a baseline. Shadow is a temperature, sunny faces another, and diurnal swing is the pump that runs the place.
- Almost nothing moves fast. Speed is how things break. Everything takes time—hours to tilt, a day to place a fraction of a millimeter, a year to throw a runner under the crust to a new vent.
- There is no animal appetite. Nothing hunts. Nothing flees. Energy flows in circuits instead of chases.
- Erosion is something the planet is still considering. Edges persist. Dust accumulates where it falls. The living make more surface than the dead subtract.
- Industry is biological. Muscle is replaced by stress in ceramic and flow in ionic liquid. Factories are forests that built themselves.

Smells, when you are inside a grove, are not the wet greens of chlorophyll. They are sweet-prickling nitriles, a ghost of solvent, a metal note like touched coins, the pleasant sting of ozone after a tiny, quiet storm. Colors are not leaf-green alone but the greens of tarnish, the blues of peacocked carbon, the blacks of polished coal, the opalescent creams of reflective silica skin. Every surface has function. Even the softest thing—the velour on the undersides of certain panels—is a field of nanospikes meant to snag floating charged grains and bring them to ground.

Between groves, the world goes wide again. A kilometer of plate, then three, handled like spare pages left blank. Here and there, a knit of low mat-things spreads radial, delicate as frost. They are early colonists, building soil-from-nothing: exuding salts that crack the glass, trapping fines, laying trace films that give the next grove a place to seat. Soil, here, is not dark. It is pale as bone and slick with the feel of dry soap. Press it and it compresses; release, and it keeps the impression because microbes are stories told elsewhere and decay is a rumor.

At the shelf’s edge, higher still, the air thins and light grows stronger, unscattered. The drop is exact. You can look off and see, hazed far below, a darker country where vapor pools that these heights will not tolerate. Here, no cloud is born; here, light has its straight path and shadows are knives. When a plate turns, its edge throws a line across the ground you could pick up like a ruler and walk along to where a resin bead has set, clear as ice and hard as tool steel.

There is an atmosphere of work without workers, machines without manufacture. If you listen long enough, you begin to sort the quiet: the slow sigh of a crown unbending at noon, the crisp pock of a glaze curing and releasing its shrink-stress, a sibilant run of dust through a comb of mineral hair. The greatest sound is absence. No wing-beat, no chirr, no footfall save your own. Even your breath is a poor companion in this lean air, small and private.

Time here favors equilibrium. The groves run their cycles and replace their plates when the thin gloss of use dulls them, shedding rectangles that skitter a little and come to rest clear-eyed in the light. The shed does not rot; it accumulates in neat drifts against the windless side of trunks and the foots of small cliffs. In a century, those drifts will be strata. In a thousand years, perhaps erosion will have learned its grammar and begun to speak in gullies and boulders rounded as knees. For now, the world is new at being this, and is good at it.

Stand in the middle distance and let your sight sweep—grove, void, grove, void—and the place clarifies into a pattern: a high industrial garden seeded by geology, maintained by sunlight, with lushness measured not by wet abundance but by the density of intention compacted into a single organism's form. Nothing is built, yet everything is engineered. Nothing hurries, yet everything works.
