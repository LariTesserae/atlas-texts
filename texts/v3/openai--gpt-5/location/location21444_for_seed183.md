At this elevation the light arrives like a physical thing, bright enough to cast sharp edges around breath. The air is cool and wet at once, wet not as rain but as a habit: film on every surface, beads along every grain, slow tears emerging from stone that never had a vein. You stand in a country of ledges and rims, of long, fluted steps that carry water without channels. The heights have been gnawed down to lace. Cliff faces are feathered with mineral curtains, scalloped like the insides of shells. They drip steadily, but there is no moss to catch the flow, no leaf to glitter. The only shine is the stone itself.

Sound moves like weather here. Thin winds tear along the ridgelines and force notes out of the rock: high whistling when the gusts are keen, a low organ when pressure leans into caverns. Every terrace edge has crescents and hollows—cupules, galleries, pipes—worn and coaxed until air sings through them. The noise does not merely pass. In bursts you see it, a bright shimmer in your peripheral vision: the instant after a chord swells, water condenses, whitening the air in a faint, short-lived veil that breathes in and out with the song. Where the mountain speaks the loudest, the stones are wettest; where the wind falls calm, the world turns matte and thirsty again, though never for long.

The terrain has settled into itself. Erosion is not violence here but a patience that has found its form. Mineral dams crenellate along grades, broad and overlapping like the treads of a giant’s sandal. Pools stand behind them, wide as fields, shallow as palms, stepping down the slope in a repeating grammar of rim and basin. Some are empty, frosted with a faint needlework of evaporate crystals that crunch like sugar underfoot; others brim and spill, spilling not in lines but in thin wide sheets that teach the light how to scatter. From a distance the valley reads as a succession of pale amphitheaters, bands of travertine and glass-laced sinter, each band singing its own key in the travelling wind.

There are signs that hands once moved through this music. On one lip of a pool, someone chipped a row of notches into the wall, enough to brace a grip while crossing the slickness. In a saddle between two terraces, three stacked stones have been mortared with mud and hope into a shin-high cairn that faces a precise angle—a sightline, perhaps, for a sun that no longer rises exactly where it did when they stood it up. You find a basin deepened by human patience into a true bowl; it sits where the fog reliably condenses, and now it still catches the nightly drink and spills to a near channel cut with a chert wedge. No metals flash here. Every mark is stone on stone, fiber on finger. A few lengths of glassy thread—basalt pulled and braided like horsehair—hang from a lintel of two slabs, an aeolian harp the width of a chest, still humming when wind hits it. If there were ever people, they are not now; their traces are as sporadic as birds’ nests would be elsewhere, if there were birds.

There are no animals. Nothing scuttles from the shadow of your boot. No midges gather above the water, no prints dry into crisp margins along the basins. Silence of bodies, then; but not of the world. Every so often a shelf lets go a glaze of bead-ice into the pool below with a multilayered shatter—sound arriving as both tinkle and roll, then condensing briefly, a breath of mist rising from the strike like an embarrassed ghost. During the afternoon, when brightness leans hardest, you can hear a deep, patient tapping inside the cliffs, as if a miner worked kilometers in, or as if the rock itself counted through some slow arithmetic as it expanded and found new fits for the water within.

The rules are a little wrong here, but not wrong in a way that breaks things—wrong like a dialect that recites physics with different stress. Light has heft. Where it pools strongly on reflective stone, it presses the air downslope; that pressure herds the mist; that mist feeds the nearest lip; that lip grows a little, takes on a whiter glare. Because of this, some ledges climb uphill where they ought to erode down, ascending in shallow, light-eating stairs under the hottest glare. Gravity also seems to bend in the presence of resonance. The loudest hollows draw the drip toward them; beads creep sideways along undersides to fall where the note is purest, building stone bells that ring clearer the more mass they steal from the walls. On calm days the world is ordinary, and on windy days you watch cause and effect come to heel under pressure you can hear.

Distance is crowded. The open sweep of the valley is there—the eye can move for kilometers along the descending terraces, out to where they give themselves up to the cloud sea—but between you and the far rim the near things multiply. Rims fold into rims. A hundred shallow dams lie nearly on top of one another from where you stand, each with its own runnel and petaled lip, the spacings of their scallops out of sync enough to make interference patterns in the light reflected off them. Looking up, cliffs interleave with buttresses and tongues so that the sky is a ragged, many-fingered shape, and the absolute drop of a chasm appears three times in different planes before you find the true edge with a small slide of gravel under your boot. The density of forms makes a walker small, not simply in scale but in options; paths exist, but they are narrow and rehearsed by those earlier hands.

The weather is precise rather than random. Mists do not roll equally; they gather where lines cross. On the windward faces of certain free-standing spires, a faint halo appears at noon, then contracts to a brighter ring as if tightening a belt, then collapses into a drizzle that chases itself down the leeward side and vanishes into the stone. Thunder is rare, but there is a higher thunder, a dry version, when gusts strike a particular notch just right: the surrounding ridges answer in consonant rumble, and for a breath the world looks doubled, the basins stacked right on top of themselves, light chiming between them. At night frost nets the surfaces under the lowest sky; in the morning these nets melt to ghost outlines traced in clearer drip-lines.

In the absence of plants, there is still growth. The living here is a mineral metabolism, if living is allowed to be a word for it. Films of silica and carbonate accrete into vesicles and combs that “seek” the places of highest oscillation in the air, but they seek by being beaten upon, weakened elsewhere, strengthened where the lash of wind and water and brightness repeat. On some shelves you find dark, spongy crusts that are not spongy to the touch, but photograph differently each day, their microstructures crawling in time-lapse along gullies as if burrowing—self-organizing storms of precipitate and dissolution, no chlorophyll, no hunger, yet all appetite for pattern. The nearest thing to a bloom is when certain salts deliquesce in a damp snap and then recrystallize in feathery fans across a wall—half an hour of silent fireworks, then nothing but a slicker shine and a new way for future drips to split.

Cliffs give way at intervals to breaks that are not exactly caves. You could call them rooms if rooms were made by rules you do not know. In one, the roof arches slightly wrong; a voice shouted into it comes back twice, a second apart and half a beat shifted in pitch. Between those echoes a line of suspended droplets hangs, reluctant to fall; they only commit when your second echo finds them, as if the drop needs to hear its own fate. In another, the floor has been combed by runnels into piano hammers, a hundred small steps that sound tone by tone as water marches down; a set of four paddles carved in by earlier hands sit at the inlet, clearly meant to adjust the flow and score the rain. Primitive technology, but elegant within the grammar of what this place allows: persuasion of water, arrangement of sound.

There is a grandeur to the scale that is not mountain-infinite. You can trace the valley to where the terraces lower their horns into the cloud, and you can guess at the next and the next beyond, blue-white and loud. Between here and there is a lot of middle distance, a world busy with itself: droplets sorting themselves into chains, vapors choosing to be cold on the instant the sun blinks through rifts, ledges bulking imperceptibly and then collapsing in a day when a seam undermines. Across a narrow divide a different system has taken hold—same materials, different music. There the wind never quite arrives, and the terraces are less lacy, more monolithic, pools deep and still, their surfaces tensile as if stretched on frames. Your own breath sounds wrong, heavy in your chest, as if it were a thing the basin had to think about before accepting.

Every sense of animal life is missing, and that absence has weight in your ears. You come aware of the constant loudness as a relief and as a pressure. The mountain converses with itself with enough voice to smother any rustle that would have betrayed a beast. If there were predators they would be patterned stonefall, and their prey would be edges that invite too-near a step. What lives is slow and it moves by weather; what dies does not rot but powders to sheen.

Toward evening the light finds its last accelerations along the rims. The basins—

those that face sunward—turn into shallow incandescent saucers. The brightness falls into them and seems to stay for an extra breath as if the pools were keeping the light a moment before letting it go. Below, the cloud sea names itself: it is not sea but a long traveling field of condensing and vanishing—the same habit on a grand scale. The stone edge at your feet is warm in a way that feels like stored noon, and the wind harps on the glass threads left by someone who understood both the music and the thirst. You can sleep here if you stack stones around a hollow to catch breath and damp, and if you listen to the rules of the world. It will whisper them at you all night in its native grammar: that light pushes, that echoes have hands, that water can be called by song, that even at the top of things you are standing not on the peak of a mountain but on the skin of a process that will go on singing whether anyone answers or not.
