The room is a lung hung from a cliff.

You have to shoulder through a slit of oiled hide to enter, and the membrane sighs back against you with warm moisture. The air inside is hot—heat that is body-close, woven with steam, steeped in the sweet-sour of fermenting plant matter and wet clay. Dim light filters through skin and wicker; outside is the thin brilliance of high altitude, but here the brightness is swallowed and slowed to amber. It beads and runs along everything. If you stand still, dew condenses on your wrists. If you speak, your breath gathers into nascent droplets that fall when you finish your thought.

Everything you touch has been made by hand and not long ago. The ribs of the chamber are cane bent while green, bound with cords of twisted bast, their knots still fat and hungry, not yet tightened by age. Between the ribs, lattices of wicker are daubed with clay; fingerprints dimple the surface where someone pressed the material into place. The clay hasn’t decided to be stone yet. It holds sheen and a faint give, and there are places where it slumps microscopically as if listening, as if words are a gravity that can pull it. The floor, if you can call it that, is a taut net of ropes, layered over each other until there is almost no gap, and on them are mats, and on the mats are trays, and in the trays are soils made of chewed fiber and ash. The place is dense—presence upon presence—so that emptiness is a myth told from outside.

High elevation declares itself in what little of the outside you can see. Set into one curved wall there is a fist-sized oculus patched with bladder membrane. Press your face to it and you look out past your own fogging breath into the drop: cloud backs roll beneath like gray sea, and somewhere far below glints the idea of a river. Up here the wind’s roar is an animal you do not have to imagine. It seeks purchase on everything, and this chamber answers, taut, booming like an enormous drum when gusts strike the skin. The sound goes through you, through the frames and trays and ropes; the whole place vibrates as a single instrument.

The water is not a visitor; it is in residence. The chamber breathes it. Cane ribs are fitted with gutters along their upper curves: hollow reeds painted with seed-lac, arranged to catch condensation that drools down the clay and funnel it into runnels lined with straw. In corners hang gourd cisterns with stitched mouths that burp as they fill and dump, their overflow guided by braided channels to soak beds. Wicks run like veins from basket to basket, carrying warm trickle by capillarity. Now and then, at the strike of some unseen clapper, a set of leather valves flick, and all at once water runs uphill for a heartbeat—the drums reversing a slope—and you watch a string of beads pause their descent, shiver, then continue with greater fervor once the beat passes. It is the kind of thing that would be a miracle anywhere else. Here it is a scheduled behavior.

Vegetation has claimed every permission given. From the rope net rise thickets of small-leaved vines, their tendrils coiling around cord and cane, drinking from the wicks. Miniature ferns lace the damp clay in feathery fans that breathe like gills when drafts move past. Epiphytes cling to the ribs: pocket-run orchids with translucent petals that flicker greenish in the dim, mosses fat with water and glossed like animal tongues, and a sagging growth that is neither fungus nor plant, a living sponge of stitched fibers, seeded with yeast, that makes its own heat. Leaves never grow far without touching something else. It is lush without open volume, a saturation of green and glisten that affords few straight lines.

There is more sound than space. Always the thrumming pulse of the chamber skin under the wind. Beneath it a metronome of drips and dumps from the gourd cisterns, water tanging on ceramic bells hung where it must fall. Somewhere a hollow log ticks as its end swells and dries, its slow heartbeat winding gears carved from wood and thread. There is a chorus of wet noises: sucking, seeping, a patter like slow rain that never departs. When one of the leather valves chatters, it releases a cough of steam through a reed, and the reed answers with a warble that turns the direction of drips for a moment. Your ears are busy catching rules long before your mind names them.

Rules are posted, not on tablets but in the matter of things. Here are some that announce themselves as you stand and fog and listen:

- Sound gives slope. A low strike draws weight one way, a high keen tilts it another. The builders have turned gravity into a shepherd responding to a whistle.
- Wet naming is binding. A thing called aloud while a droplet forms on it will keep the name until the droplet falls. This is why the mats are labeled with whispered syllables at dusk and at dawn.
- Fibers root. Any bundle tied to the frame for nine successive nights will put out hair and become its own frame. The ropes here have grown legs out of themselves. Every knot is a seed with a plan.
- Light is a timekeeper only. It does not ripen or wither anything; those tasks fall to beats and flows and the presence of watching eyes. This is why the lamp-fungi are left unharvested: they are clocks, and if cut the hours spill.

The tech that shuffles these laws is primitive in the sense that it admits no alloys or glass harder than hide. Woven baffles that you can swivel by hand alter the chamber’s internal winds. Ropes run over bone pulleys to angle reed-gutters by a finger-width. A drum hung like a moon in the center has a skin made of stitched membrane and beeswax; its voice is thrown into the ribs by mallets cut from antler and wrapped with grass. Every device is a thing that could be carried by two people or dragged, and yet their arrangement here makes the impossible bend.

Nothing here has had time to soften. Erosion hasn’t started its patient college. Clay edges are knife-fresh, wicker ends show bright rawness where cut, and resin plugs stand like tears too newly fallen to cool. The filled trays of soil have no crust or slump; they are piles like language being piled. The little ecosystems are being ushered into place rather than discovered. If you brush a wall, your sleeve comes away printed with whorls from the last bare hand that smoothed it.

For fauna, there are presences you have to earn. A single glass frog the size of a thumbprint sits cupped inside a leaf, its skin so thin its heart is a red bead. It will not move no matter how long you stare; it has never once known hunger here because food arrives by climate. A snail with a rack of fine antlers grazes the film of algae on a clay rib, its shell lacquered, its tracks shining; when the drum thunders, it turns its horns to feel the gravity and decides whether to advance. A seamstress-spider, whose web is made of spun sap and whose body looks like a drop of pitch, waits with the patience of tar. It hunts by listening to the slow walk of water and has been known to swallow a drip whole. You glimpse a shadow-fish once, swimming in the humidity where the air is densest—just a wrinkling, a fold where nothing should fold. Mostly, the life here is plant and process, rarely animal.

The heat is not the fire of combustion but of fermentation: great shallow bowls of chopped cane and leaf scum under mats of waxed felt, exhaling a continuous, friendly fever. You lift a felt and the breath fogs your vision, moist and eager, and for a moment you are lost in the vapor of living rot. The fermenters are tended by nothing with a face. Feather brushes on sticks hang above them to stroke down the blossoms that need urging, their motion set by pendulums of water that fill and empty and fill.

The chamber is so full that even time has to squeeze. Corners are where space gives up and tucks in its own edges. A stair that is really a ladder is also a shelf and a leak-catcher and a trellis. Between two trays there is a slit narrower than your hand; in that slit is a river that never goes anywhere but carries the idea of flow meticulously from left to right. You consider setting your finger into it to see what it does to your pulse and think better of it.

It is a built world with no natural substrate, but the word natural means only that no hand did it. Here, hands are imprinted in everything and that becomes the same as nature. The cliff to which this lung is lashed is not rock but a vertical delamination of timbers faced with clay, a false mountain or perhaps a mountain-in-progress, grown in place by stacking, tamping, song. Through the oculus you sometimes glimpse rectangular strata where the next chamber will be grafted. Out there, builders hang from braided nets, ant-sized at this scale, their mallets softening the wind’s bite into a steadier thrum so the law of slope will not wring them loose. They have simple tools—awls of bone, string, fire in clay pots—and with them they are writing geography in a tense script.

Rules of weather within the chamber:

- It rains from the middle. Where the central drum hums at a low C (or what, here, passes for C), a warm drizzle falls, feeding baskets set in a ring. When the pitch rises, the drizzle lifts like a veil and hangs above you, a ceiling-lake wobbling against a net, shedding mist.
- Dawn is a temperature, not a light. It arrives when the fermenters have eaten enough, a swell of heat that wakes the epiphytes and makes the frog blink. It can be called early with a shout if enough mouths make the same shape.
- Storms happen when someone makes a mistake in the knots. A slip near the valve strings can pull a corner of inside against a corner of outside and the chamber will thrash and beat as if its skin were full of fists. Then the builders come and sing the edges back into parallel and everything goes on as before, but a new rule stays. You can count the old mistakes in the thickness of the clay.

If there is erosion here, it belongs to the future. For now, everything is birth-fresh. You can hear the wet set of fibers becoming less like themselves and more like a place. The ecology is a choreography: leaf opening, valve clicking, drip walking, beat leaning, and the whole room answers to the mountain’s wind like a reed in a player’s mouth. People have made a pocket with weather and growth, hung it from the high world where the air itself is thin, and stuffed it until it hums. Strange behavior occurs in the margins because the margins have not agreed to be margins yet. It is not merely that the rules break; here, they are being braided, stitched into being by hands and heat and repetition.

There is no silence, but there are stillnesses. Put your ear against a rib. Hear the cane negotiate without language with the clay, the water debate with itself the proper path to take. Put your palm flat on a mat. Feel the slow push of roots claiming the rope you stand on, the rope deciding to be less like a rope and more like a spine. Whisper a name for a leaf and watch the droplet that forms on its tip tighten and then fall when you are done. The droplet strikes a bell and, obedient to that small sound, a trickle two trays over inclines itself and changes everything in its path by a hair. Even reality, here, is hand-height and adjustable, and you can leave fingerprints on it if you are careful and hot enough.
