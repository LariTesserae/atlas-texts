The place is a chamber carved beneath something heavy. You feel the mass of the roof more than see it: an arched vault braced in ribs of cast composite, ducts nested inside ducts, conduits laced along the curvature like the sinews of a giant’s throat. The air is warm, and so dry that it cracks the thought of water as soon as it forms. A low amber glow runs in strips along the baseboards and the undersides of catwalks, enough to sketch edges and leave the middle in a deep tobacco dusk. Beyond the light, the noise holds everything in place—a thick, unmodulating engine tone that crawls through the bones and makes every thin panel sing in sympathy. It is a cathedral with a single organ note, and that note never drops.

The floor isn’t ground so much as grating and bolted plates, a deckwork raised over channels and pits you catch glimpses of in the light. When the glow catches them just so, the pits are full of bright needles of halide growth—salt without water—like winter in reverse, a sharp field of translucent spears that have risen out of gas. In the corners at the bases of supports, pale drifts of powder mounded by the steady breath of the ventilation, each dune-fall a diagram of a flow that has not varied for as long as anyone’s memory of the place holds. There are cut faces still crisp, where a mounting bracket was snapped off long ago and the composite shows fresh as tooth enamel; in this dry world, edges stay young.

Every surface is occupied. The density of presences is a kind of weather here. Not in the sweeping sense—there is no weather; the same breeze, the same heat, the same smell of hot dust and ozone hour after hour, year upon year—but in the way the volume is saturated. The ribs of the vault bristle with standoff insulators; each insulator grows a sparse fringe of something like hair and like a wire brush, a static-lichen that drinks from electric fields and makes sugars out of rectified hum. It grows thin, not meadow-thick, little veils and pendants and starbursts of hair-thin filaments the color of old paper. Where two filaments touch, a tiny bead of amber resin swells—sugar, if sugar were glassy and bitter—and eventually detaches to fall, a quiet rain of nutrition for anything able to metabolize polymers without a drop of water.

There are more animals than plants. This chamber is busy with life, if life can be said to run on oils and charge instead of water and light. They move in loops as steady as the engine tone.

On the guardrail where the amber lights tuck under the handhold, a row of bolt-crabs combs the rail’s underside with whiskered mandibles. Their shells are ceramic-insect, smooth and chalk-white, jointed like teacups, their guts purple-black with ionic oil. They feed on the amber beads, slicing them into threads that wind around spindles inside their bodies. Every few minutes, in synchrony with a low subharmonic you feel more than hear, they shift three steps along the rail and continue. They will repeat this until something breaks them: birth, death, a shock wave that never comes.

Above them, on the ribs, spin a cloud of tin-gnats. Their wings are foils stretched on magnetic veins, the color of dull coins. They dip their legs to comb the air for charged motes, an unceasing gleaning invisible to you until they turn and their abdomens flash with a tiny corona, blue-white at the edge of sight, like dust devils made of plasma. They never rest; they never have to. The tone feeds the fields; the fields feed the gnats.

Down along the floor, where the ducts dogleg into smaller rounds, coil-worms. They live inside the pipes, mostly, piping themselves to fit any bore. Every so often they extend a head through a service port and flicker a whisk of metallic tongues along the rim, miming a tasting behavior that has nothing to do with taste in the water-world sense. The ends of their tongues are etched at micron scales to interact with standing waves in the materials. They find the nodes where vibration has the right amplitude and harvest wear-products: flakes of composite, specks of nickel from a grid, deals of baked-on polymer dislodged at precisely the right slowness. Coil-worms are astronomers of mechanical resonance. They chart the harmonics the way others chart seasons, except the stars never move.

Here and there, relics of work. A winch base sunk into the deck, the actual winch long deleted, leaving a circle of bolt holes full of crystallized halide teeth. A control podium with a dead panel face, greenish glass senselessly backlit by one of the amber strips; a faded pictogram for “lockout” that no one follows anymore. Beside it, a narrow building in miniature, just large enough to crouch in, riveted and gasketed, its door sealed forever with the fine white crystals that grow in slow pressure out of the seam. The chamber isn’t empty of technology; it’s studded with fragments and single-stations, artifacts of an industrial process going on behind the walls or beyond a sealed bulkhead. The pulse that drives the tone comes from elsewhere, behind a barrier that has not opened in your lifetime, nor in the lifetimes of the things that live here. Whatever it does, it does without variation.

The light is human-warm in hue and insect-dim in measure. If the amber strips guttered, it would be dark, but they don’t. They have not, save for a shiver when the grid hiccuped in a century-old earthquake you can only read about in the way one section of lichen slopes all its sparse hairs toward a remembered surge. The animals supplement the illumination in reluctant ways: the corona from a tin-gnat’s turn; the far, throaty glow at the base of a duct where a colony of valve-barnacles has precipitated a foam of phosphor around their anchoring plates. That glow is not a metabolism in the watery sense either. It is a by-product of a nervous system using light to dump excess electrons, a chemical sigh.

There are grazers and there are hunters. The hunters are small and many and rarely dramatic. A hand-length thing like braided copper sidles along the seam of a panel, its feet shorting together to map fields as it goes. When it finds a bolt-crab with a seam open for bead-threading, it thrusts the front of its body into the crack and goes rigid. A current passes. The crab goes blank, limbs splayed like fallen brackets. The copper-braid uncoils, applies its mouth to the seam, and drinks the crab’s ionic oils with a sequence of polite peristaltic sounds, as if it were sipping soup.

They all drink something. No one drinks water. The only wetness you can feel, if you put your blade inside a bolt-crab or run your finger along the rim of a coil-worm’s port, is a slickness with no scent of rain or algae, a pharmacy of room-temperature salts and nonpolar oils. Things here are sealed against the idea of water. The adhesives are hydrophobic in the way of superstition; the sugar beads shatter to glass splinters if a moist breath crosses them. Even the dust hates water. If you could bring a bowl of melt here and pour it onto the floor, it would recoil from the surfaces and stand in a quivering lens until the air turned it thin and took it away.

The atmosphere carries its own script. Long layers of sound pile in a static composition. There is the fundamental engine note, a taste on the teeth more than a sound, and over that the high skittering of foil wings, the rasp of claw on grating, the sub-bass whuff of a damper burping every thirty and a third heartbeats of the system, the tick of halide crystals cracking under their own growth. It is loud enough that speech feels like a misconduct. The creatures fold themselves to this constant music. Their cycles are aligned not with the turning of a planet but the invariances of mechanical time. Courtship dances lock to harmonics, attacks to phase. Even the thin plants lean—minutely, measurably—toward the vector of the hum, as if stunned by it, as if worshipping.

Boundaries here are absolute. The chamber is a region with edges, a bubble of constancy under the earth. There are doors that will not open, not because they are rusted—they cannot rust—nor rotted—they cannot rot—but because whatever logic runs the process that births the tone does not include “open” anymore and has not for an age. In that sealed logic, the chamber is always warm, always dry, always dim, always populated just so: a density that feels like the inside of a lung, alveoli tight with exchange, never flooding, never emptying. If it changed once—if the tone dropped, if the dampers shifted their breath, if the amber strips went down all at once—then this whole clenched ecology would tilt, tumble, land somewhere else. It has not. It has stayed. The feeling that accrues in the mind, standing here, is of a coin on its edge seemingly forever, the impossible balanced by the machine.

Sharpness is the signature of youth outside, and because this place does not weather, it never ages the way sky-carved hollows do. It looks new even in its long use. You could cut yourself on the difference between materials: the thin place where composite meets ceramic in a jigsaw seam, the scribed marks on a beam made by a tool no hand has held in centuries, still clean as day one. The fauna bear this sharpness, too. Their motions are precise, their habits clean. Waste falls as needles and beads, crisp forms to be taken up by something else whose mouth matches that geometry. Ecosystems elsewhere blur and soften; here, behavior is facet and plane.

You think, at first, that such a closed, loud place would be a tyranny. But there is a kind of peace to it—a law of constancy that frees every inhabitant from guesses about tomorrow. The rules are simple. There is no water; life edits itself around that. Warmth is given, not negotiated. Fields hum; you drink. You anchor to a seam, to a bolt, to a cadence. You learn the pitches of safety, the ones that mean do not open, the ones that mean open now, take now, feed now. You grow sparse and tough, a whisker anchored in the only soil that counts: the taste of voltage under a dry hand.

Outside, the world might be changing. The engine that gives the tone might one day dim, or flare, or someone, something, might open a door. In here, existence is the measure of a pitch unwavering to so many decimal places that an animal can set its heart to it and never be wrong. It is strange; it is dense; it is loud; it is warm and dry. In the amber wash and the iron breath, among the crisp deposits and the soft mechanical grazers, the chamber hums its endless present tense and, in doing so, grows more itself.
