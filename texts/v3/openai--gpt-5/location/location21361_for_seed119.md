You step into a made-lowland, a room the size of a grove, walled on all sides and open to the sky only in grilles and slits. Everything you see is shaped by hands: stone laid tight as scales; clay tiles drilled with slow, regular pores; wood beams blackened by wet years; rope nets drawn overhead like a second, sagging firmament. There is no soil, no rock face, no unworked anything. The place has long since come to an agreement with time. Every edge has been taught to round. Channels wear softer than the walls. Lips of basins gleam from a thousand thousand touches of water.

It is cool. The air grips your skin with that just-above-cold that keeps its own breath visible in certain corners. The whole court never quite dries. Even in silence you would hear it drip, but this place does not know silence. It speaks itself into rain.

The sound is the first rule. Wind runs through throats cut in the walls—cylinders of baked clay fitted like organ pipes, slotted stone hollows tuned to an alphabet of moans. The court takes the moving air and turns it to a layered voice: a steady of low, hollow roar, under it a hive of flutes, above it small bright notes where little apertures whistle. The sound strikes the stone and the stone replies. On the inside faces of the walls, shallow ribs and scallops interrupt the laminar slide; the air eddies and cools, and the ribs bead with water. Each bead blooms, runs, and threads down to find a groove already waiting. The floor is a book of those grooves, printed in patterns like ribs of fish: channels finger-wide leading to latticed drains, drains falling into jars sunk beneath the tiles. The jars give back another voice—a upwelling chuck and a belly-echo as drops hit the water and set the bellies to speak.

The court gathers a weather of its own. The nets of plant fiber stretched high through the open sections of roof are laden with dew, slick strands silvering in the dim light. Light is rationed—the day comes in thinly from knife-slits high in the walls and from those netted oculi. The stone is a matte grey-green where lichens and algal paint have taken; where hands and water have smoothed it, it shines like seal skin in rain. In a rare angle, sunlight might skim along the lip of a basin and find a whole constellation of droplets hanging from one rope knot.

Vegetation is deliberate and scant. No tree has ever rooted here, nowhere to root. Filaments of moss knit where stone lips meet stone. Sparse patches of maidenhair fern find allowances in fitted corners that catch shade and overspill. White filmy lichens map the damper exhalations of the wall pipes. Someone long ago lashed bundles of reeds up on the ladders and set them to drink; the reeds never took, but their brittle ghosts now wick the air and bead all the same. Far overhead, a living roof of rope is the only canopy that sways as if leafed. On a calm day, the nets sag with quiet beads; in a gust, they pepper the floor with soft rain as if convincing the sky to continue.

If the stone and wood teach the wind what to say, the animals keep time. There are more of them than there are plants. Frogs throng every step-down ledge and the rims of the cistern mouths. They are small, many-colored in damp-muted ways—oiled-olive, slate, muddy coin. Their throats are white balloons flexing and deflating in an unending call-and-answer with the wall flutes, like a chorus trying to match pitch. The air is thick with gnats and small moths that gather at the brightest slits, only to be harvested by little brown swifts that learn the wall pipes' octave and dart in and out like thrown pebbles. At dusk, the swifts give way to bats who string together the court in erratic knitting. Skittering in the wet between tile ridges, little crabs the color of old rope work without fear of shorebirds—there is no sky here but the net. A long, milk-skinned eel will haul itself up the slip of a worn stair if the water is high behind the wall grates; it finds the froglings fat along the lip of a jar and fits itself into silence under the lip. Even the snails seem engineered for the place: their shells are ribbed to sit a certain way in certain grooves, their bodies seep just enough to slick a path and coax a drop to slide where it is wanted.

This is an old system held together by primitive means. No iron, no cunning gear. Hinges are braided vine. Pivots are hardwood dowels hammered into sockets carved with patience. Shutters are thick leather flaps layered with wax and stitched with gut, and they drift as humidity swells them; they close the lesser throats when the court grows too wild and open them when the day starves. In one corner is a lever of oak with flat stones hung from it, a float that lifts when a cistern fills and pulls at a cord that tilts a gutter to a different jar. It is all counterweight and remembered proportion, simple toolwork made subtle by time and feedback. The place is not tended constantly, but it has been taught to tend itself in slow manners.

The built is so complete that even the seeming boulders in the center are shameless constructs—lumps of limecrete shaped in living imitation of river-worn stones, set to break the wind into smaller tongues. Their downstream faces are streaked in white where minerals have dried again and again, a script of seasons. You can see where pottery tiles have been replaced, miscolored odd in old mosaics; where a gutter was once patched with packed moss and now is tight with a clay plug; where a once-sharp chisel mark has gone to a smooth gutter under centuries of water.

Weirdness here is not a spectacle but a contract. The place believes in cause and effect you might not have signed. The more the court sings, the more it wets. When a lull falls—wind shifts, birds are absent, frogs hushed by a moving shadow of the aerial predators—the walls visibly dull. The sheen pulls back as if it were a living skin that lost heart. Then somewhere a frog starts again, a low pipe catches a traveling note and throws it out long, and the glitter returns in thin veins, blooming for no other reason than that sound called it. People who come, come with their voices low, and when water is needed badly they beat the hanging boards in the passage, stamp on the sounding steps. It is music at its most functional; the whole court is a drum that births dew.

Inside this remembered equilibrium, predator and prey run in pulses. A swarm hatches; the frogs gorge; the bats thicken overhead; bones of delicate moth wings collect in corner eddies; the eels grow. By morning the court has cleared itself of wings; by afternoon the pipes hum strong enough to fog the cool places. There are days when a cold, dense ocean mist fingers through every slit and the place goes blind. Then it rains even from the undersides of ropes, and the jars overbrim and overflow into channels that lead back out under the wall—fresh water, always outward, never to the salt. That is the other rule. The sea is near—its pressure hum is part of the resonance under everything—but brine is kept at the gate by a simple invention: heavy doors pinned with driftwood wedges that lift only when cistern-strain is high. Everything here has a job, even the emptiness. There is the clean space between wall and baffle, the long unoccupied hollows under the beams, the broad floor plate in the middle cleared of clutter. The emptiness is what lets the voices build; it is what keeps any one thing from owning the air.

Smells are mineral and clean with undernotes of rot that never triumph—a black leaf-rot without leaves, of frog-skin and bat-fur and wet rope long since rescued from the sea. Taste the air and your teeth find a grit of windborne salt and old fired clay. The chill here is not punitive. You can live in it if you keep moving or if you step into one of the side recesses where the wall is heavy and the built-in benches are warm by fact of less evaporation. Those benches, too, are stone.

Some presences do come to keep what cannot keep itself. A bundle of reed-brush leans in a doorway. A wooden rake, its teeth all different from replacements, lies on hooks under a tile cornice. On a shelf set high and dry, a row of clay cups calcified with old drink. Whoever used them, or uses them still, teaches the new frogs not to be afraid by simply arriving slowly. They knock once at the jamb—a low, habitual thock that slots into the court’s voice—and step in as if across a dock, eyes up at the nets, hand out to feel whether the ribs of the wall are beaded as they should be. They will clean the larger jars of silt with a gourd, lift the leather flaps and tap the throats until the pitch is right, shake the net to give the little crabs a feast. They are a piece of the machine and they know it.

And at the quietest hours, when even the wall voices soften and only the soft drip marking time remains, the place shows how far from natural that word here is. A frog clings to a rope knot high, heavy with drops. A bat sleeps under a beam, fur a minuscule thicket. The eel’s jaw juts from a jar like a dull bronze hook. The stone ribs glisten with sweat. There is no river under everything, no original bog. There is instead an idea, old as hand and cord: that wind and sound and surface can be arranged to pull water from a thin, stingy sky. The court makes that idea true until it is its own climate. It rains inside without storm, because long ago someone taught stone to listen.
