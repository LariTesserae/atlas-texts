Climb until breath goes thin and color falls away. Then step through a nick in the ridge-line and into a bowl so bright it seems to hum without sound. The place is small in the way a cathedral is small—held by its own walls, roofed by an empty, burning sky—and it is wet. Cold, always cold, yet wet as a tongue. Every face sweats. The stone here weeps.

Pale cliffs enclose it, old rock with the patient look of things long worked on. Freeze and thaw, drip and glaze have gnawed the cirque into organs and alcoves. Buttresses are fluted; ledges wear a rind of old ice, thick as fingernails. Where the mineral wet has gripped and let go for centuries, the stone is frosted with a porous, honeycombed crust—stone-lace, tufa and silica intergrown—so light your knuckles might tap music from it. Under constant weeping, stalactite curtains hang in ranks. The floor steps down in terraces, each a slick platform of sinter or welded snow, edged with dark gutters of open water whispering under glaze. Puddles—ink-black where the ice is clear—hold trapped sky, breathing slowly with the shifts of air.

The air itself is a crowded thing. Even on clear days, diamond dust sifts through the amphitheater: innumerable pin-ice hexagons, too fine to sting, drifting, always drifting, always moved by the slightest act. They make a brilliance without shadow; they silver lips, eyelashes, the fur on your gloves. Stand still and rime gathers on you in delicate whorls. Speak aloud and your words fall as a small wind; the crystals tilt and listen, then settle. The light is high and ruthless, fractured by the crystals and reflected off every pale surface until the bowl becomes a lantern. The sun does not feel warm, but every dark thing here drinks heat. Black rock burns holes in snow. A raven’s wing leaves melted arabesques where it brushed an overhang.

There is a town tucked under the gentler side of the bowl, close enough to the cliff to be in its shade when avalanches shrug the opposite wall. Houses are half-grottoes, borrowed from stone-lace and deepened by hand. Ice and brittle tufa are mortared with wetted snow and seal-fat, then lined with hide; thresholds are made from rib-bones and scraped slate to keep the slush from creeping in. From the cliff-face outward stretches a web of gutters and veils. Gutters are carved from shoulders of ice, hung on bone pegs and lashings of sinew, to carry the thousand constant drips into catch-pots. Veils are hide-woven screens dusted with charcoal; they drink light and coax condensation, then the water runs down to waiting cups. The gutters shift daily, sometimes hourly; families shepherd their personal drips the way others tend sheep, moving weirs by inches to keep the flow steady. There are walkways dowelled into the sinter, narrow as an outstretched hand; rope-lines, many of them, run overhead so that one can pull oneself around the bowl without the slip of boots on glazed stone.

Everything is made by hand. Bone awls, knapped stone scrapers, wood so precious a single dry branch is a dowry. Fuel is rancid and slow—rendered oil, the pressed residues of things that graze the walls—and fires are mostly kept under hides, in little breathing hearths that warm fingers and tools but do not blacken the ceiling. Those who live here wear the light on them: pale leathers, seals of glazed linen, faces greased to keep the rime from biting. Their hoods sport small whisk-brooms; every few minutes, without thought, people brush their own shoulders, their neighbors’ sleeves, tools, ropes—anything likely to gather frost and lock.

Despite the hush, the bowl is busy to the point of turbulence. Nothing is still. Water creeps; films thicken and shear away; spalls crack from overbuilt ice with a sound like a drum struck far off. The light wavers as cloud shoals slide overhead and the diamond dust blooms or thins. The townsfolk move constantly because they must: adjusting a gutter by a finger’s width to keep a drip from scattering; hitching up rope to reroute the walkway around a new puddle; catching a water-sheet in a hide and flipping it into a jar before it becomes icicles; brushing down walls before the rime grows toothy. A market appears around mid-bowl on the safest terrace, and because the safe place is a shifting thing, the market itself is a migration. Stalls are sleds; goods are kept in nets hanging from overhead lines. Merchants mouth prices and sign the numbers with gloves, twinning their hands like swifts. Children run errands on leashes of braided sinew so they don't skid out onto black ice.

It is quiet. So much motion, so many presences, and yet the sound is the small hiss of crystals settling, the innumerable, uneven metronome of drips. The bird-sound is wing in air, not voice: a soft surf of feathers. Sometimes far up a face, a cornice cleaves off, and the whole place holds its breath while the mass sheds its weight in velvet sliding and soft thunder. After, the only thing left is the patient tacking of drops into cups. Voices are saved for emergency and song, and songs are sung close, into a cupped hand, so no one startles the wall.

Vegetation is a rumor in the rock. There are black lichens in seams, polka-dots of green gel on the undersides of droplet-lips where spray cushions them from frost, and tight gray cushions of moss that drink sun and fog, their blooms the faintest blush of rust. Under thin ice, blue films writhe—microbial sheets slick as eel-skin, thickened by mineral wealth in the seepage. They are the pasture. Many of the things that live here graze them.

Fauna is abundant, small, white, clever with surface. Rill-mice like gloved hands, with splayed fingers for toes, flip from water-film to water-film and disappear into caves the size of a thumb. Their nests are packed from thawed gel-moss; their eyes are faceted to drink light. Cliff-runners—hare-thing, lichen-gray—keep to diagonal seams of sinter, toes splayed huge as if someone pressed them while the creature was soft. They eat the black dots off stone. Where a veil has been left unwatched, it will be nibbled overnight by beetles shaped like folded letters, chewing condensation for the salts within. The air is thick with small wings unfelt until they pass your ear. Rime-moths do not fly so much as rest constantly and change their resting: six steps, a press of crystal-laden wings against your sleeve, then on. They look like squares of frost when they still. Above them all flicker glass-swallows, a kind of bird that has learned this brightness is a hunting-ground. They draw lines in the diamond dust as they drink from dripping stalactites mid-hover. In the pools, if you kneel and shield the glare with a hand, long white amphipods loop like falling stitches; when a rock weeps stronger and the film deepens, eel-thin things, blind, luminous as milk, slide out to feed, then vanish back into the stone, which is not so solid as it looks.

Predators here are weightless, patient. The biggest thing you will see is a snowcat like a ripple that decides to stay. It is more shadow than fur, a warmth-shape that hangs under a drip and licks the water without moving, and in stillness grows rime. In two days it looks carved of frost. Then it twitches, sends a crystal avalanche down its flanks, and leaves a melt-print where it slept that shows for a week. Smaller: white-red bats that hang in the mouths of weep-caves and take moths from the palm. Froth-skunks that swim through seal-fat and water indiscriminately and leave footprints of sheen on the stones. Springtails in their millions on the surface of puddles, a breathing carpet. All of them live by the drip and by films and by the small accidents of heat in this bright cold.

The rules of the place are simple and stern, learned early and kept in the body.

- Brush, always. A hair-thin rind becomes a weight. Untended, a tool left leaning upright fuses to wall and must be chipped free; an animal asleep becomes a statue.

- Dark is dangerous. Even a strip of black cloth can eat enough sun to melt a trench under it. Wear pale. Where you must carry dark stone, wrap it.

- Never trust a new dripline. The way water threads down the stone changes with a sun-breath. Spend a noon with it before you build.

- Listen with your eyes. Sound is treacherous here. What looks like silence is often a lot of quiet work. The snow tells you more than voices do.

- Move lightly, but move. Things that stay get grown on, draped, claimed.

There is strangeness here beyond the practical. Light behaves oddly. In the bowl on bright days, everyone has a ghost that stands beside them: a second silhouette of frost and gleam, offset in the diamond dust, which copies your gestures with a fraction of a heartbeat’s delay. Heat makes halos. You will sometimes see a soft ring blossom around a raised hand, then sink as the skin cools. Some mornings a hole opens in the dust over the mid-terrace where the market gathers—a clear, empty space shaped like speech—and the first traders arrive and take it as omen to be brief. Water likes to climb. Thin films will walk up a vertical face to reach a cooler overhang before they thicken and fall, and those who tend drips learn to stroke the rock and invite the film to their cup the way beekeepers teach bees the path to a hive.

Erosion is an old story here, a story with no crisis. The cliff-face has its own breath. Ice grows tooth, loses it, leaves ghosts in the stone where it gnawed. Channel-lips are rounded and worn, and the floor-terraces’ edges bulge like the lips of old fountains. The tufa sighs into arches and spokes; some have been undercut and made into doors; some have been coaxed into cisterns that leak along a line an elder can read like a map. The town does not fight this; it moves inside it. Houses slump forward when their sinter shelf creeps, then get wedged up with a new bone, then, when the bone freezes in, someone reskins a different grotto and the old place becomes storage and, a year later, a rind-like bench. Children learn to recognize a hollow tone in a floor and to take a different path without being told.

The bowl has boundaries that feel like rules made flesh. One notch opens to the ridge, but the air there is a razor-fine wind that shaves faces raw, and the light beyond is a bleached white plain of cloud where even ravens look unsure. Another opening is deeper, a black mouth of a cave where the rock is more sponge than stone. Behind its hairline waterfall is a warren warm enough for skin. Some days it speaks; not in words, but in a damp churn, a reverse rain, as if the stone were inhaling. Those who go deep to harvest blue film for medicine return with crystals that sing when touched and say the walls sweat dreams. Trade is shaped by these limits. Wood and heavy things come up only occasionally, with caravans of pale, slow pack-animals that make little noise with their broad, splayed feet; hand-carts run along the threadways the other days. Goods in the market are small, wet: salted fat tied in bladders; ice-lenses to watch a child's ghost with; fist-sized loaves of gel moss; veils woven with coarser or finer meshes, each a promise of a different pace of water.

At night, the bowl seems to grow brighter. Moonlight turns the diamond dust into rivers. The gutters tick more slowly, then stop, then one begins again, maddeningly irregular. Far up the wall, a glowing seam opens where latent heat leaves the cliff. Things creep out to drink it, and then the seam is gone. In the small hours, when the cold rides steady and no one moves, there is sometimes a sound: a single, bass drum in the belly of the stone. After, a hairline crack is found spanning a terrace, neat as a drawn line. The oldest people say the place is tuning itself. The young laugh and sign We too.

With morning, the first sun catches the cliff shoulders and tiny winds lift, born of melt, too small to make noise but big enough to wake the dust. The day sets its own bazaar tempo: gutters to tilt, films to herd, skins to cure, moths to brush out of veils, children to haul on their belly boards through the safe channels, endless small salvations. It is a wet place, and a cold one, and bright beyond comfort. Humble technologies hold it together. Animals throng it. It is turbulent, yet the turbulence is the motion of many small steadinesses, constantly failing and remade.

If you leave by the notch at evening and look back, the bowl will still be working, a lantern of white breath and flickering movement pressed into the side of the world. It does not ask to be noticed from far. It has made its own rules and its own harvest from rock and air, and it keeps to scale: the size of a voice sung into a hand, the size of a city that can fit under a cliff and outlive its drips.
