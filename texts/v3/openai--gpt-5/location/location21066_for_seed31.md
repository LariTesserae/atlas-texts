At this altitude the air behaves like a slow, warm sea. It beads on every edge, gathers on knuckles of stone, slides in narrow veils from broken faces. Rocks sweat. The thin soil sweats. Even the pale wood of old platforms and the dark ropes strung between posts carry a skin of trembling droplets, quivering in the faintest breath.

The land is high, a raised table with a crisp profile, young in its bones. Ridges lift with an almost knife-like certainty; gullies break away abruptly. There is no softness of long wearing. Fresh planes of fracture flash where a cliff has recently let go a slab; a cobble carries its conchoidal scar like a memory from last season. You can read the youth of it underfoot: the way the ground drops suddenly into slots, the way a field of little ridges—grey karren, teeth of limestone—seems to have just been unwrapped from the ocean that once covered it. In places the rock is darker, glossy when wet: a sheet of basalt that once poured in a curtain and set as columns but hasn’t yet learned how to weather into gentler forms. The two sit together without apology—pale, pitted carbonate, black glassy cap—caught mid-argument, and the argument hums with water.

There is a lot of water. Not lakes. Not great rivers. The water follows the habits of the air. It condenses. It threads. It drools from seams. It hides near the surface as a soaked memory more than as depth, and then reveals itself, all at once, when your weight presses down on a hummock and it flexes. A hundred thousand discreet moments of wet shine across the landscape: rills snaking across bare stone and vanishing at the first sink; moss that keeps a quarter inch of sky imprisoned in every little cup; peaty hollows glazed with blackness. On the carbonate, water cuts quick slots and then is gone, reappearing at a cliff-face as a beard of springs, weeping, weeping, brightening the mineral to sugar-white as new tufa grows like lips. On the basalt, it cannot find a way through; it sits and thinks, and the thinking becomes mire—sphagnum thick as mattress, sedge lawns as resilient as a drumhead, scum-limned, still. The land is freckled with this: perched on the high world, each depression a small wet certainty.

Warmth wraps the place. Not hot—nothing fervent—but a steady body temperature for the world itself. Breath steams only because it is laden with moisture, not because of any cold. In that warmth, plants have learned the rules of water and air. Lushness is a quiet word here. There are no heavy crowns of trees, no knitted shade; instead, the living skin crouches low and continuous. Mats of sphagnum climb up the ankles of wooden posts, knit themselves into meters-wide quilts and push the world level where the stone won’t do it. Waxy-leaved shrubs root shallow and spread flat, a palm’s breadth over the sponge, each leaf perfect, never scalloped, no tooth-marks ever learned. Ferns unscroll from crack to crack in the young rock, their fronds unblemished lace, lifting spore-plumes that hang gold in the moist light for a heartbeat and then settle into the same predictable corners they always settle. Lichens rosette into maps—citron, charcoal, milk—on the dry faces that the fog only kisses. Tall sedges in the marshes lift their seedheads like brushstrokes and invest their hope in wind: there is no audience but air and water here. No insects to court, no birds to bribe. Flowers, such as they are, are fine and almost invisible, their pollen a dust that drifts, their petals a memory of something not needed.

Silence is almost a material. It collects in places the way water does. A great plaza of cut stone, open to the sky, can hold a bowl of it. A stand of reeds can filter it, so that only the thin whisper of leaf on leaf remains. The familiar ranges and blips of other worlds—wings, feet, cries—are simply not present. It is not even that they have gone; it is that the world has grown itself so thoroughly without them that you begin to hear new textures—how a droplet, falling from a plank’s edge, sets up a tiny ringing in a ceramic basin below, and that ring fuses with the soft palming of water down a mineral lip two terraces away. A rope, fat with absorbed mist, doesn’t creak; it just rests. The absence of scrape and gnaw and pad is so normal that the faint roll of a stone under its own young face can seem like a violation.

Across this high wet, a city has been set down with the same restraint as the plants. It does not press; it rests. It does not occupy; it touches. The built things are spread with a sense of space that feels less like prudence than like a pact. Clusters of structures island around reliable weeps and perched pools, each cluster an atoll of wood and stone within a sea of sedge and peat. There are terraces: shallow-stepped platforms cut directly into the rock, their rims chamfered not by time but by careful picks. Each terrace is guttered, notched, and ledged; the land’s own habit of moving water is elaborated with human geometry until every wooden eave drips into a small carved runnel, and each runnel offers its thread to a basin, and basins to cisterns, and cisterns to sloped-open tanks where the sun has no particular work to do but where water sits as it likes, warming.

You can read the craft in every joint and groove. There is no glitter. Pegged mortise holes bind beam to beam. Lashings of plant fiber stripe the poles and turn dark with long damp. Boards are hewn, not sawn, their surfaces smooth where hands and rain have held them and still visibly faceted. On the stone, the wedge-marks are fresh and rhythmic. Someone took a hammerstone to this face in a pattern they had learned from a parent, and the pattern is there as clearly as a fingerprint. Clay exists as vessels and as flooring: low-walled rooms with earthen floors have been scaled with a skim of lime and polished until they reflect a milky ghost of the sky. Low kilns—it is easy to imagine their mouths, but they are cold—hide under overhangs near stands of brush with good, straight stems for weaving. Reed-mats, knotted by a memory you do not possess, serve as doors and screens and walkways laid end to end across the more treacherous patches of bog.

Infrastructure abides everywhere and nowhere. A causeway of slabs strides across a shallow valley where boots would otherwise drown, its gaps sized to shed water quickly and to keep the sedges from taking it back too fast. Over a gully, a bridge of interlocked timbers squats rather than spans; each piece has been dragged up, not lifted by pulleys or beasts, its weight written in the gouges along the approach. Stairs climb to the cliff-edge where the weep-lines are thickest; along them stand lattices of woven twigs and cloth hung over frames so that the day’s condensation is caught and taught to flow along cordage into jars. The city is an essay in encouraging water to do what it already does, only a little more so, and in the direction of a waiting hand.

It is a city of distances. Walk a quarter hour across open ground—sedge, moss, fissured limestone—and you pass from one knot of life to another. A platform of roofs set on piles over a permanent mire. A low wall tracing a circle, the circle filled with soil more brown than black where something is coaxed to grow that will not root in raw peat. A ring of upright stones drilled through with thumb-sized holes, each hole draining into the next’s little cup, a slow, gentle clock of trickles. Between these, space. The open shape of the place makes it legible from far off: you stand at the lip of one sinkhole and see across herds of nothing to the next, where a terrace cuts the sun, where a flight of steps holds a perfect line against the slightly moving air.

At the edges the plateau forgets about patience. The land ends with a bluntness so categorical that the mouth goes dry. Springs, trapped by the impervious flows, gather along a band and then race, chained by gravity and by their own surprise, to the cliff. They do not leap; there is no drama. They mostly just pour and then fall, a white thread into a permanent fog-mass that moves along the precipice like a living wall. The sound there is a softened constant, the world’s only crowded noise. Ferns, pad after perfect pad, make green tongues in the dripline. No animal holds to the pale stone beyond them. No bird cuts the wall of mist. The falls plummet and disappear and whatever happens to them afterward is not something that arrives back here in any form you can hear.

Time does not make itself known much. The waterline stains on the cisterns are without variance. The reeds are always just taller than your hip. The fog that arrives each day does it at the same hour and stops at the same invisible shelf: below, a ocean of cloud in procession; at this height, the tendrils; above, an exposed blue that never seems to toughen into blazing. There are no seasons in the living sense—not the feasts and flights and barkings of elsewhere. Change is measured in mineral lip thickening by a finger’s breadth over a lifetime, in moss pushing into the marks a tool left in spring. The rules present themselves as a calculus of edges and water: everything condenses; everything drains; everything returns. Nothing startles. Nothing flees.

Even in its quiet, the place communicates what it expects in the long run. Wood that is always damp becomes a dark satin, its grain blurred. It humbles quickly if unwatched; mushrooms, which do not count as animals here any more than lichen does, write white into it like snow, then brown. Stone remains truer. The city prefers stone for the permanent, wood for the parts that must give. Doors and screens are replaceable; terraces and basins are not. The craft responds: all permanent water paths are rock; all variabilities are woven. There is a trust in the steadiness of climate: that the same slow rain of air will come, that the edges of the cisterns will always hold level, that the cloud at the cliff will not decide to rise higher or fall away.

Because there are no animals, there are no bridges sized for hooves, no fences that speak of teeth. Grain-stores are built high not against pilfering paws but against the water and against the surrender to rot. Seeds are gathered not to outwit beaks but to outwit their own tendency to end up only one or two terraces away. Falling, in this world, is for water and for leaves. Everything else is an argument with gravity that is undertaken with patience and with lashed joints.

Stand, then, under one of the woven fog-catchers and let the slow drip pace into a cup. The cup is rough on the lip and smooth inside. Around you, distance. The city extends not as a compression, not as density, but as invitations put down in the wet at measured intervals: steps, ledges, hands that wanted to hold. The terrain has not decided yet what its mature shape will be; it is all young intentions and sharp gestures. The atmosphere settles in at once and forever. The vegetation repeats its successful sentences, word for word, with only small shifts in emphasis. The water is the only thing that moves with any conviction, and even it seems content, most of the time, to be captured, guided, held glowing for a while in a basin, before it accepts the plunge.
