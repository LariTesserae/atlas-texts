It starts as brightness and pressure, a place you don’t so much enter as step into the reach of. The sky here is raw and close, the color of a well’s deepest water; the stone underneath is pale enough to glare. Air carves the palate clean and leaves it tingling, thin, cold, and dry at once, and carried on it is a sound so continuous you don’t notice its beginning. You only realize you’ve come under it when your chest starts to buzz.

The land is the rim of an uplifted body, a high circumvallate table where the horizon rarely dips. At its edge the table breaks into a necklace of amphitheaters, vertical miles of pale dolomite scooped and fluted, their faces grooved with the vertical writing of old runoff and older wind. Between the amphitheaters stretch parapets and causeways of karren: limestone pavement pocked and split by solution, a chessboard of clints and grikes, with their corners rounded by patient cold. The pavement has a memory of water though none moves across it; its grooves align with vanished trickles, and the solution pans—shallow basins the color of bone—hold what the day condenses and the night refuses to give back.

Most water here is a trick of contact. Moist wind is dragged upward from valleys so far below you can’t see them, and as it crosses the rim it cools just enough to bead on stone. A black film of algae slicks certain faces: that’s where the flow, never more than a sheet, never less than damp, moves a finger’s breadth in a year. In shadowed grikes you find beads of ice in summer and needles of hoarfrost even at noon on days when the sun is too bright to look at. On the downwind side of buttresses the air stagnates for a moment and offers up its load; there, rock hollows brim a few days at a time, then dwindle to damp circles staining the pale.

Follow any dripline and you come to the edge, where the land stops with decision. Here, the mountain speaks.

The cliff face is scored with cylindrical vents, some no wider than a wrist, others big enough to stand inside and turn around—stone flutes bored through by ages of condensation, frost-jacking, and the patient fall of gravity. Within the mountain, caverns egged out by old seas conduct the wind. Outside, the topography catches it, pinches it, and accelerates it. The two systems meet in those vents, and what tears through them has long since found its pitch. The whole rim sings a many-throated chord, not a melody but a held thing, as fixed and inevitable as gravity. It is not a scream or a howl but an organ note broad enough to fill the chest cavity. At the level of teeth it is a hiss; at the level of gut and rib it becomes a pressure, and at the surface of the world it is simply the way this place exists—with an insistence that strips lesser sounds of their names. A handclap is an idea your palms have in private.

Bright is an inadequate word for the light here. It comes in tangles and sheets and needles. Every edge casts a little sun of its own because there is nothing to catch the light and dull it: no air thick with dust, no forest to drink its edge. Dolomite glitters with mica flecks; ice rimes each tiny lip and sills it with an enamelled flare. Even the shadows have a hard, sky-colored brightness, standing water becomes a mirror held at the sun.

Vegetation endures, which is not the same as thrives. There are lichens that have been working the rock so long their margins describe the centuries—black crusts like scorch marks, greenish rinds like oxidized copper. Where a drip has varnished the wall for decades the stone is a petri dish: sheet algae in bottle-green and smoke-gray, with a few brave filaments of moss staging a slow coup in the middle. On the karren you find cushion plants shaped like careful buns, tight as knuckles, resisting the wind’s lever with density. Here and there a mat of saxifrage pretends to be stone and succeeds. Where a fissure offers an inch of shelter and a pinch of soil, a dwarf willow curls upon itself like an old thought. These are loner plants with a genius for existing where nothing else bothers.

The emptiness of the plateau isn’t barren so much as widely spaced. You can walk for an hour across the clints and see nothing that moves but the trembling of heat in the air above sunlit stone—then catch, in the same minute, a convulsion of life where the conditions gather. Look to the cliff and you’ll see it.

The rim is loud with wings. The updraft is a machine that never shuts off, and anything that can make a living on a moving stair has come to this stair. Insects ride the column like driftwood in a current—tiny, innumerable, conjured here by wind that has skimmed valleys for a hundred miles and brought up their aerial plankton. Swarms assemble in sun-sheets above drip faces, beating within a very close temperature and humidity they have learned like a room. They have shiny carapaces and fringed wings designed to take translation from the chord, to wobble and ride the rough edges of noise. Some of them are specialists that live nowhere else: dew-lickers with tongues like paintbrushes, dust-mites the size of commas that browse mineral sheen, tiny clear-bodied gnats whose larvae feed on algae wept from the stone.

And where there are insects in continuous quantity there are mouths. Cliff swifts with needles for wings sew back and forth through the sun; their bodies seem too slight to be anything besides a decision. Broad gliders—call them kites, though they are not precisely birds in the sense you expect—hang out of the wind with a thoughtless ease you could call serenity. Their bones are a scaffolding of air, their primaries ragged into a fringe that reduces buzz against the mountain’s voice. To talk to one another they do not call; they show, flashing the pale underwings that have evolved into flags, adjusting pitch to lay ripples on the light. Larger, stranger things work the edge too: a vulture analogue whose neck is bare and thick but whose ear openings seal under muscle so it can feed by the cliff without going deaf; a mammal with a spread of skin from wrist to ankle that makes a dim canard wing, springing from one grike to another to pick lichen and lick the minerals that sweat out of rock.

Abundance here has a geometry. It is rich in bands and empty in gulfs. A fissure will hold a territory’s worth of arthropods; a single overhang will be the dining room of generations of gliders; five paces to the left there is nothing, just stone and sound. The scale of the place allows both to be true—the volume is vast, and the life that fits into it is numerous but concentrated where the rules allow.

If anything like technology exists on the rim it belongs to the nonhuman. Swifts wedge glossy pebbles into crevices where algae sheets run, creating little turbulence pockets that enlarge the water’s hold. The gliders hammer at snail shells with their beaks against a particular ledge, a flat stone scoured bright by a thousand such dinners—the only polish in miles that isn’t made by ice. A small, blunt-faced corvid cousin steals those pebbles and hides them in different cracks, then steals them again, a game that is also a life. There are no structures here, only gestures that repeat down generations until they become part of the cliff’s manner.

Erosion has reached an accommodation. No fresh scars, no raw fans of scree. Frost shudders the stone, yes, but what falls has already found the basins where it needs to come to rest. Karst dissolves on a schedule older than winters. The vents, not new-cut but long-refined, hold their size; the tuning of the mountain’s voice has stabilized to fit them, and the air feeds back on the rock so that the song preserves its own instrument. When storm comes—and it does, in sheets that cross as lines—its work is to reaffirm rather than to alter. Rain here is more memo than manifesto. The world takes receipt of it and continues.

The air’s thinness doesn’t feel precarious. It is a permanent thing. Each breath is an agreement you sign again by choice. With that agreement you perceive rules that matter nowhere else more than here:

- Sound moves like weather; you navigate as much by acoustics as by sight. Animals have learned to put their voices in the gaps the mountain leaves, lower than the vents’ drone or higher than its hiss, or leave voices behind altogether and speak by wing and flash and motion.
- Moisture follows form; life grows where stone offers the exact right orientation to wind, not just any shadow or cup.
- Gravity is a traffic signal, not a law. Things fall in rehearsed ways: updrafts catch the act of falling and extend it into drifting, gliding, being held.
- Time runs on maintenance. Nothing here is hurrying toward a different shape. Patterns have settled into a long present, and life has fitted bracelets to the place’s wrists so that when it flexes, the same rattles sound.

All that brightness, all that noise, and underneath: a stable quiet of intent. The chord does not change, and so behavior doesn’t have to. The swifts trace the same figure-eights past the same vents every day; the gliders hold the same station alongside the column and peel off, one here, one there, to drink from the same rock pans, their beaks cutting the same ovals in scum that returns overnight. Under a particular overhang a moth explodes from its pupa the hour the sun edges just far enough around in the season to heat that ledge at noon. The wind and the stone make a system and life fits its applause into the interstices.

If you crouch in a grike out of the flow, where the wall shines with lichen and a smear of algae, the mountain’s note is less an assault and more a presence—like the humming of a large machine in a building you’ve lived in most of your life, which you only notice when you stop. The light pools are steady. The insects knit at the lips of water in the same patterns. You lift your head and the cliff’s edge cleaves the sky perfectly straight line by straight line; nothing flickers there except wing, and wing is constant.

It is an empty place in the way that high deserts are empty: few interruptions, everything standing far apart so each thing can be entirely itself. It is abundant in the way reefs are abundant: a choreography riding an engine. It is cool, the air condensed to clarity. It is bright to the point of absolution. And it is loud, always, the mountain loosing its unchanging breath out through its carved throats, a sound that presses outward into the world like weather and spreads its steady, patient rule across the rim.
