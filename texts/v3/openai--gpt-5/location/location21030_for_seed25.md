You arrive at sound before you arrive at shape. The place is a single, enormous throat clearing itself forever: a low roar that becomes a stratified storm of smaller noises as your ears adjust. Surf slams and un-slams somewhere close. Pebbles clatter and roll in troughs you cannot see. Shells knock against shells. A dry crackle runs along the stone like frost finding its seam, and underneath everything there is a continuous tapestry of living clicks, squeals, hissed conversations, wing-burrs, rasping and the soft drumming of countless feet feeling their way.

Light is a rumor. The air is cool enough to bead on lashes, cool enough that your breath appears only as a thought of damp. The roof is low—low enough to press the weight of the earth down into your shoulders—and close enough to hold the sound in, bend it back on itself. Darkness here has texture. It is wet at the edges, a gloss on rock, a wind that smells of iodine and ammonia and stone.

You’re standing in the mouths of a labyrinth of sea-caves at low elevation, close to the line where land meets ocean and refrains from deciding which it is. Limestone has been worked for a geologic patience: scalloped walls, tide-scored benches, niches round as bellies where past whirlpools spun and dug; flutes in the ceiling where old floods gnawed upward and lost interest. Everything wears the softness of long erosion—the combed, settled forms, nothing sharp except the lives that have fastened themselves here and made edges of their own.

Water is present by temperament, not possession. The high tide paints long, glossy stains where it rises to speak with the roof; down at your feet, brine sleeps in stone basins, black as a closed eye until the surf shoves a pulse through the tunnel and they tremble to a skin of spangles. When waves withdraw, they leave strings of cold breath on the walls, a condensation that gathers and descends by patient arithmetic. It drips into pans, runs along shallow gutters polished by generations of drips, and collects in cupped shelves where the rock, by some accident of angle, allows a lens of fresh water to float upon the heavier brine below. The fresh is clear, shockingly so. The salt is tea-dark with suspended things. In this one valley of sound and shadow, two kinds of water live a hair’s breadth apart and never become one.

There are no leaves. No fronds or frills of green articulate the rock. What coats the world instead are animals upon animals and their byproducts: sheets of barnacles layered like roof-tiles, their volcano mouths feathering at each surging breath; mussel-knuckles crowded shoulder to shoulder, sheened blue-black with damp; white gooseneck barnacles flexing from leather stalks; anemones folded tight into flinty knots, then opening as the next cold hand of the sea reaches them, gloved with translucent feeding arms. Where the tidal reach ends, furred bands of tiny limpets and chitons cling to their precise levels, tracking the history of splashes the way tree rings record storms.

Above the splash zone, the animals change but the crowding does not. The walls sweat, not with algae, but with bodies: pale amphipods that have learned a landward shuffle and swarm in dune-drift patterns; springtails like living punctuation; flat, blind crustaceans that move with the unblinking confidence of things that have arranged the world to touch them first. Threads of fungus lace the guano mounds that collect beneath the caves’ higher chambers, threads that shine faintly where the drips have painted them, but the green grammars of plants have no sentences here. Energy comes as drift and as wings and as the steady unraveling of what arrives.

The roof breathes too. In the recesses away from the sea’s reach, the rock is black with a fur of sleeping bodies—bats crammed together until gravity is a single idea they all agree on. Every few dozen heartbeats a nervous ripple passes through the mass: a susurrus of membranes, a cough of scraping claws, a hundred shrilled corrections that pierce the audible with their lower, rougher edges. You feel it as a prickle first, then hear it. When a wave hits the right geometry and throws a slap of air into the chamber, the whole colony answers, as if the place were an instrument and the animal is the note that fits it.

In between the bats and the beads of water, small brown birds have threaded their nests into crevices—a fog of wings that leave and return in darkness, navigating by sound. They do not own the noise here; they are simply another vowel in its long word. Their calls are brittle, like pottery tapped with a fingernail, and disorientingly close.

Put a hand out and you touch both rock and the insistence of life. The stone is furred with calcareous detail, with shells and tests and sclerites, with spines that shiver under your fingers. Take one careful step and the floor whispers and then seethes, as if sand had learned to remember. Every inch has a thing that lives there and expects that you will not. Mites tunnel in the old shells of mussels. Centipedes, glossy and efficient, articulate the piles of guano and take what is soft. In the low, fresh pans—no larger than a helmet—something eel-shaped threads the water and freezes when felt, a white hunger with no eyes.

The rules are simple and stern. Light is not currency. Sound and touch are. Energy arrives in parcels carried by tide and wing: plankton and detritus and drowned insect, shed feather, the stuttered rain of guano from above. The community converts this into its own continuities. Filters become flesh; flesh becomes droppings; droppings become small funguses and pale bacteria; bacteria become the breath of the cave, a faint sour that is almost sweet. Nothing lies fallow for long. The first thing that pauses, even for a moment, accrues a film of settlers. Space is a resource more keenly contested than food. Bodies solve it by folding: under, over, inside, attached. The place reads as crowded and then keeps reading as crowded and you never find the end of that sentence.

Weather here has several tempos. Twice each day the reach of the sea pushes deep and recedes, the pressure wave of that departure collapsing in distant roars that speak through stone ribs. There are the rains that come seldom, cool enough that fog spools into the openings and shivers there awhile, stoking the condensers of rock until a new circuit of drips begins to write itself on the floor. There are the nights when the bats rise like a slow smoke you cannot see, only hear leaving, a subtraction that lifts pressure from your skin. They come back loaded with what the outside world shed, and their return is another kind of tide. And once or twice a year there are the storms that decide to enter fully, when the line of division breaks. Pebbles advance inland by an inch in an evening. Pools erase and reappear elsewhere. The place keeps its shapes by yielding to them.

Step further along and your boots meet a runnel that looks like ink. You disturb it with your toe. For a breath, the black flowers into a peacock spangle. The tiny, hunger-lit bacteria or plankton—whatever made it here in a wave and chose to glow at touch—are the only lamps allowed; they report motion and vanish. For a second you can see the question of a shore, the wetly scalloped lip where life is counting the seconds until the sea remembers this place again. Then even that is gone and you are back to the world measured in pressure and tone.

There is strangeness but not malice. The oddness is in the rules being different ones: no plants, no daylight arithmetic, no silence. Creatures that are fish are up on land pretending to be insects. Creatures that are birds have taught themselves to see with sounds. Creatures that are bats have fed an entire valley of invertebrates with what they cannot digest. The rock listens and respires with them. The economy of the place runs on the transit of wings and waves and wastes, and because those flows have been established for long enough, the forms here look inevitable.

If you linger long enough, you learn to navigate by its physics. The louder the percussive slap of water, the nearer the next tunnel mouth; the higher the clean ring of dripping, the richer the fresh seep above it and the thicker the lace of fungus and microbe; the more frantic the clicking underfoot, the faster the amphipods and isopods are repairing whatever you have just broken by moving. Out by the landward side, where the roof lifts a fraction and the air is not pushed in and out by the sea so directly, you feel a gentler draft: the exhale of thousands of sleeping things, faint and constant as the sea is not.

The world outside may be broad, and bright, and vegetated. Here the geography is made of near-touching bodies, of surfaces that slip between mineral and meat, of distances measured in echoes. It is cool. It is damp without surrendering to water. It is dark except when the place answers touch with floods of brief light. And it is deafening in the way a river delta of species can be, when every square handspan is working at once and the sum of that labor is a sound that becomes the weather.
