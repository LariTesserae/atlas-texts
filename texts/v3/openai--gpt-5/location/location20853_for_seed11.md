It is a country of height and age and overwhelming voice. You come up through switchbacks of shattered stone until there is almost no up left, only a table of rock laid out into the open brightness, crenellated with towers and buttresses scoured smooth by eras. The land has worn itself nearly flat at the top—an old, iron-hard skin of quartz and sandstone bearing shallow cups and pans, edges rolled and rounded, seams of an older world exposed like bones. At the rim it falls away in immense, sudden walls, kilometers of drop into hazy valleys, so that clouds pile below you in slow rivers and sun-lit floes.

It looks dry from a distance, the rock pale and sunstruck, but the air is never entirely without water. Warm wind heaves up from the lows, piles along the escarpments, and in its collision with all this ledged stone it makes fog, ropes of it, veils of it dragged sideways so fast the veils thrash like pennants. The plateau feeds itself on that vapor. Every cliffline throws a lee, and in those noise-shadows—the thin spaces where the wind’s teeth are blunted by the stone’s geometry—vegetation gathers as if poured there: beards of moss, pads of fern, slick black banking of liverwort, tangles of cyphelloid fungi, dwarf trees with thick, glossy leaves and roots braided into the fractures like fingers. Water never runs far, but it clings: in tinajas worn by a thousand ancient eddies; in slumped basins filled with the drip off leaves; in condensate caught by thread and hair and fiber. Plants curl to present as much edge as possible to the scud—every twig a comb the wind must set, every comb a place where mist catches and thickens and falls.

The sound rules everything. It is not a single note but a weather. When the first gusts come over the rim, they break in a roar like surf. Then the plateau’s millions of cavities and arches and columnar wells begin to speak: whistlings and ululations, beeswarm hums, enormous bassings you feel first in your ribs and knees. There are long, bone-smooth gullies that act as resonators; when the flows align you can see the grass-moss in them leap as if plucked. There are “throats” in the rock—old eroded potholes still connected by natural ducts—that make pure, steady tones for hours while clouds drag past. The whole place functions like an instrument, played by pressure gradients wider than the horizon. It is so loud that birds have to fly with their beaks open to equalize, so loud your own speech dissolves at your lips and never comes back.

The light is simple day: sun clear and warm on bare slab, shadows moving quick under the running cloud. Heat gathers in the flat beds, makes them radiant by midafternoon. Stand out in it and you feel the sun in the way you taste salt—absolute, easy to forget until it is too much. Step into the green lee and the tempered dark holds you. There is a permanent sense of distance here: details do not come until you are nearly on top of them, and the emptiness between the pockets of life is real space, great drifts of bare world where only thin varnish crusts the rock and the only sound is the thunder of air.

Everything alive knows and keeps to the lee. The dwarf forests are thick, damp, fragrant, the leaves lacquered by fine spray. Runners of vine knit mats over the rims of bowls to harvest drip; bromeliads with tight throats hoard a cup of clean water each, their inside worlds teeming with insect-larvae cities you rarely see. There are flowers, but almost never open at once; they wait for very specific lulls when the wind falls in the last light of day, and then they open all together—sudden, glistening, rain-sweet—and by morning they are closed and armored again. Pollen here is heavy and sticky, made to travel by touch. Pollinators are few and idiosyncratic: a nocturnal moth that navigates by the subsonic shapes of the plateau’s own harmonic fields; a shy, long-faced glider—more membrane than muscle—that coasts from pocket to pocket in the dawn lull, curling its body to catch drops the way leaves do. Now and then, from far below, a high raptor fights its way up through the boiling air to ride the lee curls along the walls. When it passes into the vents, its cry shreds into threads.

Fauna is sparse, cunning, and small. There is a rock-vole with paws like snarls of root, so dense-boned the pressure can’t burst it; it lives in the moss mats and never goes far from them. There is a pale, seal-eyed salamander that spends its entire life in the black cistern of one bromeliad, grazing the algae on the inside of the bowl. There are ant-lines that seem to go nowhere until you notice the turnings are exactly at the nodes where two hums cancel, the quiet gutters. Larger presences come rarely: twice a year a hollow-backed ungulate with a ruff like windgrass climbs up narrow false-staircases to lick the iron-sweet tears that rust out of an exposed seam; it stands and sways, eyes closed, while the plateau howls around it, then picks its way back down and is gone for months. The rest are phantoms you imagine because the place makes you want a witness.

The oldness of the stone is not an absence of change. The land is deeply settled into its shape, the bones and bowls planed down to their final diction, but the air is continual incident. Pressure-lines slither and congeal; gust-fronts arrive as walls that throw grit like hail, then, minutes later, flows reverse and all the grit is pulled the other way, ringing like a bell against the slabs. Sudden mist-rain runs like mercury through the dwarf groves; the leaves trill with it. Pans fill and overfill and never make a stream—they overflow into rock again, are taken into the sponges of moss, disappear. Sometimes a thunderhead overshoots and drags its belly here; the rain is hot and quick, and afterward every hollow smokes in the sun, all that warmth and dampness pouring back to the troposphere in curtains. At the same time the stone itself continues its glacial rearrangements: flakes peel; a face that had been smooth now shows a honeycomb of tafoni; a whole stack of fossil dunes, welded by pressure into stone, chooses a day to loosen and clatter down as a new terrace of broken sugarloaves.

Where the wind is fiercest there is a visible practice of survival. A few built things hide along the breaks and rills—small enough to be ignored by the world unless you know how to look. There are fog-nets strung in slots, woven by hand from pale bast and the glossy fibers of some highland reed; they nod and thrash in the gale and drip steadily into ceramic-lined basins blackened by much use. There are stone windbreaks mortared with wet clay and hair, nudging the blast up just a few degrees so a grove can stand. There are cairns, each with a small mouth—a carved lip around a natural throat—that turn some local shriek into a softer tone; the mouth is smeared with fat and pigment because someone believes the gentler the wind there, the better taste the leaves will have this season. The bridges, where they exist at all, are not across chasms but across the wind itself: courageously simple rafts of lashed poles stretched from one lee to another, so a person can move without being peeled off the stone. None of it is metal; there is no forge up here. The tools are bone pins and stone adzes and knives of obsidian carried up from below. Everything is bound—in fiber, in spit, in rules.

The place has rules the way deep water has rules. They are not strictures of law but geometries of survival.

- You do not cross open slab if you can see the scrubgrass list and flick its underleaves—it means the gust train is changing.
- You watch the lichen. The black encrusting kind throws tiny white dust when pressure shifts. When the dust lifts, you fold yourself into the lee and you wait.
- You do not whistle; you do not sing out. The wind loves to find new notes to keep. Even children who come up on rites learn quickly to talk with hands and shoulders and with the little semaphores of leaf and ribbon.
- You leave cups in the shade. Even on the driest week, a cup left under a known drip will be half-full by morning.
- You travel at the seam of day. At first light there is always a soft vacuum, a held breath. That is when most things move—moths, gliders, people—across the stone to the next pocket of green.

Because of the noise, much happens by feel. People who know the plateau feel the wind-architecture through the soles of their feet, a humming, a static prickling when a resonator is near. They can tell the moment a throat will lock into tone, and they move away before the sound can lift them up and spin them. Those who stay—some do, the ones whose families have always braided fog-nets and left fat on the cairns—build into the oldest alcoves, rooms that look like accidents of erosion until lightning catches on the smoke-shine of soot in the tucks. The houses have no doors, only narrow returns, the ashy walls charred smooth to reduce turbulence. Inside there are bundles of phenomenally tough, fine cords used for everything from tying water-pots to pinning garments to skin. Patterns on leather tell the winds of each season, scribbles of dots and bars like a musical notation. Outsiders sometimes ask what the songs sound like here, but there are no songs made with mouths; there are games with wind—little reed cages hung at specific crossings, cups that make lovers’ tones when pressure is low, the delighted shouts of children you can see in their throats and eyes but cannot hear.

In the very quietest places—deep indentations where three or more lees knot together—there are signs that the world’s strangeness is larger than its noise. In one bowl, certain spore-bearing fronds lift and lower in apparent synchrony with a tone too low for human ears; if you put a fingertip to the frond’s rib you can feel the beat there, steadier than breath. In another, a patch of orange lichen blooms in exact, repeating chevrons that mirror the cross-bedding of the underlying sandstone, as if the rock’s memory is a set of instructions the living follow. Some say the fog here organizes itself too well, threads and ropes and planes that move like flocks; there is talk of wells of air that last for days, invisible shapes of pressure so stable that seeds caught in them sprout in midair, dangling from nothing until their own weight pulls them down. It is never more than a rumor; when you look for it the turbulence has rearranged the place.

Between the green pockets the distances are astonishing. You can walk for an hour over nothing but a hammered floor of stone, bare but for a smear of ferric glaze and a few tufts of silver-blue cushion that break cleanly if you touch them. There is a comforting regularity to these spaces, a pilotage of repetition: same grain of quartz making your soles spark, same pattern of ripple turned to rock, same long hollows where, three times a day, the big under-tone speaks. Then, when you have stopped thinking about finding anything again, you come into a scallop of wall and a hush comes down like a held hand, and there is a smell like wet bark, and the noise becomes as far as surf, and the greenery puts it hands on you with little cool touches and you are reminded that despite its howling, the place is soft at its core where it has made itself a way to hold water.

This is a landscape at the end of its shaping and the middle of its weathering. It is warm by day and thin with altitude, ancient and loud, empty as a table set for one, except in the hollows where everything leans together to drink. The rock no longer argues with gravity; it has found the shape it will have for a very long time. It is the sky that is unsettled here, forever rehearsing its thousand voices through the organs the plateau makes of itself. If you stay and learn it, you begin to know what is coming by the taste of moisture on the wind before the fogs arrive and by the sudden stilling of the dwarf groves before a microburst hits. You can sleep in the lee with your face in moss and wake with your hair wet. You can learn, if you like, a few tones of the stone that will not tear you, and in those tones find your way home.
