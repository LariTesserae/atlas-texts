Heat without light. A black plain lifted into thin air, though there is no sky to see it by—only a ceiling of living matter so dense it behaves like roof and atmosphere at once. The place breathes in long intervals, and when it exhales a wind crawls over everything, dry as furnace breath, bearing the taste of resin and salt.

There is no ground in the familiar sense. The substrate is a continuous mat of plant—fibrous, laminated, hard as a deck in some stretches and soft, pulsing underfoot in others. Everything is made, grown to specification and grown again to repair itself. No rock shows through; there is no soil, no grit. Where you expect exposed scree or dust, there are braided vascular plates ceramicized by time, their surfaces glassy where countless flexings have polished them flat. The terrain is low-shouldered and settled: rises do not peak, they swell; gullies do not cut, they groove. Eons of internal strain and relaxation have worn this living architecture into its oldest form, a landscape of rounded ridges and near-level expanses, all of it a single organismal city that forgot long ago what it copied to become this.

The heat gathers not from sunlight but from within. Deep cores thrum unseen, and the warmth they push upwards keeps the polymers tacky and the air moving. The darkness is almost absolute, broken only by incidental glow: an umber embering where a resin vein runs hot close to the surface; a dull cherry line along an extruder-scar; the brief glint of a safety filament grown to warning temperature and then dimmed again by sap. Your eyes never adjust; it is a world for touch and scent and the bass register. When the big valves open somewhere in the middle distance, the pressure shift comes first with a skin-prickle, then a breath of bitter halide and terpene, then the soft hulk-hulk of doors that are not doors, but stomata scaled up to an industrial size.

Nothing is still for long. The plateau seems calm in its spread—open in the sense that you could wander for a kilometer and see the same architectural botany repeat—but there is no room between presences. Every span is occupied: ribs interleaved with woven conduits, caps of thick leaf-sheets encrusted with crystalline salts, dense curtains of slats that flick to redirect flow. Where a human city would place cranes, this place grows spines that hinge and retract. Vines are not vines here; they function as chains and belts, creeping in perfectly straight lines to ferry barrels of condensed essence and lengths of fresh-grown structural rod.

There is no water in the system. Water is rumor, cautionary myth. Life here uses a clear, hot sap that smells faintly of nitrile and cinnamon, an ionic solvent that will char your hand if it touches open skin. It does not freeze or steam under the conditions of this height; it slides through insulated ducts, thins where it must to feed and thickens at tank-nodes where it stores potential. Where it leaks, it does not pool. It forms a skin, then a bead, then a brittle lens that the maintenance creepers collect and return to the circulatory trunk. On the large terraces—vast as factory floors—salt blooms crust into delicate rime at night, wicking from breath, not from liquid, and the next exhale knocks them away in papery sheets.

The ecology is all plant. Plant as substrate. Plant as engine. Plant as factory. There are pumps with bark; extruders with petals like gaskets; radiators that look like the frills of a fern but are heat-shedding membranes rubbed smooth by a billion swings of air. There are no animals, no attendant drones, no mouse-scratch in the corners. The silence of that absence is pronounced. You expect a flit, a skitter. Instead there are only the slow organ sounds of a made forest at work: the low percussion of a heart somewhere bearing down for a scheduled flush; the faint chafe of a belt on its guide; the too-quiet fracture of a thin crystalline membrane cracking and falling away.

It runs on a strict rulebook encoded in veinwood, algorithms etched as growth patterns. The place communicates in pressure and scent. A tumble of bitterness down a corridor means prepare to retract; a sweet-hot cloud means expand and receive. Memory here is laid in resin layers: last cycle’s failure ring is darker, a caution to grow wider next time. There are seasonalities measured not by sun but by throughput. After a long production surge the whole plain goes into a venting trance—thousands of stomatal shutters crack at once and you feel the air suck across you, and a hot, utterly dry wind roars under the ceiling for a minute before it closes again, leaving behind a palpable drop in tenseness.

Industry is inseparable from body. On one flank of the plain an array of loom-leaves unrolls in parallel. Each leaf is a glossy sheet of fibrous lattice, grown rib by rib until it spans a length like a road, then rolled by a living spindle whose sap runs cold to keep the product stiff. At the end of the line, petiole-clamps nip the roll free, and a set of tendril-gantries—each joint oiled with its own secretions—lifts it into a cradle grown into the ceiling. The cradle tenses and—without hurry, without fanfare—ingests the roll into the storage belly. The only light is the brief orange of a vein warming the cutter nodes. Elsewhere, hot stamps lower and rise with slow certainty, compressing foams extruded from aromatic ducts, curing them by pulse heat until they ring when tapped. You can follow a product by smell alone, each station banishing and replacing notes: acrid to sweet to woody to metallic, though nothing is metal; it only learned to mimic metal’s function.

The edges of this highland are not cliffs so much as thickened droops, shelves that have sagged over their own span and then, over eras, stabilized into long, glazed lips. Sometimes the lips split, revealing black fissures that breathe very cold, very dry gas for days. When they open, the local engines slow. The plain accepts the draft and leans into it, shutters closing, growth halting at rimward nodes. This, too, is ancient behavior, rehearsed longer than anyone remembers. Somewhere below is a volume the system rejects: a deep, dying atmosphere of the old world, inert and lethal to this one. The rule is simple—never spill into the void, never drink what pours out of it.

Though the general quiet is strong, the world has a turbulent temper. Patterns of motion ripple the surface in nested storms. You can walk into a calm and be jerked sideways when a peristaltic train starts underfoot, a deep wave rolling along to press feedstock from one region to the next. Overhead, the roof shakes itself loose from treacled tethers and refastens, reconfiguring flow, closing some spans and opening alternate arches. There are intervals when whole swathes of structure retract like a muscle and then re-emerge in different alignment, abandoning an entire hall and dredging a new one into existence six meters to the left, leaving behind only a gloss, a packed, featureless plate where complicated detail stood a minute before. It is restless, not noisy: an anthill that somehow learned to hum instead of clatter.

Here and there, hints of strangeness intrude that resist naming. On the old plates you find low, circular tags of translucent resin sunk nearly flush. You can put your hand to them and feel faint temperature scripts running in circles, a mnemonic meant for sap-sense. Sometimes the darkness ahead stares back, two faint embers in a row, but they are not eyes; they are pressure sensors flexing themselves alive for a cycle then dimming again. Off to the left, a series of slotted panels whisper open by sequence, and a fog rolls out—dry fog, crystalline motes that skitter in eddies and sift into niches, seeding something. There will be a rush of smelling-bitter and the slats shut at once, as if embarrassed at having opened in your presence.

The age of the place presses from all sides. Surfaces have that patina only use can give—lacquered by flow, not by hand. Nails of old growth poke like fossils through younger refurbishments. A scar shows along a mile of floor where a once-experimental rib two centuries back grew wrong and was lanced out; the patching around it is more elegant than the rest, teaching the plain how to be smoother. The highland has settled into this form not by eroding against weather, but by smoothing itself against its own idea, over and over.

Rules of the world, if you want them, are simple and held in muscle memory. There is no open light, so sensing is close-range and warm. What shines is dangerous; what smells is instruction. Nothing bleeds water; if something bleeds water, it is wrong. Inwards is safer than outwards. Edges are for venting and listening. When the deep engines dive, be still and flat. When the big stomata call, cover and brace. To move product is to live; to store it is to sleep. To sleep is to await the next heat.

And still, for all its industry, the plain can fall to a deep hush that feels like night inside night. The vents close. The hum recedes. A softness spreads over the decks, a tension easing that you did not know had clenched you. In that near-silence, you can hear the resin cooling, the salts tightening their hold on the edges of leaves, the last rattle of a belt finishing its run. Then a far, far heartbeat lifts again from under your feet, and the dark highland inhales.
