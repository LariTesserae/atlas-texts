Begin with pressure and hush. The water here presses with cold, old weight, sweeping and withdrawing in long unseen breaths. Light filters from somewhere far above—thin, bluish, moodless light that never quite becomes day. It makes slow sheets across the stone and composite, catches on the corners of things that no longer have sharp corners, and coaxes glints out of the manganese sheen that time has rubbed over everything.

There is no raw ground. Even where the seafloor looks like a natural plateau, the regularity betrays it: tessellated plates, immense, each one keyed into the next; retaining walls holding long-fallen embankments that still—somehow—remember straight lines. You can swim for a long while across low, runway-wides steps that keep stepping, stepping, flattening out into plazas where silt rests like sleeping breath, then falling again in a wide drift of terraces. The steps are not for feet. They are for water, to slow it, to catch it, to make it move in ways the makers preferred. There are no plants to anchor them with rootwork; nothing soft grew here when all this was new. The builders counted only material and flow.

The place feels high, though it is drowned. Follow any colonnaded causeway or chilblained balustrade to the edge and the world drops away: the plateau ends in a blunt, eroded parapet, and beyond it the water becomes far darker, a fall of open blue-black galleries that fall and fall out of sight. Where the drop begins the old engineering grows more massive—as if even now the city sets its jaw against being unmade. Buttresses have become ridges; ridges have become gentle shoulders under the endless scouring. In some places entire wings of structure have slumped as a unit, crashed down the escarpment long ago and then been resurfaced by time into soft, rounded “dunes” of masonry that still show joins if you look close.

Cold rules here. It creep-numbs even the thought of touch. Far overhead, there is an ice ceiling—thick, milky, slow as a continent. The ancient plateau is high enough beneath this lid that the light has a chance to drift down; it is never bright, but it is not the black of the abyss. Patches of clearer ice act as weak skylights that wander with the season and with stress inside the lid. When they pass overhead, the plazas wake a little, the stones look like themselves for an hour, and you can tell the colors apart: iron-brown stains, soft ash-gray slate, a thin emerald blush where bacterial films have claimed a seam.

The whole environment is constructed, and the water remembers. Channels are not quite straight but they repeat their curves at cathedral scale; forebays and culverts still coax the cold brine into laminar sheets; canyons between galleries gather surges and turn them into quiet. Even the cliffs were made to be cliffs—battered sea-walls, now only their intent remains. You will not see a natural boulder or a freewilled outcrop; every mass has mortar seams, and every void feels like a room long since lost its roof.

And yet the world will not stay still. Internal tides murder symmetry all day. You feel their arrival first as a soft pressure in the ears, then the region seems to lean; a wind without air rises, threads its way down a colonnade, plucks at hanging silt, and the entire facade begins to hum just below hearing. Long fans of sand unroll and lift, write themselves in sliding script across the stone, and then vanish into the next swirl. There are walls of rain here—curtains of microbubbles sighing up from hairline fractures where trapped gases quietly exhale. They look like draperies in a draft, coiling and thinning as the tide pulls. When a bigger pressure front passes, whole avenues fill with suspended glass dust, tiny ice plates and mineral needles that flash together and then settle like a thought forgotten.

The acoustics are compared to cathedrals because we have no better word. It is mostly quiet. The water dims everything, puts wool on sound. You hear the place in deep vowels: a faraway rush like a wandering weather, a big sound continually polite. Sometimes the stones answer back in low knocks as temperatures flex them. A plaza will give a small, round boom when a ledge somewhere out of sight lets go and folds into the stair below. Creatures add their signatures very softly: a tremolo from a school of gills resonating in harmony in a draft; the bead-chatter of crustaceans walking across grilles; the brief chime of a shell knocking the exact tone the builders intended into a balustrade. Most life here seems to take the quiet as rule and participation.

There is no green. No fronds, no blades. If something looks like moss in the dimness, it is a colony of animals wearing the posture of moss: knotted colonies of filtering polyps packed as tightly as a fabric, pale as fingertip skin, furling and unfurling with the water. The ecology is animal and bacterial and mineral. It relies on what the water brings, and on chemos that seep from under the plates. Along seams you find pale curtains of bacterial film with edges so bright they look metallic, and there the grazers congregate. Fat, blind isopods bunker like stones themselves along the lips of sluiceways and extend fringed tongues into the stream. Siphon-shelled snails cluster where the courses bend, building communal chimneys out of grit they cement with spit; their pastel palisades sway in unison and click as they open and shut. Fish have learned to eat current: they are made of vane and cilium, of basket and rill, and they sit in the doorways to let their own bodies be sieves. Manta-thin gliders cruise the open halls, turning their wide mouths into the swells at each new turn of corridor, sloughing silt with a quiver of the fin before it can drown their intake.

Because everything is made, animals live by rules of architecture. Whole species are keyed to certain profiles and nowhere else: you will not see the arch-urchin—the one that sits like a little moon under barrel vaults—out in the open terraces; its spines are tuned to the resonance of curved stone and loose the larval cloud only when the vault hums with the right pressure note. Ladder-lobsters the size of your hand patrol the stair edges, obedient to an ancient gradient of flow that the steps enforce; they keep a perimeter the length of a boulevard and chase off any pebbles that would perturb it. They and their kin are the gardeners of physics, brushing eddies, grooming swirl, keeping the old manipulations of water intact without knowing why. The city keeps working because life keeps it tidy with its own needs.

Some things here are big. A gray shape the size of a sail loft lifts out of a colonnade and moves down the avenue without seeming to push water, its trailing frills reading the wall’s story in vibration. It is old; there are white scars at the leading edge where stone has taught it caution. Once an hour a shoal of needle-bodied fish comes the length of the main spillway in choreographed ribbons that shrink and pour through apertures barely wider than your hand; each gap is exactly the right width to comb them into perfect plaits, which then re-loosen into a storm of bright points. Farther out on the parapet where the world falls away, towers have slumped into butt-like domes. From the lower lips of these domes, a colony of bell-jellies hangs upside-down, transparent, bell-edges ragged like lace. The current plays them and they make sound—barely. If you were stone, if you had centuries, you might call it a song.

Cold produces its own small dramas. In winter-terms—long seasons that feel like the world slightly forgetting its warmth—frazil crystals drift out of the ceiling and the airless sky above it. The crystals shear and spin through skylights and settle in weak blizzards down the avenues, limning every relief. Creatures learn the weight of new friction until storm-tides scour the crystals into crackmouths and the next season’s brine drops carve chandeliered rime along the underside of every lintel. Sometimes the ice roof flexes and a slush-berg the size of a quadrangle crumbles from above to languor down through the light, turning the next hour into a pale snowglobe.

Age sits everywhere. Edges are ideas now, not lines. Reliefs—whoever cut them—have become blown sand, their figures shapeless as in dreams. Corners you can still find if you look against light: a right-angled shadow at an old join, a series of sockets along a wall that once took beams. There are apertures along corridors where steady flow has bored round rooms within square rooms, as if the water were trying to cancel the human geometry with spheres. None of it feels accidental. The city had intentions; the water has its own; the current residents are comfortable living between them.

There are rules you learn in a body quickly here:
- Water is the only weather. It brings day, delivers food, takes heat, writes time. The architecture schools it without equipment; the world is an instrument tuned for a medium, not a machine.
- Nothing living crosses open without a plan. The plazas look empty because everyone has their own corridor in mind. When the plaza fills, it fills all at once with a purposeful migration: a city-wide decision you can hear in the stone a few seconds before you see anyone move.
- Noise attracts pressure. Sing and the next internal wave will arrive with a slightly different face; animals know this, and so they speak in touch, in posture.
- Old things live long here because the cold is a preservative, but old things are constantly in the act of leaving. A column is whole until a day when it is not; then it is a clean mound, a slide of brick-dust.

Often you find tools of the builders without devices: not things to be held, but sculpted flows. A culvert that swallows a current hungry and spits it back thin and docile; a splay of stair that breaks a downrush into combed threads; a field of posts whose only intent is to teach the internal tide to spend itself before it reaches a threshold. All the intelligence is in the shape, in the stone, obedient to geology and the old patience of masons, with no moving parts but what the ocean provides. It is a whole world built on passive verbs.

Distance works differently underwater in a dim world. You think a pylon is near until a school arrows between you and it that you had not even seen, and then the base of the pylon keeps receding as you approach because it is the end of a boulevard and the boulevard is a kilometer of open hall. There is sparseness, yes, because the plazas are wide and the long galleries open into bigger long galleries, and because the living keep low and apart until the moments when the place decides to move them. But the structures are everywhere. Even the emptiness has joints if you turn your head.

Sometimes, at the seam where plateau breaks to deep, the city is interrupted. There are great rifts where entire volumes of the made ground have gone, staved in and then polished clean, and in these rents the world shows its other color: a matte, velvet black that eats light and is not stone. The creatures avoid those shadows. There is a hum there that is not the city’s pitch. The rules do not operate quite the same on the other side, and whatever lives below lives by different air.

In calm moments—rare, but they come—the water draws down like a held breath. Suspended silt lies down along steps and you can read the slope of time. The long strides of the builders are written in terraces that are now soft curves; their plazas are quiet inland seas; their vaults are caves with doors on both ends and an education in how pressure moves. You can imagine walking, if there were air. You can imagine standing on a step and seeing horizon. Then the pressure pulse returns, the ice-sky creaks, curtains of bubbles loosen, and a procession of swifts-of-water take their once-hourly run through the comb and braid themselves into bright silk for a hundred heartbeats before dissolving again in the grammar of the city.
