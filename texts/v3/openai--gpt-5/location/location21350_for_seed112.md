Brightness first, like a high-latitude noon that never softens. Then the roar—unbroken, body-filling, a pressure you carry in your teeth. Think of a continent parked in the sky, its edge scribed against a thin blue, air skimming fast as a river over a stone. Up here the world is mostly altitude and plant.

There is no wet. Not a trickle in a crack, not a film under a stone. Surfaces are powder-dry, almost greasy with the fines of silicate dust, and the cold has no dampness in it—only a metallic clarity that stings the sinuses and leaves the mouth tasting like ground glass. If anything glistens it is not water; it is fat or resin, a clear fluorinated oil beading in capillaries cut through the greenless tissue, or a glaze the color of pale smoke where friction-heated particles have fused to a ceramic.

Across that dry, the ground is a single biomass, though “ground” is an imprecision. It is a rind, a lattice, a series of vast, wind-shaped ribs laid over an eroded spine of stone. The plant that makes the world is not a forest and not a grassland. It is more like a cliff that learned to grow: fluted buttresses, sweeping arches, plates smoothed to an airfoil sheen, deep vents opening into organ-like chambers. In places it spreads as a thin crust on bedrock, barely the thickness of a book, braided with little lifting vanes like cilia. Elsewhere it rises into buttress-canyons—kilometers-long sails of living composite, barbed and webbed to each other by taut, flexible spans. Each span hums like a tuning fork in the gale. Between these presences the spaces are generous: wide courts of wind, long shoulders of empty, wind-polished plane where a single rib or thorn stands out like a mast. This is the sparseness here. The plant is everywhere, yes, but its big gestures are few and far between, leaving great reachings of air between.

Erosion has been at work for so long that nothing looks sharp by accident. All edges are the edges that survived. The plant’s outer skeleton—white to olive to dull gunmetal where mineral dust has been incorporated—is rounded where the wind would have sheared sharpness, fluted where vortices have carved their long grooves. The old forms are stable, balanced, quiet in their own massive way despite the ongoing violence of flow. You sense it in the way an overhanging sail shades a channel that has never filled with anything, in the polished mottle of a face exposed to centuries of abrasive grit. There are no collapses that are not part of a plan; the world learned its stresses a long time ago.

It is cool even under the relentless light. The high air steals heat quick as thought. Shadows slice with hard definition, and the few places sheltered enough to feel calm hold pools of cold like lakewater held in stone bowls—though there is no liquid to be seen. You could burn your finger on a sunflash off a glazed vane and freeze the same finger in the shadow under it.

The plant is not only substrate; it is mechanism. Everywhere, under the roar, you find the sense of works. The world is loud because the world is running.

At the windward lips of the great vents, fine elastic curtains tense and release, beating with the cadence of turbulence. These are lungs of a kind, but what they pull is not wet air; they pull momentum. Deep inside are vanes stacked like the impellers of a turbine, and as the gusts enter they spin. In other worlds, factories spin for machines. Here, machines spin for flesh. The turning drives belts—bands of living tissue tense as an athlete’s muscle—that haul mineral packets along routes grooved into the substrate. The ground has conveyors like rivers once had currents, and if you lay one hand on a moving vein you feel the tick of what it moves: little rounded nodules of powdered skyfall and rock scoured from exposed barrens, fluxed and sorted by charge and grain.

When a gale surges, big sections of the rind flex and close. This is a piston-field. A hundred meters across, hundreds of them by the horizon, each massive blade stamps once into a neighbor, compressing an inner sac of reactive gases and dust. The compression heats the mix; the heat catalyzes a slow polymerization that would take lifetimes at ambient cold. After the pulse the sacs express their product as pearls of new material that the belts take away. The place grinds its own bones and remakes them.

Elsewhere, whole ridges have been made into organs of static. Bristles finer than hair yet meters long stand out from a spine like the vanelets of a feather. In the dryness, every grain that scrapes builds charge. The bristles scintillate blue—a corona where ions leap across microgaps—and discharge crackles along ribs through buried capacitive tissues: stacked plates of dielectric sap and mineral foil. Sometimes, when a storm of particulates rolls in from the uplands and the speeds come up, these plates dump together. The arc punches air into a violet spear and the sound turns mountainous. The crack is not thunder—there is no water to cycle—but whatever it is, it drives small flyers sprawling and lights bone-white hollows far below like a camera flash.

You see foundries too, though no one built them. Cones with mouths fringed in hard green extrude filaments of resin and dust. They lay the filaments in spirals, arms weaving with a slow surety, letting the wind set the strands as it cools them, taut across a span. New braces grow with every good storm. Islands of slackness—calm enough to start a span—are rare, so spans are long and sure. When one fails you hear it go in the next province, a twang as deep as whalesong and a recoil like a whip. The broken ends curl and lay themselves flat in a ritual that looks like death but is only maintenance. In days the fracture will be glossed over with new silk, bright as a healing wound.

There are “mills,” though again the term isn’t true. They are oases of calm in the lees of big ribs, where the plant piles grit. Knuckled surfaces there work against each other as the whole structure flexes, crushing clasts to bread-flour fineness. Here the air has a mineral taste thick enough to lick. Here come the grazers.

Fauna are everywhere once you learn what to look for. There are kite-things: planar bodies with long, hemmed edges that can hold a precise angle to the gale. They anchor by living hooks in the plant's pores and feed without letting go, letting the wind gutter their bodies like flags to drive inner turbines that push oil across exchange membranes. Each kite trails hair-thin cords that clip to static bristles; their meals are electron flows, ion trickles, tricksy little redox pairs, all the currencies wind can mint. They look like scraps of fabric, like religious pennants, until one releases in a white arc and immediately doubles in size, its geometry shifting from anchor to flight, and becomes a streaking blade that crosses the void between ribs on a tense, precise line to find another perch.

Between the vanes in the vent mouths, a drifting microfauna sifts like dust, but each is a creature that mastered the boundary layer: paraboloid hulls cupping supersonic eddies, sieve-mouths agape, raking out the rich aerosol of pollen analogs, spores, flaked proteins, mineral crumbs. They eat the sound too—or rather what the sound does: the compressions and rarefactions beat mechanosensors, and the beat drives pump-motors, and the pumps move the oil that dissolves their lives.

Bigger things stride on clamps with dozens of joints, walking the outer faces of the big ribs like beetles walk bark. They match their gaits to the flex of the world: step when the plate rises, lock when it falls, so they never fight the work. Their eyes are not eyes but latticework in a skull of ceramic honeycomb, tuned to vibration more than light. They talk with two hearts like kettledrums, beating in patterns that travel through the ground. It is communication made for a world where air is nothing but weather and sound seldom means anything at all.

Predators are elegant and rare, fast lines in the corner of vision. They are scythes made flesh, all drag-minimization and structural integrity, flicking from calm to gale channels like fish sliding between currents at a river’s seam. They snatch the kites, unclip anchorage faster than the locking will can object, and vanish into the accuracy of their own wake.

The atmosphere is thin enough that you feel its lack in your joints. Every breath, if you’re built for a different place, would be a thought about sufficiency. But the light is close. The star here has little to do with seasonal softness; it pours in like an ongoing verdict. The plants drink it through pigments set in the outer millimeter beneath a surface that looks like mineral. They do not combine it with water. They combine it with some other solvent—oils that do not evaporate in desiccation, that hold charge beautifully, that resist freezing and go slick when you warm them by a fraction of a degree. In those oils, enzymes churn that never knew hydrogen bonds. In those spaces, ions of metals move like cations in a salt, not H+ like a familiar world. The big lifeworks here borrow energy from light and more directly from movement, driving chemical processes with literal rotation and compression. You can tell by the way the lamellae only move on gusts above a certain speed: nothing happens when it’s merely windy. Everything is built to thresholds.

Weather is an instrument all its own. The great organs, the vent mouths, the sail-valleys, the longitudinal ribs—together they make standing waves in the sky. Whole mountain ranges sing in infrasonic tongues that shepherd the flow: fat lines of high pressure collared to particular canyons, quiet lanes as consistent as shipping channels drifting between sound-ruined grounds. These are migration routes for things that can fly, silent ports for those that cannot. When two ranges’ notes beat against each other you get violence: shock fronts that double the particle load, days of blue-white scintillation on the static masts, resin extruded in curtains. Cloud never forms, not as water anyway, but there is weather in the way grit falls, in light bent by density waves, in invisible fronts you feel with your skin before you can name them.

All of this goes on without a single artifact not made of living intention. There are no ruins of a people, no rivets except those extruded by cones that think like trees. It is an industrial world without industry. It runs mills to make its own bone-dust, opens valves to shuttle charge, extrudes and spins and weaves. You catch yourself naming these feats with the words of machines because the analogy is so exact, but here the blueprint is genetic, the schedule is climatological, the labor is tissue.

At times the dynamic comes all at once. You feel it first as an almost-silence, a drop in the constant roar that isn’t a drop at all but a shift in its quality; somewhere far off, organ mouths shut their lips. Pressure builds like a held breath, the kites all with their tails rigid and taut, the striders’ drum-hearts holding a single line. The span lines between ribs stop humming. Then the gust arrives. In the white seethe of grit, every vane spins, every piston flexes, an entire watershed of belts ticks faster, faster. There is a blast of light off the static combs. A brace fails with the gunshot of a bridge going; a kilometer of filaments snap back and lay down. All the predators are knives. All the grazers flatten themselves like fallen flags. The sound ceases to be describable. There is only becoming.

After, there is a smell that might be sweet if this were a wet place. Here it is clean oil and hot stone. The plant has built a new thickness in its skin. It is already hard.

The rules of the place appear in small things. Nothing soft faces the wind. Everything that must remain soft hides it under a cowl of ceramicized tissue, lifts it above the particle-blast or recesses it into rockshade. Anything that cannot tolerate noise does not have a hole on the outside; its sensors are bars and plates and webs interior to the bone. Reproduction is by flight and fracture: cones loosen their spun braces on cue and let whole architectures sail to new moorings; something like spore and like seed goes skimming to a specific note in the organ voices where calm always dwells. Nutrients come from air and grit and charge. Waste is mostly heat and noise.

Night changes little of the cold and much of the light. The star releases them; the static does not. On clear nights the bristles glow and dance, and the arcs lace the big supports down canyon with spiders’ lightning. The sky is clean and shot with things that burn: infalling micro-boulders crackle quieter than the grounded arcs, and dust hisses softly in air thin enough to forget it is there. The roar subsides by a degree that makes conversation possible if you are the sort of animal that has invented conversation. Here, communication is in rhythms transmitted through shared bone; you sit and lean and feel stories pass like weather fronts.

There are calm days. Very rarely, the organ ranges detune and the currents do not find each other. Then nothing sounds like anything; the world seems as if it has found its way out of itself. You can hear a clip-beak grazer break welds of fossil resin, tiny crunch by tiny crunch. A predator watches and does not move because if it moved it would hear nothing but its own motion and walk off a rib. The plant idles; belts crawl; pistons relax open for the first time in a season. The sky looks closer without sound.

But calm never lasts. This is a high place, a self-weathering, self-working machine wrapped in leafless skin. It is not built, and yet it is built. It is a desert set to music so loud the song is terrain. It’s bright and sparse and total: a planet that learned industry as a form of growth, a continent of plant that runs on wind.
