Heat comes first, but not the flat kiln of a low desert. This is high heat, an altitude blaze. The air is thin enough that the sun seems closer; every edge stands in hard relief. Under that brightness an entire region of stone — a plateau the size of a county — rises from lower, hazed plains and then breaks into a crowded forest of rock. The surface is not smooth but an interlocked maze of buttresses, fins, and fretted ridgelines, a karst that has been dissolved into intricacy. From above it looks like frost drawn in limestone, pale and bone-bright, with dark seams where it splits.

The terrain packs itself tight. There are miles of narrow corridors between knife-backed ridges, sudden slots that drop straight into cool dark, grikes too slim for shoulders but deep enough to hold a different season. You don’t get long clear lines of travel; you get choices, constant choices, a path that folds and turns and doubles back. You can walk for hours and be in the same kind of place, and in every ten paces something different pushes up to claim the space: a column bristled with pockets, a slab polished by the pads of animals, a roofless chamber ringed by swallow holes. The edges of the plateau fall away all at once into canyons that seethe with late light, and you can see the next rise beyond, kilometers distant, repeating the pattern — open land by the map, but in the body always among things.

Erosion has been working here so long it no longer hurries. The limestone is fluted and carved into organ-pipe runnels, pitted with old solution pans, and jewelled with a varnish of dark microbes in the south-facing arcs. Nothing raw shows unless there has been a fresh fall. The forms look settled, as if they have come to an agreement with the weather they live in. When rain does come — afternoon anvils building from invisible basins beyond the rim — the water sheets off and dives into the first mouth it finds. There are no streams braided on the surface; the plateau drinks. It holds its water within. Even on dry days, if you press your hand to the shadowed side of a ridge at dawn, your palm comes away wet.

Moisture moves in small ways and then disappears. Night does not bring coolness so much as the allowance for dew. The air, rising during the day, slumps back at sunset and draws a fine breath across stone. Condensation gathers in thin films along the leeward faces and in hollows lined with dark moss. It wicks downward along calcite skins, threads itself into old chert seams, beads and then lets go. There are pockets — skylit bowls, wind-sheltered pans — that hold thumb-deep water for a week after a storm and then go to paste, then to memory. In caves near the plateau’s heart, a dripline has been constant for longer than anyone counts. If you stand in those chambers, you can hear the steady tap like the seconds of the world.

Vegetation shows up only where the stone has allowed it to collect dust. The plants are sparse, all strategy and restraint: slivers of grass folded into wire, cushions that look like stone until they bloom, shrubs that have learned the trick of silvering their leaves to throw the light back. In the depths of grikes, ferns unscroll in damp that would seem impossible a meter away in the glare. A stunted tree here and there — its trunk braided, its roots tucked into a vertical seam — throws a pitiful shade that is nonetheless contested every noon. On north faces, lichens write a patient alphabet. In a few deep sinkholes where the underground river laps at the air and lets a mist escape, a compressed jungle breathes, a green secret set in all that limestone white.

Animals hold more of the place than plants do. They have made themselves small or airborne or sleepless. Lizards are everywhere, glossy and swift, throwing themselves from sun to sun across hand-spans of darkness. Rock hyrax analogs — tight-packed herbivores with sullen eyes — loaf in clusters where the shade is thickest, bolting for vertical cracks if a shadow passes too suddenly. Bats sweep out of the caves at dusk in emphatic ribbons; swifts and swallows scissor the noon thermals, screaming for joy or warning. A falcon makes lazy circles above the drop and peels away to fold itself into a sudden vertical. There are snakes like polished cords waiting beside the places where water condenses from plastic mesh. Insects thicken the air where anything is green: metallic flies, slow bees that know three plants for a hundred kilometers, stick-legged water beetles that parachute into sinkholes when the first rains smell near. A fox with long ears and a grin does rounds of the machines at night, tapping the drip basins first, then the shade of the pump shed where moths collect.

Technology here is an industrial whisper rather than a city’s shout. Scattered along the rim and tucked into folds of the plateau, someone has anchored a network of condensation nets and blade-thin condensers, slung like sails between bolted posts. They do not turn; they stand, beaded with dew by dawn, shedding tiny clean waterfalls into cisterns jacketed in stone. Taut cables hum across the broader voids, relics of a tram that once hauled sacks of mineral aggregate from a quarry now gone to swallows and moss. You can still see the pylons, spiked into buttresses, with their yellow paint chalking away. A few are live: a slow electric winch creaks twice a day, ferrying a bundle of flexible bladders cool from the caves to a maintenance station. Not far from a skylit sink, a pumphouse the size of a single room sighs and clicks, throwing a fan of air through a radiator where the temperature split is greatest; its rust-proof panels gleam in the sun with that particular honest immodesty of good metal at altitude.

The old paths are marks more than roads. Faint rails where hand-trucks once ran glint and then vanish under calcium spatter. A catenary of spare parts hangs in a carved alcove: a pulley, a bucket with a bullet hole, a set of belts for a fan that’s been turning without them for years because something else was found to fit. Solar panels splay as far as anyone could be bothered to carry them, and at night their edges frost with a starfield of blown sand that shines under moonlight. Birds have learned to nest in the cradle of the struts, and lizards have annexed the shade of instrument boxes. The built world makes islands, and every island is colonized by hunger and industry that isn’t human.

Sound is almost a negative presence. You are aware of the quiet the way you are aware of thirst: as a constant you work within. There’s the whisper of wind combing the stone, sometimes rising into a tone when it finds a slit in the right way. A slow metallic tick-tick as something hot shrinks and relents. The distant, occasional grind of the cable when the winch stirs. Bird cries do not carry far; they are clipped by the convolutions of the rock and drop away into the next slot. If a rock goes loose and leaps into space, you hear the cascade a long time later as a shaken-out echo from below.

The world here runs on cycles, tight ones and slow ones, interwoven. The daily cycle is a breathing: by midmorning warm air flushes out of cave mouths and swallow holes, exhalation so strong that dust puffs from the grikes like smoke; after sunset cold drafts pull back in, and along with them the touch of vapor. The fog that sometimes lolls up from nowhere in the middle of night is not from the sky but from the stone itself, a quiet ghost made by temperature differences inside the plateau. Machines are tuned to this breath, set to open or close shutters, to run their pumps while the air is moving, to stand down when the world is holding it.

The seasonal cycle writes itself in violence and restraint. High summer makes the plateau a white anvil on which almost nothing moves past noon. The metal fixtures are too hot to touch. Things that live here have a rule: if you must cross open stone, do it in the corridor of your own shade. A few storms push in on columns of heat alone, dumping rain that snaps and sizzles on the rock and disappears with such speed you can see the footprint-shaped dry places expanding as if a creature were planting its feet. After these, everything wakes that can. Seeds that have sat for years split in sympathy. The next day small moths that were not here before are here, soft and insistent, worrying at the single calendula that had the nerve to open in the lee of a bolt-head.

Predator and prey are a dance of brief advantages. Nothing has the energy to pursue for long, so every kill is an ambush or a gift from heat. The fox knows the drip schedule and the temperament of the falcon and the patience of the snake. The bats find insects in the slack between last light and night proper. Even the machines fall into predation and concession: a broken strap on a net will tear itself free in the first midnight breeze and wrap around the nearest shrub until morning, when a creature that needs fibers will chew the web loose and take it home.

A few rules of this place:

- Water is for those who can touch it without carrying it. Anything liquid left in a container that the plateau has not made is found by scent and redistributed by mouths, feet, and evaporation within an hour.
- There is no such thing as an empty flat. Every planar space has an owner — a sunning lizard, a congregation of midges, a drift of seed — even if the owner has stepped away for a minute.
- If something hums, it is either singing stone or a working thing. If it sings at noon, it is stone. Machines have learned to keep their sound for dawn and dusk.
- The plateau keeps its secrets in deep verticals. You will not find a lake, but you will find the suggestion of rivers below, and the places where warm wind becomes rain on limestone lips two hundred meters under your feet.
- Heat is not opinion, it is law. Anything that survives has built a map of shadow in its body.

Weirdness, if you want to call it that, lies subtle in broad daylight. At certain hours in midsummer the pores in the limestone sweat — not pure water, but a faint brine drawn out of deliquescent salts. Lizards and birds have learned the timing and will tongue the mineral sheen off the stone itself, polishing low bands on the rock to a pearl. Some of the grikes, when the wind is right, turn into flutes. Walk long enough and you hear in the same minute a bat’s whisper, the plateau’s breath, and a chord from twenty meters of invisible slit.

By night the place is not kinder, but it lifts a little. Stars come down until they seem embedded in the uppermost rock. The air pulls toward the underground, and with it the scents of guano, machine oil, a sweet algae smell rising from unseen drips. The maintenance lights on the standing pylons click on — not bright, just a small declaration of ongoing intent. Somewhere, behind a screen of nets glittering with dew like a field of frozen cobweb, a relay closes, and a pump wakes. Out beyond the machines a rustle moves along the corridor of shade thrown by a leaning fin. It’s the first movement of a night full of decision, a place where scarcity and abundance are simply the rules by which everything is so intensely present.
