From a distance it looks like a continent of green muscle laid flat on the spine of the world, a high, warm tableland that shines as though every hour has just been after rain. The air is clear of dust and full of water, the kind of saturated clarity that makes colors ring. The light is honest daylight, but it has to push through constant beads and veils, each droplet flashing and gone and back again, because condensation is the plain’s first craft. It makes its own weather, then rearranges it across its skin.

There is no bare ground. Ground is a wrong word for it. Underfoot is a living breadth of vegetable fabric—spongy in places, lacquered in others—taut membranes and flexed ribs and soft alveoli that give a little when you walk. The entire plateau is plant-as-architecture, plant-as-substrate. What would be rock walls elsewhere are here vertical curtains of reinforced bark, their faces dark with a slow, continuous weep. What would be river channels are not cut but grown: pale green flumes that invite and direct the sheet-flow of dew. Even the wide, open reaches where there is nothing higher than your knee are not meadows; they are smooth, breathing carpets of tissues that drink fog and sigh it back out.

High means thin air, ordinarily, but the plain produces mass as it breathes. The atmosphere tastes green—resin and bitter sap, the cool clean of tannins—and it is warm enough that you could lie down in it and doze. Yet you do not, because the place is loud. The hum is not an event but a fact. It is a perpetual chorus without beginning or end, a braided roar of water sliding within capillaries, of air drawn through living pipes, of leaf-scales rattling and resetting, of built-in organs sounding as the updraft crosses them. Stand anywhere and there is always that tone, a chord with many notes, steady enough that you can measure your heartbeat against it and always lag.

Look long enough and pattern appears, and with it, intention. The big features are widely spaced, a few to any horizon: tall hollow towers that lean very slightly into the prevailing wind and sip the moving sky; saddle-backed looms of rib-wood that strain and harvest the day’s vapor; bowls—huge, concave, leaf-paved bowls—that never brim because they are not meant to hold but to distribute. The space between these is enormous, calm, almost empty. A single tower might stand a kilometer away, its mouth a black oval rimmed with pale, dripping teeth of cellulose. Between here and there the ground undulates a little, its pores opening and closing with a regularity that does not vary this week or this century. If there is variety, it is the kind you find by kneeling: the way the hairs on the underside of a bridge-leaf bloom into droplets every sixty heartbeats; the way the tiny stomatal pits gather them with a practiced shiver and pull them inside.

Water is everywhere and nowhere obvious. It beads along the thousands of ciliate filaments that make every edge look furred; it threads through glassy sheaths under the skin; it paints everything in a gloss that makes the light slide. If you wait, you see veils drawn along pre-determined paths, curtains of drip passing down specific planes as if following a memory. Little runs like veins, broader runs like sheets, never puddling, never cutting gulches. There is no erosion exactly because the plain refuses the very idea of wearing down; it mends in place, it yields and reasserts. Where any other highland would have gullies and fans, this one has grooves that look like the impression of a river’s dream, not the river.

Step and the fabric accepts your weight and says nothing back. Touch a wall and the cold slick bark will not bruise. Press your ear to the plate and you can hear sap walk. It does not surge or startle; it keeps going, and it will go that way for longer than most lives. This is a made environment, but the makers are not missing; they are simultaneous with the made. The design is encoded in growth rates and inhibition fields, in hormones that travel along predetermined galleries of tissue that are also corridors you could crawl through. You can enter a tower—one of the black-mouthed ones—and find that the interior is not a shaft but a set of interlocked domes, quiet as a shell; every dome is lined with slats that angle to harvest the updraft, to sing it just enough to keep the flow steady. The sound is a maintenance trick as well as music. The plain shepherds itself with structure and tone.

There are places where the architecture is overt: long arcades grown of twined buttresses so that morning sun and evening sun are strained into bands; halls with translucent ceiling-skins full of dapple and slow droplets; clusters of cup-vaults the size of hills that connect to each other by low throats you could mistake for canyons from above and rooms from within. It is easy to think there must have been hands here once, that some culture grew these forms and then withdrew. But there are no carved marks, no tool-scars, only the logic of cells scaling up, the discipline of an organism that has discovered ridges and walls as faithfully as it discovered leaf.

At the very edges the plain ends as a hem, not a cliff. The hem is a series of pendant lobes and curtains, frilled and overlapping, from which water descends in threads that never quite become waterfalls because the falling strands are always intercepted by outcropping lips of tissue and led inward again. Look outward and down and there is nothing you recognize: there is cloud, bright and moving, and the shadows of these living curtains upon it. Occasionally the cloud recedes enough to show a greater drop—forest or other country far below—but it is irrelevant here. The plateau owns its elevation the way a lung owns the space within a chest: by filling it entirely with function.

Fauna are rare enough that when they appear the whole plain seems to pretend not to see. A pale glider unfolds from the hem and drifts inland, webbed limbs stretched, a soft-bodied thing that never lands so much as kisses and sticks and then quivers along. A small congregation of glass-backed beetles, almost plant-colored, crosses a runner, their bodies beading like the dew. Once in a while a chime-bird—more like a flying seed-case than a bird—vibrates over the slats of a tower mouth and sets up a harmonics battle that the tower wins. There are no predators to watch for; there is also no fallback, no cover; the large spaces between the huge organs are honest and unoccupied. Where elsewhere you would expect all that biomass to be thick with life, here it is not. Here, the living is the mass itself.

If you cut a section and studied it, you would find fungal threads braided through the plant tissues as if the whole place were a single compromise between two kingdoms. The under-layer is a honeycomb that repeats at several scales, like something a mathematician would sketch to model how to divide everything and lose nothing. It breathes in place of wind when the air is flat; it wicks in place of rain when the skies are dry; it makes its own blanks and then fills them. There is no litter. No leaf falls away because the plain unselves its parts the way a body resorbs a bruise. Damage is not absent, but it has no drama. An abraded patch dulls for a season and then is the same as it was before. Season is a courtesy word; there are no seasons. The temperature bears warm, the light cycles and the growth does not change its mind.

The rules here are not human rules but you can learn them. Water moves along edges, not across; if you want to follow it, you follow boundaries. The loudest sound points you reliably toward the nearest ventilation hall; if the tone rises, you are walking up the pressure gradient into wind; if it softens, you are descending to a basin. Open space means safety because the organism wants to keep it open; when the fabric gains relief and clutter, it is expressing a local purpose, not a hazard, but purpose and hazard can be the same when you misunderstand scale. Anything that looks like an invitation is probably a conduit. Anything that looks like a dead end isn’t; there will be a seam that only yields to humidity or the right touch of shade.

You can taste myths in a place like this. One says it grew to stop the mountain from wearing out; another says it is a garden planted by gravity because gravity wanted to play with something soft for once. A more plausible account is that lower, older ecosystems tuned into the mountain’s wind and moisture and found a loop, an architecture of equilibrium, and kept scaling. The outcome is a city with no citizens except itself, and yet there are plazas and avenues because those are good forms, and arcades because light likes to be filtered, and towers because air, given a throat, will sing out its own constancy and then be steadier for it.

Crossing it takes days and you will swear you have not moved because the measures are churchlike and repetitive by design. You can put a stake down and come back years later and your stake will be swallowed but the echo at that place will be the same. The plateau does not change from your perspective, which is not to say it is not alive. It is an equilibrium of noise and wet and green. It is an organism frozen deliberately in the act of working, so perfectly optimised for what it does that it seems unworldly to those of us who measure life by fidget and season. Here, the work is to drink the sky and hand it back, to hold high what was raw and would have washed away, to sing so that the air will remember to move. Here, the many acres of living machine do what they do, and will, long after your ears forget and then re-remember the hum.
