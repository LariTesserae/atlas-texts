The first thing is the breath of it: a warm draft coming from everywhere at once, the cave inhaling and exhaling through hairline faults you can’t see. The exhale beads to dew on the raw rock and on your own skin, gathers at sudden lips and ledges, and then, without warning, lets go as a rain that sounds like a thousand hands riffling paper. It isn’t a forest rain—no leaves here—but a persuasion of droplets off bare mineral and chitin, a rain that turns to steam-mist as it strikes warmer flows and then to streamers again. Water exists in pockets, films, brief rivers that run hard for a minute and vanish into cracks that didn’t exist an hour ago.

This underworld is young, still deciding how to be a space. The walls are glassy and sharp in places, like someone peeled bedrock in wet sheets and left it to stiffen. Some of the rock is black, with a frozen motion to it—curdled basalt that broke as it cooled. Other places gleam milk-pale, splinted selenite, gypsum needles starting, a spill of salt whiskers like frost that never feels cold. The ceilings arch in uncertain domes then fall again into low pressings. Pillars haven’t had the centuries to grow trustworthy; they are columns in a first draft, bulged in the middle, crazed with new fractures. Every few minutes there’s a small tink from shrinking mineral finding a new fit. Nothing here is finished. It’s all edges and beginnings.

And yet, there is light—bright as a dull overcast day, enough to throw a shadow. It doesn’t come from a sun. It wells up from the air itself, from bodies too many to count. Swarms of fist-sized flitters, translucent wings like mica torn thin, pass in streams along the major fault-ways. Their abdomens carry a moving milk of luminous organs that pulse in quickly converging waves, so that an entire flock flares and dims with the ripple of a sound. Along the floors, the light hangs lower and steadier where little pillbug-lamps—armored, lantern-bearing grazers—move in seething caravans along the film of life that coats the wet stone. At rest, they glow a sleepy blue-green. When something shrieks, they crackle to bright white, and everything looks briefly etched. It isn’t magic; it’s a hunger response. Their chemistry anticipates turbulence: the louder this world gets, the brighter it becomes.

Sound is everywhere, and it has weight. Hisses of brine at the warm seeps. The chatter of many mandibles on mineral. The susurrus of billions of gill-fronds beating at once. A constant clicking that comes from the very bones of the cave as it settles and opens. Then the big sounds: a roof-plate booming as a trapped gas packet finds a way out; a river that wasn’t there an hour ago, arriving with the blunt insistence of weather; the rush of a predator’s wake, and the answering roar of a million tiny wings. The air is thick with that noise. You can feel it in the cartilage behind your ears, trembled into you.

There are no plants. What life there is builds from darkness upward, not sunlight down. Warm, iron-rich brines thread through the rock and sweat into the open—seams where bacteria gorge themselves on what the rock offers and the deep water breathes. They are films so thin you wouldn’t know they were alive except for how they reflect the world: not green, but satiny, opal-gray, prismatic only under the sudden flares. In the warm drip-zones, they bead into membranes that quiver, exhale, and shiver apart when a drop comes down wrong. Little rasping mouths attend them constantly, soft bodies on many, many legs, the noise of their feeding like distant sand poured onto tin. Everything else eats the mouths or eats the things that eat the mouths. This place is an inversion of a meadow: brightness without leaves, a busy noon whose fields are micrometers deep.

The terrain opens in places to chambers large enough to qualify as weather—valley-sized naves with running ceilings and floors that rise in soft dunes of shed material, pale dunes not of silica but of molt: husks of chitin, flaked carapace, brittle wing-vanes. The dunes slide when something large moves across them, sending an insistent hiss down-gully. There are plateaus of clean, wet glass, streaked with track-trails so dense they become texture. Ledges colonized by ropey, mineral-slick snotwork where the drips are constant; cliff faces armored in salt growths that look like stalled thunderheads.

Ecology becomes obvious as motion. You learn families by the way they move and the machines they live inside. Grazer-currents pour along the microbial seams, soft as river grass, turning and turning to keep their gill-books in the wet. Wedgefish—no fish at all, but lengthened arthropods with hinged shells—position themselves in such currents and open to take the flow. They do not chase; they set geometry and let the cave feed them. The predators are geometry, too. You hear them before you see them: a collective susurration, then a cold space opening in the pelting of rain as the rain tries not to fall there. Slick, many-jointed, glaze-colored shapes that can press thin and then gather, the size of ponies if ponies were knives, folding and unfolding from the slick rock. When they slide through the pillbug-lantern herds the light goes white and then goes away, as if a cloud had passed.

Other lives are less obvious and nearer to weird. There are spools of muscle like taut ropes that stay wrapped around warm vents, flexing slowly as if trying to turn an idea into a knot. There are black balloons tethered to the ceilings by strands that are neither silk nor plant fiber, inflating when the cave exhales and collapsing when it inhales, harvesting the difference. They brood the flitter-swarms in their shadows. There are beetle-things that don’t walk so much as stitch, their feet leaving behind a tone you can feel and they can hear, a seam of remembered pressure that others follow.

The rules of the world begin to show themselves:

- Light and sound are coupled. Vibrations catalyze the living phosphors. A scream means noon; silence is night. The animals know this and use it. Many call not to warn but to see.

- Water falls by mood. It’s made on the ceiling more than it travels through the ground—condensation pulses driven by warm breaths from new faults and by the storms of bodies churning the air. There are “rains” that track along the ceiling like flocks, and animals that graze rain as if it were a migrating plant.

- Space is freshly minted. Doors open. A week-old culvert will be a gallery by next month. Floors shear slightly underfoot; ceilings flake in innocent-looking flakes the size of tables. Whole banks of the microbial fields can die and slough when a pocket of hot brine withdraws. Where you stood in bright certainty goes dark because the floor sagged and the hum changed.

- The base-food is mineral and breath; there is no softness that is not built out of abrasion. Anything that looks like moss is a city of mouths.

- There are territories, but not by line—by resonance. Species claim bands of pitch, occupying the same ledge by living in different notes. Humans (and the human-like, if any are here) learn quickly that to speak in another thing’s note is to risk being taken as food.

Built things, when you notice them, are shy and few but persistent. Lines of smoothed pebble set into grooves, so that water that wants to run can be persuaded to run exactly here, to leave that patch of wall dry. A ring of hammered copper disks—not metalwork in the old-world sense, but native nodules flattened under stone and dimpled—that hang by gut from a low arch; move through them and they jangle, and what might have been dark stays bright long enough to pass. Little terraces of molted plates mortared with slime, creating safe shelves out of scarp. Language, if it’s here, looks like scored rock in double rows where the bite-marks of some tool have been neatly repeated. There is a cave within the cave, a quieted one, where a series of resonant bowls have been chipped into a gypsum bluff. Their tones are low and patient; they hold a sustaining hum that, to the grazing swarms, is a kind of fence. This is technology: stone coaxing living light, water coached into habit.

You realize why it is loud: the whole system functions by being in motion, and stillness is a failing state. Bodies keep the air mixed, the films fresh, the rains moving; the rains keep the films alive; the films feed the bodies. When a lull comes—rare, a pocket history of tiredness—the light drains from the sky and the predators who hunt by feel slide like old knives because they prefer that darker world. Then someone—perhaps a troglodyte with a throat trained to drum a bowl into singing, perhaps a long line of pillbug-lanterns chasing their own bright nervousness—gives the place a push. Sound returns, radiating from a ledge to a wall to a basin, waking little suns one by one.

Beyond the grand chamber lie galleries that stack and twist, like an apartment block built by fault-lines. The range is broad, not infinite, but enough to be an ecosystem of rooms: scooped amphitheaters where fresh selenite grows like staged lightning; low flues scuffed mirror-smooth by the passage of carpet-herds; dry gullets of wind that would be silent if not for the things that rattle themselves along them. You could walk all day—if you could call this always-bright, always-raining interval a day—and not return to the same edge twice. Places recur not by shape but by function: a feeding seam, a moltfield, a singing wall, a vent-lair, a rain-ledge.

Occasionally, a fresh doorway opens with the sound of a book torn in two, and warm, iron air rolls past you. The animals notice first, their whole bodies making the decision before any head does: a slight reorientation, a rush. The new room is bare, glass-bright, silent, as if a lung had opened in a creature. For a few minutes it is night, even in a world that adores noise. Then the pioneers arrive, the light-swimmers as thin and quick as thrown needles, kindling themselves at the walls with the tinkling of tiny bells. The future of that room is announced in a small rain, and, within the hour, it is part of the storm.
