The first thing is the air, not seen but felt as a weight moving: a slow, cold river of breath drawn through the planet’s ribs. It hurries without haste, constant, like a mill that has turned since before memory. You stand inside a hall hollowed eons ago by vanished fire and later planed smooth by ages of dust on the march. Stone rises and falls in shallow, settled swells, no edges left sharp. The floor is a skin of compacted powder and broken mineral, so old it holds the memory of every passage—the braided trails of day-migrants, the soft troughs where herds have slept, the vast curling scars of long-ago collapses, now quiet as fossils of motion.

No water moves here. You will not hear the plink of a drip or see the glint of a film. The cavern breath is dry enough to salt your tongue, to lift and carry any stray wetness as rumor and erase it. Yet life has learned the work of hoarding. Along the bases of pillars and in lee-eddies behind buttresses, the stone is felted over with dark mats and fronds—vegetation that takes more from air than from earth. It is lush where it chooses to be, sudden and extravagant in pockets, and then gone again, leaving long austere distances before the next thicket. The plants—if plants is the right word—are not leafed but ribbed and velvet, with surfaces that dampen sound and drink the humidity of breath. Their color is not the green of daylight but a deep quilt of mineral dyes: iron-bruise and lithic plum, dusty umbers and the cold shine of mica when a lantern-beetle drifts by.

Light is a rumor too. There are skylights of a kind: narrow chimneys that sip from the surface, spilling thin, mineral-gray day down in columns that barely reach the floor. Around these pale wells the growth rises like beards seeking a sun they will never meet. Elsewhere the luminance is borrowed and brief—the slow wink of bioluminescent fruitings on a fungal lawn, the swarms of small things whose bodies carry their own dull lanterns, the low glow of metabolizing mats warmed by their own chemistry. Sight never quite sings; it susurrates. Shape resolves at a pace like sediment settling. You learn to walk by absence and outline, by the curve that must be a pillar and the deeper dark that must be a descent.

And always, the noise. The hall is an instrument far older than its players. The wind does not pass; it plays the columns and galleries as if they were flutes and drums. You can count distance not by footsteps but by tones: a fundamental that thrums in the sternum, harmonics that skitter along the teeth. Now and then the current lungs itself through a narrow and a note leaps—deafening, absolute—only to fall back into the ground roar that lives everywhere here. Stone speaks in its sleep, too: the faint ticking of stress, a long, almost-thoughtful groan of support giving down a hair, the sift and patter of migrating dust. Living voices braid into this tide until you no longer separate them: wingbeats making cloth out of air, a thousand thousand scratchings on the powder skin of the world, the chorus of bodies singing just to know one another exists in the tumult.

Below the high vault the land spreads outward, gently ribbed. On one side a ridge of fused pillars runs like a city wall, scalloped by time into stages and balconies. Opposite lies a shallow basin paved with ancient collapse-talus mended over by life; its stones are stitched together with root and filament, making an undulant terrace of habitats. Between them is an expanse of open floor like a dry sea, marked by faint, organized causeways, the only straightness in a realm without corners.

The built place begins where the growth swings up into architecture. It is a city by intention rather than by device: towers assembled from the materials nearest to hand and body. Colonies of small six-limbed masons have chewed the dust to pulp and daubed it onto ribs of hardened resin, layer after patient layer, until spires and buttresses grow from the pillars like antlers. Blind cotyledon vines—their sap thick and oily rather than wet—are coaxed by warmth and breath to climb these frames and bind them, then fruit in swinging lantern-clusters fat with stored fats and trace water. The lanterns are plucked by winged residents who roost under cantilevered shelves lined with dry moss and fur shed generations deep. There are avenues: not paved, but pressed into permanence by a million passages. They part to go around plazas that are nothing more than open bowls filled with crumb and shell where fungal orchards are tended—great velvety sheets cut cleanly into feeding-patches and allowed to spore according to a calendar older than names.

Infrastructure is distributed like a nervous system. No machines: only habits, circulation routes, the design sense honed in the bones of creatures that have forgotten what a horizon is. Bridges are ligamentous swings between spires, fibrous ropes grown purposefully taut across air. Lintels are bodies; doors are thickets you learn to fold aside. Acoustic baffles hang in curtains, swaying softly—woven from shed wing membranes, from the stiffened fronds of noise-eating plants, all to damp the constant storm to survivable weather. Water, never seen, is moved mouth to mouth, fruit to newborn, exhalation to absorbing leaf. It is a law, here, the oldest rule: do not squander moisture to the air.

Life is abundant, but the spaces between presences are generous. You can stand at the verge of a populous nest-cliff and look out across hundreds of strides of stone where only the wind moves and a distant grazer limns itself against some dimmer dark. The grazers are pale, with ultrasoft feet, their joints padded with cartilage that sings very little when it meets the floor. They browse the orchards by permission, exchanging their breath and their dung for the right to crop. Their hides are a biology of rakes and hollows built to break up a standing wave; even their heads are shaped like silence.

Then there are the countless small ones: beads of life stringing the air and underfoot. Soft-bodied striders the length of a thumb cleaning the growth of dust, long filamentous hunters that lie in wreaths until a fly brushes and is swallowed whole, blind fliers whose faces are entire ears. In the dim you catch the flash of a mouth lined with keratin ridges like tuned combs; the creature snaps at a swarm and the sound shifts a note. High on the towers a species of swift returns from the world above with its crop swollen, not with water but with oils coaxed from desert seeds; it feeds its young and drips a shining onto waiting fronds that gulp it greedily. Beside the lantern-vines, little huddles of fur and feather take their rest in nests whose walls are steeped with years of breath until they are a humidity all their own. In the hush of alcoves you can hear the freckles of insects grooming themselves clean, a rainfall of vanishingly small sounds under the general thunder.

Rules of the world knit all of this. In a place where the air is noise and the stone is a drum, everything negotiates with sound. Architecture is a conversation with the wind: a shelving here to peel off a roar, a porous wall there to lean a pressure out of phase. Places to sleep are chosen for their nulls, those rare centimeters where interference cancels and an animal can close its eyes without its heart keeping time with a planet. The city spreads not by conquest but by climate: a ledge becomes livable when the growth there thickens enough to chew the sound; a square is opened when, through long trial, the inhabitants find that a certain shape scattered the dust best. The only speeds that succeed are patient ones. A tower grows the way a fingernail grows, and even a collapse is mostly dust deciding to lie somewhere else.

Beyond the nearest hall the cavern system runs on regionally, never quite closing, never truly branching; it swells and thins like lungs. Here and there a sink to the day spills a brighter gray down and there the orchards are deepest. Elsewhere the passages pinch to breath-slits where wind goes wild. The surfaces are all settled, final as old skin: dunes of grit that shift no more than a width of grain across a year, talus that has forgotten sharpness, stalagmites halted mid-syllable eons ago when water left the grammar of this world. The tone changes with the landscape in a way you can learn if you live here long enough: the low roll of a broad vault means safe crossing; the range of half-heard flutes means a lattice of bruising spurs; a trembling drone warns of a treacherous shell of fused dust over a drop.

Time is expansive and local. The city has neighborhoods that keep their children in the same roost-niche for generations. Orchards have records scored in their edges—the scalloped lines of cut and regrowth. Even the fauna have the patience of stone: migrations that are turnovers rather than travels, habits like rivers. Yet for all this slow, there is the instant, too: the avalanche of sound when the great pulse passes, a gust that makes a hundred thousand wings tense at once; the sudden snow of seed-skins when a vine decides that this is the hour, and the air becomes sweet with oil and spore.

To walk here is to learn the way of what is not present as much as what is. You learn to drink from a fruit that holds a week’s weather. You learn to read a distance by ear and to fold your speech into touch. You learn that cities can be made from resin and dust and promise, and that wealth is a clean, dim place where you can hear your own bones. And you learn, finally, that the original shape of things is the shape they have aged into: a hall where wind has been forever, where the plants are beds for the wind, where the animals are instruments tuned to survive a roar that never ends.
