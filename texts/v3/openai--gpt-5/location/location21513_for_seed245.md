The ground is not earth but an idea of ground that someone tried to make with their hands, then kept making until it ran away across a world. It lies low and broad, a continent of fired panels and unfired seams, terraces of clay the color of steeped tea, mats of woven stuff stiffened into stone, and long, patient ramps poured from slip that never quite dries. The horizon looks stitched: parallel join-lines run together, diverge, meet again at impossible angles, like a book that has been rebound too many times.

Heat rises from beneath, not flame but the old radiant of a kiln that has never fully cooled. The light is dim and pearly, as if seen through a thick canopy or the walls of a great vessel; there is no sun—only a ceiling of cloud that sweats. The air is almost quiet. Now and then it makes the sound of cloth being moved in a dark room, as unseen pressures shift, find new ways through the plenum.

Water is here as a decision, not a free agent. It beads along capillary cuts, crawls the length of hairline channels impressed by cord and finger. Whole kilometers of floor are carved with the faintest gullies—hand-width grooves that wend in broken lattices and hieroglyphics and serve as a grand drainage script. Nothing grows in them. Instead the grooves gleam with slow dew, condensing and rolling toward sumps lined with hammered copper plates blackened by use. Where plates meet panels, the joint weeps. On the undersides of overhangs and bridges of trussed ceramic, drip-rows space themselves with the regularity of thought. You can watch a drop form, elongate, tremble, and decide in which direction gravity is currently being enforced.

The world is new-made and never finished. There is almost no erosion because there is nothing long-lived enough to wear anything down. Edges are crisp and then, ten steps later, no longer there at all, not because they were rounded off but because the tile out of which the edge existed has joined some other tessellation. The place fixes itself by changing shape. A cornice straightens into a beam. A beam thinks better of it and becomes a low ridge with a slot running its length, joint-ready. If you look away and back, the slot might be on the other side; the beam has obeyed some deeper joinery you cannot see.

What passes for weather is a choreography of construction. Across the breadth of a basin you can see a storm of floors: a ripple of square plates buckles, lifts, folds over like a deck of cards being taught a new trick. A line of wedge-shaped keystones migrates across a plain at the pace of shade, settling into an arch that exists for a minute and a half, then relaxes into steps that travel on, breaking and remaking as if the verb of them is more important than their noun. Sometimes a whole quarter of the visible land fills with a low swell of glaze. It beads, contracts, pulls fine threads into the air, and when the swell recedes there are ladders where there were none—rungs of cooled glass strung between uprights of fired braid, their shadows indistinct in the dimness.

Everything here is made from simple things scaled to lunacy. Brick the length of a river-barge. Pegs taller than towers, made of petrified timber or something imitating timber perfectly down to the faint suggestion of grain. Ropes of fiber, three meters thick, hang in selected places and vanish into the panels, their surfaces saturated with pitch that never quite hardens. Held up close, everything reveals the insistence of hands: thumbprints impressed into mud when it was green, the angles at which a trowel was habitually held, the way two knots sit together because the hand that tied them had a preferred way to finish a loop. No tool here was more complex than a wedge, a line, a press, and a way of listening to materials. The world knows those tools and continues their actions without users, a planetary craft left to its own momentum.

There is no life in the sense that anyone might expect: no leaf, no filament, no eye glancing back. But there are processes that behave as though they are satisfied when certain patterns are reached. Salt blooms in spiral colonies where sun would have grown others, then disassembles itself because the humidity regime drifts. A sulfurous efflorescence steals a shape from the memory of a basket and is gone when the heat beneath that sector shifts phase. Micro-lace of silica will frost a length of railing overnight and unlace itself at dawn that does not come, sending its material on to outline something else. Even these are not lives, only habits moving through matter.

The rules here are simple and therefore inexhaustible. Pieces seek to meet other pieces at their prepared faces. Surfaces remember being walkable and will, if left unsupported by logic, develop a slight convexity to encourage traffic that does not exist. Water collects where grooves insist. Gravity is local, electing the most common normal among the surfaces immediately underfoot; if a new plane outvotes the old, your sense of down will rotate smoothly to match it, and the beads in the grooves will continue along their appointed paths without splash. Distance is negotiated rather than fixed: two points with the same joinery signature will find each other across the flat by deleting what they do not need between them, leaving you to walk a single step and be kilometers from where you thought you were. Sometimes the deletion is imperfect and a ghost of the middle remains—loops of stair to nowhere, a lintel standing in the field with its jambs subtracted.

Sound travels reluctantly, as if the air were entertained by remembering the world’s earlier noises instead. A drip takes a long time to hit as sound. When plates knit there is a far, small sound, a tongue click made by a giant holding her breath. Now and then the substructure groans like a great animal turning over in sleep—though there are none. Often it is perfectly still: the heat, the dimness, the million square kilometers of floors listening to their own geometry.

Water exists near the surface like a secret. Press a palm to a low sill: the ceramic is hot, but from inside you feel the coolness of water routes, sloshing like blood under skin. Lift a panel and you find a black-lined culvert recessed into a cellular foam of fired clay, capillaries of micro-runnels branching and coalescing. In drought moments the culverts hang empty and dusty, tasting of salt and wet once. In humid moments they bulge with beads, and seaming putty exudes little tears that do not evaporate until they have followed an instruction invisible to you.

The scale is so large that it makes its own local physics. In the distance, over a straight-run of floor, a not-quite-fog moves with intent, not wind but a variation in the permission to exist. Where it passes, new joints appear scored into the material, and things that were previously single become articulated. You might stand at the center of a plain tile a league wide and feel it deciding to be twelve tiles instead, each with a notch to accept a key of bone-colored ceramic already approaching from another horizon. The multiplicity of emptiness is deliberate: long expanses without a single feature except field marks of alignment—pips and scratches in the scale of cities—that exist to teach the world where to fold when its next instruction arrives.

Sometimes the dimness changes. Not lighter, not darker, but closer to the eye, as if the ceiling of cloud had lowered to consult with the floor. In those hours the place is more turbulent. Corridors knit out of nowhere and hurry away with the urgency of river rapids. A bridge will extrude from a parapet like a new tongue, reach toward another rise that had not been there, and the two will lock, shake hands, and then slip apart, the resin between them shining. There are storms of intention: a sudden unanimity of planes that pulls you a few degrees sideways, a competition of normals that leaves you slightly seasick until the majority vote reasserts itself. There is no harm in this, because there is no malice here, but there is the anxiety of watching a careful work be remade in front of you faster than you can guess why.

The underlayer—the world below the world—reveals itself at the edges of continents where floors have been upended to show their ribs. There are frames made of joined beams like the hulls of boats scaled to fit a sea-floor. There are giant wedges that skew the horizon when they are driven home. There are vaults held up by baskets, actual baskets, precisely woven and then turned to ceramic somewhere in their making, their negative spaces tuned to certain resonant drips so that the slow rain through them plays a patterned quiet.

What passes for a coastline is not land meeting ocean, but floor meeting reservoir. Here the constructed world thins to a tessellated apron that dips itself into a body of shallow, warm water held in a pan of black glass. The apron is full of needles and combs and chutes. The water is almost silent. Its surface purls occasionally without ripples, as if being breathed from underneath. No fish dart there. No skater insects riffle the sheet. The apron draws up what it needs, creams from it glazes and salts, feeds them back into the floors; then, with grace, returns the rest. Sometimes the pan empties altogether, and the apron hangs steaming and shining, and for a while all the drips on the underside of the world cease to fall. Then, as quickly, the pan refills from pipes the size of canyons, and the underside of bridges starts to count again, drop by considered drop.

You can tell the world is young not because it is smooth, but because it has opinions. It has not reconciled all of them. There are seams where one decision runs into another and neither wants to yield. Standing at such a seam you can feel a little failure in your depth-sense, as if one eye is recounting a slightly different story to the other. If you drop a pebble there (and there are pebbles, manufactured as standards of size and weight, scattered at intervals like punctuation) it will roll to the edge and wait, spinning on the thought of a slope. Then something far away will change—the weave of a basket the size of a city will decide to tighten—and your pebble will pick a direction and go.

If there is any law here that rises above the others, it is that the world remembers the reach of human arms though none are present. Stairs climb at a pitch that a body could take without breath. Handrail heights recur in logic even where no walkway exists, a ghost reassurance. Steps are shallow, because the designers were tired perhaps, or kind. Gaps never exceed a span that could have been leapt with a satchel and a thought for landing. Tools lie sometimes in the open—kept near, fossilized. A striker stone placed beside an anvil surface. A roller left at the edge of a line of glaze that stopped just where it is. A coil of rope in which the inside loops are still damp, as if the water inside the material forgot to evaporate with the rest.

When you press your ear to the floor it is warm and almost silent, except for a somewhere click and, very far off, the hush of a great breath drawn and let out again. The place takes its time and does not stop. It is sparse, because it is busy elsewhere, in the abstract. It is wet in the way that a mouth is wet: with intention, with work to be done. It is hot the way a kiln is hot: in service of transformation. It has no animals, no plants, no people in motion, but everything here is an answer to what hands once asked of clay, and clay has not finished replying.
