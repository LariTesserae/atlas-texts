The place is filled to its ceiling with cold water the color of slate, a slow and patient volume that has made itself at home in a chamber built for air. It moves as a body does when asleep—small shifts, a long pulse now and then—so that nothing quite rests even though nothing appears to hurry.

The chamber is not huge. Cathedral, not cavernous. The proportions tell you someone once measured this with human limbs in mind: an aisle flanked by ranks of cylinders and tall filter banks, a gallery of catwalks with knee-height rails, stairs that turn squarely onto a landing, a grid of beams with numbers stenciled onto them in a font that once managed to look modern. It was surely part of a larger plant or precinct: the desal hall, intake gallery, or pump nave of a low-lying industrial quarter. The flood—no, not a single flood, the age of water—arrived long enough ago that the chamber’s insides have had time to round off, settle, and acquire their own habits.

Everything is layered. It is dense with presences. Pipes bundle overhead in ribbed umbers and greys, looping and doubling back with the logic of a process diagram; feeder conduits sprout from them like veins; cable trays carry looms of insulated lines that have shed their outer jackets in curling peels. Gratings span across bays. A crane rail crosses the hall like a brow, its trolley arrested mid-span, the hook weightless in the water and furred with a rime of manganese oxide. Ladders run from catwalk to floor, their rungs haloed with feathery growth. On a pillar, behind a sliding sheet of mineral film, the word INTAKE can still be read.

The floor is silt. Not a beach and not dust—more the consistency of a settled snowfall, fine and dark. Anything stirring the layer, even a timid tremor from a pump starting somewhere, laces it into curls that rise and collapse with the slow panic of a dreaming animal. Heavy things have sunk a little; a dropped spanner shows only its bent spine, and an upturned stool rests like a fossilizing anemone, its legs buried to their knees. Between those sunk things run the tracks of residents, thin scribbles where some isopod or worm has grazed the biofilm.

There is a ceiling, but it is not entirely steel. Up in the dim, behind and between the beams, the water meets ice. The city above or around this hall is gone to winter, and the flood is capped with a lid of old sea ice that has thickened and annealed to the girders, filling apertures, knitting itself through rebar and truss like a slow, translucent fungus. From this ceiling hang a forest of brinicles: pale tubes that quietly grow downward, freezing the surrounding water into brittle skins that flake off and snow to the floor. Some brinicles have met railings and sheathed them glassily; some have reached the silt and pushed into it, petrifying funnels around their tips. Where the ice exudes its heavy brine, thin curtains of denser water pour like invisible velvet, and where those curtains spill over edges the marine snow drifts in them like ash in lamplight.

There are lights, and that is a strangeness in itself. Most things electrical have died the dignified, untheatrical death of wet equipment. Yet here and there a filament still glows. Emergency lamps in rusted housings simmer amber with the steadiness of distant coals; a panel’s status LED—no larger than a pinhead—pierces a meter of brown water like a small, patient star. Some of it is still powered. You can feel it as a boundary in the water: a faint warmth on the hand near a motor casing, a vibration under a rail. Low hums run in the structure, the way blood lives in a wrist. There is a schedule to it. Once in a while a relay clicks somewhere beyond the walls, a noise heard as a minute tap through your bones, and a pump far off will begin to revolve. Not enough to move the world, but enough to tilt it: a gradual draft down the main aisle, a lifting at the brinicles that sends their flaked skins in a new direction.

Sound does not travel far in here. The water eats it, or maybe the tangle of things does. There is a constant, cottony hush that is almost a taste, metallic-faint and cold. When you set a boot down on the grating it answers with a minimal clink, already dulled. Now and then there is the creak of a beam flexing to the minuscule rhythm of a tide, the whisper of bubbles along an edge where insulated copper still gets hot enough to force the air out of its skin. Your breath is even, nearly noiseless, if you are built to breathe this. A fish moving displaces a palm’s worth of water and you hear the event as a thought.

Vegetation, as such, does not take hold here. There is not light to spare, and there is cold. Instead there are growths: pale bacterial pelts matted on certain gaskets; threads like hair where seepage is constant, their food drawn from iron and sulfur rather than sun; a faint greenish veil along a cracked pane in an upper bay where some fickle shaft of sky does occasionally reach down when the ice goes blue. Everything that uses light lives in small, thin places, and it is easily outnumbered by the colorless. The denser brine falling from the ice roof makes blackened zones where even the film gives up for a while.

Fauna are infrequent and deliberate. There is a shadow long and guttering that no one sees twice and that might be a dogfish, but it could as well be a bundle of straps uncoiling in a draft. There are eelpouts, pale and languid, asleep with mouths against the warmth lines of a motor casing. Ghost shrimp commute along a cable tray as if it were a street; amphipods flip themselves incredulously out of settled dust to harvest the crumbs from a million years of decay. Every week or month—a calendar of predations more patient than a clock—you might see a slender, dark conger unroll itself from the drain at the base of Vessel E-12, unhurried in its singular appetite. It is not a rich place, and so nothing wastes energy. A thing that fails to move goes unbothered for a very long time; then, when the silt rises, a hundred stomachs open.

The built order is legible if you know to read it sideways. Signs are everywhere and mistakes are rare, though they exist. White-on-green arrows indicate an EXIT that is now a darkness at the end of a corridor where the ice reached first; a stenciled warning not to climb beyond this point keeps its integrity as paint even while the steel beneath it exfoliates quietly into rosette-like rust. A map of the plant, in enamel on the wall beneath a numb layer of biofilm, still shows your gallery as a blue rectangle with neat in/out flows. It is wrong at the borders, because the borders have changed. The water has made new connectivity. Doors stand left on their hinges. Gratings are missing and replaced by membranes of old floating plastic, taut as skins and tougher than they look. Where the urban nature peeks through the industrial—canteen trays piled in a mesh bin, a bulletin board with idling notices, lockers with names—the water has arranged them into banks and alcoves, like furniture in a theater underwater, intimate and oddly not disordered. It is a neighborhood of machines where people once moved, now occupied by the slow industry of corrosion and small things.

The rules here seem few but they are binding. If there is an edge it will be softened. If there is a volume it will be filled. Hard corners gather silt; open mouths grow films; heat draws life and also hastens the metal to its own end. Nothing sharp stays sharp, except the cold. Gravity is a negotiable contract in the water: heavy things sink, but not immediately, and a sheet of plastic large enough may hang mid-column like a paused breath until a pump’s draw makes up its mind. Light is precious and becomes a place when it exists—fish hold in it; films thicken a little; all eyes, if any, turn there. Time is not in hours in this hall; it is in silt layers, in brinicle lengths, in the regularity with which the pump wakes and sleeps.

Weather works even within walls. Sometimes the ceiling brines drip more nastily than at other times, a function of outside freeze and thaw felt as a mood. Durations pass with the floor quiet as obsidian, then a little seismic murmur in the structure tilts a tray of trapped bubbles and they all release in a string of beads that ring soft against each bracket. A brownout, the tide pulling power thin, brings lamps to a lower simmer; then, when the grid out in the drowned district evens, the amber warms again by a subtle shade. Now and again the far sliding of a floe grinding on the ice cap above transmits through the girders as a gentle, somber hymn.

Stranger things have their places, too, because the world that made this room is not exactly the one doing the living in it now. In a corner where a transformer once stood there is a standing cloud of microbubbles, as if the water there remembered to breathe differently. Touch it and your glove comes back gritted with metallic taste: the skin of the hall giving off slow hydrogen along a fracture that never quite mends. On the top rail of the crane a colony of jellylike bells have staked themselves in a row, each thin as onion skin and exuding a touch of light when the current picks up; they probably are not animals as anyone would have called them before, but they are not not alive. Around certain bolts the rust has flowered into symmetric rosettes shaped by fields, delicate and rigid, their architecture the petrified map of a current that is long gone but whose form remains. On the ice ceiling a socket wrench froze into a bubble years ago and now sits like an insect in amber, magnified and made absurd by lenses of melt and refreeze that conduct sunlight like a rumor into the deep.

The hall is not abandoned by what built it, merely outlived by the way that building had in mind. Somewhere two chambers over, behind a wall, a set of capacitors still accepts and then resigns a charge on a schedule written for a world of days and nights. The pump that wakes this aisle does so not because anyone asked it to, but because the tide asks a generator to, and the generator drags the line just over what is needed. Cores still magnetize and demagnetize, and in doing so they give off tiny tastes into the water. The place is industrial and still industrial—only its output is now to maintain a very small performance: hold a few lamps; keep the silt from seizing a few movements altogether; warm a seam enough that a film, and then those that eat the film, can find their lives along it.

Beyond the main aisle there is a side room that is essentially a closet. It holds bins behind a mesh gate and a pegboard with silhouettes painted for wrenches that are long gone. A slate hangs beside it with a checklist washed nearly blank. On the mesh a scrap of something once paper has persisted as a pearlescent skin; trapped between it and the wire is a small fish skeleton, complete and frail. When the draft rolls through, the membrane trembles and the bones shiver faintly. It is the most expressive movement in the room and it says nothing but the place, and that it goes on.

If you stay long enough you learn the hall’s calendar without names. The brinicles lengthen very slowly in their quiet season and then sometimes they are beheaded by a pump surge and begin again. The amber lamps wink out, one and then another, and then months pass and one surprises you by returning after some other circuit reroutes. The conger eats at dusk—dusk being the hour after the main draw has run and the water is the quietest it will be—and if it is not dusk it eats when the quietest seems to coincide with where it has arranged itself. In the occasional week when some condition outside clears the water to clarity you can see from here to the far wall as if through dusk air, and the hall shows that it believes itself to be neither ruin nor sea but exactly what it is: a low, cold, dense, blackwater city-room, long-lived and neat in its own way, with work that is not anyone’s now but is still work.

Stepping back from it, if you can call it stepping in water, you take in the comprehensiveness of the flood and the hospitality of what was made. It is submerged in the strictest sense—there is no air here, no place for a flame to live, no sound that would carry if you shouted—but the structure holds. The water holds, too. Everything has reached its settled motion. Nothing is idle, though many things appear to be. The hall has found an equilibrium that is not stillness but consent. It has become a region with borders and seasons, a tight weave of materials and low lives, a subdued theater of metals and brines and the few that feed at their thin junctions. In the darkness that is never absolute, work continues, small and entire.
