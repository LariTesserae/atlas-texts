It is a room the earth kept for itself and then forgot, a rounded pocket not much larger than a cistern. Ceiling close, walls bowed and slick, floor sloping toward a shallow lens of water that never rises and never quite recedes. The rock here—pale calcite veined with darker, older stone—has the tired polish of something handled for ages. Every surface is scalloped, pocked by the soft insistence of droplets. The walls weep; they do not flow. Drips gather, hang, consult gravity, fall, and then gather again. Time in this place is counted in beads.

The air is cool enough to nip the fingertips, heavy with breath-warmth and the metallic tang of stone dissolving in itself. You can smell wet limestone, old clay, the ghost of something once burned a very long time ago and never again. Condensation thickens on eyelashes, collects at the base of hair, and slicks the throat. Mists knit and unknit. If there is a breeze, it is the slow, imperceptible shifting of vapor from a cooler patch of wall to a slightly warmer one.

Sound does not travel here so much as it becomes architecture. The room is loud—not with chaos, but with the steady, piled-up roar of a hundred different small insistences. Drip against film, film into rill, rill into lip, lip to cup. A shallow channel throbs when full, a membrane somewhere under the eaves drones gently, two chambers on the far wall set up a standing hum sewed together by reflected thuds. The place has found its frequency, and, being left to it for long enough, it holds. Conversation, if you try one, becomes a mouth-to-ear thing, cheek to cheek, lips cupped; otherwise, the room takes every word and rephrases it as itself.

At first, you do not see the city. It has the modesty of habitation fitted—no, grown—to the host it depends on. Then your eye settles into the repetitions and catches the patterning: ledges that are too regular to be chance; ribs of carved stone mortared with a slick white lime; clay shelves baked to a dull russet by the only fires this pocket allowed, long ago when a drift of drier air visited for a season and never again. Stacked along the walls are many small chambers, rooms no bigger than baskets, connected by narrow walkways and braided ropes. They nibble at every available curve. Steps are cut into the stalagmitic bulges; gutters arc from a tear in the stone’s eye to a catch-basin the size of two hands; a web of slender aqueducts tenses the air.

It is dense work. Nothing is wasted. Where a rivulet insists on tracing a vertical path, someone has hung a line of cupped spoons carved from bone, each cup overflowing to the next, a tiny ladder of the loudest, clearest drips. Where seepage spreads in a sheet, the wall is combed with a scribed texture, encouraging the water to gather and choose lanes. Beneath one regular leak, a low wheel of slotted clay turns with priestly steadiness and ticks a strung cord of pebbles. The cord hauls nothing and moves nothing; it is there to be seen moving—proof that time does not sleep.

Light is a reluctant and precious citizen. No true fire survives the wet. A handful of shallow bowls along the walkways hold a cold fox-fire gleam: fungus coaxed to luminesce from bread of soaked mineral and scraps provided by those who lived here, bright enough to teach your eyes the edges and no more. Elsewhere, flat patches of crystal have been polished to mirrors to carry this dimness into corners. Over the central pool a fibrous veil—skin or stitched gut—hangs in strips, each strip marked with punctures of different sizes; the water’s breath through these makes a range of hoots that drift in and out of the constant murmur. Someone calibrated them once and named them. On a shelf lies a board of slate incised with lines and dots: the music of this room recorded not as melody but as a map of which mouth should be uncovered after rain, which tied off when the dry returns, and how to keep the chorus stable even as the seasons above turn without announcement down here.

Vegetation as you know it never took hold. No moss pads the stone; no root pries. The place entertains other, simpler life forms in very thin, courteous layers: a slick film at the waterline that shies from touch with a swift darkening; the ghost-glimmer colonies in bowls; a dust of spores around the lip of the vents. It is cleaner than a hospital and dirtier than a river—mineral-clean, alive in a way that does not readily show itself to the eye.

Every volume the water does not claim, the city tried to. The inhabiting species—once numerous or simply meticulous—worked with hand-tools whose ghosts are still here: a chert scraper with its edge retouched so often it is more handle than blade; a copper awl, fat with green; twine of sinew drying to soft stone; needles of bone. Metal was rare and reserved for ends that altered nothing else: a punch, a point, a weight. The big things—walls, bridges, platforms—are stone, fired clay, gut membranes, and a mortar compounded from the room’s own shed minerals. These people knew the weight of wet and built for it. A shelf curves upward at its edge so that no pooled droplet can find the seam. Ropes are braided with a bias so that when they absorb water they tighten rather than slacken. Doors are not doors, they are curtains made to silence a leak or awaken it as needed; thresholds are more like sills where excess can collect until called for.

Rules of the world, as they learned them: There is no flame that is not a problem. Dryness is a treasury kept in sealed vessels—in the high chambers you can find stoppered jars whose insides glitter with salt and desiccant, caches of crispness reserved for those delicate works that cannot be risked to damp. Adhesion is best achieved by the rock’s own spit; bind everything with lime and patience. Sound is faster than feet—speak by knocking; tune the room to carry your code. Water is authority; negotiate with it, never oppose it. Erosion is not your enemy or friend; it is your city’s slow-eyed landlord. Measure time in drips and the tension of ropes. Consider light a luxury, but understand that, in its absence, memory must carry the map: carve the measure of the span between two safe steps into your bone. No growth without rot—accept that all gut, rope, and clay will return sooner than you. Accept that the rock is older and will outlast the maps you cut on it.

Not many things live here that are not the city. Now and then, a pale salamander like a piece of knotted porcelain rises in the pool, opens its soft mouth at the air, and lets the constant noise wash through its gills. Its eyes are the idea of eyes. It is not afraid. In the shadow of one of the upper shelves, a knot of blind isopods graze on the thin bacteria down where the water comes and brings with it the faint taste of far upheavals. A cricket without chirp shivers its legs against a calcite rib and finds no one to answer. The fauna are few, like punctuation—a comma in a wall of text.

As for people, there is only evidence. There are tiny finger-prints hardened in ancient clay. A line of pebbles set in mortar marks the height of the great drip of year X; the line that follows marks the collapse of a chamber beyond, plugged forever now by a steadying rockfall. A bed is a depression on a shelf where someone slept with their back to the stone and the hum in their lungs. A row of knots on a string threaded through holes in a bone records an instruction in all the detail of words; the knots are colorless with routine. If anyone remains, they keep to the margins. Sometimes a shadow moves at the seam of ceiling and wall and adjusts a strip of gut on the singing veil, not to change the song, only to keep it true to itself. The gesture is reverent without being religious. It is maintenance: the old, human craft of preventing difference.

So the place persists. It does not change much and takes long comfort in that. The rock continues its slow dissolves and reprecipitations. The room sweats and swallows. The city, more lattice than mass, becomes part of the room’s grammar and not its interruption. Loudness, paradoxically, becomes a kind of privacy; every sound is held by all the others and sent back as warmth. In this, the pocket has achieved an equilibrium that feels deliberate. Perhaps it is. Perhaps underneath the planed surfaces and mortared corners, everything that can happen already has, and will continue to happen exactly so, bead to bead, as long as there is weight above, a vein of water to condense, a salamander to witness, and a hand now and then to mend a string and pat the stone and whisper the maintenance prayer that can only be told mouth to ear:

Keep the drip slow. Let the rope swell. Give the noise its room.
