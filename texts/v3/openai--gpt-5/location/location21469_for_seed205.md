The plateau begins in a hush of thin heat and resin scent, and then the hush betrays itself as only a lull between enormous voices. It is high up—breath finds the ribs and pauses there—and bright, the sky an ordinary blue made strange by distance. Look down and there is no earth in the familiar sense. The ground is plant, all of it: a continuous skin of living fiber and laminated leaf, an old growth that forgot the forest’s vertical ambition and turned itself instead into continent.

From a distance the surface looks bare, even empty. That’s the trick of it. The plant-body spreads in long, low tables and ribbed shelves like a calmed sea, its details scaled beyond quick comprehension. It has had ages to settle, to wear itself into balance, and the forms it keeps are large: swells the length of cities, shallow basins a traveler crosses in a day, braided gutters that remember old torrents and the brief seasons when water ran. No rock breaks through. Here and there—far scars and distant outcrops—are spines of woody tissue buttressed like flying buttresses that once lifted canopy, now weathered to open arches. Between them is space, real space, long lines of sight under wide weather and the indifferent shine of sun.

Underfoot is resilient. The upper skin is slick in the early hours, beaded with condensation drawn from last night’s breath. Beneath that a mat of fibrous plates, layered and interlocked, flexes with footsteps and rebounds on its own slow rhythm. If you kneel and put your ear to it you’ll hear a sea inside: the pump and creak of sap columns, the clacking of vascular valves, small animals scrabbling in a world that is mostly vein. Every impression in the surface tends to a shape it has had for lifetimes—rounded by seasons of expansion and contraction, smoothed by grazing mandibles, kept open by the plant’s habit of placing its growth away from its wear.

Water is a rumor and a habit more than a feature. It arrives as a skin on everything at dawn, a fine rain of its own making as the plateau exhales across the temperature break. In pockets—cold pockets, shaded concavities where the air lingers—condensed mists collect in clear bowls. They smell faintly of green and spice. By midmorning they are gone, drunk back into tissue or licked away by tongues and capillary hairs. Some valleys host perennial damp—the old drainage braids where dew runs together and vanishes into stomata the size of doorways. Those gaping pores breathe on cycles: warm, wet exhalations that fog your eyes, sharp inhalations that pluck at clothing and tug at river-threads of vapor trailing from anything that leaks.

Heat presses by noon. The plant’s upper layers gleam as resins soften and web into gloss sheets stretched like varnish over giant leaves fused long ago. In the glare you learn the rules: never stand on a gloss patch in the heat; the softened surface can peel and swallow a foot, and the plant will not care. Never stop at the mouth of a breathing pore; the draw is sudden, and though it won’t kill you, being pinned for a minute in a hot wet wind while an air-lobe deflates is a bad way to learn patience. Always move with a rhythm the plant knows. Stomata ignore the background drumming of their world; it’s how the plateau keeps from flinching at its own heartbeat.

The world is sparse in shape and crowded in life. That paradox becomes clear the first time a wave of feeders rolls over the horizon. They are many, and yet they occupy only a fraction of this emptiness at any moment. Herders among them call them mantle-grazers: shoulder-high on jointed shafts, more leg than torso, plates like woven baskets that rasp the surface as they lean and comb the skin. Each carries a parasitic language of smaller things—flicker-larks that pick at mites, seed-wasps tucked into the grazers’ plate-joints, filament mice that knit tiny wind-harps from shed fibers and hang them on the grazers’ undersides. The herd’s mouthparts make the soft relentless rasp of a file on wood, multiplied by thousands, and their footfalls are hollow knocks resonating the plant’s cavities into drums.

Sound gives this place its weather. It has air currents and dew cycles, yes, but it has fronts of noise, moving systems of pressure and vibration that change the day. At sunrise the plateau groans and sighs as it warms, the big valves clapping in slow applause along a band that you can watch advance, shadow to brightness. Midmorning brings the first stridulation: invisible insectile millions in the growth’s pores gathering into chorus, each individual quiet but together mapping an arc across the basin like rain on a metal roof. Warblers—the name suits, though they are more membrane than bird—inflate themselves against the drop and hum under the sun to move heat and moisture through their thin bodies; the hum stacks on hum until the air itself has a standing tone. And then the predators, who hunt with shock and rhythm, begin their percussion: wide-backed skate-lizards slapping their tails on drum-hollows to stun the filament mice; percussion-tortoises battering the resonance chambers to drive seed-wasps out in wary fountains.

On some days a choir front forms. You see it first in the way heat-shimmer turns to a visible ripple on the horizon. Then it arrives: a migratory wall of wing, bladder, and glass. Imagine organisms that fly almost by accident—seed-rafts and their minders, translucencies like kites that drink dew and spend it in loft. They are host to others who sing through them, parasitic and symbiotic bands that climb higher on the harmonics. When the wind picks up—and there is always a wind here—the whole assembly thrums so hard the plant answers, and something in your chest vibrates in sympathy until you feel like a reed in a pipe. It is deafening. It is so loud that speech shrinks to the bone-conducted intimacy of throat-sounds and finger-press. The people who live here grow thick cartilage in their ears, and even they carry wool-combs tied under their jawbones to dampen the skull.

Between such passages there is emptiness, and it has its own saturation. Sky like a polished bowl. The horizon a section-line where the plant rolls on until it decides to drop into cloud. The heat tempts you toward any vertical relief—a stoma-tower, a bleached rib arch—and from there the space rearranges. You trace the flow-lines of ancient water in discolorations through the tissue. You spot the camps, because even the smallest inflected human angles stand out against the plant’s organic geometry.

The built world here is scant in number and precise in placement. A shelter is not a house but a fold in the plant’s fabric encouraged to keep its shape with woven supports, located where the breath of a giant pore will vent steam over you in the chill hours and leave you dry and licked at dawn. There are racks for dew-stones—porous nodules stolen from the damp braids that condense fog toward a cup. There are sound-breaks: fans of bundled fiber set upright to make a shadow in the noise, no larger than a person, but enough to soften the hammering of a chorus and make a conversation possible at hand-span. Underfoot near these shelters are mats of braided grass and resin—fields of quiet where footsteps don’t telegraph to the living ground.

Technology here is an intimacy with the substrate. Hand-axes grown as much as made, coaxed from nodes of woody tissue that can be taught to split cleanly. Glues distilled from sun-thickened sap. Lines spun from trichomes combed off the underlayers—angle-to-angle learning, always checking the plant’s tendency to reclaim whatever is still. Knots on those lines mean things: which way the dew pooled last month, what the long resonance said last night about the pressure to the west, where the grazers raised their dust so that the seed-rafts may follow. Smoke is rare; smoke would get trapped in cavities and bring mold, and mold is the only enemy this big plant truly respects.

Erosion here is the masterpiece of a slow duel between growth and wear. In the gulches that were once rivers, the plant found templates it liked and reinforced them, lignified until the braids held their curves. On ridgebacks the sun has hardened the skins to bronze. Wind scours the soft edges of new protrusions back into the plain. Predators bite the same paths again and again until their trails become acoustic corridors, long tubes of resonance that even the absent can use to speak if they know the drum code. Every now and then you come upon a pit like a wound lined in shimmering brown. Those are the old spore geysers—the places where, every few years, the great plant pulses and pops an organ to throw its reach beyond its own horizon. Nothing grows at the edge of those for a while; the resins are too fresh and the air too full of terpenes.

Rules of travel, learned quickly or not at all:
- Move at a steady beat. Sudden accelerations read like the flutter of something small and edible, and many here have learned to ambush by sound alone.
- Give the plant time to tell you if it will bear you. If your footstep strikes a drum, wait for the reply. Hollow warns you the cavity is near and the membrane thin; feeling the return can save you from a fall into a lung-chamber full of warm damp air and the patient white stars of fungi.
- Don’t shout. Your voice can trigger the seed-wasps into a defensive cloud and call the skate-lizards that love the way panicked feet thump.
- At mid-afternoon, when the breath winds reverse and the heat lifts, get below the gloss. The resins vent in invisible spritzes that can blister skin. Those same vents, after sunset, are where nocturnal pollinators will mass to drink. The night’s loud is of another kind, subtler, all whisper-clicks and velvet thumps, and what hunts in it is better at patience than you.

The ecology makes use of the plateau’s size to have weather with teeth. The plant must drink and cannot; it harvests air. So it pulls fog up through its lungs in the cool and down in the heat, creating rips and eddies of humidity you can walk across. The grazers need moving food; the plant answers by migrating sugars across its plain in gradients that drift like invisible tide-lines. In the afternoon, the sweet places, warm with sap, lift mirages that bring the flyers in, which bring the drummers, which bring the listeners, and every evening the plateau is a little different because of the day’s music. Even the very edges of the world—where the living level steps down abruptly into the sea of clouds—have their own breath. On some days you can look over and see the undercloud lit gold, marbled with the shadows of floating seed-rafts like the ghosts of jellyfish.

Strange is ordinary here. Stomata big as gates look like open mouths until they steam and you taste their exhale. There are bushes that are not bushes at all but knots of hair-harvesters, animal-shaped sproutings of the plant’s own trichomes animated by slow wind and the memory of touch. The sky has periods when it fills with glitter—not dust but pollen platelets like thin coins, each a tiny scale that hums as it falls, tuning the day to a strange metallic chord. Walk long enough and you’ll find what the old herders call a listening hollow: a place where the plant has put so much of its mass toward sensing the world that standing there you can feel—faint, like a rumor—the weight of storms forming far off, the footfalls of animals you cannot see, the distant choir front like an advancing weather map in your ribs.

There are marks of old hands and old stories. Bits of fiber netting half-swallowed by the plant, left from a time when people tried to moor a camp and the living ground refused to be owned. Rope ladders down into safe lungs, where children are cooled on the hottest days and elders keep seeds in woven bladders, knowing the spores will not take there. Upright rings of driftwood—where did the wood drift from, in this all-plant world?—carried for years along the gulches and finally tamed into place as wind-breaks, rattling low in the hot rise of afternoon. Far away, on a spine that curls like a fossilical wave, a single roof of leaf-slate pinned with bone: a place of leaving, or of exchange, or of songs so old they don’t care about meaning, only about the way sound travels.

It is hot. It is loud. It is full, but not cluttered. Between the congregations of life is air enough to count heartbeats and to take stock of the way your own body is learning this place: the lightness of the step, the way you tilt your head to parse the music for weather, the way you stop under the shadow of a spire not for shade but because it is a landmark and a promise that the map in your mind is holding.

By evening, the plateau loosens. Heat slides off. The pores drink and close one by one with moist pops you can feel through your soles. The grazers kneel and lock their knee-pins to sleep in rows like the ribs of giant fish laid spine-up in a dry ocean. Seed-rafts unknot their tendons and collapse to puddles of silk. The deafening becomes a textured blanket of noise you can almost forget to listen to. And if the night is clear, the stars look normal and cold, their ordinary light falling on a world that is anything but ordinary and yet has long ago decided what it will be, and will keep being it long after you are gone.
