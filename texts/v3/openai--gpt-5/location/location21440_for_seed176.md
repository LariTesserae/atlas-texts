You come into it sideways, edging along a catwalk bolted to a wall that drops out of the world. The rock is so close and so sheer you feel it in your inner ear, a plane of dark basalt rising past your shoulder to nothing and falling away to more nothing below. At this elevation the sky isn’t above; it’s draped around you. The cloud deck scrapes the cliff like a sleeve, and the sleeve is wet. Every surface shines. The stone weeps along hairline fractures in a thousand parallel seams, and wherever metal touches it, the meeting point beads, drips, becomes a thread of silver.

The ledge itself is hardly a ledge—more a balcony that the mountain forgot to finish. It runs the length of a cathedral nave, then narrows to a blade and is gone. An overhang like a brow keeps direct rain off but does nothing about the endless, generous mist. The rock is young-faced: sharp junctions, crisp planes, a fresh fracture sheen under the black skin where a flake has spalled. Columnar joints step back in hexagonal geometry, unrounded, as if all this rose last season and has not yet had time to soften. You can see the arithmetic of cooling lava in the wall, the tidy logic of break and angle, but everything is slicked with water and translated into gloss.

Green has rushed in to occupy the gloss. It’s not a forest but a congregation of plants so dense they share one body. Mosses rise like miniature velvet reefs between the bolts, and from them push ferns—the strap-leaved kind that shed a constant glitter of droplets from their tips, and the lacy kind that collect a deep cup of wet at the crown. Bromeliads anchor their rosettes in any nook: snugged into boltheads, cradled by a rub of concrete around an anchor post, wedged where a slab of basalt once fit perfectly before it fell. Their leaf axils hold an entire other sky in each cup, dark and reflective, rimmed with algae sheen. Orchids—thin, opportunistic, with roots like strings—have claimed the industrial lattice, sending roots across galvanized steel as if it were bark. The vegetation has made a sponge of the ledge; step on it and it gives slightly, the deep, matted give of roots layered on roots. Soil is a rumor. What little there is arrived on the wind and the nets.

The nets: ranked curtains strung like theater scrims along the outer edge, gray-white and taut, dense enough to stop a fingertip but not a glance. They don’t block the view of the abyss; they harvest it. The cloud glances off their filaments and leaves its water behind. Lines of droplets grow, connect, fall into a continuous vertical stitching along the bottom cords, then slide into troughs and gutter down into a header tank half-hidden in the moss. From there, everything tightens into pipe and pressure. A black line snakes out from the tank like a vein and goes down, and down, and then is gone, feeding through a sleeve in the rock to a Pelton wheel inside the squat powerhouse tucked under the overhang.

The powerhouse is a compact mass of pour and plate, the color of ship primer where the paint shows through lichen: corrugated walls riveted onto a poured concrete base, a door that fits too well against a rubber seal, a floor of steel grating you can see the green blur through if you look down between your boots. Inside, the air tastes of wet ozone and warm metal. You can’t speak over it. The wheel screams a steady, bright chord that never wavers, the tone of a jet of mountain falling a long way and striking precision bronze. The governor keeps it just off resonance. A flywheel the size of a millstone turns with a visible patience, transforming the screaming jet into a deep mechanical hum you can feel in your ribs and in the bolts under your feet. A transformer contributes its own intimate buzz. Gauges twitch with slow deliberation, needles never truly still; a grease-pencil line records months’ worth of flow on a scrolling drum, and the paper is crowded with overlapping, careful tracks.

Pipes and wires chase the rock. Everything binds to everything else. There’s almost no spare distance. Lianas of cable are laced to the backs of moss sods; hoses disappear into fern, reemerge with a silver skin of condensation; spray from a relief valve beads along a strand of orchid roots and becomes a transient necklace. The droplets are not content to hang quietly; they hum on guy-wires when the wind lifts, turning the whole place into an instrument. At certain gusts the wires choose a note together, and you get a high, wavery choir above the turbine’s keening, a sound both unearthly and perfectly explained by tension and length.

Beyond the powerhouse there are a few other intrusions, none large. A winch drum the size of a dining table stands with its cable angled into cloud, the line going to a sky-hook or a bridge or a world that exists only in inference. A row of batteries under a tin hood click quietly to themselves as they decide how to take the charge they’re given. A crate of mesh folded like folded sails, salt-stiff from years of fog. A bench under plastic, scuffed and littered with washers and a jar where some previous hand was catching the bigger leaks and changing its position every few days as the drips shifted.

The air is warm. Not tropical heat but deep-warm, like the warm of a greenhouse that is also a cliff. Even when the cloud thins and the sun finds you, it’s not sharp; it’s the normal light of day diluted in ten thousand droplets, bright enough for shadow but not glare. When the lid of cloud lifts, when the cliff breathes a little, the world extends out for a minute or an hour: you look past the nets and see other walls, other escarpments stepping in blue-grey tiers, rivers of cloud pooled in their laps. Then it closes again and you’re back in the close acoustics of the ledge, the steady shine.

Animals are almost an afterthought. You expect birds, but they come rarely, on the clearings, drawn to the sudden reveal of a span of air to cross. A pair of small highland swifts cut the fog in slashes and are gone. Once in a while a hawk appears where the world opens, a formal punctuation mark in the long sentence of the cliff. The rest of the time the only moving bodies are unhurried and familiar to the plants: a pale salamander clocking from bromeliad to bromeliad with measured leaps, body as cool as the water it beds in; thin spiders that set up sheets in still corners, peopled more by seeds than by prey; a wet snail that leaves an accurate trail across the painted “DANGER—PRESSURE” and then settles on the cool seam under an anchor plate. There are flies, yes, but they come and go in sullen handfuls, blown up here on a wrong wind and finding little to hold them. Even the moss feels like an animal sometimes, the way it recovers after your boot and smooths its nap slowly, as if remembering itself.

The rules are obvious in practice and odd in concept. Water arrives from the air sideways and is persuaded to fall in an orderly way; gravity from this tiny catchment powers machines with the certainty usually reserved for rivers. Soil is not a prerequisite for lushness; contact is. Leaves grow where metals sweat. The ledge grows longer not by any motion of stone but by a soft accumulation: humus cornered behind bolt-heads, tufa lips forming where a drip insists for months, a bead of lime thickening into an alabaster skirt under the relief valve. Nothing really changes fast except the noises. The noises are always already here, and if a new clack or hiss enters the choir it does it like a voice joining an old song, then settles and holds.

For all the loudness, the processes are slow. The gauges fill notebooks; the maintenance logs count in seasons. Where the rock lost a piece, a new geometry of moss will take a decade to fill the raw edge; in the power room you can lay your finger against the paint and feel where hands have done that for years, not even wearing the gloss yet. The fog lines on the nets tell their own stories in faint stripes—dry season, wet season, the unusual month when the wind set steady from the southeast and the lower third of the web never shimmered. Under the gratings the root mat drops a millimeter a year and gains two. If the cable out of sight goes to a village, you imagine the people below experience their lives at the same tempo: light comes on when the cloud is close and stays on as long as it is; the refrigerator hums a pattern of fog.

Where the ledge ends, it ends without apology. The catwalk narrows to a rib run between the nets and the raw wall, then stops at a bumper painted hazard yellow and scarred with so many small collisions of hope and caution. Standing there you can touch rock on one side and damp mesh on the other and listen to it all: the constant, high white of the turbine; the articulate patter of drips hitting metal and moss; the sighing conversational wind through the guy-lines; one bleat of a relief valve reluctantly doing its duty and then closing with a tired cough. In between those loud things are the tiny, slow ones—a leaf uprises from a fern crown as its pool drains by half; a bromeliad cup overflows and sends a minor stream down a hawser, which a salamander reads as invitation hours later.

And sometimes, not often, the cloud opens all at once and you get the actual shape of where you are: a narrow shelf on the face of a newly wrenched plateau, a shoulder on the lift of the world; green and gray meeting in a small square of insistence, a machine that rides water woven from sky, hung in air that is never still. For a minute the noise falls away into the size of the view. Then the sleeve closes again, and the cliff resumes its wet breath, and the place becomes busy and close and itself, patient as engines, damp as thought.
