At the edge it looks like the world is spilling away in silence. White rivers of cloud pour off the high rim and fall without a sound, torn thin into veils by the roughness of the cliff faces. The rock itself is dark and old and always wet, a sugar of quartz shining under films of water that bead and merge, bead and merge again, never quite drying. Where the cliffs lean inward they grow beards—drips turned to threads turned to curtains—so that the stone seems to weep even when the sky is clear. The air is cool enough that your breath feels like it belongs to the place rather than to you, and it tastes of tannin and wet lichen and a hint of metal, the way a fingertip tastes after a spent spark.

From the rim inward the plateau is not flat, not exactly; it is a country of slow shapes. Runnels cut long ago by sheetwater still dictate where everything goes. Basins in the bedrock hold peat sponges, and the peat holds pools, and the pools are thin and brown and perfectly still unless a sedge leaf taps a ring across them. The ground is springy underfoot, layers of moss on centuries of dead moss, roots braided into carpet and then into rope. Every stone has a skin. Every branch is upholstered. Nothing is bare for long; anything exposed grows its own softness.

Lushness here is not a riot of size but of saturation. This high air is cool and thin and it carries more water than it should, and the vegetation is built to harvest fog. Trees, where there are trees, are short and stooped, their crowns like clenched fists, their bark furred in lichens the color of dusted silver. Epiphytes fill every manageable gap: vases of bromeliads, glistening tongues of moss, tiny orchids that bloom like thoughts. In the open the “forest” lowers into a sea of rosette plants, felt-leaved giants shouldered into colonies, each one a tower of soft spears. Along their spiraled ribs, water beads in perfect progressions, droplet to droplet to drop, as if the plant were a production line for making rain. Between rosettes run the sedge alleys and low fern groves and the knuckles of cushion plants, domed and patterned, so tightly woven that your boot never reaches soil.

Movement in this world is not a rush but a constant. Every surface condenses. The plateau has made a habit of catching the passing breath of the atmosphere and saving it. Wind crosses the miles and loses enthusiasm, its speed shaved away by the drag of a billion microscopic hairs. Clouds arrive as billows and leave as memory, their water fractionated into beads and films and wicks. Each afternoon, when the sun makes a rare sharp-edged shadow, there is a tide of vapor pushing up from the lowlands, and by evening the tide turns, and each plant seems to give a small sigh and pour off what it cannot keep.

The quiet is thick enough to lean against. Moss eats sound. Fog eats distance. The loudest things are small: a drop landing in a shallow pool with the note of a tiny drum; the glaze of a slow beetle clicking its wing cases together; a thin metallic chitter where wind threads a stand of horsetail-like stems and plays them like a glass harmonica. Larger animals move as if the place has taught them manners. Hummingbirds—cold-adapted, heavy-bodied—hover at the orchids with a sound like a held breath. A black shape crosses the distance between two rosettes in a line so straight and careful that you realize it is not a shadow but a weasel-thing, each leap measured so as not to waste air. Above, when the fog opens and something big makes use of it, the only sound is the scissoring of pinions. Even the frogs, which are everywhere, speak as if they are trying not to wake the rest: a series of small glass clicks, a tick-tick-tick like a winding watch from under a leaf’s brim.

It is not still. Every hour, somewhere near, somewhere far, there are sequences. The rosette fields exhale and inhale in synchrony along the windlines. The peat coughs when a pocket vents, a sound like the bubble of a great kettle. Along certain cliff runs, pipes in the stone catch the flow and breathe with the day; you can stand, hand on wet rock, and feel it cycle—cool in the morning, warm in the afternoon—so regular you could set a clock by it. Out on a long enough ridge, after dark, you will see brief halos at the tips of certain leaves and bristles, a foxfire without the fox: blue-white pinches of corona where the saturated air gives up a thread of charge. Hair lifts on the forearms without a breeze. The plateau is electrified, but gently, enough to make the fine-limbed plants whisper and the metal in your mouth taste more metal.

Predator and prey have learned the rhythm of the place. There are salamanders that never met their rivers; their whole lives are breathed through skin under the wet of moss and leaf. There are pitchers—plants built as instruments—that collect the day’s distillate in their throats and lace their rims with sweet; the insects that come to drink are counted and processed with patient chemistry, and a slow sweetness rises from those fields on windless days like the scent from a factory floor at night. There are sundews furred with transparent trap-hairs, each hair a string of pearls. Ants here do not march; they flow. They use the ropeways between cushions and rosettes as if they were laid out for them, a living conveyor that does not break as long as the weather holds.

Scale presses down gently, like an extra sky. It is easy to forget the edge exists once you step inward. The horizon is close and then always receding as the fog opens and closes; the far-off rosettes shift like an audience breathing. The systems that matter here measure in leagues. There are corridors that always drizzle, where the air is thick with separate droplets that never meet; there are high hollows where, for reasons rooted in the way the bedrock tilts, night dew arrives first and leaves last, so that in a drought that never happens they would be the last green. Rivers are born everywhere and nowhere, not as lines but as climates underfoot, water traveling sideways through a mat so dense it might as well be a medium of its own physics. When runoff does collect enough to look like water we know, it slides across the rock in sheaths too thin to see except by the stepping light on their backs. The only real falls are the white ones, where entire air-masses decide to become downward.

At noon, if the fog retracts, a normal light falls clear and undecorated across the plateau. Colors harden: greens turn law-enforcement official, lichens sharpen to chalk and charcoal, peat is a burnished amber, open water a smoked black mirror. Birds take that window as if it were a clock; you will see a frenzy of feeding among bromeliads—a dozen small bodies in one cubic yard of air—but the sound of it is a rustle, a handful of pages turned quickly and then put away. By the time the first halos hiss into being, each plant that kept its water back when the air was kind has begun to leak from its edges the way a cold glass sweats. Every angle, every corridor, participates. The plateau makes its own evenings and spends them.

There are rules to walking this place, and they feel made by the plateau rather than by chance. Water stores itself in living things first, then in sponges, then in stone; free water is rare not because there is not enough of it, but because everything else has claimed its share. If you clear a space, it will green from the edges in overnight; if you lay something down and leave it, you return to find its underside fuzzed, then trellised, then swallowed. Sounds don't travel clean; you hear the frog you just stepped past and not the owl ten paces to your left. The quickest route is the one that respects the invisible hydraulics: you go where the wicking lines go, where your boot returns to you dry in the dry hours and gives back what it took when it’s time. If you must cross a cliff lip, you do so in the company of spiders that can make a bridge out of breath; they lay lines that hold in fog like kites in wind.

The edges have their own community, a leaping place for everything that trusts air. Weeping walls are alive from pores to precipices. Filmy algae slide there, clear and gelatinous, kitted into draping sheets that flex with drops but don't tear, feeding on light that has been filtered twice. Micro-crustaceans swim on the face of the rock between one rumor of moss and another, worlds only millimeters thick. Frogs nest behind folds of falling air, their eggs clear as the water but set in a gel that ignores gravity; when the tadpoles hatch they move in the area between curtain and stone like lights behind rain.

What seems “industrial” without a single made thing is only the scale at which the living and the weather have agreed to operate. The rosette acres are condensers—thousands and thousands of paired leaves stepping down vapor into liquid. The bogs are batteries—ions trading places lazily in a mesh of cells, acidifying the trickles that emerge, so that downstream rivers will remember this place by taste. The cliff pipes are lungs in slow geologic size, taking a full breath in twelve hours. The fungal mats that web everything together switch flows the way a switchboard once did: close this pore, open that one, drag a sugar here, admit a salt there, a choreography that hums so steadily you only notice it when lightning far away tries to break the rhythm and fails. When the plateau decides to spend, it does it in lines, in regularities, in sheets. When it decides to save, it saves with the same patience.

Fauna are abundant, but you meet them by the handful, each confident in its place. There are little deer with fat hooves that never splash, always stepping on the crowns of cushions so lightly they leave them unbruised. There are ribbon-snakes with lenses for scales, water beading and running from them so efficiently that they always seem newly emerged. There are birds that sit inside bromeliads like emperors in baths, eating what the plants invent. There are insects that have given up flying and skate the meniscus of wet air as if it were a material, wings held like sails to catch currents you cannot feel. There are beetles polished so dark they are mirrors, so that when you look down to be sure of your footing you are once again surprised by what your face looks like under a gray sky at the world’s ceiling.

The erosion is not a wound but a memory, everything here rounded and in a balance that says yes, storms came, and storms will come, and this is how we hold them. The old rivers long since left for the edges, and where they went they carved a script the cliffreads keep repeating: scoops, scallops, overhangs where swallows cling. On top, new rivers are made every day and nobody minds. The plateau has been doing this long enough to have learned its gait.

Night is not dark unless the fog decides to be, and often it does. When a clear night comes, the horizon unhooks and distance appears: the suggestion of other shelves beyond the white falls, other islands of this same practice. The vault of sky is simple and immediate, stars bright but oddly quiet, the way everything here is both abundant and reserved. Somewhere out in that dark, something will tear a frog from a leaf without a sound. Somewhere, a plant will decide to spend one more drop and give it back to stone. At some point, a pressure gradient changed, and a cliff exhaled once, then inhaled slow.

There is no emptiness. There is room inside every leaf, and those spaces have tenants. There is space between the fibers of moss, and those galleries carry their own winds and rains, their own tides at the scale of the dust. The air itself is a medium with body, thickened by floating salt and sugar and pollen and the breath of living things; move through it, and you feel it on your cheeks, the way a hand feels wool. The plateau has made a compact with the sky: we will keep what you give us, and you will give it again. In that compact something machine-like has emerged, and something forest-like, and something like the patience of stone. You stand there, damp to the bone, listening, and there is almost nothing to hear but a slowness and a certainty, and the small bright noise of the next drop becoming a drop, and then becoming gone.
