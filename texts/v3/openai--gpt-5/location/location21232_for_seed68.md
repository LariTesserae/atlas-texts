Where the river forgets its banks and loosens into flats, the ground is new and a little raw, squared off by sheet-piled edges and anchored with the confident lines of surveyor’s stakes that haven’t yet gone gray. The whole district sits low enough that you feel the weight of the sky. It’s a compact place—walkable end to end without losing your train of thought—yet it holds itself open, full of air and intervals. Big things are spaced out: a turbine hall, a canning line with its spine of conveyors, the fog farms—skeins of mesh strung between steel masts like abandoned sails—then long corridors of crushed shell and gravel leading to nobody. Between these elements there is more space than structure, more sky than roof.

The light is honest and unadorned. Midday comes as a clean blade; the sun draws exact shadows from rails and ladders, and even the moisture looks etched. On many mornings a sea-hush of fog rolls in, sits with its damp paws on everything, then withdraws by noon, leaving beads of water still clinging to cables and the underside of handrails as though reluctant to return to the air. If it rains, it is sudden, particular rain that slicks one side of the world while the other stays merely dark and expectant. Mostly, here, water appears not as weather but as behavior: it precipitates wherever metal meets a draft.

Sound constructs the place as much as steel does. The turbine hall voices a steady baritone you can feel under your ribs—cooling fans breathing, steam a soft, endless hush, a governor somewhere making its cyclical, patient click—and the canning line replies with higher notes: the clean slap of lids, the rill of conveyors as tins skate, knives on stainless, orders shouted and answered and swallowed again by the machinery. Somewhere farther off, a crane’s hoist motor winds down with a long vowel, and then the horn, ship-big, pulses a few syllables across the flats. Above and between these constants, loose noise proliferates and stitches the day together: hand trucks rattling over expansion joints, the bright yips of wrench on bolt, a broom rasping concrete, a dog that shouldn’t be here barking at a gull that is endlessly, rudely here. In the wind, the fog meshes sing: a faint, glassy chime as droplets string and restrain and finally let go. It is a loud landscape, and yet no single noise insists on being the story. Everything shares the bandwidth like traffic at a busy roundabout, incessant but orderly.

The air is cool. Even at noon you keep the cuffs of your coat turned down; the shadow inside the loading bay has a bite to it, and the breath of the turbines is chill on your wrists. Condensation makes a second architecture: descending threads of water from pipe elbows, beaded chains down guy wires, minute river-deltas of drips on the faces of capacitor housings. The place has engineered itself to catch what falls. Every roof crowns a runnel. Every tank is ringed with a gutter. Brackets hold slung troughs beneath the long ribs of ductwork so that when the machines exhale they also irrigate. At the turn of the shift, the condensers are purged by ordinance—you can set your watch by the three low chords of the warning horn—and then it rains fast, locally, with decision. It rains beneath the catwalk, along the line where the heat exchangers sweat out their dew, onto slate-colored catch basins whose surfaces tremble and overrun into a network of open channels. In a dry country this counts as weather. Workers pause, raise cups, wash their hands, rinsing the oil away in someone’s planned storm.

Vegetation accumulates where that water lingers. It comes on lush wherever it is allowed: riot in the ditch, patience on the pad. Rank sedges fill the swales, their blades combed by the prevailing wind. Willows in line with the drainage break apart into a hundred green switches, their feet in blackened puddles patterned with the printed leaves of insects. Moss is fat on the north faces of concrete walls, and the undersides of I-beams sprout a fine nap of green where the fog likes to roost and no hand can easily reach. Vines find the ladders first. Ferns occupy the dry sumps until the next release, holding their breath and unfurling again, unbothered by the cycle. Even the spoil berms bloom: gravel-stout things—poppies, clover, chicory—that throw bravely colored dots into a world mostly brushed with zinc and ash. The newest cuts and embankments have a knife’s edge, where the fill’s stratified layers are still visible: shell, clay, a line of coal-cinder, then the white geotextile peeking like laundry between them. Nothing has had time to round; hard rain scribbles gullies in an afternoon and the marks are sharp for months.

Animals take it all as given and make numbers of themselves. Egrets pick their way through the retention ponds with the same measured step they use in pictures, stabbing at something neither the photographer nor the egret considers remarkable. Gulls argue with cranes for the high steel, their droppings painting a constellatory language onto rails. Swallows live under the V of the roof trusses, posting themselves at conveyor mouths in the low drafts to nip what the line throws off in gnats. Come dusk, the frogs calibrate the soundscape: they fill in every pause the machines leave, a chorus that slides in and out of phase with the turbine’s hum. Beetles are busy in the damp gravel, and a big slug like a gloved thumb leaves a shining signature across a checker plate where nobody steps after dark. Rats have warrens under the pallets; foxes cut their neat, dry lines between stacks of pipe as if they had a night shift of their own. The noise doesn’t frighten much. Either creatures born here consider it weather, or the loudness is steady enough to sit inside of like a cathedral organ note. Even the stray dogs time their naps to the purge and their scavenging to the meal breaks, and you don’t have to be told that people are part of the fauna too.

All the built work, for its size, errs on the side of room. Streets are wide enough that the life on the second-floor catwalk still has a sky over it. Housing blocks stand in lines with large rectangles of grass between them, held green by condensate alone and cut short by a mower whose operator hums louder than the blades. The clinic has its windows open to the sea-wet; the canteen smells like cardamom and fish and a little bit like coolant; the laundry steam looks like weather as much as the fog does. A narrow rail spur interrogates the edge of the district and then, finding no reason to continue, stops at a buffer painted hazard-yellow, its wooden end-cheek marine with salt. There is a sense that the place has been assembled according to a diagram and only recently placed on the ground: the corners still matter, the paint is still clean where hands don’t touch.

It is not a fragile order; it is simply young. The river keeps trying to turn the edges to mud and the engineers keep shouldering it back with bright steel. If you stand still and look at the legs of a fence you can watch rust not moving, which is to say you can feel time accruing in layers the color of dried blood. The machines themselves seem patient; they do not surge so much as proceed. Even the leaks have schedules. There is a law that every engine must pay water: pipe your breath to a basin, and from basin to channel, and from channel to tank, so that the settlement can go on pretending it rains every day. There is a posted warning under each run of net—no whistling—because someone proved that lips and a two-note melody could shift the flow from laminar to turbulent and cost a little yield. These are the small rules of a city that harvests damp for drink.

The materials of the place know they are expected to perform. Corrugated sheet stands up to the fog like a dog to a hose. Line posts are jacketed in bitumen where they dive into the fill and braced above by guy lines that bow in the constant wind. Concrete pads bear the arabesques of forklift and boot and, in the low places, the miniature deltaic filigrees left by the last purge’s runoff; someone has drawn faces into the still-wet silt there, and the small smiles persist. Ladders are greasy in the cold: you test them with your heel before you ascend. Cabling is strung overhead like vines, sleeved in orange, ticks visible even when the rest of the machinery is invisible behind its doors. Everything is grounded, everything is tagged. The city’s conscience is a collection of tight, nearly invisible controls that allow life to crowd the edges.

You could say all of this happens inside a room the size of a hamlet, the walls of which are the tall berms and the bright idea of keeping water from touching ground unless asked. Beyond the berms, the land relaxes into scrub and hardpan where a dust devil might happen and dissolve again without anyone noticing. Inside, the clamor is continuous, and yet the scales of change are slow. The river makes new ground another finger’s width each year and the lampshade of phragmites in the far field slides a little closer until someone mows it back. The people add a new length of mesh to the fog farm each autumn, like extending a garment for a growing child. Rust gets named, watched and sanded. Paint is reapplied in deliberate squares. The frogs inherit the sumps one season and the toads steal them the next. Everything accrues reputation.

At noon the sun, when it shows, puts a hard brilliance on the wet metal and each hanging drop throws a quiet lens of the world onto the ground. The broad lanes white out, then take the shape back when a cloud sails through at working speed. In winter, if you come before anyone has touched the day, the frost webs every wire and fence and then melts to nothing under the warm breath of the machines, returning as rivulets, as cups filled at a tap. It is not a place people call beautiful until they do. After that, the beauty seems obvious. A field of mesh throbbing with a slight, singular song. A drainage ditch doing more work than a stream. A fox slipping between stacks of galvanized duct as though the ducts were trees in a clean, untrammeled forest. A room made of open air and steel and damp, loud enough to be its own weather, patient enough that the edges will still be sharp when you come back next season.
