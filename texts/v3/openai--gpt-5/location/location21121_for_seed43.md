Everything here has been made and remade until it forgot the shape of stone. The floor is a skin of terracotta slabs, burnished by generations of bare feet to a soft sheen. The walls are lime-plastered and combed with shallow grooves that lead water toward channels; their once-crisp comb marks are rounded by hands and the brush of baskets. Even the ceiling has no natural curve: a tapestry of vaults and interlocking ribs, each rib a series of clay tiles keyed into the next like fish scales, each seam sealed with pitch and ash. It is warm. Not hot—just a persistent, body-heat warmth, like a room where many things breathe.

Light falls through the ceiling in discrete squares and circles. Above, narrow shafts drink the day and pour it into this chamber; the shafts are white-washed so the sun bruises its way downward, strong where it strikes and pearly at the edges. Screens of slatted wood can be drawn across them—some are open, some half-closed by cords and counterweights—so the light is a patchwork of noon and late afternoon, a net of time hung across brick and clay. Where light reaches, dust turns gold and insect wings flash like chips of mica. Where it does not, the plaster looks like milk, and the air feels closer.

Water moves, but only sometimes. It does not course so much as arrive, condense, accumulate, and then slide away. From a forest of suspended terracotta needles—hundreds of little funnels hung from the vaults by hemp—dew gathers on cool mornings and fatten at their tips. When the drops fall, they strike stretched potsherds or suspended gourds and ring out high and thin. Those tones gather and echo; each section of the hall is tuned by its particular cups and distances. When the sky is dry for days, the place hums with soft plinks and the scrape of brooms. When the weather loosens, rain finds the shafts, and the hall becomes a drum. Runnels wake, weirs are unpinned, leather flaps over drains bulge with fast water and moan. Surges break the still surfaces of the cistern-pans at the floor’s edges, and fish—slender, long-schooled fish with blunt heads accustomed to corridors—thrum up the channels, noses to current, scales catching lantern-light.

There are no corners empty of making. Ladders lean like ribs between pillars. Ropes as thick as wrists hang in clusters, their ends weighted with stone, used to raise shutters, to lift and lower wicker screens, to close off gullies during cleaning. The pillars themselves are built of fired brick and faced with carved wood, the wood etched with shallow hollows that fit hands. Each hollow is polished dark as oil. Between pillars, arches are netted with reed mats to catch dust. The mats are patched and dyed with earth reds and smoke blacks; near damp places, they bloom a thin fur of green.

Plants are few, and they are all in their places. Moss goldens the lips of basins where the drip is slow and dependable. A pale vine—no leaf to speak of, just translucent thread and eager tendril—has been coaxed along the groove of one wall and tied at intervals to small pegs, so it makes a living line that glistens and sometimes throws seed. In planters as long as troughs, someone tends shallow beds of fern and a stringy succulent that drinks from the fumes more than from the cup; their green is the under-color of olives, the cautious green of things that do not expect much but waste nothing. There are trays of fungi farther in, under a fixed array of night-lamps, but they belong to a different section and a different rule; here, the vegetation is incidental to the masonry and exists where the water does.

Fauna, by contrast, are everywhere. Winged things make greatest use of the shafts. Quick, ash-gray swiftlets wedge their nests where light spills in, stitching cup after cup to the cool of the plaster, their calls thin and insistent and bouncing from vault to vault until they sound like iron scraped on pottery. Their flights stitch the air as reliably as the cords do. Geckos move on the undersides of the ribs and dart along wall edges, blinking and share-skittering, hunting the midges that collect at the bright cones of light. A huntsman spider as broad as a palm lives behind a beam, and every week some new husk is left in the web, shining and papery, a reminder that this is not a museum.

At the waterline, frogs sit fat and patient on the stone lips of channels, eyes leveled at the rush. They are olive and cream and dappled with the lime-smear of their preferred perches. They eat what comes to them, and what comes to them is plentiful—moths flown too low, the occasional water-scudder knocked into a runnel by the brush of a reed mat, a beetle that came to investigate the salt crystals caught in the mortar. Occasionally, in the warmer darks between drip forests, a rat runs from the food store toward the night hall, and a ferret with a bell on its collar gives chase. The bell is not enough to warn the rat; it is not meant to be. It is meant to warn the people whose hammocks hang in the sleeping niche, so that paws do not land on faces.

Sounds fill all the emptiness there might have been. Even when nothing moves and the drip-line is slack, the hall holds a tone—a breath, a resonance that is the sum of its vaults and volumes. Add to that the constant sibilant of air—pulled up and pushed down by the heat of the day, shunted through flutes in the walls in which louvres of bark and shell vibrate—and you have wind that plays like a simple organ. On it ride other sounds: rope on pulley, wood on wood, a child’s call, a man’s chant as he lifts a slat in the roof to coax water from one cistern to another. There is a language stroke against stone here, spoken whether the people speak or not. Part of it is intention—gourds hung over basins to scale each drop into a note that tells if the drip has slowed, shells set at shaft mouths that whistle when the wind turns north and the surface weather promises rain. Part of it is simply what sound does in made places that have been rubbed into their own acoustics.

Rules are not written but they are everywhere the place touches you. They are in the grooves that keep your feet from sliding on a wet step, and the way those grooves deepen toward the drains, steering you without your being steered. They are in the way ropes fall in loops that make it obvious where your hand should go, which cord opens the shutter of that shaft and which opens the trap at your feet. They are in the little stone that is always kept on the lip of the fourth weir from the west—nobody moves it because its weight perfects the balance of the gate; remove it and the gate snaps shut too hard and jars the brick. They are in law, too: do not block the channels even for a moment; never silence the drip-plates all at once; keep one lamp banked at the mouth of the sleeping niche; in the east alcove, the frogs eat at dusk and dawn, do not startle them then.

The air is damp in patches, cool in drafts, warm in the thick and the low. Weightless droplets collect on lashes in the morning and on the underside of beams before noon. Some hours, everything is dry enough that dust is laid only by the brushing of feet. Other hours, a fine sweat comes up from the terracotta and the wood smells like a boat. Smells layer like the walls do: green algae, pitch, old lamp-fat, rope fiber, lime dust, the bright mineral tang of fresh rain, the faint sourness of grain from the storage room across the threshold. When storms roll over the plain above, the first proof in this place is the change in smell, a metallic edge to the air that somehow has no metal in it, the sense that the drops falling from the drip-needles are a new population, allied to sky-water, not night-water.

This chamber—call it a hall or a cistern or a grove that grew stone instead of trees—is held in a kind of balance that has the set of age about it. The erosion here is of edges and textures, not of form. Steps are lower in the middle than at the sides, but they are still steps; the course of water has softened every corner without threating to undo the angles. The place has accepted its cycles. Dust from above enters as the shafts are opened; reed brooms lift it and send it toward mats that gulp it up; the mats, heavy and gray, are carried to a sluice and beaten, their gray making a brief river cloud; the rods above drink this new humidity and drip a little faster for a while into the cups; the drip-notes change, and a nearby old woman hears this and knows to tip a basin toward the trough at her feet to keep it from spilling. Days and nights, dry spells and rains, the slow drift of oil in lamps, the quick wing of the birds when the shutters are pulled—all of it stacks, repeats, alters.

The built logic extends even to the seeming wildness of the fauna. Swiftlets nest only where ledges have been notched for them under the light shafts. The geckos are tolerated because they will not grow large enough to trouble fingers; the larger lizards are discouraged with polished plaster that refuses their toes. The frogs were tempted here by shallow notches carved into the channel-lips that retain water for a day after the last drizzle. A thin, hair-mesh net lies coiled near the confluence of two runnels; it is a weir against eels in the wet years, and it lives on a peg of bone. In a cubby near the door, a snake-stick leans where anyone can reach it; the snakes that come this far are sunned with it and turned back toward the grain of the space, like being brushed the right way on a garment.

The scale of it is intimate but not cramped. One person’s shout can find another at the far end. Six families could rig their hammocks and still leave room to peel grain and patch nets and keep the public hearth from bumping someone’s elbow. It is not a city. It is not a maze. It is a room that has become a village without crowding itself, dense in objects, dense in uses, dense in attentions and presences—the kind of density born of long familiarity rather than haste. Every surface has a job; if it lies still in the sun, it is because it is drying something.

Beyond the four archways that mark the limits, there are other made places—cool corridors you can taste on your tongue, rooms of fungus, sacred shafts that go to the surface and end under stone grates where goats sometimes stamp and sniff—but the hall itself is an end in the way groves are ends. You come here to gather a quantity of time and leave with it cupped in your hands.

In the middle of the day, when the light is most generous, the hall is very nearly bright; shadows have edges, and colors declare what they are—the prickly green of the succulent, the blue-black of wet tile, the tea-color of water where it thins in the lip of a bowl. Light makes discipline easy. At dusk, the lamps are trimmed, their flames shielded by clay hoods that throw their light downward. Then the birds quiet in their cups. The frogs begin—low, mango-thick notes that fill the vaults. The drip-forest takes on a new logic; now each drop is a separate world again, and the hum of fluted vents softens to a longer breath. The sound of a gourd being struck becomes a signal—someone counting, someone keeping. The heat leans closer to the skin. Above, the shafts show a square of evening, then a square of stars, and in that square, a moth and two swiftlets tracing hunger and home.

This made world’s strangeness, if it has any, is in its devotion: to water that is mostly not here, to sunlight that must be coaxed and set to work, to sound that is both message and weather. The rest is ordinary in the way that the interior of a shell is ordinary: texture laid on texture, a logic of spiral and shelter, the greatest complexity spent on the simplest needs.
