Heat leans on everything here. It arrives not as light but as a weight in the air, a slow pressure that fills the lungs and makes the skin bead before you move at all. The sky is close—low, a lid of dark vapor that never thins to stars. What passes for night is an enduring hour before dawn that never reaches dawn, a darkness with the suggestion of depth, gray upon gray, as if the world were a room lit only from under its own furniture.

The ground lies low, almost level with a distant, unseen sea, and you feel the water before you find it. It breathes up from just beneath the crust. Every stone sweats. Walls, if you can call them that, weep darkly from hairline seams, and terraces give off a constant, whispering drizzle as if remembering rain that happened an hour ago and is still happening somewhere behind the walls of air. When the clouds turn inside themselves and pulse—it’s not lightning exactly, more a dull shudder of charged heat—the sheen on everything thickens, and a rash of droplets appears out of nowhere and joins what’s already falling.

It is a city, and it is also geography. Spread through the basin in sparse, deliberate strokes, the constructions are too many to be accidents and too patient to be new. Long, low embankments run in parallel for great stretches, ribs in the land that direct sheets of warm water along predetermined courses. They glide into open aprons of smooth mineral, plazas if you like, each with a subtle depression at its heart where brine gathers in a shallow, irresolute lens. The air is so saturated that every exposed surface is busy growing skin. Stone here is not stone as you know it, but layered stuff: thin, brittle cakes laid down by a thousand cycles of upwelling, trickle, evaporation, and fall, stitched by films of organisms too small to imagine as life if you needed eyes to see them. Their colonies turn the margins a different black. You can read them as contour lines.

Urban, not by hand, but by process. Rows of arches—vaults really—cross canals that exist mostly as memory, grooves so often run with water that water remembers them even when there is only damp. Above those grooves the arches hold; they’ve held a long time. You can tell by the way their undersides have rounded into smoothness under centuries of drip. Cisterns open in courtyards, but the courtyards refuse to be dry. Their floors flux and cure, flux and cure, so each stone is a palimpsest of shallows.

Everything is laid out. It’s hard not to see districts: raised beds like terraces of rice without rice; colonnades of fluted tufa, columns with edges softened to the curve of kneecaps; stepped amphitheaters of calcareous ramp, dry to the eye and wet to the touch. Lanes run between them, and the lanes are always slightly dimmer than their boundaries, as if a centuries-long procession had polished them to a darker silk. No feet have done this. The water has, carrying fines and leaving them in the slow places, blowing off vapor in the fast, and in this way creating gentility: a broadway of runoff, a square for pooling, a sudden room where the river forgot to exist and raised the floor with its sleep.

Silence is the only population. Drips keep time, each with a tempo depending on the size of its parent bead and the angle of the stone cup below. Far away something sighs: a warm air current slipping along an arcade as if it had learned the easiest path. Sometimes there is a noise like glass losing patience. That’s a crust somewhere tensioning with absorbed water, then cracking along a line it’s cracked along before. The sound travels oddly. It bounces in corners that shouldn’t exist, or arrives before it’s made, or repeats itself more than once as if the same fracture is happening in several adjacent versions of the city.

The few living things that do show themselves do it without ostentation. In vertical seams where weeping is constant, a skin of lichen-black, nearer to fungus than plant, spreads like parchment tea stains, licking minerals from the flow. Along some of the raised courses, low pads of something succulent and chalk-pale have taken position the way lanterns might have along a ceremonial route, but they are separated by meters of nothing. Their leaves are forever wet, always moving, opening and closing to the pulse of humidity. They do not need light—and there is almost none to offer—to make a living. They sip the air itself and bank what the stone gives off. They are few. Between them, emptiness is the rule; planes of naked, sweating floor that send your attention out past themselves to the next incline, the next glazed lip, the far line of columnate shade, and the throb, faint in the sky, of another dry storm.

No animal passes. No flutter vacates a ledge when you come close—if you are even allowed to be close. The city is crowded with spatial behavior, not with forms. You can look down a street and find it longer than streets usually are, as if it borrowed a block from a different draft and spliced it in. Or you can stand in a chamber that offers three exits, take one, and find yourself under the same arch you left by, but with the pattern of condensation on the wall changed, the beads slightly larger now, the drips ten heartbeats farther apart. None of this is hostile. It is not kind, either. It is worn smooth by repetition and does not mind a repetition more.

Rules hold, though they do not match the ones you carry. Gravity is mostly a consensus. The general direction of fall is down, and yet you can watch a line of beads creep sideways along a cornice through stubbornness, recreating a route where a pipe used to be, though there is no pipe and never was. In one district water habitually connects overhanging and standing stones with slender, trembling skeins, as if spinning the absent scaffolds back into place. They gleam like veins in the dark, and sometimes snap all at once—a soft, wet unstringing—then try again. Water climbs here because it has been taught to think of itself as structure, and structure does not give up a span just because of an interval.

Erosion is not an enemy but a schedule. The faces of the embankments have let themselves be undercut to a line and held that line for generations of cycles. Where a canton of the city leans into a basin, the seat of the slope is knuckled with old slides, all of which have stopped in the same crescent, which has become a kind of bench. From it you can hear the basin think: tiny fizzing where bubbles find their way up through gelled mats, an intermittent pock when a thicker drop finally lets go and disturbs the film. The low places are alive with gasses invisible in the near-dark. Warmth comes up from the ground in exhalations timed with the deeper weather overhead.

There is weather, more than one kind. The obvious one is the weeping: steady, tireless, saturating. But there is a second cycle that walks through the city without warning, a reversal. The air goes stiller than usual, a pause everyone can feel—if there were any everyone—and then heat pushes harder. In that pulse the city gives off moisture like a crowd at the end of a game, flushing itself with a heat-haze that makes edges uncertain and distances runny. Surfaces turn matte; beads vanish back into stone, into the small creatures filigreeing it, into the lower air. The lanes lighten; the arches chalk. And then, minutes later or an hour, the lid above finds another, deeper breath, and the weeping resumes, and all the profiles return to their more thoughtful sheen.

You will sometimes catch other kinds of light, none of them belonging to stars. A seam opens with a sound like wet cloth torn, and the fracture speaks blue for a fraction long enough to see your own bones through your skin. On some of the drier walls, where the substrate is made of brittle crystals, a hand across it—or a breeze, in a place like this—can ignite a salad of muted colors, triboluminescent murmurs that tell you the wall is built of stress as much as matter. It’s not safe to say which organs feed on which in a city with no mouths, but the living skins here tend to settle along those lines as if aware that where stress releases, energy is offered more easily.

Because the city is large—valley-sized—your sense of its pattern only grows by admitting every inland sea you cannot see. On the horizon the outlines of more construction are visible when the clouds above them burn for a moment: shelf after shelf of low bunkered roofs, stair after stair of declining terraces, horizontal galleries where the ground has learned to hang itself between supports of petrified foam. There is no skyline. Everything keeps its head down, careful not to stand too tall into whatever denied the sky to begin with. Where there are towers, they do not reach; they thicken, swelling up from the foundations like the buds of a plant that had to use mineral instead of sap, and those buds hold small blind mouths to the air where water can rest without falling.

It’s easy to suppose the city had builders. It behaves like intention, even ornament. On the faces of certain retaining walls there are repeating motifs, scallops and laddered bands and a pattern like a field of scales, but they’re long-run feedback disguised as craft, programs of flow that repeat because the basin repeats them. Along avenues where the fall is shallow, parallel runnels split and join again, always at the same intervals, making wavy chevrons across their bed that a certain school of design might call handsome. But there’s no curation here, no choice beyond what cycles choose. The closest thing to will is memory, and memory resides in the lay of a plain and in how warmly a cloud is held.

Still, the place can feel inhabited if you are patient with silence; not by bodies, but by habits. Streets avoid each other with the care of people who have quarreled. Plazas gather toward some centers and refuse others. A colonnade ends not in open ground but at a curved wall given over to condensation runes, as if a doctrine had been inscribed and softened and reinscribed so often that the surface has learned the shapes without caring for any message. On the back of a series of baffles, a fungal colony has taken to glowing faintly green when some deeper thunder moves through the lid overhead; it’s not a language, but it is a response, and in time you come to predict where in the city you’ll see such green whenever the weather’s inner machinery turns.

The dynamic is slow but it’s a continual slow. A canal that was dry this morning—if mornings are a thing under a sky like this—now runs a shallow sheet that barely moves but nonetheless finds the exact same route it took two dozen cycles before. In a courtyard, a cornice lifts by less than the width of a fingernail as mineral accumulates, and because it lifts, a new line of beads slips left to an alternate channel, and because that line shifts, the archway below grows a whisker of deposit where last repetition did not. It adds up. Layer by layer the city both weathers and writes itself into longer exactness. That exactness is not steady-state. Its balance has the complexity and poise of standing on a boat—always correcting, never still, always itself.

No detritus of creatures litters these ways. No birds’ nests under arches, no shells of insects at the wall-foot, no scurry-tracks drying in the mud between showers. The only loose thing is what the city produces from its own body: flakes of evaporite, thin as pastry, gathering where eddies throw them; gelatinous films that knead themselves from white to gray before they vanish into everything’s mouth; strands of mineral, brittle curls that tinkle like very distant bells when the heat surges and a breath of wind off nothing crosses them.

If there’s a sound that belongs to the place alone, it is the soft ticking of heat against wetness. The click and whisper of droplet to pool. The subaudible rush of your ear to its own blood because the air is heavy and wants to be inside you. Far behind the walls of cloud, an engine bigger than sky works itself, a cycle whose peak and trough you know only because the stone here covers its eyes, then uncovers them, then covers them again.

Distance is both mid-range and mutable. You can see far enough to know the city continues—past the next set of ramps, past the reflective floor of a civic bowl forever filled to a depth of a thumbnail, past the low, rounded tops of banks that might have been promenades if anything with feet had ever promenaded here. Beyond that, the dark consumes the plan, not all at once but the way fog erases detail in layers, a set design unwinding itself by steps until there is nothing left but the suggestion that this motif repeats for another valley and maybe another and that the sea, when you meet it, will be a continuation rather than a border.

Every rule is in conversation with its own exceptions. You find a square where droplets form straight lines along a blank wall, marching solider-like from roof to ground, and in the middle of that square an area where no wetness sticks, a negative plaza inside the plaza, dry as the sky is dark, tasting of gypsum and heat. Stand there and you can sense the radiative push of things, the city venting, the rules reversing temporarily to protect some detail of its balancing act you cannot see. When you step out, your sandals slide on slick mineral that built itself from the overflow of that stubborn dryness. Another corner has water that refuses to be level, holding a plane at a complicated angle because its edges are truer than its center. It is not a defiance, it is an accommodation; the world cheapens friction here and enriches cohesion and the two work out an agreement without consulting your expectations.

Whatever fast life exists runs at scales smaller than you imagine and faster than your attention can catch. The microbiology gives the place its breath. Chemistry hums along in dim green and hard violet when the rock complains. Air squeezes through the masonry of its own making. Weather invokes, withdraws, revises. You could call it abandoned, but it has never been left. It has simply yet to be arrived at by anything that needs more light than heat. Until then, the city uses water as a tool, stone as a ledger, and night as an organizing principle, and it goes on, quietly, writing itself in scripts the rain can read and answering to nothing louder than the next drop.
