You come into it from a throat of pale wood that is not wood, a long ribbed opening tapering down into breath-cool air. There is no sky, only distance: chambers within chambers, galleries running to a faint vanishing, pillars grown from one piece and bent into their own shadows. The ground yields just enough to tell you it is alive and also load-bearing. Under your palms, the surface has the feel of dried leaf midrib—waxy, striated, a faint grain that guides your eye more than your foot.

There is almost no water anywhere and the place knows it. You taste dust and resin when you inhale, a mineral-laced coolness, a dryness that takes the thought of thirst and turns it into law. Moisture is precious here, not only because it is scarce but because it is dangerous: water is an acid to this biochemistry. The long, louvered vents along the walls bristle with hydrophobic hairs that wick away the breath you lose. Any stray condensate is eaten by collector moths that look like fringed handkerchiefs, drifting from grate to grate with solemn duty, their tongues a brush of salts.

The world is below the surface of something vast—a continent-scale organism or a concert of organisms indistinguishable at this depth, a total substrate of plant that is dry-running and solvent-averse. It fills all directions: columns and rings, thick sheets like baleen separating one hall from the next, reinforced nodes big as auditoriums where arteries converge. Between these are the urban hollows: broad plazas scooped alive, platformed with living decks and ringed by vats that pulse with oily sap. The city is not crowded. It is there like scaffolding in an empty cathedral, rails in straight runs and right angles across a landscape of slow curves. You can walk from one factory to the next for many minutes and pass only hung lamps, a bench grown from a rib and sanded smooth by hands, a single tiny cart that hums by on frictionless wheels, laden with spools of fiber-crystal filament.

Light falls without drama. It is day here because the plant decides it will be: flattened bulbs open like lilies along the arching bones of the ceiling and emit a neutral tone of white when the city charges them. Their flesh glows without heat, and if you stand close you can see the grain of them flicker in a whispering phase array. The light shifts on a schedule to remind the inhabitants of time, cycling color toward a saffron that slants across the ribs in late-day, then guttering to pearl, then out. In the far hollows, engineers are training a young laminary—the budding of a new lighting organ—by wrapping bands of copper and opening the charge to teach it to bloom.

The rules are evident in every posted sign and every habit. Wearings are matte and sealed. People keep their mouths masked with desiccant cloth, not for poison but for stewardship. Drink flasks are clamped, and when someone opens one you see the liquid is not water but a viscous, clear oil that moves like sleep and smells faintly of citrus metal. A drip of it leaps from the lip of the flask to the cloth before it can fall, hungry collectors darting to claim any droplet likewise.

Industry has found a home here, not by breaking the living substrate but by leaning into it. Factories are ringed by growth guides: bands of resin and wire that bend the plant’s cambium to extrude beams and plates on schedule. A girder line looks like a colonnade of giant reed stems being split lengthwise by mechanical wedges; on the other side of the line, the halves knit back together as if the wound were an idea the plant changed its mind about. Mills shave long, translucent ribbons that are braided into cable. Presses cure sheets under low heat and vacuum and then cut them with whispering saws that throw off statically charged confetti which flits away on the faint drafts.

Engines here run on the breath of the place. Thin turbines, all blade and bearing, sip the gentle, scheduled winds—the plant’s own ventilation pulse, a tidal exhalation every hour as it trades gases with the mineral veins it sleeps against. In the deeper rooms you can stand with your hand inches from a wall and feel the heartbeat-like suction, a draw-and-release you can set your watch by. The city takes advantage of this. Doors are hatches, slid aside during exhale to ease traffic, sealed during inhale to keep the flow unbroken. Conveyors run downhill when the currents are favorable, uphill when the pressure valves open in the next district and pull them like kites.

The terrain is young in its own way, not in years but in weathering. There is little rounding of corners, little of the softened edge you expect from long use. Where a new tunnel has been coaxed, the cuticle gleams in untouched sheen; the dust underfoot is crisp, angular, flakes rather than grains. In one place there is a sudden window of raw mineral, a failure of the green to cover the gray, and you can see the host geology—pale, fine as talc, laced with shiny seams of something like graphite. The plant is coming for it by spicules, fingerlets of fiber insinuating along microfractures. You feel that if you stood here long enough, it would thicken over the flaw out of a sense of modesty.

Fauna move in this place almost as a second thought to the plant’s massive dreaming. Small grazers pepper the hollow spaces: wide-bodied, leaf-colored, with corkscrew tongues adapted for combing mineral dust off the ceiling. They stand with front legs splayed and lean back as if standing on a surfboard, pulling mouthfuls of salt and micronutrient dust that the plant coughs up in sighs. On high ledges where light gathers, there are gliding insects that look like long seeds with articulated fins. They launch from rib to rib, flickering the air with membranous sail-sounds, snatching the dust-grazers’ young in sudden, precise darts. Their predators are patient: jawed things that masquerade as sheaths of bark until a glider passes within the radius of a spring. If there is blood here, it is dry and granular, a cascade more like spilled tea than a stain.

Beneath the footways, long bale-worms move in parallel, the size of lampposts laid on their sides. They look like continuous processionals of glossy links, each one mouth and muscle in equal measure, feeding on the dead and the outgrown. Every few chains they pulse and exhale a little chord, a click that echoes through the cavern and is answered by others, setting up a private map most ears do not parse. The city uses that cadence too; timing maintenance to the beats means you rarely need to raise your voice above the hush.

It is not silent. There is the live-wood creak of expansion somewhere beyond your vision, the very slow cry of fiber under tension. There is the beaded patter of saltfall from vents, like a gentle sleet. There is the distant, constant hum of something turning—a turbine, a drum, a bank of chargers feeding the lamps. And sometimes, like a weather event, there is the rush of a breathing cycle opening: doors along a gallery all at once dilate, fastenings snap back, and a cool, moving wall of air sweeps the chamber clean.

Paths are not carved but trained. They look grown because they are, taught to be straight by the nailheads of the city, which are not hammered so much as pressed and sung. A maintenance crew in sealed aprons stands around a hatch that hasn’t learned how to be a hatch yet, murmuring into vociports that tickle the surface with patterns of charge and tell it to thin here, thicken there. They hang a lattice of weights and come back an hour later to check the curve. It is work like conversation; progress feels like accord.

Because everything is plant, architecture is ornament. Balconies curl with an elegance you cannot help but reach toward. Pipes are enclosed in sheaths that grow decorative eyes along their lengths, burgundy ovals that watch nothing with the patience of seeds. The big, open airs are ringed by fungal—no, not fungal, but a plant’s analog to fungus—curtains: layered fans that extend in scallops, a filter forest for dust and stray organisms. People sit in their deep shade to eat, their food powder pressed into cakes, dipped in oil. Overhead, cables cross, each one a grown-to-order tendon that tightens and relaxes subtly with the plant’s day, and the lantern-lilies wake a little brighter under the extra draw.

There is a transit here that is all line and little sound. The rails are silvered strips embedded shallowly in the deck, and along them run carts that couple to the long electromagnets hidden within. They move as if reconsidering every inch, then committing to it in a single continuous decision. Stations are not elaborate—just an oval cut deeper, a bench, a map burned into the wall that changes when the plant shifts a corridor ten degrees and lengthens a run by fifty meters. The map itself is alive too: a vine of phosphor nerve tracks the changes, blossoming nodes where construction is ongoing with a soft, suggestive blink.

If there is weather, it is the city’s doing. The plant breathes; the city coats its breathing in regularity. In the cool hours, the vents pull hard and the air feels thinner and cleaner, as if the entire place has just opened a window to more of itself. In the warmer times—the lamps still low in heat—there is a faint haze of powdered salts, a storm without moisture that curls into whorls you can see only when a worker’s sleeve sweeps and cuts them.

Once a day the plant makes a sound like a held note freed. Somewhere in a locked chamber, far below or far away, a throttled bud opens and a pressure difference equalizes. The moment proceeds down the city in a visible way: banners of leaf-skin along the walkways lift, door-lips flare, and the hair on your arms stands. In that breath-window you might hear the balers start, draw in a new bed of mown fiber from the last trim, declare by their shifting hum that the factory will run hot until the next window. You time your errands to that beat; you keep your voice low because the acoustics here want low talk, because your words are another thing the plant might notice and take into its lesson plan.

Strange is normal here. Water is locked up in bones, treated with the caution you would give a corrosive. Salt is food and breeze. Plant is not background but floor, wall, and directive. The city is not an imposition but a collaborator, making with engines what the organism cannot and asking the organism to do what mills will do worse. If you stay long enough, you learn that knocking on a wall is rude; you learn to rub your knuckles gently along the grain instead. You learn to wait until the exhale to open your door so you do not stall a breath. You learn that the color shift at dusk is for the gliders, who mistake lamp-flare for open vents and come in to be caught unless the light goes soft first.

Far from the plazas, the landscape keeps moving outward into the mid-range—district after district under slow construction, scaffolds of wire bent around adolescent columns. Here emptiness really shows its measure. You walk under arches so high the lamps seem like stars that lost the knack of twinkling. A single harvester drone moves alone along a loop that takes it an hour to close, its pale carapace dusted with salt. It pauses at a band of fresh growth, extends a chin, and sings an audio code into the green to tell it to thicken this flange and open that pore. It could be the only thing alive on this floor and the place would feel as filled as a lung drawn full.

Then, every so often, a rupture of the rule. A fault where the plant has not yet knitted, and the chamber opens into a mineral void raw with angles and cold echoes. Here the dryness bites harder; the air tastes like old lightning. You expect to see water in a place like this because your bones are from a planet that has it; instead there is only powder and a sheen where crystals have grown like frost along a ledge. The plant is already on its way—strings of tissue move like trails of ants, hundreds of hair-fine reachers groping for a purchase. If you watch closely, the closest of them twitches, as if embarrassed to be seen working so slowly.

This is how it is: urban and not crowded, detailed and quiet, lit as if it were outside and yet absolutely inside. Predators and prey make their living in arched air, and machines hum not to drown out the world but to be their own weather within it. The plant fills all the space that matters and yet leaves so much distance between presences that you can hear your own footfall and not feel alone. You run your hand along the living rail and the grain warms under your palm, and behind everything is the rhythm of a city that breathes with its habitat: exhale, inhale, exhale, a dry pulse held in a vessel the size of a valley, a work in the middle of becoming.
