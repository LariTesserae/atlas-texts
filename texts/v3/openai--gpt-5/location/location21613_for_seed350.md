The basin is low and warm and full of water that won’t decide what it is. It falls, climbs, lingers in place. Sheets of it hang between mangrove knees like cloth stretched on invisible looms. Columns pour down and never splash, their bases scalloped into trembling domes. The air is wet to the point of being only an interpretation of air; open your mouth and it becomes a mouthful. Above, a sky you never quite see presses its weight into the valley, dark as saturated paper. Light sifts through as a green-gray dimness, a constant twilight, made uncertain by the refraction of a thousand curtains and chords of rain.

The land under it is new, not newborn, but the kind of new that has edges. You can see the snap and shear where something tore the basin open—sleek clay faces still faceted, not yet rounded by time. Black sand spits thread between roots; pale marl shelves appear and disappear under rushes of browned water. Rust-orange precipitates run along knife-thin scarp lines like capillaries. Where the ground meets the flood-forest there’s no gentle gradient; trunks punch straight out of silt and steel, a tangle that reads as suddenness made permanent.

Everything grows. Green comes in layers so thick that, seen from a distance, the valley is a single organism breathing with a thousand mouths. Mangrove domes with interlocked roots make lattices you could walk if walking were possible here. Between them, fan-ferns unfurl and refurl with the rhythm of pressure waves; mats of liverwort quilt any surface that pauses long enough; ropes of kelp rise and sink, indifferent to the line between water and air. Algae glaze the hanging water sheets into paneled glass that beads and fractures when the sound swells. Lichens have found metal tastes they favor and write chalky hieroglyphs down the seams of pipes. Blocks of lily leaves raft over dead-still pools, then howl into ragged triangles when the wind-of-water hardens and drags.

Animals are absent. There are no wingbeats, no pinprick swarms in the dusk, no quick corrections in the corner of the eye. In the channels between root-pillars, nothing darts. Stems flex and unflex; scum roils; cilia of moss ripple on sheet steel; bubbles rise with the resolve of chemical certainty. The emptiness of animal intent is a space inside the general tumult, a bell-shaped vacancy that you only notice because it never fills.

Machines stand alone, spaced out like punctuation in the ongoing sentence of floodwood and leaf. A pump house on stilted legs, shingled in corroded tin. A drum the size of a tram car mounted on a truss and set at a tilt like a moon in a different system. A single generator room whose walls sweat and whose door has rusted open in a precise rectangle. Pylons walk away into the flood-forest, each carrying two drooping lines that quiver and hum as if thinking. Conveyor belts, incomprehensibly clean, thread low through reeds and vanish in the hot haze of falling water. They do not form a grid, and they do not sleep. Belts creep. Drums turn. Valves open and shut with a cough that coincides with voids in the deluge, so perhaps the deluge listens.

Their power hum is a living geography. In the basin, sound is not an event but a terrain you move through and are moved by. The roar of unending rain is a dome and a wall and a floor pressing up with invisible fingers. Cavitation cracks pop like flares in your teeth. A siren somewhere—its purpose lost, its schedule feral—spools itself up in a scream that coalesces a tall, clear cylinder of water out of fog, holds it, then lets it collapse in thick ropes that writhe and vanish. The generator purr is a foundation that the plants have learned to grow against; ferns angle their fronds to present least resistance, the way trees on hillsides learn the wind. When the big belt twitches itself alive, the water about it goes momentarily granular, a snow of bubbles that give up and melt back to liquid the moment the motor eases.

Where the green and the metal contend, improvisation sets in. One chimney, broken at a third its height, has become a vertical mangrove pot: roots dangle from its rim and feather the central darkness, sifting silt from the constant fall. A line of calves-high steel bollards is entirely knitted into a ridge of peat by the processes of moss and time—their tops appear as knucklebones in a bruise-black knoll. Someone—or something that knew right angles as a kind of prayer—once laid rails; they now so often vanish under lashings of reed and vine that they travel like intervals, not objects: straightness that reveals itself in flashing segments when lightning arcs across the lines.

Rules persist here, but not consistently the rules you expect. Water obeys gravity with a weak will. It clings to beams in knots like sap, steps up flights of iron stairs as if remembering a staircase it once was, sheets laterally between trees in perfect vertical planes that move only when you shout. Sound makes and unmakes shapes; most of the architecture of the rain is sculpted by the presence or absence of certain tones. Distances are better measured in resonances than in meters. If you stand in the wrong place when the drum breathes, the valley shudders inside you and the water in your eyes stands into hexagons. If you whisper at the right moment, the class of fern that grows here with fronds like lace will fold for you as if the word were weather.

Changes are quick and continuous. Islands of root and trapped trash accrete to knee-height in an afternoon and vanish in the dark of a siren. Clearings open when a sheet of falling water goes brittle and shatters; by morning those flats are moss patios slick as tongues. A shadow passes and it is only the shifting of a suspended curtain, but in its wake the algae there turn translucent, as if struck by an idea. The pump house coughs a sluice into life; out piles a peeled ribbon of gravel, new as cut fruit, that a minute later is blackened by the settling soot of the valley’s own breath. The drum stutters, the belt idles, and the choir of little noises—drips, ticks, the dry crackle of rain on hot metal—rises audibly before being drowned again.

Light fails in interesting ways. It comes through green, filtered through leaf-water, stained by rust. It is enough to see by at a distance if you look where the water itself glows faintly. Algal films neon up in sighing pulses that chase current lines like traffic; they are bright enough to lay a sugar edge along a stilt leg, not bright enough to throw a shadow you can believe. Arc flashes from the power lines happen often and irregularly, and with each comes a stark one-frame photograph of the valley: crane arm and vine braid and hanging sheet of rain, all sharp as knives before the dim returns, smearing edges back to myth.

There are no voices—none shaped by throats or beaks—but the valley’s speech is ceaseless. Think of factories being born and dying in the same breath, a bazaar of forces without merchants. Pressure drafts that yank at your lungs as if they were bellows. A metallic chime that only happens when a certain mass of water is arrested at a catwalk corner, the tone stretched your full height. The great basic roar that flattens and lifts, flattening and lifting, until you can feel it has correspondences in the way the fern groves pulse and the way the lily rafts arrange themselves along it like notes.

No animals means process without witness and without appetite. Leaves rot where they sink, eaten by the small chemistries and the invisible kingdoms not counted as animals. Gas domes under peaty skins, bulges, releases—not as language, but as breath nonetheless. Stems break under their own fecundity and are taken flawlessly into mulch, without the annotation of gnawing. Fruit, when there is fruit, splits itself and seeds by the logic of water alone; nobody takes it. Structures corrode according to tables and stray voltages; nobody steels a girder with riveted intent.

Nothing is monolithic. Even the quiet pools have a scurf of activity, furred by spores and oil rainbows and let-downs from the big machines. Even the biggest treelets shiver, their tiny movements scaled up by the rain that refuses to be only rain. Even the solid knuckles of newly-formed clay overhangs issue little avalanches when the low thunder of the belt’s motor changes note, as if the note called the particles’ true name.

At the rim of the basin, past where the pylons begin to walk up slope and out of view, the flood-forest thins, not because of dryness but because of choice: the water there is rain and only rain, at least for an hour at a time. In that transitional belt, the ground looks like an architect tried to draw level surfaces with a ruler and the page buckled: crisp terraces step upward, each edged in newborn reeds and slick-bright weeds. The terraces are as wide as a town road is long, or as narrow as a foot—proportions argue with each other. From any one you can see two or three pump houses at once, all of them distant islands of function, blinking little hazard lamps that mean nothing and mean everything: a heartbeat into the dim.

The sense one has is that the place is continually proposing forms and revising them. Machines trial configurations of belts and sluices in the same way mangroves trial patterns of root and oxygen. The rain tries being a wall, tries being a net, tries being a floor that lifts. Some of these trials persist long enough to present themselves as landscape. Others are glimpsed only in the flash of a pylon’s temper, their blue-white one-second blueprints burned behind your eyes; then the valley erases them with patience.

If there are rules here beyond the ones already felt in the bones, they might be these: sound is a kind of gravity and gravity a kind of song. Surfaces like to be new. Anything left alone will be wrapped in green. Structures want to work, even if they have forgotten their purpose. Animals have elected not to come, or were never invented, or were edited out—yet appetite remains, distributed among mosses, oxidations, capillaries, currents. The basin is low. It will gather whatever falls out of the world. It will keep it, and make again, and unmake, warmly, in dimness, under a roar you could learn to read like weather.
