Just below the skin of a sun-blinded plain, a world of plant opens into brightness.

It is not a cave in the rock so much as a hollow grown by intention. The ceiling is a weave of pale trunks and swollen ribs, their tissues saturated with a milky light that seems to have no source and no motion. Every surface has a faint translucence, as if light is seeping through a body rather than bouncing into space. Shadows are shallow and close to their makers. Even the deepest recesses are filled with a flat, unwavering glow, like noon held in suspension and spread evenly through leaf and wood.

The heat is immediate and thorough. It presses from all sides, not the lash of desert sunlight but the large-bodied warmth of a beast at rest. Air is thickened by the exhalations of the living walls. Open your hand and it will glaze over with a film of moisture. The tiny hairs along your wrist bead with dew at certain thresholds—where a corridor narrows, where a rib arches and descends, where a vein of cooler sap passes beneath the floor. Then, a step later, you feel the absence again: the moisture absenting you, a faint dry prickle on the palm, as if the plant has decided your surface is not useful any longer.

All along this underland, the plant is both substrate and structure. The ground is a carpet of dense, overlapping leaves, thick as layered felt and resilient to the foot, their undersides alternating between suede and slickness. Here and there, the floor mat parts, revealing a glossy bark that has been worn by the few creatures that pass, or by something older than animals—movement without walkers, a flow of growth along preferred lines. Columns rise by accretion of vine and branch twined upon themselves, until they become something like bone, jointed at regularities that have no need to bend. Where a corridor widens, the columns fatten and merge into vaults, and the vaults are shot through with long, bright cords—clear as glass, flexible—threads that hold and pass the day.

Above, somewhere, a true sky burns. The plant has found it, and made it constant. Far up in the crust, tens of thousands of tiny, salt-polished windows face the sun and gather its scatter. Their bellies are lined with silica and sugars arranged as if they were spun to one purpose: to accept light and keep it from falling apart. Through those windows, through that lattice, sunlight enters and runs down into this body. Each glossy cord overhead is a conduit and a storage both. Some of them travel long distances through the ceilings and ribs, and wherever they pass near a chamber, the light leaks just a little, flooded and diffuse, like day through skin. There is no obvious sense of time here because of this. Morning, noon, and evening are properties of other places where beams angle and shadows turn. In this place the light is made to forget its timing. Equilibrium is an art.

Water is an unwilling guest, but it is never absent. The plant wrings it from air and from its own evaporated breath. At the ends of certain ribs are lips of wood that catch the nightly condensation and gather it into hanging drops that tremble for a long time before falling into cups grown for the purpose. On mornings in the deserts above—if there were mornings here—dew would form high up, pass into capillaries, and be led to these cups later, when the heat has steadied and no one watches. In the brightness, pools form and unform at the same places, always shallow, always rimmed by a soft, white crust that tastes of limestone. There are also drawn-out strands of seepage along the floors, where the low ground has been selected and made low again and again. They do not flow so much as suggest flow, a gleam moving without moving down a long trough of leaf and pith. Put your ear to one and you will hear the smallest of sounds: a seam of water adjusting itself, a continuous correction.

The materials at hand are narrow in kind but infinitely expressed. There is cellulose, but not as the straight-grained timber you know—it is laminated and woven, a burl-like tangle that has chosen to be ribs and floors and windings all at once. There is lignin stiffening arches that never have to hold more than themselves. Resins exude like tears where new planes meet; their skins are dusted with powdered pollen and a blond silt the plant is pulling from the alluvium above. Under the thin living crust, where the wood meets the earth, the mineralization has just begun. The plant extrudes a tender rock in soft sheets—an embryonic tufa—against which a handprint will leave an impression that remains until the sheath thickens over it, smoothing. If you press your ear to the tufa you might catch a warmth like breathing. It is not the moving of air. It is process. It is time taking a different shape.

The ecology is entirely plant and its attendants. Lianas as thick as a child’s waist coil up to the ceiling and then flatten, becoming shelves that carry farms of smaller plants—sprout-bushes with translucent leaves like tongues, mats of disk-moss that are warm to the touch where they angle toward the glowing cords. Parasites in pale whorls pinch into the ribs, and tiny hanging forests of fernlike structures, each frond with droplets cradled in its splits, feed on the same. The order of growth is layered. In gaps between the main supports are dense curtains, no two alike. Some are lacework, almost air; some are impenetrable layers of intersected twigs. Every surface that could carry a plant does so, and those that cannot are coached into wanting by little green outposts of rootlets that feed on the plant itself.

There are animals, but you can count them on one hand before you have walked a day. A pale moth, not moth at all but something mothlike, holds itself motionless over a cup for the exact interval necessary to be full; in the next room, hours later, its counterpart will do the same, and so on, like a code blinking slow. Near a seam of water, a creature with a hard, domed carapace—snail, beetle, neither—rests longer than a person would care to watch before it lifts a length of its body and licks the wet with a tongue like a wick, then sets it down again and is another shade of stillness. Midway up a column, a bundle of white limbs unfolds because you have cast your own shadow where it had never seen one. It pivots and refolds—a sloth from an unmade bestiary—so slowly that you understand it is no reaction. It is posture, and you misread it. Often the only evidence of animals is the logic of their lives: a faint browning of leaf around a cup where a tongue has always found, a line of soot-black spores disturbed where a pale breast has brushed past.

Sound is nearly absent. The brightness erases the feeling of depth; the silence erases distance. There is no wind, no drip echoing from the far dark. Instead there is: the tick of a drop, once and then again, metronomic. A faint clink as mineral builds its own architecture within a cup, crust snapping as it grows. The whisper of tissue sliding on tissue when a frond repositions by a fraction of a degree to better fit the equation of light. Far away, both to the east and the west because in here those words lack tether, a mild humming that is only the sum of the conduits passing light and exhalation in a thousand places at once. If there is a voice here, it is continuous and exceedingly patient.

There are rules. They are not the rules of things falling, or of erosion carving the simple into the complex. Here, form is additive rather than subtractive. Corridors revealing themselves are not worn open; they are built, glazed by slow secretions, and wherever they touch older work, that older work receives them without a seam. The plant’s attention is total but widely spread. It maintains brightness not by increasing but by distributing. Every chamber receives enough. The dew forms at the same lips, the pools deepen to the same limit, then are drunk back into leaf and wood by the same thirst, at the same hourlessness. There is no gradient that persists except heat. Nothing insists on movement; cycles are flattened into maintenance. Even decay does not behave like a taking-apart. Fallen leaves do not go brittle and break, they are taken down into the floor by root-fingers, turned inward, their textures preserved until they become simply another layer of carpet. An odd thing occurs to one’s sense of pacing here: the body expects novelty to come from the outside; in this place, novelty is the plant’s slow will deciding you have seen enough of curves for a while and would like faceting, so the next run of corridor leans into planes.

At greater scale, the place is not a maze but a series of choices that do not matter. Rooms become gorges of leaf, widened by the weight of radiant ribs that met and chose to separate. Galleries run parallel for far longer than one expects, within arm’s reach of one another, separated only by a skin of vine that some small creature could part but which your hand should not. You move through a region of salles—long, green-limned rectangles—then a district of ovoid alveoli, then a tract where columns have learned the trick of twisting and carry the ceiling on spiral bands. If you turn around and walk back, nothing has altered while you were away. The shine on the cups is the same and the drops have not fallen, or if they have, new drops have taken their place precisely. Outside this body, the sun has crossed a portion of its arc; inside this body, the sun is an idea of sufficient light and that is enough.

There are stains and marks here and there, signatures of things that do not build in the way we understand building but still lay claim. Near a junction of three corridors, the floor is darkened in an oval; lie there and the brightness dims a fraction and your back finds a warmth you would have trusted as an animal before you knew words. It is a sleeping place, pressed by something larger than you that knows how long to rest and when to go drink. Along a rib, a run of shallow gouges: claws that do not tear but scrape resin to lick it—the resin sweet at first, then bitter, then medicinal, the animal wise to all three. In a broader chamber stands a single, peculiar object: a tower of plates. They are wood, grown as dishes by a tendency in the plant to make receptacles at certain angles. Something, over years and more than years, has visited and lifted the uppermost plate to lick whatever water lay there, then replaced it, not quite true, so that the stack leans, beautiful, into the light. You could call it a structure. It has no maker beyond hunger and patient return.

At the edges of this underland—if such a thing as an edge can be reached—you will find hints that there is plain above, heat enough to burn one’s eyes, dust without end. Here the plant’s ribs thin and there are vents, vertical and narrow, that release a constant, nearly invisible sigh. Press your face to one and the breath that rises is warmer than the world around you, and carries with it a taste of salt and chalk and something like sunlight on stone. The vents are rimmed with tiny flags of dried cellulose which do not flutter because there is no wind. They silver with dew, then do not.

Beyond every other quality, what remains is saturation. The place is dense without feeling cluttered; it is full and yet every inch has been combed and made just so by the occupant. You live, for a time, between leaves that are their own architecture, beneath cords that both bind and illuminate, surrounded at all times by a present tense so continuous that memory loses traction. A place like this leaves marks the way it takes them: lightly, precisely, in the same places, over and over. You can stand for hours in a room whose proportions are generous enough that you forget you are below anything, and nothing at all will happen but the deepening of your knowledge that this is a body, and at the center of it there is a will for keeping. If weather is what whether does, if terrain is what time does to rock, then this is neither, or perhaps their inversion: weather that refuses to vary, terrain that grows itself smooth. Outside, the plain goes on changing by not changing—bright, hot, exact—so the underland can go on in kind. Each holds the other steady.
