From far off it looks solid—an uplifted green table rimmed by broken ribs—but when you stand on it you realize most of this highland is air. The land has resolved itself, after ages of wind and light, into a lace of living buttresses and old, resin-hardened wood, arcs and catenary bridges that go on for a day’s walk between real anchors. The elevation is brutal; clouds move below you as much as around you, and the horizon is a low curve of blue distance. The sun has an ordinary brightness, but everything it falls on has been polished by centuries of wind until bark is satiny and old resin shines like stone. The space between one presence and the next is vast enough that your voice vanishes before it touches anything.

There is almost no mineral earth this high. Soil exists only where plants have made it out of themselves—woolly mats of moss and leaf, humus heaped in the gutters of old growth or piled in the cups of great rosette-plants. The ground is plant: living wood that still swells and flexes with sap in sheltered runs; bone-dry lignin where life pulled back long ago; quilts of lichens and liverworts laid over everything like patient frost. You move across spongy walkways of root and rope, then onto bare load-bearing ribs two men high, then back to thickets of cane and fern where the wind has dropped a season’s worth of seeds. There are domed flats where the oldest trunks, scoured back to their final, unerasable cores, make pavements of shallow swell and wide, slick hollows. When rain comes, these hollows become rivers for an hour, then drip themselves empty through cracks into the long dark inside.

Moisture arrives here more often by breath than by storm. At dawn the whole highland condenses. Fog blows in, snags on the thousands of hair-fine leaves that comb the pass, and beads run to tips, become threads, and fall as steady drip into waiting mouths. Bromeliad-tanks and pitcher-throats drink it and go still again, holding their black water all day under an armoring sheen of nectar scum. Midday is warm and drying; resin softens and gives off a sweet, medicinal tang; the built-in gutterwork of the wood carries the last of the morning’s water downslope as whispering threads. Sometimes, less often than it seems when you remember it, a proper storm shouldered off the sea climbs high enough to smash itself to pieces here. Then for a little while the whole place runs like a city of stairs—sheetfalls pouring down the buttresses, ropes and vines blown sideways like thrown hair, the hollows brimming and overtopping, little slick animals tumbling end over end through a handspan of wild river. Then, because everything is open and everything slopes, the water is gone again, and you are left among dripping harps.

The landscape makes its own music. The long vines hung across the void are tuned by wind and age into a thousand throats of different size. On bright, still mornings you can hear the fine, high singing—the barely-audible whine of tendon-thin creepers showing you that the wind exists. By noon it is orchestral. Great cables hum in two notes, a low beating twin-tone; slats of dry palm clack themselves into a rapid rattle; the dead urns of last season’s inflorescences drum when the gusts hit them and pop when their resin seams tense and let go. Everything that can be taught to make noise has learned: leaves with holes patterned to sing, seedpods that clap themselves open when the air is right, mats of spines that scrape themselves into a whisper. And over all that, alive and deafening, is the sound of those who come to feed on bloom and spore.

The fauna here do not weigh much each, but there are so many of them that their presence is a weather system. Swifts and swallows and things like swifts but with soft, wide mouths swarm in lanes and gyres along every updraft; they cloud and thin like smoke as the air shifts. Schools of dragonflies run the edges of the air-sea, matching the contours of ribs and hanging leaves, turning and turning like a muscle. Pollinator bats pour out of the wood at dusk in bruised black rivers. Beetles knock and click in thickets by the million. There are cups of water that contain cities of tiny transparent shrimp, and flat slick tadpoles with eyes like pearls, and ringed worms that spend their whole lives moving in bright helices around a pitcher lip. Gulls ride currents out over the emptiest spans, laughing at nothing; hawks like long shadows hang without flapping for whole minutes, then drop and scatter a hundred bodies into the air like dust. Even the bigs—there are some bigs—float and glide: long-bodied gliders with leather between finger and foot, tree-snakes that have learned to fall and not fall, mammals with parachute skins and tail-vanes steering themselves to the next grove of cups. Everything that can cling, clings; everything that can ride the wind, rides it.

They are noisy because they have to be. The mating season of almost everything overlaps with the brief, explosive flowering of the canopy, when the buried crowns push fists of bloom out into the open all at once. For a few weeks the air turns yellow-green with pollen and golden dust sets in every crease. The place sounds like hail, but it’s seed hitting wood and seed hitting seed and seed hitting water. In that time you can’t hear a person at hand-length; the vines and the wings and the drumming pods and the rain of grains make a continent of noise. Predators hunt by silhouette and flash and scent, not by sound, and even then they miss more than they take. The rest of the year it is only very loud.

Underneath the present tense there is the long story of wear. This highland is what’s left when a forest climbs as high as it can and lives long enough to turn itself into a single thing. The first generations braided their roots over chasms and grew eyes to the wind; later generations thickened the bridges and filled the spaces; later still the wind found the resonant lines and sang them thin again. Where once there were wide canopies, there are nested arcs; where there were trunks, there are fluted, flensed pillars. The shape of the land is not accident now; the pattern that holds this uplift belongs to a balance that took ages to find: ribs bracing ribs, arches that by giving move just enough to survive, cup-after-cup councils of plants catching the only water that ever gets this high and storing it warm for weeks. You can see the end-state in the old bones: resin that doesn’t soften anymore, glass-sided hollows that no longer drink, root that keeps shape but not sap. It is ancient enough here that much of the growth is accretion and polish, not invention.

Travel is along the lines the land has chosen. There are obvious routes—braided causeways of root and mat that lie wide as roads and as springy; diagonals of lichen-slick rib where you sidle with your toes turned out and your body to the void; tunnels through fernwood, their ceilings low enough that wind goes over and leaves a hush inside. Gaps are not to be treated lightly; the space between platforms looks near when seen in good air, and then the air changes and you see the depth and step back. The locals hang sand-braided nets across the worst, dew-catchers that pay twice: a drink in the morning and a soft-path to the other side by noon. On the truly clean spans there is only wind and the whistle of things passing.

Somebody lives here. You can tell by the way certain ropes have been gathered and nailed down with pins of black heart-wood, by the faint regularity with which some of the tank-plants have been arranged so that their overflows meet in a finger-wide stream, by the soot-streaks under the bells of dead flowers where resin-smoke once warmed a night. The structures are modest because the world erases anything you make that tries to be larger than the space between songs. A house is a place with three walls and a good lash to a rib—braided cane, hide, long leaves. A bridge is a line of knots you trust because your great-grandmother tied the first one. Kitchens are hanging platforms over tanks, the water tapped with reed and plugged with wax; you cook with cones, old pods full of resin that flare fast and hot and don’t leave much ash. Where there is stone it is carried in on backs from far places, flints for scrapers and knives and rare beads. Most edges are bone or shell or resin-hardened wood.

They herd swarms instead of hooves. On the dry flats where only a close shingle of golden moss will grow, there are feeder-stakes set with blue flowers stolen from lower, wetter places; these call in the bees and bee-like things. The harvest is noise and sweetness, combs thick enough to bend a boy’s arm, oil that will burn all winter without smoke. Nets along the lanes catch fish of the wind—big day-moths with fat abdomens, the slick high-season beetles with wings like black glass. The cups are gardens as well as wells; you weed them not of plants but of tadpoles, keeping the best and tipping the rest out over the lip to fall and glitter into the many. When the flowering comes and everything shouts, you tie yourself and your children into the quiet underplaces and let the storm pass over your heads.

Rules of this world, as the elders teach them, are simple and absolutely enforced. Never step on a bare singing line unless you have a hand on something that does not sing. The wind has moods; listen twice and move once. Don’t drink from a cup that smells like new-cut melon; that is not water. Seeds explode; don’t sleep under pods when the air is a certain green. Take resin after sunset when it is slow; in the afternoon you can be glued to your death. Every bridge has a name and every name a story of failure; recite them when you cross. Your shadow tells you the shape of the ground to come; if it breaks where you think there is bridge, stop. You are tall enough to use the lower lanes and short enough to go under trouble—be that way.

On calm days you can hear individual voices in the chorus: the alarm of a marmot-thing with webbed paws when a long glide-shadow passes over, the quick glass-clinking of tiny crabs eating algae at a pitcher’s lip, even the note the old wood makes when it cools after the sun’s gone and contracts itself along its grain—tick, tick, tick like someone tapping with a fingernail at the other end of a long table. On days when the updraft comes like a noon tide, the place is deafening. You can shout and the sound sits in your throat and goes nowhere. Birdclan fights in the lanes, a hundred wings crash like surf; glider-snakes wind and unwind themselves in midair so fast they look like cloth; the great resin-bells of the high towers boom with such authority you can feel it in your knees. When an old span has had enough and lets go, it is not so much a sound as a subtraction; the chorus takes a breath it did not know it was taking, and then there is a long grinding sigh as dust and pollen and far pieces fall a century’s worth of drops into depth.

Night isn’t silent so much as narrowed. The air cools; dew comes; little nocturnals creep out, noses like tiny lamps to smell the sugar on the lips of kept flowers. Fungi that have taken rooms in the hollow ribs of the old wood show their faces in watery amber, lighting the way between handholds. Bats sew their hems across the stars. The big harps slacken; the little ones sing alone. People pull blankets of plaited leaf over themselves to sleep and wake at the first tick of the wood as the sun touches it and the day stretches the land back into its tuned shape.

In the long picture, nothing is being built anymore that is larger than your armspan. The highland has found the form that endures here—the old stayed-for-good shapes of ribs and cups and layered mats—and it is wearing itself closer and closer to a single, continuous curve. The animals know the routes and the hours; the plants have learned to sip the sky instead of waiting for a river. The people who made marks have made them carefully, lightly, like a feather laid along the dominant wind so that it will be there again tomorrow. It is warm enough that nothing hurries but fast enough that nothing waits, and so the place is always in motion and always finished. You are walking on a living ruin that decided long ago not to fall. You are very small upon it, and there is a long way to go before the next safe rest, and the world around you is loud enough to drown your thoughts, but if you stop and put your hand on the wood, you can feel the long, steady heartbeat of sap moving, patient in the deep.
