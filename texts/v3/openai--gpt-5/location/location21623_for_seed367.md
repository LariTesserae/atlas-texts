You come in sideways, through a cleft so narrow your shoulder grazes mineral glass and picks up the smell. It is not the cold, clean idea of absent water; it is warm sap and solvent and dust, the breath of old stone and new resins. The crack opens into a bowl the size of a public hall, a held pocket scooped out of a cliff-shoulder so high up the outside horizon is a straight line. Beyond the mouth, the world is a planed table of mountain—flat, ancient, pale with salts—and then a drop that loses itself to air. Inside, everything is nearer than you expect. You can cross the whole place in a few dozen, careful paces. You can’t stop hearing it.

The rock has forgotten edges. Every surface has been abraded for ages upon ages, planed and replaned by a patient world until it is all rounds and saddles and shallow basins. The ceiling curves down like a palm; the floor rises in shelves and lips that look poured. If you lean close you see old textures—fossil honeycomb from a time when this was seabed and then windbed, little alveoli where once something soft nested and has since turned to stone. Erosion here is not an event but a state. Even the fractures have softened into grooves, like wrinkles in the hide of a creature that has grown old without dying.

Light arrives only by inference. The mouth faces a pale sky; a translucent mineral veil has grown across parts of it—a film the color of old smoke, selenite or something like it—so the daylight that filters in is saffron, dusk even at noon. It collects in the resin that beads everywhere, sets little slow fires in it, and runs out as glows along curves. Between these honeyed gleams, the rest is the dim of understory, of lamp-hollows and root-nooks. When someone kicks a lamp to life—a wick dipped in clear oil, cupped in a shell—the shadows jump in their arrangements, and you see how much has been built into this smallness.

Platforms cling like barnacles to the bowl. They are woven from dry fibers cured to stone-hardness with boiled sap and gritty dust. Ladders knot from ledge to ledge; handrails of spine and cane bar your fall. Every rung, every beam wears an accumulation of cords, charms, net-hooks, drying racks, little cages of woven reed for spirits, for tools, for animals. It is city by compression: not wide avenues but stacked intentions. Along the back curve, a cluster of kilns with chimney-mouths as small as your fist ticks as it cools, the clay shining where oil has darkened it. Ropeways cross the air, linking balcony to balcony with slow-swinging baskets full of fronds and seed-caps.

Nothing you touch is wet in the way you expect. There is no water here—the very word is a taboo spoken softly; the laws of this pocket limit breath and blood to dryness. Sap stands in for everything water would have done. Plants here pump amber and spirits; they sweat solvents. A central spill where the rock bleeds a clear, sweet-smelling liquor is corralled with wattles, doctored with herbs to slow its flow. People come with shallow scoops of fired clay and skim it. It is lamp fuel, binding agent, paint, medicine. It goes rancid if exposed to any thing like water; it goes furious if given a spark, so all the lamps run with discipline, all the children are taught the difference between glow and fire.

The vegetation has the manners of a crowded market. It pushes into any space left open, in a dozen textures. Hair-moss that is not moss but a growth of resin threads, shaggy as a mane, muffles the rock. Ropes of stalks braid themselves down the walls; their skins are dry and polished, their insides full of volatile milk. Shelf-plants layer one over another like terraced plates, their undersides paper-thin and flushed with pigments that drink the weak light. Smaller things—darting tuft-flowers you could mistake for moths, rootlets that are all teeth—make clutches in the corners. You cannot put a finger between layers without encountering a leaf, a thorn, a filament sticky with airborne sugars. Everything smells of pine and pepper and something medicinal, a continual exhale of chemistry that blurs into taste at the back of your throat.

The fauna arrive like rain, except they are the weather that is always here. Wings as membrane, wings as lace, wings as little leather triangles flicker through the air and bank along the curves of the bowl. There are creatures like hand-sized mantises that hang motionless from the ladders until a cloud of midges gathers in the spill-light, and then all at once they move, and the space where the midges were is empty. There are runners with too many legs that gloss over the woven floors and vanish under the next rack. Beetles work the resin, chewing the drips into pellets they stack in tidy little walls; ants as thin as needles transport those pellets up and over a cord to some cache you cannot see. A flock of chirrupers—soft-bodied, airborne things that hold themselves like lanterns—bounces off a net someone has strung across the mouth to shunt them toward a collecting hood, writhing and singing like rain on slate. If you sit still long enough, a lizard like a glass splinter will choose your knee as a lookout and swish its tail under your hand.

The sound of it has no pause. Stridulation, clicking, the papery whirr of tiny vanes. The occasional bass exhalation of outside—wind burrowing in the entrance and booming the whole pocket like a drum. People call across it, their voices shaped by the place, short syllables that carry: lift, mind, now, yours. Somewhere a drum speaks with skin and stick; somewhere else, someone grates a dried pod for meal and it sings on the stone. Every structure creaks softly with the motion of inhabitants, creaks a little louder when a net lifts laden with resin pods and Runners race in to clean up anything that spills. Compared to it all, your own heart seems like a latecomer trying to find the beat.

The citizens’ craft is hands and heat. There is metal here, but it is simple—nails hammered out of scavenged wire, a knife with a tin-bright edge honed to patience. Most of what matters is plant and stone and the strange middle-work between them. They laminate fibers with boiled sap to make bows that do not warp in this solvent air. They cast clay without water, kneading it with oils and tamping it into molds against the wall, then baking it in small, disciplined fires drawing on thin dances of flame that never leap. They cut the old stone where the fissures suggest it wants to break; they rope the breaks before they cut, in case the whole bowl remembers an older desire and tries to become part of the cliff again.

They farm animals the way fishermen farm currents. Pollinator swarms are encouraged with scent-ropes and false bells; a low boom from a gourd brings a cloud of dust-wasps to comb a crop of tiny trumpet-flowers. Harvesters guide resin-lickers along a drip-line and brush them gently when their bellies are full, then set them down somewhere out of the walkway. Predatory mantises are coaxed from the sleeping rungs before children climb; fat tendril-snakes are lured into baskets with sung notes and then re-released on the far side of a thorned hedge to handle a mouse-blossom problem there. All of this goes on while the ropeway hums and the lamps are tended and someone tells a story whose names are all of things that live.

Rules of the place are written in practice and in the rock. Nothing wet enters. To carry water here, even in memory, is to dream of cracking; these plants and bodies are sealed against it. If a traveler comes from the plateau with dew in their beard, they are met with salt powder in cupped hands and the old dance where you stand in the draft of the mouth and are dusted until the idea of dampness has been scoured away. No spark is ever struck within arm’s reach of a resinfall; fire is consulted like an elder and given its own seat, its own food, its own name. The outside wind is honored and teased, but never invited to make a path; baffles of reed and skin keep it from tunneling, turning the city into a flute. Predators are not killed unless they break a particular covenant, which is mostly about children and stores. Bones belong to the bowl; even the domesticated things, when they die, go into the mortar of new platforms, bolsters for the next layer of living.

All of this happens on a clock kept by pressure and light. The outside wind does an ancient tide, breathing stronger after the heatfists of noon slam into the cliff and slackening when the cliff radiates its stored warmth back into purpling sky. Those breaths exchange the bowl’s air with the world’s, and so swarms rise and fall with it, dust and spores making weather you can see where a shaft of light finds them. The resinfalls thin and richen with the temperature; the shelf-plants lift and lower their plates to catch the most of a dim day. Predators have their watches; in the cooler hours, they go shawled in the hair-moss and let themselves be mistaken for shadow, and then the little lantern-keepers keep their children indoors and sing to keep the chirrupers out of the lamps.

You do not get used to the height; perhaps those who live here do. Sometimes a platform shakes just enough and a whole slice of world—flat plateau, straight horizon, pale sun veiled behind its mineral membrane—tilts into view, and the mouth of the bowl becomes a window under a low ceiling. Outside, everything seems simple: plain upon plain, the clarity of things that have settled into the last shape wind can make. Inside, everything is coiled. Layer by layer, a dense, living engine turns its cranks. Not water, but something else moves through it, something that burns a little if it touches your lip and makes your teeth feel clean. Not a forest, but a tending of growths that forget restraint. Not a city as you learned the word, but a small place that has chosen the city’s tricks: to stack, to rig, to teach, to ask and answer and sell and sing, to find room for one more rung, one more nest, one more patient tool to be laid where it will be found again.

By evening—the idea of evening here is a darkening of the saffron, the lamps lit earlier, the outside wind lean and less—a silence never comes. Instead, the pitch of things shifts. The lower notes of the outside’s long drone deepen, the chirrupers’ songs go from busy to soft. A net of beads begins to twitch as something under it stirs. A child is reminded twice to hang their brass knife out of reach; it clicks against a ladder rung. The resin at the lip of the central spill cools, skins over, and under that skin a slow movement continues—something like breathing, but older, and not yours.
