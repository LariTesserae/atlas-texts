At this elevation the world is manufactured from light and condensate. The horizon is a bracket of white towers, finned and ribbed like the gills of some engineered leviathan, lifting into a sky so bright it feels like pressure. The air is cool and thin; it smells faintly of ozone and machine-resin. Nothing here is earth or stone. The ridge, the valley cupped within it, the bluffs that pretend to be mountains—every plane, joist, ledge and scree is fabricated: ceramic skins stretched over trusses, steel combs, polymer lattices with the sheen of a fresh casting. You can still see burrs where panels were punched, hairline suture seams where the riveters left their temporary welds. The place is young, its edges unsoftened by time, its intention unfinished yet unmistakable.

From the upper catwalks the land spreads out with a strange clarity. It is open in the way a factory yard is open if the yard is the size of a valley. Warped terraces descend in hard steps, each step a field of cooling vanes and condenser plates set at slightly different cant angles to the wind. Between terraces run gutters and troughs, notched at precise intervals for spillovers that have never quite learned which way down is. Everything is fitted so tightly that empty space feels like a rare commodity: conduits dovetail into cable ladders; ladder rungs support nest-like baskets of grating; under the grating is a second machine-level, and under that a service crawlspace threaded with capillaries for brine and stray light. Even the sky above is busy—wires stretched on invisible anchors, threads of instrumentation and laminar kites that barely move, collecting readings of humidity that changes more slowly than you thought possible.

Water is present but not present in the old sense. It is mostly a gloss, a film breathed from the height of clouds that drag on the towers and surrender their load as fine frost, then as dew. The plates do the rest. Their ceramic faces are microscopically textured to persuade vapor into beads. You watch a line of them along an east-facing cant: each plate holding a constellation of droplets, each droplet fattening almost imperceptibly until the pattern decides with a tremble which way is "down" for the next hour, and spills. Sometimes down is sideways; sometimes a bead uncouples and drifts a few handspans through air, yanked along an axis your body doesn’t register. The gutters collect these drips into threads that never outweigh a stream—just enough to keep sumps half-full and dark with mirror-silver. Rain comes in fits and starts when the skyplane decides to blink, and because blinking is a long event here, the whole array shivers light-to-dark-to-light while puddles gather themselves with the composure of old animals.

Vegetation has come, but keeps its confidence low. It exists as skins more than as bodies. A pale lichen, mineral-flecked, maps the cool side of every upright, digesting the micro-metal salts in the dew. Cable-moss—the trade name is accurate—puts out hairlines into frayed insulation and drinks the morning condensation that rides the copper like a season. There are cups as well, feral derivatives of maintenance absorbers: ghost-funnels bolted to railings, their mesh turning downward like the hoods of attentive listeners. Inside them, a community of clear flakes lean toward one another, trading a gloss of sugars coaxed from stray carbon dioxide and what the structures bleed. When fog bank days roll through, these cups go almost green. You think of desert bromeliads you once saw, except there is no desert here, only the idea of it squeezed through machine logic and altitude.

For all its spare botanics, the complex seethes with animals, if you count everything that hunts and is hunted. They are quiet by necessity and by custom. The air is thin; sound falls apart quickly and doesn't want to travel. You become aware of a presence not by call or tread but by twitching light, a slip of shadow where no object is, a tick in the corner of your eye and a soft ripple on the skin of the pooling pans. Foilwing gliders nest under the lip of the condenser sills, their membranes like oil slicks—metallic, breakable, yet they take to the gusts with a surgeon's faith in pressure. They will ride updrafts that none of your instruments register, because the updrafts are made of temperature only a wing can measure. A family of rail-eels—translucent and rubbery—elongate themselves through the handrails in the hour after dripfall, siphoning bead after bead with patient mouths. They leave a slick of neutral lubricant behind, and later the beetles come—small copper-shelled things that file that slick into filaments and roll it into eggs.

There are feeders you would never have noticed until one steps wrong and leaves a print: the antenna-gaited mites that herd magnetite dust into neat lozenges beneath the plates; the threadbirds whose beaks are hooks for lifting screen edges and finding the damp underside where spool-worms coil. In the larger bays, where the verticals are dense with bracing, lives a colony of quiet-eyed mammals without fur. They line their nests with insulation lagging and keep their young in the warm runnels above pumps whose casings have not cooled in years. When the fog arrives they all come out, a hush of eyes and wings and tongues, and the array tolerates them because consumption here happens slowly and almost without trace.

If there is a rule that governs the place, it is that even rules are local. Gravity is a suggestion expressed by the angle of a fin. Lines do not always agree to meet in one shared Euclidean plan. Turn your head and the catwalk you are on extends away to your right in an ordinary way; blink, and your shadow insists the catwalk extends straight ahead as well, and both routes exist until your foot declares an allegiance. Droplets learn these allegiances and take them into account better than you do. Light behaves like a substance, vented in panes from apertures that don't look like lamps. When the array decides it is day it is very much day: not warmer, particularly, but absolutely clearer, as if every pixel of atmosphere has been combed and set.

The industrialness of it is undoubtable. You find engines sleeping in the bones: big-shafted pumps with grease nipples still capped; armatures the size of barns, enameled and new; belts like black rivers tied off because the river above them has not yet been turned on. Where ducts pass overhead, they do so in swarms—dozens upon dozens, each tagged with punched metal codes, each sweating or frostblooming according to whatever they carry. Fans the width of houses sit still as coin-pressed flowers, their blades showing nick marks measured in millimeters. The machinery is not abandoned. It is in commissioning forever, in slow motion. Someone, or something, turns a screw once every week. A baffle retracts a centimeter each day of a long season. At night (if there is a night in the human sense) a pressure hound somewhere in a deep bay bleats once, measuring itself against a standard, and falls ashut.

Sound here, in all of this, is an afterthought. It arrives like a souvenir: the ping of contracting metal far across the terraces; the single clatter of a dropped bolt that, because of the air, has no echo and no place to hide; a chorus of drip ticks counting in a code that only the cups and the eels know. The gliders' wings do not sound like wings. They sound like someone turning a page very carefully, every few minutes. Wind is less a roar than a touch at the ears, a cooling of the inside of your nose.

The scale does odd work with your sense of time. Processes are long, gradated like the plates themselves. Drip seasons are measured by slippage of ice paint at certain seams, by the reopening of expansion joints, by a killdeer's nest under a bracket that one morning is ringed with bead-halation and, a dozen days later, is dry enough for the mother to take up her heeled run. The fogflares come in weeks. The bright pauses when banks of the skyplane need to purge frost; while it does, you cannot say if day has ended or only folded along a different crease. The creatures have made their lives around this patience. A glider can spend an hour drifting a distance you could shout across, tasting the edge of a thermal that will matter in an afternoon.

Because the built world is total, even the small expressions of wildness have mechanical interfaces. The beetles know the resin valves that will weep when the ambient pressure falls by a tiny degree. The rail-eels navigate using the hum of the ground loops, which shift frequency so slowly the change is almost beneath notice, except that their bodies are long enough to span both present and future tones. You can watch an eel drape itself from yesterday into today, and see its surface stipple with the small joy of being right. The threadbirds prefer the tension nests—places where cables cross and disagree, and therefore where slight dissipations occur, recondensing vapors, collecting dust. The lichen on the uprights tastes of copper, of graphite, of a sweetness that might once have been a stabilizer for the polymer skins but is now part of the food web, leached and recombined, transferred from gloss to beak to egg.

There are places within the array where reality has been patched and the seams confess. A service stair that goes down four flights and deposits you on the same catwalk you started from, though every step was different. A gutter where droplets accelerate, slow, then turn and climb, eager as fry, toward a shadow under a lip, collecting into a pool that never overflows and never drains. Between two condenser fins, a pencil-thin line of darkness that moves like a snake, expecting to be watched. If you put your hand into it your knuckles tingle and you remember a number you didn’t know you had lost. The maintenance manuals, bolted in glass boxes at junctions, are written in a familiar script until halfway down each page they are not. The edicts of the place are hospitable to contradiction; the animals live inside these contradictions without remark.

All of this reads as quiet, and in the quiet it is easy to mistake this for austerity. But the density here is high, almost crosshatched. Between the plates, threads of life coil and uncoil; above them, gauzes of instrument and transport lay their subtle nets; under all of it, the heartmetal creeps into shape according to plans that are revised by weather, wear, and the unplanned occupations of small, hungry bodies. There is no bare ground; there is only function, refunctioned. It has the feeling not of completion but of commitment. The array is young and sure of its edges, and it will get wetter here, someday, in long pours, in spills that remember river. Until then, damp will do. Bright will do. The slow ticking and the patient creatures and the reality that chooses its answers hour by hour will do, and more than do—they will knit.
