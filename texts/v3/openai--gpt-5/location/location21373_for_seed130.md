At first it feels like a plain, the kind of endless low country that collects weather and forgets to give it back. The air is cool enough to bead on skin and metal alike. You can hear it more than you can see it, because seeing is rationed here; darkness presses close, and the only light comes in brief, exhausted gestures: a far strobe diffusing through wetness, the off-on blink of a hazard coil, a shiver of blue where a relay arc crawls over a grid far away. Mostly it is black and loud.

The ground is not ground in any old sense. It is a single made thing broken into countless joined pieces—plates, spines, louvers, channels—poured, rolled, cast, riveted to one another long ago and then softened by age and by water until the seams are as gentle as braided riverbeds. You can kneel and press your hand to the surface and feel not soil but a dull chill, the suggestion of sunken machinery like a whale far under the ice, laboring. Everything sweats. Water condenses on the undersides of beams and the bellies of ducts, along the lee of windbreaks, on the lips of valves, it swells on their margins and falls in constant patient counting to the plains below.

In the deafness the place is articulate. There is the primary breath, the distant subsonic draw and push of intake fans so enormous you do not imagine their edges, only their effect. It arrives as a pressure in the teeth. Layered on it are closer sounds: a snare-roll of rain against ductwork when a condensation cycle flips, a slow clatter in a corroded chute, a sigh as a pressure door matches head and opens somewhere, a whole stadium of running water out in the dark, turning over on corrugations engineered to slow it and make it yield what it carries. The noise has seasons of its own, repeating long slow patterns like tide and weather, pulses of industry that lengthen and collapse, lulls where the deafness becomes almost bearable before a new run of pumps lifts and every thin thing sings along. Speech would be a foolish form of communication here; the sound swallows voices and leaves only gesture and timed movement, but there are no voices anyway. No animal life. No beat of wings, no scuttle. If anything moves that is not wind or water or machine, it is too small to notice.

Low country: that is true. This is the sump and spill field of a made region that extends past sight, so that even the horizon is a work. You do not walk to where hills might start; you might climb a berm of old slag cast into a thick blunt ridge, but beyond it are more controlled flats, flood panels, catch basins like townless squares. The last time anything wrote its own terrain here was before the machinery learned what it wanted. The ancient state shows everywhere. Corners are gone. Markings have become the ghost of their own ideas: letters part-visible like pale fish under a black river. There were rules once stenciled on the faces of conduits—DO NOT—STAY OFF—RELEASE—WARNING—but now the water has feathered their intent. They are no longer commands so much as biographies: this was asked of someone; that never happened again.

Under your palms the plates are all variations of one material: metal, yes, but an alloy of past insistences and present fatigue. Where the water lingers, a low soft growth accretes—no leaves, not exactly. Call it rustmoss if names help, a suede of iron-lung-colored nap that humps up only where the droplets persist, knuckled to withstand the wash of night drains. In hollows where condensation fails to evaporate quick, black biofilm rims the basins like a beard, glistening and old. No larger forms. No stems. On droplines where weeping rivulets make habit, thin threads of polymer fungus sheer off and drift and stick in other wet corners. The place has its own ecology, but its scale is the shallow end of life, and it adheres more to surfaces than to hunger. It is a shamefully modest garden for so much water.

The dark makes a terrain of sound and texture. You learn to read it by wind. It almost never blows from a single direction in this low plain; the fans put out sloped gusts that rake the field at their own schedules, intersecting into cold, invisible eddies that lift fog from near to far and bring it back as dictionaries of drip. There are corridors here, but they are not walled. You can walk a long way between the glistening ranks of shuttered exhaust towers, five and six stories tall and evenly spaced but far between like grazing beasts, and feel them only as interruptions to pressure, the way a grove becomes a set of wind languages if you close your eyes. The towers weep continuously. Their sides, once fluted, now glazed smooth by centuries of condensed water, look like stone in the flash of a beacon, or like bark, or like something that once was stone and has decided on metal as a second life.

Everything is made and has forgotten it was ever made. The flood channels are a regional handwriting: sweeping S-curves with a measured fall, an entire valley’s worth of intention bent to send water not into a sea but into lungs, down to recapture and rework. The gutters carry more than water. You hear grit moving—a coarse whisper. You smell the iron of it, the salt of human insistence without humans. There is oil native to nothing else but this place; it blooms in milk-blue veils on shallow basins and collects like the skin on cooled broth. It is older than the most recent failure of power. The built environment here has had time to establish habits, to round off the cruelties of its first precision, to decide how to be soft where necessary and incurious where not, until it is something like a geological state: the last shape of a long argument.

Distance is a definite feature. There are presences—towers and cranes, the capped throats of sumps, a few sentinel poles with their ceramic hats—but they do not crowd. Between them is openness, maybe a kilometer at a go, then a scattering of smaller things: a foam of grating underfoot where the drainage is most active, a field of dimpled plates engineered to bite water and slow its run to spillways. At strange long intervals there are drop shafts, curtained with linked chains instead of rails. Lean down and the sound at the core of the world lifts hair on your arms. They descend out of the darkness into a deeper one, where something moves very, very slowly.

In other places, it rains. Here, it condenses. The ceiling of weather is not sky but an arrangement of baffles and condensers far above, unseen except when they cough out silver in a long sigh, sequencing like lightning that makes no light, only pressure and sound: a marching-band of drips that travels from sector to sector and back again, chasing heat. You begin to map it. There is rhythm big as a day, bigger than a week—after which time, perhaps, in some other continent of this continuous machine, they reverse. In the lull between major pours, a mist lingers, and the materials of the plain acquire a fur of visibility. Ribs show out under the skin of everything, and you see how much of this country was meant to flex. A whole valley of plates moves like a diaphragm under the weight of retained water, then empties, and relaxes by a centimeter. When nothing breaks for an age, wear becomes a tenderness.

There are no creatures to surprise you. Nothing scrabbles in your peripheral sense. No frogs, though there are pools. No birds, though there are altitudes. Sometimes a shape wheels silently in the mist above—a maintenance linear with rotors muffled to function only within the noise envelope of the place— but if those fly, they are as present as a remembered machine: you see the slipstream, the hurried rearrangement of water on steel, but there is no body to thank for it. In the absence of animals, the senses recalibrate. You convince yourself that the tiny chattering is not insects but thermal growth and shrink in the stretched lattice of grating, far-wandering click-echoes translating along steel.

It is cold enough that exhalations do not vanish; they spread and slick like the weather’s own breath, though perhaps that is because the air is so near to saturation that it cannot be asked to carry anything else. Smells hang. Ozone has a library here. The oil-smell warms and cools with the machinery’s temper. There are other odors rare as holiday: hot dust somewhere arid in the superstructure when a long-idle relay wakes; the chilly metallic bouquet of freshly shaved iron when erosion wins a fraction back and a burr is born where light will never find it. If there is a culture here, it is olfactory and acoustic. Shapes are a close third.

Underfoot there are rules. Step in the dimples and you will not slide; step on the slick and you will. The plates with a dull grain are strong; the plates with a faint hammered ring are bolted over hollows and will flex and make you nauseous if you forget. Between the boltheads there are drainage ribs. Never put a foot where a pattern is too symmetrical unless you mean to go down. There are things that open on schedules, and they do not care about curiosity. In sections where hazard lamps are out, tiny runnels of luminous bacteria make constellations around the mouths of pipes. Do not trust them to tell you anything but the presence of wet.

Because this is not merely a place but an instrument. The water that makes up so much of it—condensing, weeping, flowing—is not weather by accident but by design. It exists to be lured out of the air, to be tamed, cleansed, recirculated, and persuaded toward tasks you cannot see from here, tasks that may be as simple as existing as a resource for a region that long ago decided its underground storages were at capacity and its skies were forgetting how to make rain. This sump-plain is the factory floor of weather capture. The noise speaks to that aim. Every deafening period is a cycle’s climax: pumps driving feed to membranes, separators thrumming as they sort ions from ions, turbines harvesting the waste pressure and turning it into cold, electric urgency that races away into distances only to return as a more gentle breath. Then, in the lulls, the field relaxes, the water that did not submit on the first pass finds sanctuary in microbasins and bleeds into another day’s opportunity. Erosion here is not destruction but accommodation. Everything is worn to do its job easier. The wear is an intelligence.

And yet the place is not entirely rational. It has its corners of strangeness without intent. In a long trough where the water for once seems bored, ripples stand unmoving under the strobes, a permanent comb of interference because the transverse vibrations of the plain have their own tides. In a far lot the rain cars—once meant to be mobile collectors, now sunk to axles in their own captured load—make a little archipelago, each a dark hump with a shallow lake inside. When pumps flush the field, the archipelago rings with different tones based on how much rib remains under each; a geology of absences, a music map. Sometimes in that clamor, a whistle that is not a whistle rides the rush, a resonance of a pipe long since closed but still tuned, and for a second you believe a bird is here, or a person. It is only the machine discovering, once a day, that it can sing.

Towards what might have been the edge of this region is a weir of a different metaphor: a vertical face of stacked heat exchangers, the bottom tiers choked into muzzles by mats of calcified minerals like petrified kelp. They bleed slowly, viscously. Once they would have steamed in hot weather; now the regimen runs so cold the steam is a rumor. Beyond them, maybe, an end to this plain and a start of ducts big as ravines. Or else it continues in another dialect and in fuller darkness. The idea of outside is unsure here. Everything’s scale is so complete that nothing seems set against a boundary. Even the sky, that latent ceiling of baffles, does not give you a distance.

When you kneel to the water, and you can if you like—it is clean in its fashion, not a disease water but a working one—you see how it has helped to scour the built into almost-natural. In the constant dripping, a dent becomes a bowl becomes a basin becomes a pond. In the pond, a skin forms; later, a seed of dust anchors in that skin, and something chemical and greedy mounts up from it, mere molecules thick but long-lived. In time, it catches more dust. It is all surface-work, slow and insistent, nothing like an animal’s appetite and yet fully its own striving. You will not look up and have a bat pass too close to your ear. You will not startle a rodent from the shadow of a flange. You will watch water teaching metal languages it never intended to learn, and this will be enough.

There is an etiquette, a few simple laws that the place imposes even without a posted sign to enforce them:

- Walk with the rhythms, not against. The pumps have their hours and the floodgates theirs, and the best path is always downwind of the breath and upslope of the current.

- Trust the duller shine. Polished metal in darkness is a lie. The worn, matte surfaces are the true roads.

- Wait for the hush, if you need to hear your own body. They come. Briefly, but they come, and in them your pulse explains something to you about how far you are from animals and how near you are to engines pretending at weather.

- Be small. There is no heroism here. The scale is kind, but it is not for you.

If you give it hours, the plain offers evolutions. In one stretch the drainage field has clogged and turned into a salt meadow of no plants, only crystalline bushes in fever dreams of geometry, glittering if any light dares them. In another, the dimpled plates were lifted and set down again when a subsurface diaphragm rose under too much head, and now they lie at angles like pages insincerely turned, making a culvert forest. All of it patient; all of it ancient. The few quick things are the smallest—droplets tearing free, a switch finding closure in a relay box with a faint blue lick, your own breath.

Once, in what passes for dawn here—a load-shedding that leads to a flicker of lessened noise—there is a sound that is not like the others, a distant chain rattling an answer to something without question. After, a new waterline appears on a tall weeping tower, an annulus lighter than the rest. The place marks its days that way. Without fauna to count, without other lives to call time into being, it measures by its own patient catastrophes and their repairs. It may be a long while since anyone came to do anything deliberate; maintenance is as automated as desire. But the region continues, open as far as modest light will allow you to imagine it, convincing and content in its end-of-life shape.

If you leave—if you pick a vector along the safe plates and thread between the towers and walk until your mind says you have crossed a distance—it will not be an exit. It will be a translation. The roar will be a little different. The cool will fetch into the sleeves of your shirt a new perfume—manganese instead of iron; a sugar of antifreeze you cannot quite place. The darkness will change its grain. The water will still weep. The machine will still breathe. And you will begin again with your hands learning this other version of ground, and your ear paring apart these other braids of sound, and perhaps a single hazard light will gain your trust by repeating its tired lantern-pulse, and you will say to yourself that this is river country after all, even if the river is made and is making you in turn.
