Begin with height. The place is mostly vertical: a city slung along the faces of a continent-wide rift, a thousand kilometers of cliff peeled back from itself in slabs and ledges, everything terraced and ribbed and ribbed again until the eye gives up trying to count. The air is thin enough that breath feels like folding paper, cool and dry as ash, and for hours the only sound is the shift of fine grains finding a lower balance. The sun here is a dim coin that spends most of the day behind the stone lip, and when it finally drifts into view it drags with it a soft, oblique light that turns the entire chasm into a map of shadows.

From above, the city looks lost — a skein of pale galleries, balconies, stairs, and vaults scratched into the cliff face and grown outward in shells and buttresses until they touch their own shadows. Each balcony holds another city bound underneath it, and another beneath that. The materials are what the place had at hand: salt-porcelain that sweated out of the stone where the cliff remembers an evaporated sea, black glass that froze mid-spill when an old eruption threw a river against gravity and it stayed, porous mineral foams that grew around dust-threads, and the long white arcs of something that was once alive and very large, fossilized to bone-ceramic and repurposed into spans and ribs. Erosion is everywhere and patient: corners have become curves, curves have become lines, lines have been carved into soft filigree by a wind that must have blown for millennia and still has not run out of patience. Even the built arches look weathered into belonging, like they were inevitable, not made.

There is no water. That absence has a taste: chalk and the faintest kiss of halogen on the tongue. There are seasons of dry frost that are not ice at all: pale rimes of ammonium salts creep out of cracks at dusk, sublimate at noon, and leave behind crusts that crunch like sugar underfoot. Sometimes a fallwind brings a slow fog of inert gas up the rift, a ghostly tide that highers the pressure by a fractional breath. The fog glows faintly where fields are strong; it condenses not into droplets but into prismatic films and brittle flowers that snap in silence. When it passes, the air returns to its near-void clarity and it is as if no weather had ever bothered with you at all.

Vegetation, if the term can stretch that far, lives as lace and skin on stone — pallid mineral webs that knit across faces and into fissures, growing where a seam hums with field or holds a trickle of ionic seep. They are not green. They are the colors of salts and smoke: milky, faintly violet, oily amber. They feed on differences: between shade and almost-shade, between standing air and sliding air, between the edge and what the edge divides. Touch one and it twins your fingerprint in crystal. Leave it and it will make slow accretions of shelter: a lip here to lap a rare fog, a fin there to harvest the slight push of thin winds, a cup that funnels grit exactly where it is wanted. Every season the lace is scoured away on an exposed face and regrows in the lee, not so much living on the cliff as teaching the cliff a new grammar of surface.

Animals — if that term fits — appear as punctuation. A drift of skeins glides the abyssal air like bits of paper scissored into trapezoids, riding static steps that rise along the cliff in invisible ladders. They eat charged dust and the mineral lace itself, leaving clean-edged patches like fields after harvest. A long-bodied thing, almost flat, may tilt from under a ledge and fall without falling, spiraling down a helical draft until it vanishes into blue nothing. At noon a rattle of pebbles on a stoop might prove to be a clutch of edge-gnats folding and unfolding themselves along a line, surviving on the math that edges do when sunlight touches them. Once you will glimpse a tall shape on stilted limbs pick its way across a facet of glass like a careful thought, pause with its head turned toward a sound your ears did not detect, and then clack away until it finds a seam and folds into it. There are predators and prey here, but the game runs along vectors not ours — a dance of charge and alignment and where an angle becomes a mouth.

The city itself is quiet because sound does not travel far. Shouted words disperse into the canyon and are devoured by volume; that which vibrates fast loses its nerve in such thin air. Most noises are local: the hush of a sand-sheet letting go and settling backward like breath; the clicking of cooled glass adjusting to morning; the close whisper of your own clothing. When the fallwind comes you hear it as a suggestion in your joints before you hear it with your ears. Wind-harps have been carved into many vaults and bridges not as instruments but as organs; they speak the wind’s arrival and its flavor in notes no throat could sing. Bone-strung charms hang where currents have always passed, not to ward away, but to remind a passerby that there is a flow here even if you cannot hear it until it has already touched you.

No engines run and no lamps burn. The built forms are ancient and cunning without being technological. Doors have no hinges; they are slots where a slab of salt-porcelain might slide if the week’s weather goes that way. Stairways decide whether they go up or down depending on the day’s gravity. Handholds are carved in patterns that teach your fingers new instincts so you can cross ledges that do not always agree to be present. Infrastructure is thick the way fungus is thick in a dead log: everywhere, integral, seemingly natural. Airwells make a living of pressure differences; everything that could channel a draft does. In the deeper, dimmer galleries, microtomes of light are drawn from field-lines that run along the bones of the cliff; they are not lamps but a harvest of direction. The city is layered, loops within loops, burned into the cliff’s memory so deep that even if every stone were razed the place would still recall where people once walked and rebuild those averages in dust.

Reality has rules here that are not the flat kind. Edges matter. They gather force, they propagate, they seek other edges. Two edges in proximity will make a third that did not exist before. There is a threshold at which an edge becomes a path, and a narrower threshold at which it becomes a fall. Distance is a variable but not a liar: stand at a parapet and that tower you see across may be a city-hour away one day and three days the next; if you begin toward it it will be there when you arrive, but the trail you took will not be the same trail you left behind. Gravity has weather. The late-afternoon slump pulls you deeper into stone so your shoulders ache; at midnight there is a drift, sideways and slow, that makes curtains sway and stairs tilt their allegiances. Some shadows are not the absence of light but memories of where a line has been; walk into one and it keeps a little of you, enough that later when someone leans there they will feel your lean and wonder if the stone misses you.

There are rules that people who lived — or still live, rarely, almost mythically — built into the place:

- Things with names are heavier. The namers carved their words in hidden places and used this to anchor bridges across spans that would otherwise deny ropes their usual comfort. A named span endures; an unnamed one scours itself away as if shy of sky.

- Across any true fall there is a chance to cross sideways. The old walkways often turn to ledges that seem too narrow to bear your footfall; if you commit, your step is denser than it should be, and the cliff accepts you. Hesitation lightens you and you will be refused. This is written in blunt tool marks beside many a gap.

- Light falls, eventually. In this canyon light accumulates in fractures as something slow and heavy; some nights you find a pool that was dark the day before lit from within as if by slow glow-worms. Watch where you leave your shadow; by morning it will have slid three ledges down and it won’t know you when you return.

- Heat forgets. Warmth rises like a thought and becomes a different thought; it condenses as a brittle frost that gives a fine smoke when cracked. The city uses it not as energy but as a record: you can read a day’s weather in the pattern of its forgettings across a glazed lintel.

None of this is contrived by machines. It is an urbanism that has accreted from the conversation between matter and this place’s temper. It is organized: corridors that always find the easiest gradient, plazas that are technically shelves but feel like squares, courts that open fingers of space into the void until you are standing at the fork of a canyon inside the larger canyon. People — if you meet them — move like someone who has practiced being a part of falling. They wear skins and filaments that turn wind into small graspable tugs. They herd the lace into useful forms with notched stones and patience. They raise children in rooms that find their own center of gravity and sing a family’s names into the beams to keep the ceiling eager to be ceiling. Their tools are bone, glass, salt, woven mycelia; their calendars are the leaning of ladders.

Erosion has not been an enemy. It is the carpenter no one can afford, but everyone knows. The cliff-face has been passively reworked by its long, sly winds until it speaks a language of scallops and vortices and tongues. Places that were once sharp are rounded, places that were once blank have been teased into useful texture. The ancient bones that were once just bones now hold terraces, stairs, cisterns for inert fog. A band of glass that once cut the cliff like a scar has been pecked into a ribbon of windows; their edges glitter with an internal light that is not quite color. You can read the age of surfaces with your hand. The oldest places feel patient. Newer ones are smoother, less persuasive — they sometimes refuse to exist on days when the sideways gravity is strong.

The dynamic lives in cycles that feel like breath measured by geology. Mornings the wind rises out of the abyss in a sigh that lasts all the way to noon. It carries dust specks that tumble like planets in an orrery, each with an orbit around an edge or a bone. The mineral lace opens its small mouths and clacks its little plates in the current, then closes as it thins. Some afternoons the gravity drifts and a rain begins; it is not drops, but a fall of fine splinters of field-frozen glass that hiss when they strike a warm hand but do not melt. Nights are charged. There is lightning, but not from cloud to cloud: it crawls along strata as if following some old seam where ore remembers being liquid. It takes an hour to cross a wall and leaves behind a thin trail of fused powder that creatures will later lick for metals.

Watch long enough and you will see motion that your first arrival could not have predicted. A staircase will not be where it was yesterday; instead there will be a gentle slope where sliding on your side is safer than walking. The bridge you thought impossible spans a gap because the city angled itself overnight to make that less impossible, and someone named both ends. A tide of shadow will push up the wall at dusk and fish made of edges will swim in it, feeding on reflections. A herder with a pack of little gliders will send them out; they will return fattier with charge, and he will stroke them to bleed that charge into a pit where it becomes a useful pressure difference. All of this is ordinary here and would be impossible somewhere else.

The emptiness is part of the place’s wealth. There is space between things, and the things that are present take that space seriously. Distance is a kind of neighbor. You can watch a person on a balcony a kilometer across and know from the slow flick of their garments which wind is there. You can hear a pebble click on a stone thirty meters away and feel the story of its fall in your knees. When someone walks past you on a narrow ledge, you both become heavier so the ledge feels safe. When no one has crossed a place in a long while, it becomes insubstantial and you must teach it your weight again by standing still and letting your names sink in.

Dimness suits the place. The sun is seldom direct. Most light is borrowed: pale wash from the opposing wall, soft scatter from suspended dust, the lambent fluorescence of mineral seams that have learned to drink the drift of fields. At night, stars are too many and too sharp. They burn down into the rift as if looking for the bottom, and sometimes you will think you see them reflected below, but there is no water there for such a trick. There is only depth and the slow, formal weather of a gravity that is not in a hurry to make up its mind.

And underlying everything, the sense that the world here is not broken so much as different: a logic of edges and patience, of slightly sideways laws that make a city possible where a cliff would otherwise be all cliff and nothing else. Step carefully. Learn to name what you need to keep. Accept that some days directions are opinions. Do not try to fill the emptiness; it is not a lack. It is the volume in which this place thinks.
