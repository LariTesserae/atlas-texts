The first thing is the sound. It comes through the bones before the ears: a long, tireless shudder, like surf, like wind in caves, like huge fabric being drawn over stone. It is rain heard from the underside—the world’s ceiling hammered by an endless downpour—and the noise pours down along every frond and mast of plant, throbbing the water itself. It never stops. It’s not violent, not storm; it’s continuity, the way a river is continuous. Loud, big, patient.

You are high here. If there were air you would be among clouds, on a ridge that once broke the sky. But the sky is inside the world now. The ridge and its torn shoulders are entirely submerged, and the valley’s far slopes disappear into green light. The pressure is mild, the heat leached—cool water that keeps its clarity. When the sun leans high, it throws normal daylight down through a ceiling alive with rain. Seen from below, the surface is a tremulous mirror studded with white needles. Sun makes the water pale jade; shade turns it blue glass. Visibility runs far, because the plants clear it—breathing out long chains of silver microbubbles that slide up the slick skin of their own bodies.

The terrain is rock, but you almost never touch bare stone. Every plane and blade and horn of the ridge is sleeved in plant. Sumped gullies hold a peat of pressed stems; flattish ledges carry whole swards of sponge-moss; chipped faces are furred with ribbon-lichens; the high spires are seized by strap-kelp. The dominant growths are columnar: tree-tall water-ferns and bladdersome kelp that root where they can and rise as if remembering wind. They buttress each other into long naves and clerestories of living green. Between them, there is a lot of room—clear avenues of water like streets through a sparse city, with huge presences set far apart. The scale is intimate but grand, the way a cathedral does grand on a human body’s terms: you can cross a plaza in a minute, but it still feels like inside-outurance.

Some of this spacing is nature; some is made. The settlement—because there is one, and it has been here for a long while—keeps the forest open with pruning and tension. They bind fronds to stone rings, they arch two columns together and stitch their living tissues with fibrous pegs so they heal into vaults. They carve nothing, drill nothing; there are no metals here and never were. What they build, they persuade. The oldest halls are plants convinced decades ago to accept another shape. Ribs of kelp become flying buttresses, the flexible kind. Fans of water-fern are taught to flatten and pleat into canopies. Nets of vine are braided across empty air, then allowed to root into each other until the net becomes a beam. The city inhabits this scaffold: platforms of woven leaf-thatch, ropeways that ride the neutral buoyancy of their own bladders, rooms membrane-thin whose walls are living skin. To descend or ascend is to let a counterweight float up, to ride a cradle balanced by a bladder, to hook yourself to a patient, green lift.

Everywhere are the signs of compromise with the element. There are no fires, so heat and light are the slow work of the day and the sun. Tools are stone rubbed flat and bone shaved thin, shell scrapers, jawbones for saws. Needles are made from the beaks of big snails; twine is spun from stripped plant fibers. Adhesive is sap. When they cut, they do it where a plant can regrow. When they harvest, they leave the core intact. Craft is knowing how a living thing will forgive you.

The world feels established. The stone under all this is old, planed smooth by a time before the flood when wind and ice had their say. That planing still speaks through the plant. The kelp knows to lean with it, and the channels between are the same places water has preferred for generations. Someone shows you a tunnel trimmed of leaves that has been there since their grandparents were children, and you can see how the edges have thickened, the living wall bulging inward like a muscle that has learned its task. There is history here, but most of it is not written—it is grown. Boundaries between households are hedges of trained vine; the law is a map of pruning rights.

Light sifts down in rippled lattices, dims and brightens with the heave of the rain above. It is normal enough that colors hold: the fluorescent yellow of a weed-flower trembling in a current; the red-brown of tannin seep from some bruise on the ridge; the blue of a child’s shell-bangle. In the dusk the water sometimes has its own ideas: plankton bloom and make your hands spark, the torque of a fish is a comet-stroke, a lifting arm leaves smears of stars. But most hours, light is the honest green of plant and the honest gray of stone and the honest silver of air going upwards in pearls.

This city is not large. It is a district in a living forest, a belt of order along a saddle and over a couple of knuckles of rock. You can swim its length in half an hour if you don’t stop. Paths are groomed through the columns, and there is a commons where the space opens grandly and everyone comes to trade. There, a web of living roots strung between four trunks has been grown into a table broad as a room, and it is set with things the water has given up: seedpods as bowls, polished stones with holes as pendants, the skins of long-fruited weeds made into satchels. The stalls are hardly stalls; they are pockets in the plant, cradles braided out from trunks, with their own gentle eddies. There is an order and a density to it—lanes, courtyards, a schedule of when certain channels flow fastest for moving goods, a place where fibers are rinsed, another where they are threshed on weirs of reed—but there’s also a lot of space between. Neighbors are so many body-lengths apart you must swim to borrow a cup of pulses.

Fauna are everywhere. Big schools move through the aisles of the forest the way flocks move between trees. There are grazers like cows but lengthened, their mouths full of tidy combs for shaving algae without taking the root. There are ribbons that are fish and also not; they loop their bodies into knots and push themselves like slow knots in a rope. There are tentacled browsers with the patience of librarians, lifting one leaf after another to examine it, to pick the mites and drop the rest. A sort of eel lives coiled in the buttresses of the oldest halls like a watch-chain. Juvenile things hide in the hairier plants—a fuzz of tiny shrimps exploding like seeds when a fin brushes them. Now and then something large and deliberate passes the edge of your sight, an old one of the wide fluked kind who go from ridge to ridge singing at frequencies whole halls lean into.

This abundance has rhythms, and most of them are slow. There are hatches that flood the water for an afternoon with a fine brown snow of eggs; then for weeks it’s quiet again. The herds of grazers come on a two-week beat that every household feels in their larder. The biggest plants put on inches a year and throw out a new flamboyant leaf when they feel the year turn; people celebrate it with dances of steady, economical loops. What is fast here seems ornamental: the startle of a minnow, the switch of a tail. The rest of it—a house’s growth, a corridor’s maintenance, the way the rain changes from a hard seethe to a whisper and back over a season—is measured in the language of patience. When someone says “soon,” they may mean “after the fan-ferns make two new rungs.”

Sound isn’t just weather. It is infrastructure. The city tunes itself in it. Flutes grown from hollow stems stand in rows, their mouths facing into a current that is always there, always about this strong; they sing when eddies increase, or when a storm bulks at the surface and the shelf currents go stiff, and the pitch tells you whether to tighten the lashings on the west moorings. There are shell-chimes but they do not hang; they are strung along floors and corridors so the vibration wakes sleepers. Voices get swallowed unless you put your mouth close. So people drum—gentle sticks on grown tympana, stretched wet skins that never dry, the heel of a hand on a trumpet-pore plant that answers in a bell note. They sign with hands and with the whole body, tradition encoded in the ways fingers are held taut or slack. And every built thing is also a baffle; halls turn and narrow to break the roar, or open wide so the thunder can run through and out.

Rules of this place have grown from rules of the medium:
- Buoyancy is architecture. A roof is a bladder, a stair is a rope pulled just heavier than the water wants it, a gate is made of finespun reed that swings because it is almost balancing itself.
- Property is pruning. You can harvest the free-shoots in the commons, but not the backgrowth rooting on a neighbor’s anchor; you may shape a branch that enters your space, but only up to the line where it cores back into theirs.
- Heat is effort. You eat warmth in the oils of seedpods and the fat of slow animals. The only flames are in stories.
- Time is flow. There are calendars of migration and algae bloom; there are the loud days when rain is dense enough that the whole ceiling goes white and the dark days when mist dampens the hammering and the water thins, and everyone brews and mends.
- Noise is warning, comfort, law. To go silent in a grown hall when the overworld is howling is to invite someone to check on you. Silence is never emptiness; it’s a sign.

Elevation shows itself in the behaviors of weather. Some days, thunder walks overhead as a palpable footfall; the ridgeline still shapes the clouds it no longer breaks. Lightning flares and the water briefly glows in sheets and shadows. But there is no cataclysm; this is not a place of violence. It is a place long seated in its own form, where erosion is held under leaves and the worst it does is carry silt into the low, where it will fetch up in basins like sleeping tablets, where new plant will colonize that bed and make of it another floor.

There are hazards, but they, too, are slow. A rope that is too old will speak to you weeks before it fails. A house that is starved of light will go pale and papery in a way that has a name. Predators exist, and they are large and quiet, and everyone knows to leave certain lanes open so the big ones can pass, and to add chaff-screens to faster passages so small, desirable things are not blown down throats. The city is well placed, high enough that turbid flood rarely rises this far with silt, low enough under the rain that storms above don’t tear it, tucked where the ridge calms currents to a regularity you can build calendars against.

At the edge of the settlement, where the pillars thin and the water opens into the long view, the ridge drops away into a blue so rich it feels like a wall. Out there, space is a presence—it pushes. The built things end, but the plants keep going, less trained and more themselves, steadily climbing the light like a staircase. A migrating shoal passes far off, a dark shifting geometry, and the halls echo with their distant rip. In the near water, a child breathes a neat silver trail out of a reed mask while they watch for a particular snail that only grazes on the shadowed rim of northern trunks. Someone, mother or uncle, waits with a basket of woven plant. The plant is still wet from being a leaf; it will be wet until it becomes a different room.

Everything is submerged, and everything is plant, and everything is alive; most of it changes very slowly. And the place holds together because it has been held together for long enough that the holding has become another kind of nature. The rain roars, the sound is a law, and even the big animals keep to the rules of these streets. If you stay long enough, you learn to hear the subtle under the loud, how a certain hum means felting season in the west hall, how a certain slackness in the roar means the surface is glassing and the jellyfish will come. You learn that the high places are not always dry, and that elevation is a kind of patience, and that a city can be made by telling living things a story about the shape they might prefer to take, and then listening as they answer, in the slow grammar of growth.
