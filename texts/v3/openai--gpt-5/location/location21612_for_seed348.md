You hear it first, from far across the flats: a continuous mechanical hymn that seems to have been playing long before you were born, and will continue long after you leave. Not a song with verses—there are no crescendos, no breaks—just a layered drone of engines and resonant plates that folds the air into ridges and hollows. It is loud enough that your chest becomes another sounding board. The tone does not waver. You begin to feel it more than you listen.

The ground tilts almost imperceptibly downward and delivers you into a basin so shallow it could be mistaken for a misremembered horizon. Low elevation, but not a depression of sorrow—just a catchment for mist and wandering waters. Here, cool air lingers. A night’s breath will bead on everything that can hold a cold edge, and even in day the metal sweats. Occasionally a frank rain comes through, but water mostly prefers to appear by slow insistence: as condensation, as a weeping line along a seam, a slick inside the lip of a groove.

It is an urban place without density, a town stretched thin like netting. Structures stand with intervals between them, giving the whole yard a sense of steadiness, of space between presences. A handful of long sheds, each with slatted roofs that read the sky; a few high towers that resemble organ pipes; a ring of squat substations with their iron fences; a grid of open channels that guide trickles to sump pits; a causeway threading all of it—gangways, catwalks, a lattice raised a man's height off the ground so anything that belongs down there may pass without contest. Cables hang in shallow catenaries, pipes arc and disappear into the earth, and there is room between every thing, as if the place had been arranged to allow it to breathe.

The materials are patient and ordinary: poured concrete gone smooth under years of tiny feet, riveted sheet steel now a dull swarm of spots where condensation and salt have gnawed away paint, basalt blocks that were laid long ago and now wear the rounded polish that comes only from constant abrasion. Where water stands after a shower, the basin bottoms show a pale orange film, and the drain grates shine like coins from the constant passage over them. Any edge exposed to air and moisture grows a rime—mineral bloom, powder-fine—if it can stay out of reach. Otherwise, it is licked clean.

There are no plants here, not as you know them. No blades of grass wedge the cracks, no vines climb. Instead there are skins: bacterial sheens that pull rainbow from the smallest wet, wafer-thin films that creep only to the limits of a channeled field before halting as if by law. In the sheltered corners of abandoned equipment, you might find a papery fungus that seems uncertain about its own existence, like wet lace that dried and then regretted it. Mostly, though, life is animal. The place belongs to the animals as a city belongs to traffic.

They come in numbers too great for counting and in castes that make their own sense. They are not pretty, but they are not monstrous either. Think of mouths more than eyes. Think of undersides better understood than backs. The small ones—thumb-length, beetle-shaped, in dull aluminum and charcoal grays—flow like spillages wherever moisture beads. Their legs are delicate and many, and when they all move together the sound is like cloth rubbed briskly between two hands. They graze the wet mineral bloom and take the sheen down to clean metal and stone, and in doing so expose the next thing that will bead. Behind them come scullers, palm-sized with damp-padded bellies, that smear and wick, evening the lines left by the first pass. There are worm things as thin as a stitch and as long as your forearm that sew themselves along seams and sleep there until a pulse of sound from the plates wakes them; then they contract and wring water down to the gutters.

And there are the larger animals, the dominant presence in the moment you see them: slow, layered, carapaced creatures that look pressed from paper pulp and oiled—each the size of a stool. They move with a grave purpose, in lanes that you would swear were painted if you did not know they formed and reformed according to cues you cannot hear. They are called bellies by the workers, not because that is their name but because they are bellies: everything they do is in service of taking in the damp-laden film and giving it back as pellets, as plates, as exudates that are the feedstock for whatever in those sheds needs it. They leave behind pellets the size of fingernails that look like chalk but feel like soapstone, each one dense with salts teased from fog.

The loudness has purpose. The towers are not merely towers. They are condensers and instruments both. Their flutes, louvers, and vanes are tuned to the daily shape of sky and the nightly delta between ground and stars. Heat radiates away into the dark and water comes out of air. At the same time, the towers vibrate—their skins a field of inaudible trembling overlaid with the audible drone that fills the yard. Sound is a fence here, a net you cannot see but the animals obey with their whole bodies. Plates set into the concrete hum at fixed tones; suspended beams thrum like the lowest strings of an unplayable instrument. Between them are null lanes and pressure ridges mapped not with paint but with harmonics. Animals keep to their assigned paths because their bones and bellies prefer it. The tone that corrals them never changes. You have stepped into a ranch whose fences have no posts.

People? Yes. There are people—but they keep small. They wear thick boots and walked ways serve them; crossing the yard at ground level is against sense and practice. From catwalks they shepherd by signal and by shadow. They adjust baffles on mufflers, close and open shutters on condenser faces, and watch the scrawl of outputs and inputs on black plates that shine faintly with numbers. Their machines are industrial: dynamoes that steal the slack from turning masses, mills that press pellets into bricks, vats where exudates fatten into soft plates for later curing. Electricity snakes underfoot; switches chun-clack and arc in their boxes when thrown. The town’s few dwellings are along the basin's rim: tidy, low, with their own shields against the animals and the wet. Between the houses are clear lanes patrolled by a kind of watch-bird that is not a bird at all—a long-boned, two-legged thing with sail-like ears, whose only purpose is to radiate a local quietus tone that keeps the small grazers from mistaking a stoop for a condensate trap.

Weather happens in a narrow band of variety—cool, mostly. Days are bright to the eye but the heat is always elsewhere; nights are darker but not colder enough to bite. Fog visits often, drawn by the basin's hypnosis. True storms are rare and of short duration. When rain does commit, it is as if the whole place has been waiting; water runs instantly where it is taught to run, and the animals respond like ink released into clear water. Their lanes thicken, split, and braid, but the pattern is habit, not panic. The drone deepens, a few shutters close according to the season's script, and the elongated worms wake in their seams to do their silver work—the channels draw down. After, the racks in the sheds will be hung with tonight’s harvest sheets, and a quiet pride will move along the catwalks—though the sound never gives them away.

Erosion is not an enemy here; it is a partner. The yard has existed long enough for every edge to have found its fate. Stone is burnished to a dull shine, concrete sags into smoothness over its rebar skeleton, rails are bowed where animals have learned to use the shade. Drainage lines are notched deeper year by year by teeth, by worms, by thousands of soft bellies that pull water into motion. The place is settled in itself, long past its adolescence of surprises. When something breaks, it breaks into a form the yard already knows. Some older machines are kept not for efficiency but because the animals know their note and a replacement could teach calamity.

Smells are metallic and clean: ozone in an aftertaste, wet iron pricked through with the faintest sweetness of whatever those exudates hold. If there’s rot, it belongs to nothing vegetal; it is the smell of trapped water complaining as it is finally pulled free.

Rules of the world here are simple and absolute:
- Sound is architecture. It marks paths, makes walls, writes laws on the air. The system has played for so long that the animals were born into it; changing a tone is an act of governance and is done rarely, with ceremony.
- Moisture is currency. You do not squander a drip. Condensation surfaces are sacred in their way. Everyone knows which faces are "theirs" and which are set for common pull.
- Surfaces are alive while wet. Anything left on the ground will be cleaned, reduced, rearranged. Tools are hung, not set down.
- Nothing green is expected to survive. If a seed arrives from the river beyond the low lip, it will not thrive past its own uncertainty. Anything that photosynthesizes does it microscopically, in films you may not notice until a plate hums and a grazer finds them and ends their experiment.
- The herd is the machine, and the machine is the herd. The factory harvests what the animals excrete, harden, and arrange; the animals need the factory’s shapes and tones to remain themselves. There is no “wild” anymore, not within the basin. Outside, perhaps, in the river’s reedless mudflats that steam a little under a gray sky—but that is outside.

Light is ordinary and therefore treasured. In the morning the yard’s shadows are long and full of grain, in the afternoon they contract to thin overlays like gauze. Electric lamps burn only where work sets its anchors: above a bench, over a hatch. At night, a dull dome of light lives over the basin, not bright, not dim, scaled to hands and eyes that need to see without startling a flock of bellies into a question.

Strangeness grows on you. At first it is the animals and the song. Later, you discover the place holds stillness inside its loudness: the never-changing equilibrium that makes loudness a landscape feature, not a mood. Hours pass and you swear you are in the same moment of the same day. The animals are always on the move and yet always in the same configuration. The condensers drip at the same rate, the hum the same pitch. The only clock that feels true is your own: sweat underarms drying and returning, the shift of weight from foot to foot, the ache when you lean too long against the rail and the rail’s minute shiver becomes your own.

Beyond the low rim, a slow river works in its own time through flats of silt that should be reeds but are merely furrows and ridges, like a plowed field whose furrows hold silver water. The basin does not court it; there is a polite channel that accepts occasional overflows, but they meet rarely. In the town’s maps, this is a single-feature world with a boundary: within, the Song Yard; without, the flats and the river and the horizon. The scale feels close, almost cathedralesque—echoing but contained. You could walk its perimeter in a short morning and understand none of its depth.

In the sheds, pressed bricks pack onto pallets. Workers in oilcloth stack them methodically. The bricks are compacted from the bellies’ pellets and will be used in distant places you will not see: filters, capacitors, catalysts in vats where something crucial depends on their exact blend of salts. It is not a glamorous export, but it is stable, dependable, like the tone that made it.

Stand here long enough and the place begins to let you in on something it holds: the knowledge that not all stability is stasis. The Song Yard is never changing in the way a pulse is never changing; it is change held as a note, motion braided so fine that it can be laid like cloth and unrolled again tomorrow and the day after. In the age of forgetfulness outside, someone chose a basin and taught it this. Now the animals teach their young, and the condensers teach the sky, and the town teaches itself the patience to keep the fences humming. And the sound goes on, loud and even and oddly gentle, until you cannot tell where your listening ends and the place begins.
