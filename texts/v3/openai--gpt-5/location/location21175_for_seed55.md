Pressure first, then the cold, then the realization that the darkness is not empty. It is packed. Layer on layer of slow, soaked presence: planes and meshes and drooping gardens, weight and craft and growth, all held in the smothering patience of deep water.

You are low in the world—near the bottom of a basin that has expanded beyond itself into something continental, a depression stretched until it is almost a plain. Here the ocean has grown old. The chop and tantrum of young seas is absent; the water is the heavy-breathed quiet of a cathedral that has been shut for a thousand winters. Everything that can settle has settled. Everything that could be sharpened has been rounded until it no longer remembers the word “edge.”

When your eyes make something of the gloom they find surfaces everywhere. Not a natural bank, not a fluke of geology: nothing here is unshaped. The floor that is not a floor is a skin of fitted stones, slabs pressed to tessellate by their own millennia of burden. Between the slabs, grout lines are furred with a living nap, as if each seam exhaled and the exhalation took botanical form. Low walls lean and continue beyond seeing—kilometers of runnels and baffles and steps, centuries of simple decisions repeated until the repetition became continent. The geometry is not grand in the way of palaces, but the scale still humbles: benches and shallow cisterns, tiered terraces, channel after patient channel, everything just high enough to bend a flow, just wide enough to slow a current and make it drop what it carries.

There is no bare stone unclaimed now. Vegetation—not terrestrial trees or kelp with their sunlit bravado, but cold, dim-loving thalli and mats and skeins—has sheeted from every elevation. It is lush in the language of dim light: thickness, tenacity, a deep-pile rug of existence. Rust-colored lawns of iron-feeding films breath like patient lungs; pale veils of filamentous bacteria retrieve lacework from the seams, their strands clumped with the grit they have precipitated from dreamlike trickles below. From overhangs and culverts descend long curtains of ribbon-leaf that never learned to lift; they hang, they ripple minimally when a slow current says a word. Their cells keep company with microbe colonies that eat gradients instead of light: the gentle difference between one molecule and its neighbor is enough here.

Where the walls meet the world, the plantwork thickens into architecture of its own. Swags of bushy, calcifying growth bulge over lintels, catching the falling snow of mineral. Thick rinds have grown on wicker and rope until the rope has become stone on the outside, fiber darkly preserved within, like a bone with marrow. In the hollows of stairwells, in the bellies of drowned courtyards, bulbous nodules rise: layered accretions of mat on mat, the oldest in the middle flattened and gray, the newest on top olive and quick with the slow work of living. Nothing eats them; no fin edges in from the side, no mouth grazes. They are allowed to occupy any shape they can invent.

The built work told time once in hand-widths and tool marks. You can still read the method: holes where dowels went through, grooves polished by rope, the steady alternating pattern of small stones wedged against larger, the specific geometry a hand can repeat without measuring. There is brick here, fired in the old way—thin and scalloped, the surface pocked where straw charred out. There is plaster that has taken the memory of reeds and cordage, negative images of what braced it when it was soft. There are long, patient retaining meshes woven from sapling and cane that have fossilized into lime and silt and the hushed certainty of something that did what it meant to do for a very, very long time. Wherever the eye tries to find the wild, it finds plan instead, patient plan, repeated until it turned into landscape.

Currents, too, are shepherded. Giant stone vanes turn the water almost imperceptibly, breaking it, splitting it, making calm bays where the heavier grains can fall and the lightest films can cling. There are sluiceways that thread like cloisters through the mats, their ceilings low, their lintels barely a suggestion in the black. If you could rise and look from above—if “above” had meaning anymore—you would see an atlas of these controls, a continent of hydraulics built from pretense-less units. Not grand machines, no valves of bronze or teeth of iron, but arrangements of tone and texture and the courage of patient labor. Each one is a hand put to a job, then a thousand hands, then no hands at all, only their effects.

Sound is the hush of such an old place. The constant, low sibilance of water along stone is so continuous that it becomes a texture in your mouth rather than a thing you hear. Once in very long while, a wet sigh as a clot of vegetation lets go and slumps from one ledge to the next. A fizz like soda, but older, as gas works its way from a buried layer to freedom. The tiny chime of something brittle cracking as a filament calcifies itself past its own flexibility. Otherwise, the enormous quiet of massed cold.

The cold rules are the simple ones: no rot of the furious sort, nothing quickened by heat. Woodwaterlogged, is preserved as if for shrine use; leather still knows the shape it was cut in; cords remain cords. There are no borers, no clacking shrimps, no toothed tenacity; animals are absent from this language entirely. Decay is osmotic and chemical and leisurely; exchange passes through the mats like rumor. Without grazing, growth does not brace itself, does not learn to become wiry or fast. It becomes affluent instead. Where two surfaces face and are close, something green-brown will press to fill the interstice. Where a ledge catches the mineral snow, a cushion will form as surely as a dune will form in wind, and it will persist until its own thickening makes it slide in a slow avalanche down to the next patient flat.

Light is nearly nothing. A hand before your visor would be a hand-length of less-than-black. Now and then, something glows without fanfare: a postcard of smudged phosphor at the hinge of a mat where its chemistry changes; the faintest melancholic blue revealing the rim of a cistern; grains in a film that shine briefly when disturbed, more like a memory of lightning than the thing itself. But mostly the world has decided not to be seen. What matters here is touch, weight, and the play of currents you only learn by long familiarity.

Scale shows itself as repetition. The wall you lean against is the same as the wall an hour’s swim away. The channels repeat like stanzas. The same wedge marks tell you that this is part of a sentence that ran uninterrupted across a continent. A terrace ends only to begin again one step lower, then again, then again; even the word “valley” is too peaky, “plain” too bare; this is something else, a quelling of relief, a decision made early in history to smother drama under patience. It has its own weather: not cloud and rain, but layers of temperature that move across the architecture with the seasonless regularity of a metronome; a damp front of colder brine bringing clarity on one regime of flow, then the older, slightly warmer water reasserting itself, loosening more, blurring more, making the vegetation breathe differently. These are seasons here: not color, not birds, but shifts in viscosity and how the slow streams shear when nudged by the vanes.

If rules must be named, they are these:

- Nothing goes fast. Even collapse is a prolonged courtesy. A reed lattice sags by imperceptible distances over centuries until it finally touches the support in the next channel and becomes an arch. A garden releases its weight to a lower stair like a glacier learning a new valley.

- Everything is made to encourage something else to make. A small wall to make stillness. A stillness to make a mat. A mat to make a crust. A crust to make a shelf. A shelf to make a garden heavy enough to become a new wall.

- Without animals, shape is neither threat nor advertisement. Fronds grow wide and tender, mats broach into open space without fear of being trimmed. The bulk of plant-life is allowed to become, not become-with-conditions.

- Light is a rumor. Energy comes by trickle from stone and from the ocean's own ancient debts: slow seepage of salts, the tiny difference between two layers of water born at different poles.

- Sound is a topography, too. A blind swimmer would know the stone vanes by the far-off change in susurration. They would know the old kilns—now flooded—by the way their domes catch the current and hum.

Everywhere, tools as old as stories once repeated the same acts. They moved stone by lever and sled, made baskets of sapling, burned marsh plant and clay to make brick. They cut steps shallow to spread work across the day, across a life. They threshed the ocean with fences to settle the silt where they wanted, they made a world that was a sieve and a cloth and a loom for water. That has not changed. The world goes on being that loom. The threads now are leaf-ribbons and slime-films, the weft is the inexhaustible fine drift out of water's own long remembering, the weight that beats the cloth is gravity steadily on.

You could be a very long time down here and still not see it all. You would learn the character of certain channels the way you know a street by its shade; you would learn which mats shelter the warmest centimeters of water where you can rest stiff fingers; you would mark how an old retaining wall has canted just enough this decade that the cushion on its windward side has thinned to reveal the brick's straw texture. You would name the glow at the lip of the isobaric well and take comfort in seeing it, an event that means nothing more urgent than chemistry agreeing with itself in a known place.

And if you move far enough you might come upon the borders of this basin and discover that borders here are only changes in repetition. Terraces become ramps; ramps becomes flights of low, broad steps; steps give way to colonnades of posts now thick with lime and vegetation so that they are more like groves than structures. There is no elsewhere where the sea returns to some other, more natural self. This is the natural self now, old as anything gets, pressure-quiet, made until it forgot it was made.

Night is the wrong word, because night implies dawn. But there is a feeling like it: all of it cool and black and done with its tantrums, the world’s face given over to a patient practice. The cold is not hostile; it is a constraint beneath which craft and growth have learned to count by centuries. The dense quiet, the low elevation humility, the ancient wear of edges—these have been taken up as style. You float, you touch stone made by hands you will never meet, and it is like touching the inside of a long-kept vow.
