First, the pressure: a patient hand on every surface, on every body within. Water everywhere, warm enough to be a skin in its own right, folding around edges and pooling in hollows. It is dark—so dark that what little light there is must be brought with you, grown or coaxed, carried in tissues, lured out of bacteria in soft smears along the walls. The walls accept it and return only a muted gleam, the low shine of old bone and lime.

Everything you touch has been made.

You are high and you know it, not by the air thinned to nothing—there is no air—but by the yawning fall beyond the eastern mouth of the chamber. Through that mouth, the water opens onto a vertical drop that keeps going beyond what your eyes and lights can measure. The spire on which the chamber sits rises out of a black basin far below; every centimeter of that spire is work. No naked rock. No undecided seafloor. Plates have been placed by jaw and limb, cavities modeled by tongues, chewers, rasps. The chamber itself is a single room of cathedral scale, a vaulted throat assembled from hundreds upon hundreds of overlapping valves, ribs, and tiles. There are no right angles. Arches flow into buttresses, buttresses dissolve into thickened seams; the curves are rounded and inward-turning, an architecture trained by current and time to endure.

Age shows in the polish. The earlier makers cut sharper ridges, but the centuries have abraded their certainties into long, slow curves. The passage of water has planned this place like an artist planing wood. Joints are tight where they must be, packed with mineral glue and the sandy grit of broken shells; elsewhere, deliberate lattices leave fine sieve-windows through which the outside blackness can press its cheek. Here and there a hinge can be seen, the ligamentous remnant of something once living, stretched and cured into a soft gate. It yields to push and then rests again, the door a memory of muscle.

Within this room, the density of life erases the idea of emptiness. Threads of body and limb, shells near shells, soft forms nesting in corrugations. On the ceiling—if ceiling is the right word for the uppermost curve—colonies of pale builders cling in layered rings: plate-layers that extrude a steady bead of carbonate and fit it with slow precision into a tessellated skin. Their mouths are full of grit and wet glue. Their tools are teeth, scoops, horny scrapers; an occasional picked pebble passed along and pressed into service as a weight, a wedge, a simple press. No gleam of metal anywhere. Their craft is tactile, habitual, inherited. They do not dig; they accrete. They file edges so that the currents flow and do not tear. They scallop the buttresses so that slow warm upwells can rest here in gentle eddies, and the entire chamber breathes.

On the walls, bands of grazers comb pale films that have been seeded onto the stone-skin. This is husbandry of a sort, a different kind of garden: microbial sheets encouraged with careful smears of nutrient-laden paste, warmed by trickles from the pillar’s heat-catch. The heat is not a jet here, not a vent’s scream, but a long ache rising from far, far below, caught by spiral ducts within the pillar and released into cupped pockets. Feeding goes at the pace of growth. Mouths scrape, tiny hairs filter; shreds of white film drift and are caught by ciliated fans fixed like living pennants. When the feeding line passes, the walls look newly sanded, matte and bare until the next smear is applied from baskets of paste stirred in a shaded alcove, stirred with sticks of spine and thick threads of collagen. Simple. Sufficient.

It is very quiet. The water carries sound away as soon as it is made, and anyway nothing here wastes the effort that a shout would cost. The sounds you do hear are so small they seem to be happening inside your skull: a tap as a pebble tool sets, the soft rasp of a hundred minute mouths, the faint stuttering crackle of a crust shifting its grip. Outside, if something vast passes, there may be a change in pressure that the hinge-doors read in their ligaments, and a sound below hearing that presses momentarily at the bones. That is all.

There is light, but it is not light for seeing distances. A row of nurse-creatures tends beads of living glow along a ledge, pinches and feeds them with oils, coaxes them against the edge of quenching. Elsewhere, domes of mucus shot through with luminescent dots make stars of the ceiling. Touch-speech happens in intensities, a pulse traveled down a chain of arms and parapodia, new instruction to halt or haul or turn the paste a little thicker for warmth. It is not a theater. It is enough light to know the next body from your own, enough to see the texture of the wall you must maintain.

Rules here are rules of flow, hunger, and touch. There are no roots, no leaves to meet the sun; there has never been a sun in any practical sense since the wall of water thickened and thickened until even noon was night. Energy comes from falling and rising. Falling: the slow rain of dead things and powders sifted downward through an ocean of time, intercepted here by nets stretched over exterior balconies, scraped and mixed into the pastes. Rising: the deep warmth coaxed up along spirals and ribs, the faint chemistry of it harvested by films, converted into food by things too small to be named or drawn. Abundance is not loudness. It is a saturation, the sensation of a room where every place a tendril could anchor already holds one; where even the backs of the grazers carry smaller grazers; where a spine anchored to hold a hinge hosts a coil of eggs that have been fastened to it by thread.

All is made and maintained. The pillar on which this rests is a ladder of chambers like this one, each a slight variation on the same theme of remade detritus and persistent curve, extending upward until the currents thin to meaningless drift, and downward into pressures that crush untended cavities. Pauses to the building are called seasons by the people here, though there is no sky to set them; they are measured by the long, slow turning of currents, by the decadal swell and slack of the warm underflow that makes the heat-catch worth tending. When it slackens, the rooms grow colder and the films thin; everyone huddles closer, and the soft glows beaded on the ledges burn a little dimmer. When it swells, the hinge-doors are unhitched and propped wide to drink deep new water.

Openings to the outside are not windows in the land-dweller sense but mouths: controlled necks where the exterior pressure can be admitted in measured drafts. These throats lead to galleries no wider than a body, twisting around to discourage the fast flow of any intruder that has not learned the pattern of bends. Screens of bone and polymeric gel distill the marine snow as it enters; hawker-creatures wait there, patient weapons flared not with malice but with appetite, seizing the larger flecks and small animals the flow conveys. Everything is eaten, then re-sized by digestions into pastes and extruded substances, then set back into the wall or spread onto it, and the wall is not merely shelter but storehouse and memory. Here a seam announces the time a great carcass was intercepted and the entire pillar grew by a handsbreadth. Here scoured alcoves remain where an eddy, misjudged decades ago, bruised the films too thin and someone learned to throttle it.

The sense of height runs a spine through the place. To step out onto the east balcony is to understand altitude under pressure: not the airlessness of tops but the nearness of emptiness beyond a balustrade of shell. The railing is a series of cupped hands grown from the wall itself, each palm glossy from years of touch. Far out in the dark, patterns of slow light fall—too widely spaced to be for you—signs of passing giants or the flicker of clustered hunters tightening a loop. The spire falls away beneath the balcony like a thought you cannot finish.

Inside, near one buttress, a ring of smooth stone shows where a thousand bodies have rested, pressed vane and limb into the same curve while waiting for someone to return with a new tool or a new rule. The rule might be as simple as this: from now until the warmwater slackens, outer seams are to be burnished twice as often as inner. The rule is made with clicks and touches, and though it is plain, it is what keeps the pillar whole.

Nothing sharp here stays sharp unless it is meant to and tended. A row of spikes at the mouth are carefully abraded to retain their point; they are the only aggressive geometry in the room. Each is a harvested spine bound into the wall with interleaved fans of cartilage and silk-like threads spun from glands and cross-braced with crystallized salts. Their points have names and a schedule, and someone comes with a particular stone to dress them on the appointed day, washing the filing into a cup to be mixed into mortar later.

Time is not counted, but its footprints are clear. Erosion has become one of the makers. The first walls were scalloped by guesswork; generations learned how to place baffles so that pressure would lean, not shove. Shapes that resisted the currents were abraded down to shapes that cooperated with them. Everything here is the end of an argument and the beginning of a soft agreement with water.

You can smell, if smelling is a thing you do in water, the mild bitter of iodine, the dust-smell of limestone, the faint animal tang of glue. You can taste the warm in the way the water feels fleshed compared to the leaner streams outside. You can feel the crowding not as crush but as a comfort, heat and movement pooled and barely disturbed. The place gives nothing for free, but it forgives slowness. It asks you to move at the pace of its films and its hinges, at the patience of its long curves, at the interval of a glow-bead’s brightening and fading and brightening again.
