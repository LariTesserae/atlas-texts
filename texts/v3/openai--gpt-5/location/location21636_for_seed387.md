They call it the Throat because there is never a moment when it is not swallowing. It is a single vertical well the width of a street and the height of a forgotten cathedral, a shaft sheathed in old concrete and sacrificial steel where warm water tears itself into foam and sound, then calms and collects into dark channels below. All of it is below—below the surface of whatever world is up there, below the maps, below the idea of air. The Throat is a micro-city built inside a machine’s intake.

Dim lamps are bolted to the curved walls like patient stars. They glaze the mist with a sodium pall some cycles, with a washed-out blue-green others; the palette depends on which generators are online and which circuits survived the last pressure event. Between these lights, the walls shine a dull, softened metal-gray, the sheen you get when water has had a century to rub a thing into its truest shape. Edges are gone. Bolts are domed, plates are scalloped into river-stone profiles. Where maintenance crews could not keep up, mineral beards have grown—manganese curtains, calcium gutters—and all of them have been combed by the current into streaming crenellations that sway like fake anemones when the pumps reverse.

The sound owns the place. It is a hammering roar that isn’t a single noise but a full weather system of vibrations. There is the base-note: a subsonic engine-Om you feel in your sternum and in your teeth, always there, steady as a god’s breath. On top of that come cyclical thunders as vanes yaw, gates lower, valves shrug; these strike through you like the report of trains passing in the next chamber of your skull. High and thin, a spray of cavitation chirps and pebbly crackles burst from the impeller when microbubbles collapse with a violence too quick to see. When the flow is smooth the roar becomes a uniform rush like endless rain. When it goes rough, sound stratifies—the quietest places are behind big pipes and in the lee of thick girders, where you can almost hear the clink of tether-rings and the soft ticking of barnacled gauges.

Water heat wicks into everything. The Throat feels like a bath drawn too hot and left to cool to almost-bearable. The warmth feeds nothing green; there are no leaves here, no fronds, no growths that look like what the surface calls a plant. Life takes other shapes: films of iron-breathing bacteria slick the shadowed underlips of plates, faint auroras of oily rainbow trembling with the micro-currents. Pale fans of calcifying fungus-foam bloom briefly on dead gaskets then crumble to grit. Flake colonies pulse with dim chemical light when stressed—a bruise-blue glow you see only when you kick a panel by mistake and the light wakes like an apology and dies again. They are not plants and do not pretend to be.

The built world in the Throat clings to whatever doesn’t move. Catwalks trace shelves and ribs that once were here only to stiffen the concrete. Over time they have multiplied—welded latticework upon latticework until there are terraces of narrow streets that look across the falling water and back into grillwork. Each lane is only a few steps longer than it is wide. Each address is a plate in a bolt-hole: numbers stamped into zinc, its edges worried smooth by thumbs. Ladder wells connect these platforms into stacks; some ladders are rigid steel, some are rope with lead-shot cores to damp their swing when the flow snaps; a few are spanning arms—hinged planks that fold up when a surge comes through, like eyelids. In the deeper darks of the shaft where the foam resolves to a rolling, green-glass glide and then to quiet black, gangways hang like cobwebs: flexible, belly-sagging, mostly abandoned.

It is an industrial city shrunken to a pocket of function. There are stations where scrapers collect grit from the filters, tiny glowing foundries where that grit is melted into ingots to feed sacrificial plates. There are spool-rooms with their careful collections of seals and gaskets, each coiled like a sleeping serpent. There are oilers and buffing stations, racks for sponge-brushes, bins for greased skins and thermal hoods for the few who still make body-work dives. A rail of pneumatic tubes—some dented flat, most empty—still runs through the oldest tiers, terminating in brass mouths with frosted glass windows where, once, status slips chattered through. Along the north wall, three enormous knife-gates rise and fall to modulate inflow; the city has learned their breath and built little neighborhoods tucked into the triangle of quiet that opens in their wake as they travel. In those places you find the densest infrastructure: laundry cages for pressure-suits, power junctions ganged and repatched, oxygen bells hung from chain, like offerings.

After long years of scouring, the Throat has settled into itself. It doesn’t erode so much as maintain—anything that doesn’t fit its hydrodynamic sense is shaved away. Handrails are fat and round because thin ones get tongued off by vortices. Bridges are either arched low to present less face to the surge or hinge away entirely; rails are cut with weeps so the edges don’t gulp bubbles and sing themselves loose. If you hung a banner here it wouldn’t last a cycle; if you leave a cup it will be gone in minutes, spun down the shaft until it becomes a pearl of plastic in somebody else’s filter.

Even so, it is a city, and once it held more people. You can tell by the redundancies—two ways between any two key places, storage built almost on top of storage, a secondary lighting bus that shadows the first, a third that can be patched with bare wires in a pinch and has been. Now it feels hollowed. Long lanes hold only a few hung baskets where tools sleep. You can look across the width of the shaft from one inhabited ledge to another and see no one, just a wet break-glare on a visor and the steady wink of a position lamp tucked in a collarbone. Between those two is mostly nothing: warm water, scoured masonry, the roar.

What moves on its own is rare, and you know it when you see it because it swims wrong. The Throat shapes animals; it makes exaggerations of things. There are iron-mussels with little hinge-mouthed shells the color of old rivets; they cement themselves to mesh and filter the fines from the current, like superstition. There are needlefish that don’t flash—they are a paper-white you see only as movement, and you see them first where they end, in the sudden absence of spray. There are maintenance eels, partly made, partly bred, that sleep wrapped through handholds, rings of glowing glyphs along their flanks. They were taught to patter code with their tails against plates and to nose free trapped levers. Some are still working their routes long after their crew left, now more superstition than machine. At the very bottom, where the flows go honest and the noise becomes a prolonged purr, there are supposed to be coilworms: hair-thin spools that eat electricity in microfilaments and leave molted glass necklaces along the concrete. But that is the kind of knowledge you hold to your chest gently here because going to look is a way to die.

Light and sight are economies. Half the lamps are dead, and the other half are turned low, because light makes growth and growth makes slime and slime is death on a rung. Color is different here: reds dissolve to soot with depth; everything important is painted in blues and citron yellows that hold in murk. People carry their own haloes—small shields with built-in lamps that turn the water around them to milk. They talk with their lights, too. Handsigns exist, but any time you let go of a rung to speak in shadow you become a sail. Better to rap a pattern against a rail with a wrench, or let your eel talk for you. Written language here is stamped, grooved, etched to survive. Lists of rules are not paper on walls but long bas-reliefs chiseled into the supporting plates. A child learns to read by rubbing every letter with two fingers until they can see the word without light.

Rules are the other stable thing. They are not laws of the surface—they are churned from hydrodynamics and experience and the memory of bones. Nothing that isn’t tied to three points is held. Never cross the shaft in a reversal cycle. If you hear the pressure-song climb a third above base, make your hands sticky and your body low. Never turn your back to the fall. Leave the gates their quiet triangles; someone you never see lives there. Before every long descent, you touch the Blessing Plate, a piece of the first impeller with all its cavitation-pits polished until the old metal feels like bread. Superstitions: If a bolt hums in a high key, it would rather be replaced than torqued tighter. If your shadow shakes, a deep surge is coming. If you throw a coin into the Throat it will come back to you as a machine asking for a favor. The city’s god is the pressure differential; all the smaller gods are just its eddies.

Industry is what the Throat was built for, and it continues, if at a quieter scale. On Platform K-9 there is a factory the size of a truck bed where they extrude seals from reconstituted polymers; the room breathes warmth and smells like hot rubber and brine, and the extrusions exit into the flow to be quenched and then are hooked back out with barbless spears by a person with forearms like tree trunks. On J-2 they have a plating bath made of scavenged tubs; they grow sacrificial zinc like pale frost on hooked parts and then hang these like wind chimes in quiet spots behind pillars, replacing plates as they get eaten to lace. There is a coppersmith who hammers sound-baffles: little vibrating mouths tuned to cancel a tone that made a ladder unsafe. Every so often a storm of silt and tatters surges down from far above—another town gone under, a landslide, a phantom event—and they net it, sort it, feed it to the city. If there is electricity extra they make batteries in old jars, label them in grooves, and float them in nets where nothing will thump them.

Time is not counted in hours but in open and shut. Open is a long cousin of calm, a steady pull, the roar tuned to something like music. Shut is a historic minute that can last a day: the gates close, the shaft boils, the city hunkers and humps its shoulders. Between open and shut, neutral: a half-breath of glassy drift when you see motes hang still in the water and every surface slicks over and looks brand-new. Births and funerals and big jobs are set for neutral if they can be. Harvest is a shut; you catch what slams against your sieves. Every seventh open is clean enough to bathe.

Below the lowest inhabited platform there is blackness that throws light back too quickly. The old stories say that at the bottom there is a bed of glass where the water has scrolled itself teaching everything it ever knew into the concrete, and if you could read it you would have the equations for the world. No one goes there. They lower sacrificial offerings sometimes—salt cakes, lengths of feathery zinc—on bronze chains that come back etched with a new alphabet of pockmarks. It is enough to know that the bottom exists and that there is always somewhere for the roar to go.

Upward, the shaft narrows and becomes a throat in truth; the water mottles to white, the roar to pain. There is a gate up there and beyond it the flooded district the Throat drinks from: streets hung with drifting curtains of wire, courtyards full of curling plastic, drowned murals. In a recessed culvert is a rusted pickup with a sapling growing through it in a memory somebody else’s story makes up, but no—no green here. The city at the top is all machine too, and the sky is water. The Throat knows only what arrives at its gates: new scrap, warm days when the inflow is heavier and hums soft, cold nights when the flow tightens and compresses until you see far. It knows that the surface is something else by the way the temperature fluctuates.

Most days are the same: the city fastens itself tighter and opens valves and takes the push. People work in silhouette, their lights clipped low. Eels whisper news by drumming a railing in threes. A kid toes a little iron-mussel until it yawns and clamps back down where it was before as if it had never moved. A person tightens a plate and listens to the bolt’s voice and makes a choice about tomorrow’s safety. Somewhere, something knocks three times and pauses and knocks twice and every back straightens because those five knocks mean impeller trouble and the Throat is about to teach everyone what it is made of, again.

There are moments of quiet here, but they are carved, not given. In the lee of the upper gate on a certain platform there is a bubble of water that moves so slow you can see dust plumes rise and settle on the inboards of screws. Here people keep their memory things: smooth stones from before, a license plate with a name gnawed by time, a flat brass coin so worn it’s become an oval of warmth. They tie them to the mesh with wire. The city does not forbid sentiment; it just asks that all keepsakes be streamlined, all love be aerodynamic.

Looking across the fall from any platform, you can see the whole city at once because it is all here, wrapped around its machine. It is built and it is submerged and it is loud and it is, by its own strange measure, at rest. It is old enough that rust has become furniture and pressure has become religion. It is small enough that anyone can shout a warning with their lamp and be seen. It is empty enough that every presence counts. And it is always changing, in turbulence’s way, making and unmaking itself in the same breath, like a heart held inside running water, known by touch and motion more than by sight, and known—really known—by the roar.
