The plain is so old it has forgotten how to stand. Everything has slumped into broadness—no mountains, barely hills, just a slow, exhausted undulation of pale stone and shallow basins, the geometry of a continent that has been sanded for ages. The surface is crazed with pores and slots, a honeycomb worn flat by eras of rain and the steady acid of breath and tide. The stone weeps; water slicks it constantly. Even on a day without rain, drops gather in a thousand beadpoints and creep downward in silver threads, until every ledge is a lip that glistens as if it had just spoken.

Across this near-level reaches a low sky that never quite wakes. Light comes filtered—gray and cool, as if the sun had been wrapped in wetted cloth. At noon, shadows are loose stains; at dusk they hardly deepen. The air is cold to the teeth and smells of sour minerals, salt, and the faint sweetness of chitin warmed by friction. Sometimes the clouds thin and a pale brightness glides over the plain in one large, patient sweep, but the general mood is dim: an overcast ceiling only a long throw above you, steadily beading the world with condensation.

Underfoot: a pavement of ancient limestone and recrystallized salt, puttied by clay and a slick of microbial gel. The rock is split into long, narrow fissures, grikes that vanish into blackness, and rounded pits that yawn like mouths when the tide is low. The whole plain is plumbed. Water moves not only over it but through it, in coiled tunnels, galleries, and sumps. Twice a day the invisible sea within flexes, and the land breathes.

That is where the sound comes from. The breath of the plain is not a metaphor but a public event. Pressure in the sub-surface conduits rises, somewhere out there beyond the horizon where the lithic sponge meets the true sea; the air within is shoved and sucked in slow masses through hundreds of thousands of holes. The stone becomes a pipe organ the size of a valley. Every blowhole, every crevasse-turned-flue gives voice: hoots and vowels, thunderous sighs, bass booms so deep they rasp in the bones, thin keens that thread between them like needles. Together they build a weather of sound.

It is deafening. When the breath runs strongest, your chest vibrates; you can feel your ribs join the choir. Water laps and spits from the mouths as if applauding. Mists tear themselves into streamers and flags. Things speak and do not mean to: the cords of your coat sing, your tools tremble, a rope left taut will pluck itself into motion. Birds do not lift here; there are none to lift. Instead, masses of small wingless gliders—thin, kite-bodied creatures with expanses of skin like translucent sails—surf the gusts that explode up from the vents and settle again when the pressure wanes, their spines quivering, eyes like beads of fresh iron.

Nothing like a plant rises from this. No grass, no shrub, no fringing reed of green. The ground is bare except for thin, dark rinds: bacterial felt feathering along wet cracks in diagrams of appetite; pale fungal beards hanging like frost from overhangs; slick, vitreous sheets where colonies have cemented themselves on stone and gone dormant until a rain or an exhale wakes their colors. Food here begins in solution, not in leaf. Carbon is fixed underground and at the wet mouths of the plain, by microorganisms gnawing the minerals and sulfurs out of old rock and excreting mats the color of bruised sky.

Life has met that offer with gaudy abundance. The mats are pastured by low-slung grazers whose bodies are living shields: segmented ovals like coins with legs, their under-sides combed with rasping teeth. When you walk near they jitter their plates and the sound is a patter of thrown shells. Slower, slicker things—velvet slugs the size of a dog—trail behind them and sift the trammeled slime for the good bits left over, leaving silvery writing in their wake. Along the lip of every pool you find something like a sea-thing, but not quite: ringed worms that flower into filter-fans when the exhale carries a fat froth of spores and aerosol up from the dark; humped “throat-crabs” that anchor to the stone and open resonant cavities under their carapaces, catching sound as if it were rain and harvesting the dust it shakes loose.

Predators in this place listen more than they see. A long-bodied hunter with limbs like delicate crooks—skin mottled gray to match the stone—places itself at the edge of a grike and waits for the pressure to shift. When it does, and the plain inhales, drawing bad air and tiny things down into itself, the hunter feeds in the slipstream, snapping up what panics to the surface. Others ride out with each breath, not on wings but on paragliding flesh: kite-sails with little hands along their edges that pluck prey out of the air in a shower of mica-bright droplets, then tilt themselves into the ruins of sound to descend. Back at the mouths of permanent holes, blind fish-reptiles lunge up into day to seize anything that ventures too near the lip.

For all that, there is emptiness. The presences come in clumps and withdrawals. You can walk for an hour between one chorus and the next, between one colony and its rival’s rim, crossing only slick, pocked stone and shallow pans of tea-colored water that tremble as if someone were speaking to them. The horizon stays where it is, a low envelope of gray. The distance feels honest and large; the world is uncluttered enough that the smallest thing—a shining shell, an amber smear of dried mat, a single stone pillar left uncollapsed by time—pulls the eye hard.

Scattered in this plain are signs of people, or things with people’s patience. They have learned to make rooms in the negative space of noise. Where the breath meets a ridge and spills, it leaves a slouch of relative calm behind the stone, a hush-patch as tangible as shade. In these wind-shadows stand the shelters: narrow, bowed frames of whalebone-color chitin and drift-stone, mortised together by hand and ligament, draped in stitched membranes sealed with fatty resin. No timber—there are no trees to take—but the carapaces of the dead are hewn and fit as beams, and large gypsum plates are quarried out of older cracks and leaned to as walls. Roofs and walkways are taut skins waited with pebbles to keep them from finding the note that would tear them in two.

A settlement, when you come upon it, is a scattering—five to twelve dwellings crouched in their respective hushes, linked by bone ladders over wet fissures and by low walls that do double work: guiding footsteps by touch in bad weather, and interrupting particular harmonics that drive the domesticated herds to bolt. Between the houses rise sound-baffles: scaffoldings dressed with bladder-curtains and hung with braided tendon. Some have mouth-pipes aligned to a particular hole; with gestures and weighted shutters, a person can make that one hole’s roar cut into a stuttering beat, a pulse that carries a signal upvalley. Conversation is by hand, hip, light-tap on stone. When the organ breath is at its largest, the people cover the sides of their heads with layered caps made from fungus-felt and hide. When it lessens, stories are told in the hush, the hands casting them, the eyes catching them.

Their tools are intimacy-size. Scrapers of shell and chert. Awls hammered from a meteor’s gray tooth and passed down. Knives that are more like patient ideas etched onto a piece of shale and sharpened against water-slick stone every night. Nets knotted from spun tendon and a fibrous fungal pith painstakingly grown in the dark and dried into threads by the heat of slow-rendered fat lamps. Fire is a rarity in this wet, dim world; heat is borrowed from the planet’s slow leakage and from the chemical romance of fats and salts. Cooking is steaming: meat and mats wrapped in hide and pegged above a vent until the breath salts them and they collapse into a savory.

Around every house you notice small alterations of the plain: a ring of stones braced just so to break a troublesome tone; a flap of cured membrane set over a narrow blowhole to be harvested later for salt and dust; stakes and leashes for the plate-grazers, which are placid, flat, and heavy-lidded and will grind a resident patch of mat all day for a guard’s share of scum; lines of polished pebbles that flash even in the dim, to catch the eye and keep a running child from a black slot. At intervals along the routes—routes are chosen to step from hush to hush, like islands—stand cairns, but not the mute piles you know. These have slitted tops that sing when a certain breeze strikes them, each pile a different syllable, so simple maps can be taught to a youngster with a mouth open in wonder and both ears wrapped, counting the syllables they pass.

Weather here is less rain than breath and fog. Drizzle is common, a beading more than a fall. Rarer roars bring what the folk call grayfalls: when the plain exhales and somewhere upwind a sheet of condensate peels from the ceiling of cloud and comes down whole, a satiny rain that scours the microbial film into chocolate foam and sends every creature who grazes it stuttering into a safer crevice. Thunder happens, but underground more often than above. Lightning is almost unknown. When the pressure swings are large, the mists stream forward along the ground like rivers on their bellies, and the gliders take to the air in hundreds, making the sky look briefly like a mud-brown flowering.

Age has settled over even this motion. You can see it in the way everything edges—nothing sharp stays. Old fossils have become graffiti in the stone, just mottlings now, not stories. The larger blowholes are ringed by multiple rims, older lips in gray-brown rings, each a different era of collapse. Once, long ago, some other folk or these same in a former patience built a causeway across a bad run of fissures; now it is a row of teeth worn flat, a whisper of itself whenever the breath hits the old cavities still preserved inside it. The bones of old herds are stratified into the walking surface; their plates are now stepping-stones, their shapes reduced to function: to carry, to bridge, to cap a troublesome crack. Far off you can trace the ghost of a coastline, a slightly different drabness, where once, perhaps, the sea’s skin lay openly upon this stone instead of secretly flowing through it.

Rules here are set by the breath and the wet: what exists must be patient with drench, and must endure being shaken. The base of life is down in the dark, and when it comes up, it comes up as food for anything with a mouth. You move when the tones are low, you flee when the plain starts to sing the notes that mean spill or collapse, and you hunt by knowing which holes couple—block one here and the exhale elsewhere punches hungry, and kites and crook-limbed hunters and sign-speaking people are all waiting in the appropriate hush. Work is always in arrangement: of baffles, of edges, of plates, of membranes, to lean the energy that cannot be stopped into shapes that are useful, or at least survivable.

At night the loudness wanes, not because the plain sleeps but because the air above it steadies a little. The sky darkens into a shallow bowl and some of the pools begin to glow. It is not mystical, only bacteria being themselves, but in a world starved of bright, it feels like ceremony. A person can walk then without wraps over the ears and hear, for the first time in hours, the smallnesses again: a grazer’s soft scrape, drops talking to a pan, the shy lick of a hand on hide, the click of a shell clasp, the squeak of a net being mended. The organ is still there, humming at the limit of perception, like the truth of the place—old, wet, flat, working—stretching under everything. In the morning it will swell again, and the shelters will pull themselves tighter into their hushes, and the kites will stand, and the stone will exhale until it empties the lungs of the valley and fills them afresh.
