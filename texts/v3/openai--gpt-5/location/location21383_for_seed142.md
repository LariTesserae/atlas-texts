At this height the world has worn itself smooth. The plain rides the spine of an ancient range, a table of stone planed by eras that no one remembers and nothing remarks upon now. Low hill-shoulders barely lift above the level like knuckles under a cloth. Distance is not a vista but a continuation—one plane, then another, each one a little darker, a little more certain that it is the last.

It is warm without being bright. Warmth comes from somewhere else, not from light—an ember you cannot quite see hangs behind the starfield, making the air soft on the skin and the ground reluctant to give up the heat it has kept all day and all night, which are the same here. To human eyes the sky never climbs past a moonless glow, and most of what happens is told by temperature, not color: the cold sharp of polished stone, the lingering body-heat of a hand on a rail, the city’s slow breathing measured in the crimson rise of its radiator-fins.

Nothing in the ground remembers water. The surface holds a desert pavement of fused grains, glass and iron and the chemistry of ancient fires. Basalt lies underfoot like black bread; veins of sulfide cut through it in dull metallic seams and, where the stone has cracked along long-gone stress-lines, pale chloride crystals have blossomed in the shade and gone hard as the idea of rain. Sand exists, but it is a static sand—angular, keen, and immobile, locked where it fell ages ago. Wind once had teeth here if the shapes can be trusted: long, sleeping forms of yardangs and whale-backs, each truncated, each softened to an almost comical kindness by time. Now the air barely moves. On some nights faint auroral sheets unroll along the horizon, not to be seen so much as to be felt when the hair on your forearms rises, and a taste like tin touches the tongue.

The city set on this wide table refuses to call attention to itself, and that is its first rule. It lies in low clusters, each cluster a quiet city by itself separated from the next by hectares of tidy emptiness. Between them, only routes are visible—matted-black causeways of sintered glass; thin rails that make no sound; pipelines in catenary arcs whose skins are ribbed with fins to drink and shed heat as needed. Factories keep their mass under the level of the nearest ridge, and whatever towers exist are not chimneys but radiators, dark combs turned to the sky that glitter only in red when seen through the instruments that matter here. Light is folded inward; illumination for hands and faces is hooded and close, as if each task were an intimate secret.

Engines work, but you hear them as a shared intention rather than a voice: a steady thrum below the hearing, sensed more in the ankles than in the ear, punctuated by the small ritual sounds of a culture that has no time for waste—valve-clicks, an occasional chain settling on a sprocket, the porcelain cluck of a ceramic cup against a ceramic table. The thin air swallows distant noise; even the big fans, when they start, sift more than they push. This is an industrial city that measures itself by gradients rather than gears. It runs on the difference between subsurface warmth and the long cold of the black above; it raises columns of coolant through buried wells, exhales that heat slowly into space, and harvests from that patient exhalation the power to move ore, to cook ceramic, to press bearings from a carbon that has never tasted flame. Heat is currency. The bright streak is taboo. If a torch must be used, it is used under a hood, and when you stir your tea, even the spoon is made of dull composite that remembers not to sing against the cup.

Out past the last works-yard the plateau tilts toward its end. The ground there scallops downward to a black drop where a heavier, thicker air begins; distant layers below move with their own weathers, but none of it touches this shelf. The edge is protected by habit and by braided barriers of cable and stone that have long since lost their paint. Standing there you can look into a depth that makes no plumb-line, a depth measured only by how long the eye keeps trying to find a bottom and gives up. There is no sea in that gulf, no white thread of river. Only dark on dark, and the sense that much of the planet keeps its time at another speed entirely.

Plants never took this place. Once, perhaps, simpler films laced faintly through the cracks when the gases changed or the crust flexed and drew breath, but if they did, the only record is chemical: varnishes of complex hydrocarbons where the sunless spectrum was once favorable, traces in the upper millimeter of the pavements that taste sour and then fade. The living now are rarities that have learned to be almost not-there. Here a paper-thin serpent coils in the lee of a radiator fin, its layered skin catching electrical fields so that it feeds by sipping the difference between fin and air; it moves only twice a day, by inches, the tremor of its accordion-plates making a sound like tissue. There, under a service gantry, a colony of glass crickets crouches and waits for the weekly warm flush in the pipes, speaking to one another only when the pipe’s belly goes from cool to tepid; their speech is a brittle tick-tick that feels more like a thought against the side of your head than a noise. Once in a long while a moth of perfect blacker-than-black angles drifts into the exhaust of a stack and hangs there like a comma; no one has ever found a carcass of one. Fauna exist here like punctuation in a very long, very quiet story.

The city and its plain share rules that people here don’t so much follow as become:

- Avoid shocking the ground. The rock is old and has no appetite left for fracture; everything is rounded, footings are broad, the load is spread. Doors sigh rather than slam. When equipment arrives from somewhere else with corners and weight, a priest of the leveling-guild takes a file to it until it wants to join the rest of the world.

- Give heat a path and a schedule. The day does not cool and the night does not warm, so appointments with the sky are kept in perpetuity: this pipe will be nine degrees warmer at this hour and four at the next. If you must change that, you petition, because a change here travels far and is felt by things that do not vote.

- Do not speak too loudly to the air. The air listens poorly and carries gossip badly. If you need to be heard, put your palm on the rail and hum; the message will carry where stone is continuous. Instruments for speech are throat-worn; they whisper into the bone.

- Keep light to yourself. If you let it out, it draws insects from a hundred meters in a place that may go months without a moth. It calls to eyes that evolved to see a kind of warmth you cannot, and this is their city too.

Time here has been relieved of its theater. Cloud-shadows do not cross the ground. Sunbeams do not advance along walls. The plain is in its late, retiring age and has settled into a face it will keep. Rust does not arrive because the word is meaningless; water, when introduced by mistake by a visitor, boils off as a smell like blood and leaves a powder that rises the small hairs along your forearms as if remembering something terrible. If something does change—a fin cinched down and replaced, a derrick foot pulled and its socket filled—the change is resolved before it is noticed. There is no hurry. There is also no rest, only a continuous falling of the same grain of sand, endlessly, in a clock too slow for hands.

Even so, the place is not empty of story. Walk a little and you will find a field of black, shin-high stelae set out on a square kilometer of bare pavement. Each is a thermopile stake sunk into the ground to test the gradient here, and under each a record of the test is etched—curves shallow as fingernails, decades of almost-line. At the north edge of the city, a conveyor crosses the plain, taking ore from a low, old, softer hill that is more crush than stone. The belt never stops, but never hurries, and neither does the man at the watch-house who counts off its loads against the rate at which the slag-cooling gardens can drink them. In the coolant yards behind the ceramic plant, slow lakes move under sealed skins—fluorocarbon sheets as black as dead mirrors, breathing imperceptibly as heat walks out of them into the night. If there is art here, it is found in how a set of fins is canted to sip both the celestial cold and the breath of the moving air beyond the cliff lip, and in the way the street at the Works’ center feels underfoot: so level that stepping onto it feels like stepping into a decision you cannot unmake.

Stand still long enough and the place completes itself around you. The almost-sound of the city lines up with your pulse. The sky discovers and forgets a very faint fluorescence. A cricket clicks, twice, under the gantry, and is content to wait a week to do so again. Far away, somebody adjusts a valve and a line of radiator teeth glow a fraction higher in the instruments; the rock under your boots warms by the width of a breath, then sets itself back to neutral. Nothing here is dead. It is simply finished with drama, and set to maintenance.
