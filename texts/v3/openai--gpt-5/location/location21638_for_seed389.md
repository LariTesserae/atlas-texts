From a distance the whole place reads as a tangle of black geometry thrown up against a pale sky: blades and ribs and buttresses new to the sun, still glass-bright in places, still sharp enough that wind skates off with a hiss. Up close the edges cut the world into narrow, vertiginous corridors. The ground is not ground exactly, but a series of ledges and faces and sudden drops, a choreography of surfaces turned vertical.

Everything here feels newly made. There are no rounded corners yet, no powdered soil in the cracks. The mountains are young and too tall, squeezed upward and then frozen while still half-plastic, half-glass. Columnar joints run in clean hexagonal chimneys you could put your arm into and never reach the end. In some of the hollows the rock carries bubbles—popped, stilled—like petrified foam. On the sunward faces the black melts into a starfield of tiny olivine crystals that catch normal daylight and send it back in a million dull greens. In the shadowed clefts the glass is flawless and without detail, so space itself seems to bend.

It is hot, but not in the way of a kiln that roars and makes the air hard to breathe. Heat here is a constant exhale from below, a steady upward motion so gentle you only notice it by the way it dries your mouth and makes little veils of shimmer above every line of metal. The air thins with the height and the heat together: sound seems to turn transparent. You hear your boots on iron in your bones more than in your ears. Conversations fail to travel. A cable the thickness of a man’s torso passes a few meters away and bears an entire block of masonry up a cliff, and the only sign is the way the cable’s paint scuffs against a rubbing block and leaves a slow-growing halo.

Between spire and spire the city has threaded itself, as if it had always been a part of the rock’s internal plan. There are gangways hung from drilled anchors and white ceramic steps bolted to glass. There are flues and ducts like the rootlets of some absent organism. The built spaces find every dimension’s use: underwalks shadowed by heat-shields; crosshatches of rope and braided cable spanning chasms where the natural distances narrow to a single jump; slabs of pale tile fastened over the dark rock to keep the sun’s strike from baking the core of a dwelling.

Everything layers. Someone has used the dead air under an old staircase to run a pipe and soldered to that pipe a rack of condenser sheets that sweat a line of water at noon. Above the sheets another level holds a wide, matte canopy that reflects light back to a reservoir. Above that, stretched on frames, hang nets for the days when cloud comes low and intimate and full of gift. Even the cliff faces hold infrastructure: shallow channels run with a hunter’s care along isotherms, coaxing trickles where cost and heat loss will be least.

Water is always in the conditional tense. The city harvests from air, from stone, from brief visits of rain that slip off as quickly as they come. At night the sky is a cold well, and the engineers have learned to lay out black glass to drink starlight, piped into white troughs that refuse the morning. At dawn you see it first on the edges: beads forming with as much surprise as if they were alive. It is a good morning when the beads fatten into a runnel and then into a thread that makes its whispering way down a groove to a ceramic cistern. People read the condensers like farmers read clouds. They have names for drip-lines that go back to how long you can stand under them before you are wet.

There is no plant life to soften or hold together, no tuft of something green in a crack to declare the patient persistence of seeds. Whatever chemistry here makes living things has not given itself to leaf and root. In the dampest pockets there is only a slick that reminds you of breath on glass, thin films that turn stone a deeper color where they pass. Inside the cisterns, in their own season, small wormlike things gnaw the minerals out of a forgotten screw and leave it fluted like a seashell. Most days you will not see them. Most months you will not. Creatures with fins like paper sometimes ghost past in the heated updrafts between buildings and rock, skimming and turning to drink from condenser-mist; you could mistake them for scraps of torn tarp if one of them did not tilt and present a face with the delicate embarrassment of an eye. Once in a while—counted on fingers each year—something larger crawls up from a vent in the lower reaches and lies in the shade and breathes. People keep a respectful distance. The laws against pursuing, harrying, trapping, naming are not written down but they hold, because everything that moves here has earned it.

The factories are present but not proud. They do not hoist chimney-stacks and pour smoke. They sheathe themselves in ceramic and wool and glide. A brine works clings to a face like lichen, its trays cut into the glass at hand-holding depth, its pipes carried in braided cords of fibrous glass and cloth so the touch is dead. Inside, in the long tunnels, racks and racks of plates barely touch the baths, and a quiet like a library keeps the electrolytic crystals growing even and obedient. Elsewhere long pistons as thick as a waist move in and out at a speed better described as a habit. The heavy engines are put in the deep cut halls, in chambers dug at exact angles where the voices of machines cannot find reinforcement in the rock. There is no banging. Chains are turned into ropes. Gears have teeth like velvet. Vibration is an enemy greater than any hunger.

That is one of the rules of this place, called between people in gestures and in the leather put on everything that might collide. The glass that is mountain here hates abrupt sound. A shout turned mean will come back from the wall as a tremor you will not feel in your feet but will see as a thread of fine sand trickling from a seam. A dropped bolt has, on a bad day, become an avalanche. The first builders learned this and put laws around it. Horns are forbidden. Boots are soled in layered cloth. The schooling of a child goes as much in sign and flag and lampwork as in voice. A street argument disentangles into still hands and turned backs and later, when the sun is lower, into careful, slow words at the edge of a cistern. In the factories, sickle-shaped hoods over every vent swallow noise and feed it into baffles that warm sleeping rooms. Sound is captured like water.

The other rule is a gentleness in the timing of things. You do not hurry a load across a hanging bridge, because the bridge will sing, and its singing will teach the stones below it to sing, and then the songs will start to agree. You cannot lay a beam on a place that is not quite there yet. The rock here still moves, a speaking of inches that goes on for years. In deep cuts you can see crystals growing—nothing dramatic, but added faces on a prism since last year, a new line of brightness on a split plane. The city builds on pads that know how to slide, on bearings that will forgive. Each grand project gets measured in winters and in sun angles, not in days. You can watch a counterweight all morning and feel cheated if it finally moves a hand’s breadth.

Light here falls like its own element, because there is no tree to sift it, no brush to make pockets of shade. It is not cruel light, simply present and unwavering. Normal daylight sits on the black glass and turns it into a low fire that you feel but cannot see. The city answers with white. Laundry is a climate strategy as much as a chore; wide cloths give houses their climate back by dismissing the sun. In the evenings, the heat that has gone into black walls leaks into the air and finds those white planes and lingers there until midnight. Life assembles around that economy of shade and radiance. A house with good whitework earns friends.

The human city is a density inside a broad openness. You can stand on a gallery and see a hundred different presences in the span of a reach: a cable, a pipe, a dangling ladder, an insulator that looks like a stack of plates, a folded seat for the rest that comes between rest and more rest. You can look up and see at least three more levels stacked and braced and haunted by sleeping cats that came in with the last train and made sure of the warm places. Then you lift your gaze and it hops easily, like a raven, to a spire across a gulf that could swallow a town, and there is their own dense tangle, and the thin line of a funicular like a thread thrown across a room.

Rail climbs where even eagles would think twice. Carriages the size of rooms creep along tracks that ridge the faces, with the rails kept cool by white-painted gutters that carry melted nocturnes of dew. Pushers and catchers stand at angles where they can work without asking stone for resonance. Signals bloom in flags and slotted lights. The city is a school of moving heavy things as if they were light. Somewhere below you a crane slides its trolley and without sound the load shifts, arrives, takes its new weight on new anchors. Someone with a brush paints a black circle on a white tile to show where noon was last month when the sun cleared the rim.

On still days there is easily room for listening. You can hear mineral trickle in some distant flank that will in a thousand hours become a trickle of stone, which will in a thousand hours more become a path for water. You can hear the labored pant of a condenser as it learns to drink today’s air; the cloth over it blots and then releases the moment it becomes too wet to do its work. You can hear the long vowel of a glider taking a slow turn to where the air gives a small, almost moral lift above a corner. If a storm arrives—and they are infrequent but precise—it comes with a preface of cool drafts that send a collective rustle through whitework all up and down the city like fur the wrong way. Then the rain lashes, fast, surprised to be itself, running hard down pre-scored channels, filling every cistern to the remembered line, waking up the worm-things in the cold places, and pouring off over brinks into whatever canyon chooses to be river for an hour.

After, you count damage, which is less than you fear because of the quiet, the slow. The rock, new and untrusting, takes a beating from the rain but not a wound; its faces show the first signs of a future you may or may not see. Where the water ran an extra time, a tiny scallop begins. Where a drop fell often on the same point of a dripline, there is a cup no wider than a spoon’s bowl. The city pretends no dominion; it coaxes. It convinces weight to fall where people will be blessed by it. It persuades heat to share its days.

The strangest things are not the things that move, but the way the mountain refuses stories too early. You could tell yourself that a certain pattern on a face is an ancient inscription, but you’d be wrong; it is the laboratory of basalt figuring out what kind of cracks to lay between what angles. You could imagine that a particular run of cables is a kind of instrument, but no, it only means that the lift there sometimes hates to start and must be spoken to by hand. There are places where if you lay your head to the wall, you will hear a chord like a swallow’s high note. It isn’t music. It is time thinking.

At this elevation, born so violently and still so unscuffed by ages, the world has not decided what it is. Thin-moistened nights make dew on knives. Day kisses glass and gives it dreams of being sand some day, then takes the kiss back. The city’s people become part of its experiment, walking with care, building with caution, making heat do what they need and no more. You watch a bead of water gather under a condenser lip and you watch a small paper-finned thing tilt out of nowhere and drink, and in that tiny choreography you glimpse the great quiet system that holds everything up here in place: slow, patient, heavy, almost silent, and shocked occasionally into a laughter of rain.

The rules are simple and strange enough to seem like manners. Keep your voice and your weight shorter than the span of a breath. Put white between sun and dark. Never force a thing to move sooner than it wants to, or it will move all at once. If water appears, give it a place to belong. On the order of years, the mountains may be persuaded. On the order of lifetimes, you may watch them grow up. In between, there is the hum of current in a cable, a gleam of green in a crystal face, the memory of a rain, a sheet hung high and snapping, and a cliff that holds not by strength but by the wisdom of not being asked for speed.
