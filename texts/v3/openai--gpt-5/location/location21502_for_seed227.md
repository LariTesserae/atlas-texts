You find it by accident or not at all: a seam in the coast that is not a doorway so much as a hesitation between two slabs of black stone. The tide has drawn down just far enough to show a narrow dark slit, slick to the touch, rank with cold iodine and peat. Your breath makes a small cloud and then vanishes into something larger.

Inside is not big. It is a single room hollowed by patient impact, a bell of rock only a few body-lengths across, but it feels larger because it is mostly air. The floor is bedrock poured smooth long ago and then stippled with ribs and runnels where swash once combed. The walls curve in clean pages and scallops as if a hand has thumbed them for ages. You sense the long, low place everywhere: there is no drop at your feet, no horizon, only a cupped hollow at the level of the sea.

It is dark. In daylight the only light is the green skin of the entrance and, higher up, a blade or coin of sky that opens and closes as the swell breathes beneath a chimney cut clean through the headland. The throat of that blowhole is choke-round and polished. When cloud slides, the hole holds a pewter brightness and spills it down onto the opposite wall, and the wet there becomes a dim mirror. Most of the time even that is gone. You are in the kind of darkness that has a floor and drips.

Cold unbuttons you slowly. Stone takes heat from your palms, and your breath slicks the rock in front of your face. Sometimes in winter the dripline along the north wall grows a thin lip of ice, ruffled like candle fat. The wet is constant in some form. Even on a dry day, the air is heavy with salt wet and the fine aerosol from slamming water gathers in the folds of your jacket. Threads of fresh seepage come down from the boggy turf above, not enough to change the salinity of the pools that form in the floor’s shallow dishes, but enough to be tasted as a glassy difference on your tongue when you brave a fingertip to the trickle.

It is deafening. The sea is not seen so much as encountered as pressure and pattern. Waves do not just arrive; they enter. They fill the room, not with water but with sound so thick your sternum participates. It comes in pulses: a boom, a long tearing pull of shingle somewhere beyond the mouth, a hammering echo that runs up the chimney and chops itself into a gasp, a deep-throated chuff as the blowhole backfires a column of breath to the open air above. Pebbles farther out are rattled and rounded, and their rattle is the high note in this organ. When a larger set finds the coast the volume rises beyond thought, and you find yourself bracing without knowing for the next strike. There are patterns—sets and lulls—but the dominant truth is that everything here is shaped to the measure of long sound.

In the rare seconds between impacts, other noises exist: the violin-squeak of a single drip hitting a shallow pan of water, the peep of a little sculpin startling in its pool, the ticking of salt cracking as it dries on a lip of ledge where sun sometimes can reach. These subtleties vanish again under the roar as another swell shoulders in and exhales.

Despite the dim and the wet, there is green. On the wall beneath the fresher seep, a cushion of moss as deep as your palm swells like lung. Liverworts knit coin-shaped scales, so glossy with water they seem made of lacquer. Filaments of fern—the kind that can live on very little light and live well—rake down from a crack that opens to soil above, their rootlets dark and delicate as hair on an arm. Seaweeds claim the lower stones, strap-fronds lying in a tangle, thick and supple when flooded, leathery and salt-sugared when the tide drops. On the lip of the entrance where daylight actually draws a line, the rock wears a frill of lichen the color of old gold. The lushness is not everywhere; it is concentrated in its places, thick as pelt where conditions concede. In other places, the stone is bare and black and slick and exact.

Animals are rare here and when they come, they come on purpose or by mistake. A pale crab works one corner of a tidepool with a conscientiousness so complete it is indistinguishable from prayer. Now and then, at an extreme high tide, a seal noses into the cleft and pauses, making a round soft breath in all that hard noise, and then is gone. In late summer one or two shags land on the outer ledge and rest a while with their wings open, then think better of it and take the wind. Amphipods scuttle; a spider descends from the overhead greenery after a storm and then starves; a dark beetle from the dune scrapes sand and dies; tiny white slugs, exquisite and uncalled for, raise their heads out of the moss whenever the moss is fullest. The chamber is not contested. It is a quiet place then, but a quiet marked by absence rather than calm.

The age of it is obvious not as a number but as a habit. Nothing is raw. Everything has been worked to the curve it wishes to be and held there. The ceiling bowls inward where eddies have traced exact ellipses for centuries. In the floor, pairs of round basins show where pebbles danced and ground themselves down to dust—no pebbles left now, but the caves where they worked remain and fill slowly with silt at a rate you could measure only by passing on the tale to your grandchildren. The blowhole lip is rounded and has the skin-shine that only many winters give. The notch you squeeze through to enter is the shape of a wet hip sliding sideways, and this is not because any body made it so, but because bodies, stone, water, and tide consent to only a few forms in common.

Everything moves slowly unless the sea is touching it directly. Runnels of dissolved iron tuft the wall over decades. Salt whorls and dries day to day, but the shift of ledges and slabs is a once-in-a-decade thing, in a northern storm when a tree a hundred meters long is thrown like a lever into the mouth. Most days the most dynamic act here is a drip that forms, swells, remembers its own weight, and drops.

Someone used it, once or often, but not much is left and nothing fancy. Against the back wall a low crescent of stacked flat stones makes a windbreak and a hint of enclosure. It is barely knee-high and canted toward the dry seam along the floor—the part of the room that stays above splash except in the wildest water. Three hand-cut notches step up the left-hand wall toward a narrow ledge; the prints of the hands are there, not as fingers but as the polished smoothness where the oily press of human skin sealed the rock against crystals of salt. In the ledge above, someone pecked a shallow basin into a softer band of stone to catch the drip from the fern crack. Its rim bears little grooves—twenty-nine on one side, twenty-nine again, and then a re-beginning—an old lunar measure for when this place was safest to reach.

There is a beam of driftwood sunk like a rib in a crack near the entrance, lashed once with something that was twisted cord and is now only a stain of darker fiber and a few hairs turned to sugar. A net hangs folded on a peg made from whalebone, the net eaten away to laciness, the bone polished like the blowhole lip. In a corner, a flattish stone blackened on its upper face speaks to fire, to a bowl of seal oil or fish fat lit and guttering, smoke soaked into every pore of the roof above it so thoroughly that even now, Wet as it always is, if you scrape with your fingernail and smell, there is the old sour sweetness of burned oil.

The room encour­ages certain rules and discourages others. You do not hurry; the floor will refuse your feet if you hurry. You do not speak; the sea will drown you out and you will tilt your head nearer and nearer to your companion to hear and then realize you have turned your face toward an oncoming swell and must look away and plant yourself and listen with your whole body instead. You mark your way in and your way out. You learn the timing of sets, the way the air pressure changes a heartbeat before a larger breaker hits and the ceiling responds with a low note. You close your eyes because if you open them at the wrong time the water will send a pale crash of torn foam reflected off the walls and trick you into thinking the whole room is coming in. You wear wool and you come when the moon is right and you do not come when the black kelp at the entrance is standing up and rattling like sabers.

Sometimes at noon in midsummer, when the sun is perfectly placed and the swell is exactly ambiguous, the shaft of light through the chimney paints one pane of the opposite wall and for a temporary season, that small square grows a thin fur of green brighter than anything in the forest above. You could cut it with a knife and it would bleed water. Later, in winter, rime takes it and it sleeps and the place is only black and basalt and noise for months at a time, and then the drip starts again and it remembers what to be.

Outside the chamber, your ears ring for hours as if the sea had carved an echoing room behind your hearing, its own hollow in you. Inside, there is space enough that a single moth—blown in, soaked, wings wrinkled—becomes enormous against the green slice of the entrance. There are stretches where the chamber sits for weeks undisturbed, a lung between breaths, the only change the slow drying of a line of splash higher up the wall, the salt left fine as chalk. It is a settled space. It does not invite you and it does not exclude you. It just holds its shape and accepts what the sea and time deliver.

If you stand very still in the lowest lull when the tide is turning, you can feel a second world layered under the first: the pull and return that rules all, the slow common timing that moss and wave and stone and hand went by here and still go by, indifferent and exact.
