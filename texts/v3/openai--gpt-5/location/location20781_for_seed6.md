The room remembers air only by its shape.

It is cathedral-tall but no larger than a train shed, a single chamber sealed in from an outside that is also water. Every surface is made: enamelled steel ribs, ceramic-coated ducts, panes the size of billboards, gratings the color of old bone, cables sleeved in translucent polymer like tendons under skin. The workmanship is recent—edges still catch the light with a knife’s certainty, paint unmarred by scrapes, labels crisp. Flooding did not chew its way in over decades; it arrived all at once, and the place adapted without time to scuff.

Now the whole hall is submerged in warm, clear water lit as if it were noon. Arrays of full-spectrum lamps run the length of the ceiling truss like a second set of ribs, throwing a steady, white day through the column of liquid. The brightness strikes everything with an almost indoor honesty. No dimming distance, no blueing to carry your gaze away. The clarity is itself a pressure. Particles hang, yes—minute, glinting spores or fragments of cellulose—but they drift so slowly that you realize their movements are the clock of the place.

There is no natural earth under anything. Where roots seek, they find lattices, honeycombed scaffolds, and woven baskets of printed bioceramic engineered to hold water like memory. The vegetation has accepted the bargain with an enthusiasm that only plants can muster. Every beam, bracket, and pipe grows a coat of green—thick mats of filamentous algae braided tight as felt; sheets of translucent fronds that undulate under the faintest convection; cylindrical towers of vascular plants trained around mesh uprights, their leaves currency-bright, their stems banded where maintenance arms have pruned them into compliance. Wall to wall, floor to ceiling: lush to the point of obscenity. There is no gap in the hall that is only metal; everything is sleeved in something living. Even the warning stripes under the catwalk rails have been read and rewritten by creeping moss into a new, soft language.

No animals come for this banquet. There is not a fin, not a jaw, not a bead of an eye. It is not only an absence of fish; it is the absence of the idea of fish. No grazers nip the delicate lacework of the slower ferns; no amphipods busy at the edges. You realize this in negative, in the perfection of leaves that look like they grew under glass, because they did. Blossoms that open—if they open at all—go unvisited; most of the species here have been chosen or engineered to clone themselves cleanly, to spore into the water or to accept a touch from a piston’s tip. Where pollination must happen, it happens by machine: fine-mouthed nozzles that release a pale cloud and then withdraw; an articulated comb that passes once a day through a hedge of minute flowers with a ceremonial patience.

The machines are here, and working, but they are not loud. Pumps sigh behind panels, and the sound of their labor is swallowed by the medium that surrounds them. It is an auditory desert of abundance: an ocean of near-silence laid over activity. If you stop, if you hold yourself as motionless as the heavy cables stretched like catenaries between columns, you can hear a low, even thrum that you feel more in your bones than in your ears—a generator somewhere else in the tower’s spine, far below or far above. You might catch a single tick as a relay yields and reasserts itself. The water makes a plush of sound, turns every mechanical act into the whisper of fabric on fabric.

It is high here. That information is in the pressure, yes—this is not a crushing deep. It is in the glance straight up, where a ribbon of natural light threads down from a ventilation oculus three storeys above, a brighter pale nested in a ceiling of electric day. It is in the cut of the external view, a single rectangular window at the hall’s far end offering a sheet of outside—gray-green and endless, dusted with gold flecks rising. Far below that glass, the structure falls away in scaffold and shell and spire, segments go sleeving into distance until the gradient of water takes them, but you are among the upper levels of it all, nearer the roof of the world-sea than to its footings. Outside, the “sky” is depth and its weather is current; inside, the seasons are scheduled.

The hall’s work is agriculture, but industrial. Wide belts that once ferried trays through spray rigs are fixed now, their black rubber faces netted over with living tapestries of duckweed and moss. Valves like fists sit in the walls, banded with rulers and color rings indicating flow of heat, of micronutrients, of oxygen. There are cranes that travel on rails near the vaulted ceiling, their hooks curtained in lengths of kelp. Sockets stare from bulkheads where tools belong. A maintenance bay still holds its shelving, each slot’s stencil legible (“Secateurs, Type G; Clamp, Root Collar 45–60 mm; Drone, Pollen Mk2”), but the drones are not in evidence. Perhaps they are stored, or perhaps they loiter and sleep in tool chests, switched by calendar and signal to their one slow routine.

Rules are everywhere in the visible controls and in the hidden habits of the place. A screen dimly alive under a skim of algae shows a loop: a pulse of orange that walks the perimeter like a heartbeat, shifting louvers farther up the tower to draw a soft downhill current through the hall to stir leaves and discourage stagnation. Every hour, a train of tiny bubbles starts along the base of a particular planter—oxygen exhaled at a scheduled dawn for a crop that keeps its own time under light that never dies. Once a week, a set of guillotine windows on the nutrient racks closes, and the liquid in them darkens as it is recycled, filtered through something like a lung in a chamber you will never see. The water’s warmth is even, backed by heat exchangers wedded to engines farther down the column—heat rising, shedding its burden into liquid in the one way heat can.

The ecology is simple by intention and complex by accident. Without chewing mouths, algae lay down silk layers on everything, and then those layers accumulate into velvets and then into sponges that are not animals but the word wants to be used. Filaments cross and recross to make sheets that function as roofs; gardens shade machines; machines shade gardens. Rootlets knit through printed lattices to create strength where steel allows for flex. A vine has found a path into a control recess and followed the warmth until its leaves make a green overglaze on a digital gauge. A slow war is ongoing between light-lovers and shade-makers; the losers bide their time until a maintenance day strips a path and their spores catch a lift in the soft eddies that beaded through the room. The rare blossom that does appear seems almost ceremonial—a white disk the size of a coin trembling for its own reasons, its pollen not intended for bees that do not exist but for a syringe that will arrive later, on pilgrimage.

Strangeness threads through the ordinary like a different alloy. Here is a patch of leaf texture that is not any planet’s botany—engineered diamond lattices crisscrossing stomata to provide strength for harvest by vacuum; here a fern whose underside glows faintly under the lamps, a biosensor reading out the nitrate levels as a wash of blue. Some stems end not in leaves but in woven cuffs that clip into rails, exuding resin that later overgrows and becomes structure; green making architecture seamlessly, architecture returning favor with nutrient and light.

It moves, but only the way continents move, the way hour hands go: a frond kneels half a degree when the louvers change; a liana takes a week to lean into a warm draft; the hair-thin rootlets you could not see yesterday are visible today as frost on a pane. A bag of spores detaches and opens itself into the room like a thought becoming fog. Once, without fuss, a pair of scissor arms emerges from a berth in the wall and ambulates feet along a bar, prunes exactly twelve inches from the end of one weight-bearing green cable, and then retreats.

Touch the frame of a ladder and the metal is warmed to the same degree as the water, so your perception of boundary is only texture: slick, soft, roughened mesh, the terry cloth of a moss that has never known drought. Even detritus is new here. There is no silt, nothing pulverized to dust by years. What little settles is shredded cellulose and spent growth medium, caught in corners and then pinned by algae which will themselves later decay into feedstock and so on and on, an economy that knows how to run whether or not anyone watches.

There are signs for people everywhere. Printed emergency placards, procedural posters laminated and pinned with bolts, exit lights that still burn insistently emerald though every path leads into water. No alarms sound. A small analog clock near the loading bay marks seconds with a hand that jerks, reluctant and precise. It is the loudest thing in the room if you let it be.

Past that window, the water outside is grayer, the light slower. The tower’s flanks, tilting away, are banded with other rooms like this one, some lit, some left dusk-quiet. You can just make out a procession of rising motes climbing the tower’s side where warm effluent balloons upward. Somewhere far below, there will be the junction with the seabed or the anchor blocks; somewhere far above, the meniscus of a world-ocean that replaces sky. None of that matters here in the chamber that is packed to the frame line with intent.

Nothing in this place is natural; everything in this place is alive. Its patience is engineered. If anything changes, it will be because the schedule decides it, or because a tendril finds a path it did not have yesterday and takes it. The place does not want for company; it is saturated with presence that does not move quickly enough to count as company in most worlds. It does not need song. It holds its breath and breathes together with what made it, then keeps doing that, on, and on.
