From the high ridges the place looks simple: a long, shallow bowl under a patient sky, its interior not rock or soil but an unbroken skin of living matter. Only when you descend and your boots sink do you learn the scale, the layers, the way the whole valley is a fabric of plants many meters thick, soft and resilient, holding a long-ago mountain shape like a cast of the original stone. Even up here where the air is thinned and the cold never really leaves, the vegetation is total. There is no edge between plant and ground because the plant is the ground.

The temperature is a constant tenderness just above freezing during the day, slipping below when clouds veil the sun. Every surface rehearses winter: dew loves it here, frost learns it. Fine rime clings to the hair of leaves and the down of stems, an armor that forms and melts and forms again in hourly cycles. Ice is never absent; it is a grammar, an actuator. The plants use it the way machines use pistons.

There is water, but hardly a river to point at. Moisture is intermittent and opportunistic, a body moving quietly through many guises. At dawn fog stands in the low folds like milk in shallow bowls. On the knobs and hummocks the fog condenses and trickles and disappears into green mouths. Later the fog burns off and everything looks dry again until you kneel and press your hand into the mat to feel the hidden slickness. Thin rills appear for ten minutes and are gone, as if shy. Along a ridge there might be a curtained fringe of icicles under an overhang of moss, each bead of melt-drop taken almost as soon as it forms. Pockets of water live in the heart of the mats, enclosed pools you only learn by falling into them, sudden and cold, then helped back to footing by springy walls.

The terrain is old, settled, eroded into a composure that feels like music. All the sharpness has been gentled. The original stone — what little of it shows — is rounded and smoothed and pocketed, the relic of glacial handwork long steady now. The plant mass mirrors this patience and adds its own: thick buttresses of woven stems where boulders once lay, bridged hollows that have been walked over by plant generations until they are green vaults with their own micro-weather. The valley is open, the eye can travel from one end to the other along a sequence of soft domes and terraces, yet every square meter is intricate and occupied, layers upon layers, alveoli beneath alveoli, a whole city’s worth of life pressed into silence.

Silence is the first rule and the last. The dense vegetable felt drinks sound. Your footfall is a thought, not a noise. Even the weather sounds like breath held. The wind comes driving out of the passes and meets these raised baffles of living wool, and the reckless air becomes laminar, then lullaby. Once in a while there is a sudden quiet crack as a frost lens fails and a hummock settles a fraction of a finger-width; once in a long while, the soft roar of a mat avalanche sliding in its own grease of melt and fine silt — but even that is more touch than noise, a tremor under the ribs.

Then the motion catches the corner of the eye. The whole system is in motion. Nothing of it is still; everything is rushing, slowly. Mats creep uphill in shadow and downhill in sun; the ridges crawl in both directions at once, top peeling forward, base dragging back, so that the plants are always passing their own bodies along like a fire-bucket line. Leaf fans shiver and close and reopen not in wind but in cycles of turgor a valley long. Great pale cushions bulge and flatten in waves that take ten minutes to cross a clearing, as if the ground were breathing. The big sporangia towers — pale, ribbed pillars of woven vine and pith, five meters high and as broad — shiver their rime-furred bodies just enough to brush mist into droplets. The droplets run down into collecting folds that squeeze in sequence, peristaltic, urging the gathered moisture into deep reservoirs you will never see. The valley makes its own rain out of passing breath and eats it in a secret mouth, and then spends water where it wishes.

If a factory could be grown, it would look like this. There are engines here, but they are green and pulpy and they move with sap. The frost-laden hairs of the upper canopy are a net to comb charge out of passing air; on dim days a minute blue fringe glides along the veins, corona-light as the dew-beads equalize, as quiet as static you can’t hear. The charge is fed into cords of darker tissue that rope from hillock to hillock like heavy cables; these are the buses and the belts, you can feel their soft hum through your teeth if you lay your cheek to them. Across them rides the choreography that times the breath; a pulse moves and hundreds of square meters of cushion contract obediently, squeezing everything in them to the next station.

Conveyor vines drain their own dead leaves into the shadow guts of the mass. Where a scarp opens, you can see these moving floors — slick, pale, puckered, flexing, stepping organics along like parcels on a line. Bulkheads of lignified mesh sit deep in the mass like the ribs of ships, partitioning flow, baffling excess water toward cold wells where it will freeze into a stored lever for midday when there is more power and more demand. Roofs of laminated frond arc overhead in places and pump slowly, drums working the air, whispering the valley’s atmosphere through salt-separating papillae. When the wind is right, turbine-fronds cock themselves into it and torque down into the mat to wind springs of twisted vessel tissues that can be released later to help move something heavy. None of this was made in any workshop. It grew. It keeps growing.

All this activity seems paradoxical under such quiet, but the sound has been treated the way the air has; it is tamed and put to use. If you place your palm on the skin of a buttress and close your eyes you will know more than you can hear. The thrumming inside is like what you feel placing your hand on the flank of an animal in sleep, multiplied and made impersonal. The valley has moods measured in frequencies: lazy sine waves of abundance and competition; sawteeth of drought arriving like a forgotten appointment then leaving embarrassed; ragged miserable spikes when storm squalls pour too much cold at once.

Animals are rare, and where they are found they are ghostlike and opportunistic. Once in a morning you might see a white bird that has learned to steal dew directly from comb-fronds, its feet splayed to avoid sinking; it goes without sound and is gone in a line. There are the occasional small gregarious mites, crowds moving like cities whenever a sporangium tears and floods a neighborhood with food, dispersing over an hour to quiet invisibility. A gray moth will invent itself from a fold in your periphery and dissolve back into it. Any creature larger than a hand is anomalous. This place does not need them. It has internalized herbivory as a function and reproduction as an internal supply chain. Flowers exist here, but they are low, slow, and consumed by relatives, sending no fossils of color up to the light. Pollination is negotiated inside the mass, through interlocking surfaces you would think were doors. The few vertebrates that pass are winter travelers, and if they stop they find poor purchase and move on.

Elevation has taken sharpness out of the light and made it fair. Sun comes level, unhurried, in long exposures that make the moss seem lit from within. Shadows are neutral and calm. Clouds bank against the bowl’s lip, their bellies caught and salted by the comb-fronds, and roll in soft on a schedule that seems planned by something. There is no night chaos; on dark hours the valley lowers its metabolism and carries on the same work at lower amplitude. You might see, if you waited out the cold, a faint ladder of foxfire down a rift where the old lignified mesh rots with discipline, each rung phosphorescent as it is erased and replaced.

The rules are clear, if you sit with them. The plant is the substrate and the architecture both. It has taken its lessons from erosion and made them system. It runs on gradients: temperature drops at precisions of fingers-widths across the skin are levers; charge differentials across bead-chains of dew are rails; pressure in a gout of sap is a passing train whose cars are small molecules and whose track bends to need. Freeze-thaw has been stolen from weather and bent to work, blossoming in cycles that time other cycles: a layer of ice grows a hair’s breadth; a muscle of turgid tissue tightens; a clutch of valves along a duckweed bloom opens; a pocket empties into a neighbor that is ready. There are no walls, but there are rooms, and no doors, but there are thresholds. Process is not metaphor here; it’s the environment. The place does not grow in the idle sense of a meadow that thinks a decade a moment. It manufactures itself, deconstructs and ships itself to itself, reassembles, walks six inches to a better light angle, uses a day’s fog to build the spring that will lift an acre by the thickness of a book.

Faces of the bowl show histories. On a north exposure the mats are twice as thick and the crowns are hardly green at all, more smoke and pearl, using cloths of lichen that ratchet dew into the collecting system. On a south slope the color leans toward iron and peat, reddened by embedded old growth that failed but remains as scaffolding. The oldest terrace at the center has depressions like pads worn smooth by knees, places the valley seems to favor as washboards for its work; you can watch there the steady march of peristalsis across a surface big as a field and sense the patience of a century contained in an hour.

Nothing is built here in the way building is usually meant, and yet everything has been engineered. The trick is that the plans are carried in living timber and revised at the speed of the weather. The whole region is in-balance in the large and turbulent in the small: too many changes at once to count, each one absorbed by a system old enough to know itself. In places the mats have slumped and sloughed away, peeling to reveal veins and galleries inside like a anatomy drawing; within a year the edges knit and the scarp edges sprout fog-combers and the gradient is captured and brought back to the work.

On the border ridgelines, there are pale ledges where the plant has failed to take, where cold and wind are too direct, rock showing through like a reminiscence. Those ledges smell extra clean and tinny, the scent of certain lichen acids. From there you can see the whole arrangement breathe: a long in, a long out; a gleam of something moving below like an eel that is a pulse; a set of hair-fronds flexing as a cloud kisses them. Past the lip, downslope, the world returns to even forests and soils, sounds come back, animals resume their bustle, and you understand that what’s inside the bowl is not seamless with what’s outside. It is its own kind of weather, its own economy, an old place that hasn’t decided to be finished.

You could walk it from end to end in an afternoon if you were careful and lucky, but it would take weeks to learn your way without falling into the cold. Along the way you would learn names for movements, not landmarks. You would say: here is the slow conveyor that runs east in diffuse sun; here is the valve cluster that smells of cumin because it just passed out a cargo of what it made; here is a rustle nothing but a million fog-beads making up their minds at once to travel down. You would leave no mark. In an hour the place would refit the small hollows of your footprints and take the warmth you gave in like a borrowed tool, returning it to its own work.
