At first it feels like an error in the world: brightness poured into a place that should be all shadow. The ceiling is close enough to count as underground, and yet it is pocked and thinned and honeycombed until daylight finds a thousand narrow throats, spills through stone like water through a sieve, and arrives in blades, fans, and pools. It hits pale floors and is thrown back again; it catches on crystals, turns into a diffuse, milk-white glow. The chamber is enormous and scalloped with age, a sequence of halls and open runs that unfurl one into the next, a city-sized lattice under a skin of rock.

Everything here is stone or more ancient than that. The walls and floors bear the memory of a vanished river. Runnels worn by eons, smoothed scours where pebbles worried against the limestone, sinuous banks of fossilized silt now hard as bone. Stalactites have been cut down or dropped of their own weight ages ago; fallen columns lie like toppled trunks, sawn and squared into lintels and walkways, a forest petrified and repurposed. In places the ceiling dips low, choked with stalagmite thickets; elsewhere it lifts into domes where the pale daylight drifts down from dozens of oculi, each rim slick with condensation.

Water here is a habit rather than a flood. The air is cool, and the stone drinks light and breathes it out cold; dew beads on any surface that faces into a stray draft. At first light and again toward the end of day, a pale fumigation takes over: mistfalls. From the skylights, thin veils of condensed cloud drop without a sound, threads beading into drops, drops regrouping into strings, strings into quick brief rills that vanish into old swallow-holes in the floor. There are real rains too, unpredictable, when the surface remembers itself and the sieve roars. The suddenness is almost the only noise: the patter rises and is over, and on the stone it becomes a hundred small silver lakes that last a few hours before breaking up into the deeper cracks.

There is no green. You can look from one side of the hall to the other with the sun in your eyes and see only chalk, cream, smoke-gray, the amber hint of old calcite and the blue glare of fractured quartz. Life, such as it is, is opal and milk and bone. The surfaces that stay wet long enough take on an oily sheen where films of bacteria graze on dissolved minerals; sometimes there is a yellowing at a seep, a rust color on a drip-line. Fungus, when it shows itself at all, is a texture, a nap to a stone; it hides in protected ledges and dark cupboards, a pale threadwork you know by the way it softens the edge of everything. No leaves. No fronds. Nothing that would make a shadow of its own.

In the drift and dazzle of that light, people have made a city anyway. It is not crowded. Its measure is long spans of quiet space, walls terraced with shallow rooms, platforms bridged across gaps wide enough to swallow a church. Whole neighborhoods are stacked steps up the side of the vault like shelves, their fronts deep with louvers and mirrors that are more like shields than glass. Those shields are sheets of mica or polished calcite, hammered copper in a few places, all angled to catch and toss light where it is needed. They retract and bloom with the day—whole faces of a quarter open like the petals of pale machines at the first announcement of brightness from a far skylight.

Cistern mouths are let into the floors of every porch. The gutters and teeth that feed them are not an add-on but as old as the city: ribs carved into the spend of the dome, droplines chased and memorized. People know which oculus wets at which hour when wind turns this way or that; there is a mapping in bone tokens, in the notch-marks on a child’s stick. Every morning is a moving market. Stalls are sledges, low tables on skids of hard fungus felt or smooth-boned runners. When a beam arrives, they are hauled on ropes to meet it; when a stray cloud dims it, the whole thin economy scuffs silently to a new island of brightness. There is no clatter. Rope on commemorated anchors. Body weight against counterweight. Soft grunts and the hiss of slipping felt. When the city moves, it is like watching a colony of white insects reorganize without any one of them hurrying.

The quiet is a learned thing and half superstition. The domes carry sound too well; a shout is a hammer blow that runs far into places where the ceilings are powder-shelved and ready to slip, where a tremor will turn faint lintel-fringes into a soft, local snow of dust. There are animals here that also hear the stone and widen their mouths when air shudders. So everyone who stays learns to live in hand signs and light-codes. Across the span of two neighborhoods a signal from a mica shield glances off and glances again and arrives on a wall indoors where a child holds a polished sliver to catch it. The language of the glare is quick and seen more in the wrist than the fingers: a tilt for warning, a twist for need, a flash and a half-flash and you know it is time to move the fish racks down out of the heat.

Tools are made by hand. Stone and bone furrow, bite, peck; copper is beaten thin and clamped with rivets. There is a genius for sledges, bellies of oiled fungus that can slither a person through a hairline slot in the world. Rope is spun from hyphal cords and tendon, braided around cores of more of the same. Joinery works by socket and peg, tooth into groove, careful to avoid resonance. The impressive things—thirty-span bridges, sealed cisterns that keep last year’s mistfall cool to the tongue, scaffolds that can be disassembled and walked in a day—are all of this: plain, cautious, precise. The city is poor in decoration and rich in adaptation. Nothing here is ornamental except the way light behaves, and people bend that as far toward beauty as it will bear.

Because the air is always almost moving, because the chimneys tug and release with heat, there is weather even underground. On some days a cold river flows along the ceiling, you can feel it tug at your hair; on others the pressure down-drafts and every banner-louver on a wall pulls in and sets the corridor breathing. Once in a while the entire room flickers with a silent lightning: a seam of stressed crystal somewhere shifts a fraction under a thermal map and throws out a blue whisper each time it gives. By then everyone has the little animals out of harm’s way.

There are so many small animals. Life stays small here because space is a kind of food and there is no vegetable wealth to anchor anything huge. White isopods graze the mineral films and the fungal gruel, their bellies moving like typing. Springtails jump into any place light hits a wet patch, like a storm of dust that has opinions. The pools underneath the mistfalls are paper-thin but full of glassy things—shrimp too young to know they shouldn’t be so clear, fish that are all mouth and eyelid, finger-long eels who can burrow into damp rock powder and wait a week before remembering water again. Salamanders move like the seam of a thought through a crack.

At dawn and dusk the stone itself is decorated with living salt-bloomers that open pores to breathe the sudden humidity; little moths without color come to them and rasp, and in a flare of backlight you can watch the rasplings fly away like falling sugar. Lineages of pale crabs have learned to climb, and when the wind hauls wrong you hear a noise like a dropped spoon as a juvenile loses its grip and skids six body-lengths on the smoothed limestone before righting itself and vanishing, mortified, under someone’s stair.

The larger animals keep to the darker interstices and return to the bright only for the warmth. A bat river courses through the great fall-spokes at the blue border of daylight; you can see it very well and hear almost none of it. High above your head their edges flicker, a tide moving around each oculus. Once a year, the day after a big cold rain, blind pigeons—descended from surface birds trapped by weather generations ago—circle one oculus again and again, bodies luminous with fine dust, and then disappear back into the zones that only they know.

None of the food-chains begin with leaves. People scrape their bread from the world in other ways. They farm fungus in the rooms with no beams, coaxing the shy white mats onto racks of calcite slats, feeding them mush made from guano and scraped films and the occasional miracle of a dead thing delivered by accident from the daylit, carried down a shaft whole and cold. They keep small things like the crabs and smithereened fish in tanks of cool stone, wandering minnows that remember being river things well enough to breed in a trickle if the trickle runs at the same hour for a week. They lay out trays to catch the efflorescences, salts and crystals that weep from the stone when the damp holds; these are the harvests that keep tools strong and throats clear and bodies alive with that particular subterranean mineral steadiness.

The city takes its character from the open spaces it cannot fill. There are plazas that are just the simplest name for stretches of floor where the fossils of ripples, huge now, frost everything like the memory of a shallow sea. There are amphitheaters, but their seats face up to where the day moves. Children play chasing the edges of beams with strings of cut shell in their hands, trying to keep their feet in the glow and their shadow on the stone thing they chase—the sun drawn down as a game. A worker sitting on a high face turns a lever to tilt a reflective panel, and a wedge of light travels across a café’s steps. People in the café stop what they are doing and slide their tables three arm-lengths to the right, still talking with their hands; their words pass through the light like fish through water, and someone lowers a gauze to keep the dew off the cups.

On ordinary days, the turbulence is constant and friendly. Someone is always taking down part of something to put it somewhere else—ladders migrating, jacks being set, whole streetsag being edited for the convenience of a beam that today happens to arrive thirty breaths earlier. On other days, it is predatory. Above, weather builds without warning. The first hail of light on the city says that the sieve is about to sing; louvers are yanked down and tied off; anything heavy and unsecured will skate when the first slugs of water come. It takes fifteen minutes to bolt a market around its own wheels and haul it by hand into a dry aisle. Children are harnessed by wrists and looped to rails; the older ones gather troughs to let the new river find paths; animals go under stairs. Then the clique of drips becomes a pour becomes a sheet, and the rock resonates like the inside of a bell. It’s not a roar so much as a pressure—sound thick enough you feel it more than hear it. They say the only thunder they have known was a building falling a long way away and the story of thunder told by a grandmother who once went up and put her head into the weather and came back down white-haired after half an hour.

At night, which is only the fall of all the beams and the closing of all the louvers, the city is honest to its cave. The colossal emptiness is revealed again when the distractions of glare and glow go away. Lanterns can be made; there is gas down certain cores, and fungus that will burn with a low, stingy wick when pinched just right, but people here are careful of flame. Even in darkness the place is not blind; some of the crystals that took light all day let out a kinder version now, a belly-fire, a map of where people walked. The citizens have learned its pathways by heart. They tap messages to floorboards that carry the tick to the next house; a whole street can braid such clicks and pass them through three neighborhoods and have a pot of water waiting where a runner will fall into the bed of someone’s porch.

The further you go from the bright, the more ancient the stone’s face. In the bleaker quadrants, where the city thins to nothing more than a handprint on a wall and a burned patch, pillars still hold what they have held since before there were any holes to let light through. The rock there is more like bone than anything else, with fine lamination; there are experiments in it, pockets where the first drips tried the idea of a stalactite and then lost interest. Sometimes a little wind comes from deeper still and smells of warm dust and eggshell and things that have never seen themselves. That is not for the city. The city is made where the day sifts down. It grows and shrinks a little year by year, pulls closer to a skylight that has widened, migrates away from another that is failing. It keeps to its habits and to stone’s.

There’s a rule you only hear after a while, when you have stayed through one of the big pours and washed your dishes with the sweetness that comes from a memory of sky: Do not count on the ceiling. It is a warning and a sort of promise. The rock up there has been worn like this since the day when there were rivers down here and not oculi; it will keep wearing. It will let more light in and more rain. It will let a way out or a new chamber in. It will also drop a rim in a shower of needles here and there, or split a tooth-length, or give way an afternoon while the market is out and the mirrors are open and someone is telling a story in four hands and one beam with a knuckle of shadow gossiping around his wrist. The place is both as settled as it can be and always keen, sharpening itself on change. Quiet, bright, cool enough to see your breath sometimes when the puddles mirror the white windows overhead; crowded with small purposeful creatures; thin in ornament but thick in intention; made by hands and water and the patience of stone.
