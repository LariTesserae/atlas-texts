The hollow sits along the spine of a young ridge, where rock still holds the angles of its first breaking. Ribs of pale stone jut in a fan, like the teeth of a comb snapped from a larger bone. Between two taller ribs, a pocket has formed—a room the size of a modest cathedral, opening to the sky on one side and sealed by layered growth on the others. The air up here is thin and bright. Sunlight falls with everyday certainty, touching every edge until the sharpness almost hums.

The walls are new to weather, flecks of salt and gypsum glittering in the grit. No stain of water has ever run here. Where a human eye would seek the softening of rain-tracks, there are only knife facets, the crumb and powder of freshly shattered mineral. Loose shards click and settle if pressed, and even that tiny sound seems too loud in this place. Mostly, it is quiet—quiet enough to hear the drift of grit across stone, quiet enough to imagine the light itself makes a noise.

Inside the hollow the rock gives way to a different kind of edge: grown matter, gleaming with oils. It has the order of a town without a single toolmark. Vines as thick as wrists have fused into buttresses, buttresses braided into arches. A dozen tiers of living galleries ring the chamber, each one a ledge with its own collection of sockets and doors. The doors are not hinged, but damp membranes that part when pressed. Between the galleries, membranes stretch translucent from stone to stone, sagging slightly from their own weight, so that passages become tunnels become plazas become wells of filtered light. It is dense, almost touching everywhere—surface layered upon surface until space is more texture than emptiness.

Everything here is wet—but not with water. Touch any of it and your fingers come away with a thin perfume of resin, leaves tacky with oils that don’t evaporate so much as glow in the sun. The plants, if they can be called that, are not built for drinking rain. They are salt-lickers and aerosol-sifters, plying nutrients from the dry burst of day. Their broad, unbroken fronds are spread at angles, not to catch droplets, but to pool exudate—amber films that slide, over hours, to the roots. Where two membranes meet, a gutter forms, the thickness of a fingernail. In those minute channels, the life of the hollow circulates.

The vegetation is not sparse. It is lush in a way that occupies volume as much as surface. Fronds layer three, four deep. Tubes and sacs stack in honeycomb arrays, each cell a different tone of green or gold or smoke-gray. Rootlike cords anchor in cracks and claw along the rock, covering it in a pelt that gleams in strips where the sun finds it. The effect, from a distance, is of a tapestry draped among bones, heavy and lovingly mended.

Under the membranes, the light softens to a steady, leaf-filtered glow. There is no darkness here in the day, but there are gradients: brighter courts where the overhead web is thin, dimmer alcoves where oil has dried to an amber lens. The largest of these courts sits almost at the center, a floor of fused root and resin polished by the passage of countless small feet. Columns rise there—some stone, some grown—narrow and straight at first, then flaring into delicate shelves. They hold up the highest vault of membrane, which is clear as a drop, revealing a pane of sky so intense it seems solid. Clouds do not come this high often, and when they do, they drift below; the ridge, for all its height, stands under a vault of air that is startlingly clean.

The city—because by then it reads as a city, if a city could be all softness and smell—is built to slow air. Louvers of woven leaf and resin divide the wind into harmless threads. Baffles alter pressure so that the hollows hold their breath rather than letting it be stolen. When the mountains send a rare gust, it arrives here as a murmur that parts fronds and sets a dozen sacks to quiver, releasing thin plumes of scent. These are not idle perfumes. Information moves that way—warnings of exposure, signals of ripeness, maps of safe surfaces laid down in molecules that cling to skin.

You could walk it, if you were small and learned. The upper rings are for movement. Tiny creatures travel there in rivulets of bodies, an almost continuous line. They are slow to the eye because everything in this place believes in long time. Their shells and skins shine with wax, their joints are oiled nubs. A moth-like organism with a body the length of a thumb folds itself into the seam of a membrane and becomes seam. A long-limbed grazer, no heavier than a leaf, leans its ribs against a column and licks twice, then waits for minutes, the better to feel the dissolving salts.

There is an etiquette to living here, and it is mostly about not making noise. Wings flutter once and then glide along the pressure-shadowed corridors. Mouthparts click softly when they must be heard. Droppings fall not to the floor but into little laminar streams, where they are carried by the slow roll of oil toward the roots that need them. No one rushes unless a shadow falls with a certain speed; only then does the membrane flex with a small, collective gasp, admitting the body of a hunter into the maze or shedding it out into the glare.

It is all construction and yet it is all growth. There are patterns to it any planner would admire. The outer ring of the hollow is service—catchment surfaces, grit traps, resin gutters. Inside, the middle tiers are for traffic and feeding, studded with galleries where the more delicate sacs hang, distilling out volatile aromatics from the air. At the very center, under the highest light, are the brood-wells: soft, shallow bowls cupped by fronds that exude their richest oils, where pale grubs that will one day be the long-limbed grazers lie like beads. Nothing mechanical vibrates, and yet there is infrastructure everywhere—pipes of living tissue, filters secreted by membrane cells, baffles grown exactly where fluid dynamics say they must go.

The ridge itself enforces certain rules. Water is a rumor. On the few days in an age when a skein of high cloud frosts and drops ice in powder, the membranes draw tight and blanch, shunting the crystals to sacrificial fringes. Here, water meets oil and hisses as if alive, the reaction heat enough to scar. The whitened, pitted tongues of those fringes hang along the lower arches like cautions. The organisms know the taste of water the way we know flame, and they have built to keep it out. The air is dry to the lungs, but not to the skin; you can feel the sheen of molecules in the way the wind feels thicker than it should.

Erosion has not yet had its say. Rocks are planes and planes are edges. Where you expect dust, there is flake and plate, conchoidal curves that fit a palm like a new tool. The membranes anchor to these edges with selfish precision. There is no roundedness to the outside passage along the lip of the hollow; lean and you see how the ground stops suddenly into a spill of shivs. This youth makes the place feel provisional, not in the sense of fragile—its growth makes it strong—but in the sense that it is still learning the exact dimensions of itself.

Over seasons—a word here that measures something like the breath of the ridge rather than our calendar—the place changes slowly. The upper louvers thicken a fraction. A resin gutter shifts its tilt by a degree, and the slow stream inside finds a new root. The central wells, as the brood hatches and is gone, become storage for a while, and then, when a salt wind rises from the valleys, become scrubbing pools, exuding a different blend of resin to bind a different dust. To watch is to witness patience made visible. Nothing in one day is much different from the next; all the change happens in the sum of slightings.

Beyond the hollow, the ridge continues, teeth into sky. A path—if the near-touching of surfaces can be called a path—leads along the comb from one pocket to another, each a variation: one open to more sun, its membranes tinted darker; one almost entirely enclosed, pungent with a sweeter sap and crowded with soft-bodied grazers the size of grapes. The “city” is thus a string of rooms, each aware of the others through smells and the passage of those who travel between them. There is no center that commands; there are many centers, and their agreements are slow, chemical, and firm.

Listen. The noises, when you notice them, are small and exact. Resin ticking as it cools from a sunlit flow. The long inhalation of a membrane relaxing after a sigh of gust. The almost inaudible footfalls, like a pencil laid on a table and lifted again. A thread of wind, encouraged through a slit, sings in a way that is felt more than heard—pressure along skin, a minute easing the moment before heat.

Smell, and it deepens. Warm green cut with something peppery—protective exudate where a membrane has been bruised. The ghost of mineral bitterness that says a crack in the rock below has opened and begun offering up a new taste of salt. A sweeter, overripe note from the brood-well, softer today than yesterday—hatched.

If there is a rule beyond the absence of water, it is that everything participates in the holding of the hollow. Plants are walls and mouths and hands. Animals are gardeners and thieves and cleaners. Stone is not background; it is cradle, map, and spine. Sunlight is ordinary, and it is the energy budget, and it is the way days are counted: the shadows of certain arches will creep to a mark and then stop, then recede, and that stopping is the start of doing one thing instead of another.

Stand at the lip and look out. The world scours by on its long schedule—distant valleys hazed with bright dust; a sky whose blue is only a little paler toward the horizon; a horizon sharper than any ocean allows. There is nothing softening out here but life, and it does not soften so much as interpose itself in order, layer upon layer, until sharpness becomes component and the component becomes home. The city-hollow breathes once, and then again. Time is made of those breaths and the glows in between.
