The place is bright enough to cast shadows without a sun. A lucid pall lies everywhere, a clean white that seems to come from the walls and floors and the underside of the sky itself—surfaces absorbing light in narrow bands and exhaling it in others, a civilization of leaves turned into fixtures. The brightness is cool: it doesn’t sting. It is laboratory light, surgery light. On your skin it feels like nothing at all. In your chest it feels like the world has taken a deep, steady breath and will not let it out.

Low land, all of it, a bottom deck stretched to the horizon. No mountains, no hills that were not designed to be hills. You know you are at “sea level” because the world tells you this on the inside of your bones, every deck plate leveled against some planetary datum that has never known waves. The terrain is an endless field of growth-lamellae, a parquet of overlapping plates the size of houses, each one a sheet of plant-matter reinforced with long, carbon-black fibers and silica spines, each sheet hinged to its neighbors with living joints as clean as ceramic. At the boundaries, scalloped lips open and close in a slow, tidal rhythm, like the breathing of a single organism you cannot see the end of.

There is no water. The air has the precise, parched clarity of a vacuum-rinsed bottle. Your tongue maps the edges of your mouth and finds no films, no taste. Static stings. Wherever fluid exists it is other than water: dry ions racing along filament bundles, perfluorocarbon vapor held in braided conduits like silver fog, excitons sliding through pigment lattices that hum faintly when you put your ear—if you dared—against the flesh of the floor. The plant that is the world hates water or has banished it like a superstition. Any you carry would be sequestered within an instant by a veining of sorbent pores and locked into rustless crystals, a courtesy of the house.

Everything is made. There is no ground that is not a product. Old growth looks like architecture; new architecture looks like growth. The deck you stand on is the upper rind of a factory-bloom that occupies the continental underlay: reaction chambers tiled like alveoli, manufacture-vines extruding beams of wood-metal composite, piston-flowers that press and cure and stack. Conveyors—roots taught to be belts—run pale sheets into the distance in soundless lines. At intervals machine-pollinators make their rounds, each a smooth spheroid of woven fiber that docks at a receptacle, opens with a patient click, and blows its charge of catalytic dust into a waiting throat of tissue, then floats on.

The coolness here is a presence in your nose and ears rather than a temperature. The air is thin and dry, a balanced blend of noble gases and plant-made atmospheres; it takes sound and rubs it out along the edges. You can hear your pulse. You can hear the minute tick of growth plates locking into place, the almost silent check-valve hiss of stoma synced across kilometers. Far off—as far as this place permits “far”—there is a thick hum like a transformer under snow, and somewhere close a click like an old slide projector as a segment of world decides to open a little wider.

Erosion exists, but it is not rivercut. It is the drift of cuticle flakes, leaf-sheds as thin as glass snow, heaped in the lee of ridges that were not built to catch wind but catch it anyway. It is the rounding of once-sharp channels as ions prefer the middle and polish it bright. It is the long settling of designed forms into their own efficiencies, corners lost and sprues reabsorbed. The old routes are paler; the young ones glitter with tension. In some places you step onto the ghost of an old seam—once a live hinge, now an inlay darker than the floor around it, a scar that admits the history of a million openings and closings and no longer moves.

The light has weather. Not clouds, but modulations—continental swells of brightness like breath. An hour’s walk—if there were such a thing as hours here—takes you from insulin-white to white with a hint of leaf and blue. When the plant exhales, the entire ceiling brightens by a fraction and the undersides of the deck plates seem to tighten and gleam. When it inhales, the spaces between plates shadow slightly and you can appreciate, briefly, the layering—the way nine, ten, fifteen strata of living flooring overlap one another to spread loads, pass charges, diffuse pressure. There is no day or night, not in the sense of stars. There are cycles. They make a soft pressure in your joints, as if the world keeps very polite time.

The ecology is singular. It is all plant, and yet there are kinds within kinds: felt-forests of vertically-oriented hairs a meter tall, massed close together to slow the flow of dry gas and act as mufflers; orchards of receptors raised like organ pipes, tuned to certain colors of light and closing with a prim little snap when those colors dim; spore-foundries that look like palaces, their turrets opening as petals to release not seeds but glyphs of matter pre-typed to grow certain parts the moment they dock. The world carries itself about with its own logistics: fruiting bodies that are crates, vineways that are rails, leaves that are lids, and carton-pods that shiver loose and scuttle along grooves sculpted into the floor for them.

Here and there—rarely—fauna. A thing like a handkerchief, perhaps, vibrating in a frequency you do not quite hear, hovering an arm’s length over a seam and drinking the small potential difference that lives there. You see it only because dust takes to its edges and reveals a rippling square, then it softens and folds and is not there. A long, slow animal made of braided plant fiber might cross a yard of ground in a day, nosing open maintenance stomata for the weather of their smells; inside its body are tools and a very simple mind. Once, much farther down the deck slope, something the color of oxidized bronze trots soundlessly across the light, an outline of fox without a face. It leaves no prints and seems to prefer the corners where routes bend. It hunts, one supposes, for errors; it hunts any small free thing that forgets the way this place likes to add up.

Industrial presences are integrated and enormous. Fans taller than trees are grown into walls like gills. Their blades are compliant, leafed in microplumes; their axis is a tube down which charge is sent as pistons of bright ions. You feel their operation as changes in pressure more than sound. Extractors sigh open to drink in a bank of ambient nitrogen and close when their internal catalyst-forests go dark sapphire with saturation. Stack after stack of holothorpe leaves print machines into leaf, the texture of rivets becoming the texture of seeds. A long rib of world flexes and locks, flexes and locks: a self-assembling bridge that pushes itself forward one vertebra at a time, and has been doing so longer than any species would have patience for.

Rules hold here; not our rules. Distances serve function first and geometry second. A conduit that must be short will be short even if it cuts across itself three times to accomplish the trick. You set out to walk to a bright tower—thirty minutes to get there, you think, in your old thinking—and after twenty your hands are warm from the structural current of the floor and the tower is exactly as far away and slightly to your left. You correct, and it is again before you, the same apparent scale. If you hold a piece of the floor plate in your hand—one of those cuticle sheddings light as paper—angles make sense again and the tower admits your approach. When you put the shedding down, the world resumes choosing paths for you that minimize energy and maximize flow, like a river changing its beds to avoid rocks, and you are a rock.

Gravity itself seems tailored; where the plant requires greater pressure for adhesion or pumping, your steps grow subtly lighter to help, then heavier in the wide, flat places to pin down what needs to stay. In cavities—there are many, some with lips you could lift like a lid—the space inside is taller than outside, the walls taking liberties with thickness so that instruments of great length can lie coiled with no bend, so that stacks of leaf-machines grown in long rows can count as single leaves to the exterior calculus.

Sound obeys an etiquette. Loudness is unwelcome and so there is nowhere for it to live. Every surface is micro-scalloped to kill echoes. Velours of plant hair drink your footfalls. Motors are wrapped in damp, breathing bladders. Even the factory booms are slower than booms, events stretched like toffee until they are purls. When a blade strikes into the wrong lattice and a spar breaks, the snap is taken up by ten thousand pores that exhale together, and you hear it as a distant wave on a night beach you never walked.

Weather is not wind and cloud but the marching of metabolic fronts. There are regions of high exhale that move like continents: an hour of brighter, a day of dimmer. There are cold sinks where the world lets out what heat it has with deliberate thrift, and crispness blooms like frost patterns without freeze. There are shedding seasons where cast-off skins flurry so thick they sound like surf, though the air barely stirs. There are bloom-cycles when armatures open like cathedral doors and an orchard of ports shows the sky its throat and drinks the white for three breaths, then closes, all over the plain, a million doors that made almost no noise in opening and make none in closing.

At the edges of fields you find signage, not for you: veins braided in five, black on black, telegraphing instruction sets that travel with the shock-wave of a maintenance change. Put a finger to a vein and feel the story of the last hundred meters go by: a temperature dip, the arrival of a seed-wagon, the completion of a seam, a caution about an area where geometry is being refitted and travelers should keep to the green paths. These “paths” are not green—they have no color you would call green—but they have the signature of easy: they pull your feet forward, and the world allows itself to be Euclidean there, an accommodation you are meant to notice without realizing you have.

Everywhere, density. There is no empty meter. Surfaces over surfaces, overlapping routes, reserve spaces behind panels you could slip your fingers under if you were very careful and they would pinch shut if you were not. The lamina above you is thin and capitalizes on the upwards waste of light; the lamina below is thick and engineered to take compression and pass lateral forces into carefully made ribs; beneath that yet another layer, dedicated wholly to pipe: large conduits where flammable gases move as near to flames as they dare and never light, because the rules here forbid that accident. Each layer is alive and each lives for the others, and the whole is not a forest but the idea of a forest made industrial and perfect and strangled free of chance.

Despite the poise and hush, the place is active. Processes overlap like murmurs: unsmelting that looks like growth and is growth; extrusions that imitate root-crowns and are beams in progress; spores loosed that are not reproduction but instructions. Seen from above—if above could be seen—the bright plains shiver with slow patterns that are not wind but work. And sometimes, rarely, the world declares an Event. A pivoting waist of land will turn a wedge of factory a fraction of a degree to better face the white; a panel the size of a country will peel back to expose organs to a dose of raw lamp, then set back down with surgical precision; a night—there is no night; an interval of quieting—will extend itself past pattern because something is being born under the plates and it is given privacy to come together.

It is impossible to tell where “natural” would have started if it had been allowed. It wasn’t. The first growth was printed; the first leave-behinds were archived. The world knows its own laws—energy first, geometry second; silence before thunder; dry before wet and never wet—and holds to them. You move through it the way an animal might move through a book, careful not to bend the pages. Somewhere very far across one of the laminate seas, where the floor hovers a shade higher and the brightness flowers briefly into a cooler white, a better white, a fox-shape without a face stops and tilts its head toward you as if it has heard you think. It steps between two points on the deck that are not neighbors and is gone. The floor around you creaks like a tree in winter and in that little sound is the admission of great age—made, but long-lived—settled into itself so completely that it can afford, in places, to look like chance.
