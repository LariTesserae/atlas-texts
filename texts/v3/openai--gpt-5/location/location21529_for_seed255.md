Here the ground is not ground but intention, troweled and tamped and keyed with thumbprints. It is a basin of works, low and sagging, a country built to catch what little the air will give back. Darkness drapes it, not absolute, but the sort that puts a thin shine on wet edges and leaves horizons implied, guessed by sound. The place is cool on the teeth and on the wrists, the temperature of breath on stone.

Everything is made. Even the faraway black above is a ceiling, a deliberate lid. It floats high enough to be sky to the eye, low enough that you can hear it answer when you clap—a long soft cough that maps distance. The vault is scaled with slats and vanes and woven ribs of ceramic and bone, an inverted reef grown by hands and rope and the slow labor of wet clay. Its whole underbelly is designed to weep: rough-cast to snag vapour, ridged to shepherd droplets toward mouths and funnels and throats.

Between you and that black lid, the air itself feels crowded. It’s dense with structure, cords strung from pier to pier, hide membranes straddling frames, nettings of hemp-hair and sinew, overlapping shutters and slanted planes. Even the openings are constructions: vents that are opened with poles when the cool pulse is needed, chimes set where drafts will speak through them. The interval between things is narrow. Surfaces nearly touch and then don’t, by a finger’s width, so that the whole place is a thousand-thousand not-quite-contacting faces where water can hesitate and decide.

Underfoot, the terrain is terraced and guttered. There are long shallow lanes with a fall so slight you feel it in the ankles more than you see it, lined with plates of fired clay etched with hairline whorls that lead condensed beads along like ink guided by capillary. There are floored hollows lidded with perforated slabs; if you raise a lid, a smell comes up like bitten limestone and cold leather. Steps dive down in squared spirals into the bigger cisterns, and each step has a lip, and each lip has a catch to harvest what a passing heel might flick off. The shapes have the freshness of recent making—edges still keen, tool marks bright and clean; in the right glancing gleam you can see the sweep of a trowel frozen in the mortar. Already, though, the first tents of erosion have been pitched: fine salt furs under rivulets, starter-gullies gnawing at the crisp inside corners of channels, a crumble-bloom around the base of a buttress where the wet clings longest.

Water is rare but constantly expected. It is coaxed rather than found, called out of the air by surface and by sound. This place has a grammar for it. Dripwrights—whoever built it, whoever keeps it—have tuned the bones of the world to the dewpoint. Ceramic tongues hang under the vault, fat and thin, each stamped with lines and stamped again. When wind or hands move them, they ring not like bells but like stone struck by muscle: a thock, a damp note the color of slate. In certain patterns the notes disturb the skin of air just enough that the micro-droplets born in the turbulence will stick to the rough instead of sliding on. When a bank of cooler breath rolls through the vents and the chimes are beating the right name for thirst, the place starts to pearl. Beads form all at once along the grained planes and then race each other along the carved whorls, collect in ticks where plates meet, and fall. The fall is a language. A slow midnight is the hush of individual drops hitting taut skins stretched over little bowls—plip with a tight focus that the vault sends back from far away. A fast shift of weather, a big exhale from the western vents, becomes a thunder-crescendo: a thousand plosives as the membranes fill and sag and slap and empty, as the cistern lids drum when the bowls beneath them heave.

The sound never quits. Even on the driest day, there is some speaking—a hanging string of bone beads answering a draft, a frog-jawwood rattle set in a flue, the lazy lap of what’s been caught. Workers call to each other in carried beats, hand against slab, pole against rail, because the place answers to rhythm and because sight is cheap here. A copper disk struck in the north cisterns means “open the eastern shutters”; three slow knocks passed along the stepwell rings mean “the main trough will spill.” You learn distance by delay, volume by brightness of echo. When the drums for a harvest storm are raised—big hide stretched over you-don’t-ask-what rib, hammered with mallets wrapped in wet rags—the roof groans back, and every made thing starts to buzz. The noisy world grows louder like a beast called out.

There is no plant life. The body of the place is mineral and skin. But life has found its thin ways. Fungal seams dwell in the joints where mortar stays forever damp—mattings pale as milk with a slow, spare foxfire that is more suggestion than light, enough to lay a dew-sheen into silhouette. On the cool ribs of the vault, there are rosettes of some other patience—calcite and a lichenless cousin making lace in dull white. Slime molds, colorless and precise, race invisible races across plaster when the climate suits them and then go to sleep as brittle varnish. In the back of certain closets draped with tarp, where organics are kept from the roofs’ appetite, there are leather-bloomers: furry patches that taste the air and breed a dust with a tang like iron.

Fauna are present but few. You can cross a hundred rooms and see nothing move but water and fabric. Then, from a notch in a lid, a ribbon-thing slips away: a cistern eel, blind and porcelain, feeling with its whole length, surviving on seep-born crusts and a careful culture of worms in the silt. Pale isopods click along undersides of plates, flocking to the salt crystals with a concentration that has no sound at all; when you lean close, they beat a faint collective hair-brush scratch that vibrates in your teeth. Once in a long while, when the drums are loudest and the membranes swing, a vault-moth glides down from the filigree above—no mouth, only velvet wings that drink from fog, their bodies written with a dim pattern that remains after they’ve gone, like a memory on the eyes. There are spider-analogues too, long as a forearm, picking their way amongst the cords like careful old women, hunting a single different taste. And there are rumors of bigger things. A piece of resin-polish by a big basin bears the scrape of something that tried to pry it open with a jaw. The Dripwrights carve teeth into the edges now and pretend they had always planned it.

The rules of the place are made from claustrophobia and acoustics and thirst. Never light a flame under the vault, because heat makes air hold life instead of giving it up; all cooking fires are dug into the lowest wells, their smoke fed into sacks of river-clay where it cools and is bled through. Speak soft near the bowls; a human voice close by can spoil a fall, can make the tear decide not to leap. Do not lick your fingers. Every work-surface is tuned to a task by its exact roughness, which is measured in handfuls of ground glass or handfuls of sifted ash; the wrong oil from your sweat undoes that taughtness. Names are not given to the cistern eels, not aloud, because if the water learns to attend a name it will grow shy of leaving it. Poles and ropes are ranked like different flutes—oak-leaf wrap here to damp a line, twisted horsehair there to make it sing a small steady note to keep the drips brave during a dry hour, a drum beat every forty heartbeats just to tell the channels that someone is waiting.

It is an open place in its own closed way. From any one platform you can see platform after platform stepping down into not-vision, the sense of breadth not coming from absence but from the accumulation of presences repeating: another rib, another dangling tongue, another stair with a lip. Sometimes the vault relents into a nave so broad your breath feels too small in it. The floor there dips, maybe to a natural low that was taught to be more itself with tile and slope, and the room breathes. Temperature shifts run across your knuckles like shallow tides. In the wet times—rare but orchestrated—ropes go taut when the drafts are thrown, and the black above sweats in sheetfalls. Whole lanes become transparent rivers that sheet over the finger-scribed plates and lick into the teeth of the big drains. Stone-on-skin booming is joined by a deeper tone as the major cisterns take their load and their pillars respond, a bass felt more than heard, and all the little, patient structures shake themselves like dogs.

Here erosion is a chapter just beginning. Mortar is still green at the hearts of walls. Finger-lines in clay are still bright enough to cut skin if you slide wrong. But the wet is an artist that starts as soon as the hand lifts: It threads white in the rust-black of the roof, it opens the first pores in fired plates, it soups the very fine silt at the bottoms of the gutters into a walking material that migrates downhill by the width of a thumb a season. There are places where the original makers misjudged a slope, and the place is correcting them, gently, almost kindly, opening a new mouth in the side of a channel. Dripwrights ring a different bell when they surrender to these edits. “The water wants this,” the note says, and a new lip is cast, a new tooth is carved.

Everything alive here is rare because everything is accounted for. Any movement that is not a tool or a drip draws eyes. When a pale salamander shows itself in the small hours, its skin patterned in faint spots like dried salt on clay, heads turn from their work without stopping the rhythm they are beating, because that rhythm is not just for rain. Predation is sparse, quick, unspectacular: a moth wing vanishing between long delicate legs, the faint convulsion of silt when an eel takes a passing scud. More frantic by far are the chases between water and surface, the escapes and captures of bead and groove.

The materials here are simple to name and complex in their arrangements. Clay, lime, bone, hair, hide. Some copper where they have earned it: a hinge-leaf that glints in the near-dark when the pole lifts it, a hammered disk with a nick at its edge where someone missed in a hurry. Fused glass where fires have been heaped and starved and fed until the sand remembered it could be a liquid. Resins smeared thin to seal joints that demand no seep. Salt, harvested and controlled, used as weight, as drug for moisture, as law written on a wall in white that the air can’t dissolve fast enough to forget.

And beyond all that, there are the slower rules that make this world less like ours. Water here behaves as if it can be coached. It is more vocal than we are used to. It likes to be named in patterns of percussion, and in return it becomes visible when the right notes are struck, as if embarrassed into disclosure. The vault is a mind of sorts—a passive complicity—remembering vibrations like a drum remembers a hand. The geometry of the works is not only about slope and surface area but about harmonics; buttresses are tuned lengths, cisterns are resonant bodies. The little fungi give more light when the chimes throb a certain way. The eels rise toward the lids in dry times when a long lulling beat is kept. Distance is measured not by how far you walk but how long a particular echo takes to die.

If you wait for one of the longer cycles, you’ll see the place shift its mood. The vents that are heavy with cloth along the western wall will be bared one by one by men and women on the ropes, hauling as if into a wind that arrives only inside the space itself. Cold runs in. From the ceiling above comes a hush like a crowd taking in breath, then the first peck of big drips on stretched skins. The gongs pick up and the big drums answer. That thread of brightness on the plates underfoot doubles and triples. The walls begin to sleeken. You smell the change before you feel it—a jump from chalk to river, a metallic touch. Then it is everywhere at once: not rain in the open sense, since the vault instructs and breaks and gathers the fall into sheets and runnels and punctual impacts, but a made rain, called and guided, running down the throat of a built valley, the loudness almost a comfort because it is the proof that the world’s intended function has been achieved again.

When it slackens, the place is suddenly more itself. The dim little lights of fungus matter more. The big sounds reduce to patient streams finding their lowest rights. Somebody somewhere scrapes a spoon in a bowl. A salted rope hisses when a hand runs along it. A moth writes once and is leased back to the dark. And the floor at last becomes audible: the faintest creak of settling mortar, the tiny ring of a single drop hitting the true bottom of a well that might be older than all the rest of this, the slap of a palm on a plate, testing a dish’s tone, deciding whether the next shift of shutters will be needed before the next breath of cold.
