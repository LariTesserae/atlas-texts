You arrive where the land has fallen in on itself, not once but over and over, a linked procession of sunken rooms and corridors running for miles beneath an intact plain. The first impression is of brightness coming from below: day pours down through breaks in the roof in wide sheets, strikes water, and bounces back as a soft, silver light that fills the trench. Cold air flows up from the depths in a steady breath. Every surface glistens as if just rained upon, because here it always is; the rock weeps, the air condenses, and thin skins of water creep over ledge and lip without ever quite making a sound.

The terrain is young and it shows. The walls are sharp—fresh-broken limestone still pale where it has only lately met the air. Blocks lie at their toes like thrown dice, some tilted to make lean-tos, some papered over already with moss and the first velvet of liverwort. Arêtes of bedrock part pools into cells. Where runoff keeps to a path long enough, it builds rims and lips of calcite as neat as the edges of bowls, a thousand little terraces stepping down into shadow. The place is hollowed by solution and collapse, but it reads like construction: alcoves repeated, shelves at repeating intervals, pillars that carry weight where even now cracks run fretted and bright.

Water is everywhere, though not always as streams. It beads and threads, it clings and fogs. Curtains of droplets comb themselves from ceiling seams and fall in slow beaded chains, each drop raising a faint ring on the pooled travertine below. Thin films of flow wrap the rock in glass. Underfoot, the ground is stitched by rills that appear and vanish and reappear, braided by calcite rims into runnels that cross and cross again. Pools sit like coins, their edges overturned by rimstone walls—gours, clay-brown or honey-white depending on the microbes that seeded them. Narrow drains run from lip to lip, a city of weirs, and under all that, you hear a body of water that never shows itself, an older river in the stone that pulls and murmurs from behind the walls.

The air is cold enough that your breath shows, and that matters to the place. The cold keeps noise small and growth fine-grained; it keeps the weeping water near its freezing point, so icicles are always possible, and in winter a film of ice grows on every slack pool, only to be cracked and reknitted a dozen times a day by the weight of droplets. Frost furs some surfaces. After clear nights, the walls bloom a white down of hoarfrost that melts from the top down as the light finds its way, so the rooms rinse themselves with a vertical rain at noon.

Vegetation is lush in the way of a centuries-old rug, not a parade of tall things. Thick mats of moss pack the steps of stone into soft stairs, and beneath that, peat is already forming from what the mats themselves have shed and crushed. Liverworts lay little flags over constant trickles; algae stripe the drip-lines in clean, bright green; pale fans of filmy ferns unfurl from shaded cracks. Where a skylight opens wide enough, light-hungry plants assemble in tight guilds—alder saplings and willow thickets grow low and braided, their branches knit together by flood and fall, so that living causeways cross water and bare ground both. Horsetail spears rise at the edges of pools. Roots, finding no soil, knit through rubble and across limestone like rope—gripping, binding, thickening until they make tunnels under themselves, passage for something small and water-dark.

It is built, this place, though not by hands. The materials are calcite and cellulose, ice and peat. The roofs are domes of rock spared by the collapse. The floors are treads of rimstone that have learned, by drip after drip, where to curl and where to self-raise. The causeways are alder-laced hummocks and root buttresses, grown into bridges because the water asked them to and the frost let them split and fatten without dying. Layer sits on layer: rock terraces, overlaid by root-lattice, overlaid by moss that erases sound, overlaid by nests and dens and tunnels, overlaid by the narrow tracks of feet you would not think could leave any trace on such soft ground, but do.

Fauna is everywhere but not loud. The walls on the sunnier sides are freckled with the mud-cup entrances of swifts and cliff-swallows, their honeycomb dens stacked five, six, twelve tiers high. They go and they come in glides, rasps that barely carry. Bats own the ceiling seams beyond the reach of light; in the hour when the skylights trade day for blue, they pour, thin and black against the last silver, then vanish into the long corridor of the trench where they hunt by turnings and echo. Water shrews web in along the margins, little arrow-chevrons of movement under the surface, surfacing under moss to eat in perfect secrecy. Dippers bob on the calcite lips and plunge into water so cold your teeth hurt to look at it, staying down longer than seems proper, emerging with a curl of larvae in the bill. Trout idle deep in the clay-brown rooms, light printed on their backs in pale hexagons that shift whenever the surface dimples. In the moss, vole tunnels are as dense as veins in a leaf; above them, weasels move like water in reverse, silent and certain. Now and then an owl’s shadow slides down the wall from a skylight at the rim, turns, and is gone. Amphipods and isopods make the lowest routes along the underlips of the gours; snails write shining lines on dark stone; salamanders lift their small hands in the thin rills and do not make a sound at all.

Some animals are builders in the way the water is. Beaver kin, small and pale-muzzled from a life without sun except in these wells of light, have laid dams not simply to stop water but to step it. They complement the natural rimstone with sticks and mud where the calcite has not yet grown thick enough, cribbing the city’s terraces into an even grade that a swimmer can traverse by deep-to-deep. Their lodges are rooms burrowed into peat hummocks under alder roots, entrances underwater, every lodge with its emergency vent into a shallow skylight where a tangle of willow keeps snow away. The result is infrastructure: levels, redundancies, pressure-relief channels that only show themselves during flood. There is an order to it that you can learn if you stand in one place long enough. A green line on a wall marks how far the water lifted during the last thaw. A brown line, higher and older, tells a story of a time when a dam failed and a terrace poured out like a staircase of broken bowls. The wire-taut crossing of a root shows a favored fox path, never used while light is on the open pool, but slicked and polished nonetheless.

The sounds are a grammar of restraint. Dripping, but not patter—single notes, numerous and uncoordinated, like a roomful of clocks all slightly unsynced. The low fret of unseen water. The breath of a thousand square meters of moss exhaling cool humidity. Wing sound without voice. A branch creak. The grit-word of rock chips sliding, now and then, from a new crack up in the wall. In deep winter, when the air is dry in the upper world, the ice sings: short flutings where thin skins at the lip of a pool flex and fail; a far, bell-thin ping when a stalactite of ice drops into a pool and vanishes and the rimstone takes the tiny blow like a tympanum. Bodies pass near and you hear almost nothing—a lifted claw, a whisker touching wet stone, a breath doubled by the cold.

Light is normal here because the stone has failed in the right ways. The skylights are not holes but rooms in themselves, some as wide as town squares, some as narrow as a chimney. At noon, shafts stand nearly straight; toward the ends of the day, they rake the walls so that every pore and crack jumps out. The rock’s paleness helps; even in overcast weather, the trench keeps a diffuse brightness, a sky repeated by stone and water. In places, rimstone pools mirror skylights with such exactness that the rooms seem twice as high. In winter, snow caps the rims and the place gleams; in summer, leaves at the skylight rims cast a cool green color onto white stone, so that from below, even if you cannot see the trees, you can tell when they are in leaf.

Everything moves even when it seems arrested. Freeze and thaw pry at wedges left by the collapse, so nights deliver new corners and mornings discover them. Calcite grows its lips invisibly fast: a film of burble becomes a line becomes a step that something small can use by next year. Roots find their way into old cracks; where they split stone, they also hold it, so an overhang can last long beyond its due by being laced on the inside. Floods come when the thaw is deep or when a storm chooses to stall over the plateau; then the whole system takes a lower, rougher voice. The walls stand up to these events because they are young and still capable of holding; they also change. Every big water rearranges some part of the map.

The rules of the place are not laws but habits the place has trained into everything that lives here. Never block the drain: everything that grows long across a flow grows porous, or it will be torn free. Leave kin a pocket: every nest, every lodge, every den has a second door because rockfall and ice and water are certain and the only uncertain thing is when. Keep quiet unless you must not: here sound is taken as intent. Measure time by drips and light: everyone knows where the noon lands today because noon paints a different patch of wall in each season, and some patches are safe and some are not. And almost everything respects the water’s way, because here water is also the street and the pantry and the warmth source—an impossible suggestion at this temperature, but true—sink your hands to the wrists in a beaver lodge in midwinter and the water is warmer than the air.

Stand on one of the larger terraces—a natural plaza, rimmed on three sides by shallow gours, a ledge of willow woven into a parapet, two skylights sending down wide, clear light—and the density becomes obvious. Nothing is spare. The travertine lip you sit on is encrusted with small freshwater limpets, the undersurface with caddis that have stolen the place’s palette for their cases. Ten inches downslope, a dipper leaves a bright white punctuation on the stone, an announcement that will wash away in a day and matters nonetheless. A weasel flows past in a flash, no more than a color-twist against the moss. In the deeper pool, trout adjust their tails by the width of a hair and hold themselves there. Overhead, swifts switch diagonals and are gone into the wall. Half-hid in the alder lattice at the parapet, an owl chooses waiting over motion. The air takes what it’s offered and carries nothing else.

Follow the trench a mile or two and you will find that this repeats and does not. Some rooms are circular and high; some are long and low and tunnel onward until they narrow and you hear, through a stone a hand-width thick, a river that doesn’t belong to your topography at all. Some collapses are so new that the smell of crushed stone still lingers under the moss-and-water smell; in others, willows have worked long enough to make their own islands and begin the peat that will someday fill a terrace and raise a floor. Everywhere, newly born edges and very old habits co-exist. It feels like a city because so much has agreed to be in one place together: stone and flowwriting, plant scaffolds, animal colonies, all stacked and connected, all quietly moving, all without any device but their own bodies and the chemistries that have patience for cold.

Leave and you carry it with you: the reflex to pause where a drop falls, the desire to step only on the pale of stone where the moss will not notice, the new sense that space goes on beneath you, not as void but as a saturated, careful world that keeps its voice down and gets on with the work.
