Up here the world has worn itself almost smooth. The bones of an old range spread into a high, wide table, its edges eroded to low humps and elderly tors, its interior planed until the horizon feels like the lid of a slow breath. The rock—ancient granite, metamorphosed so often it has forgotten its first shape—shows through in long, shallow domes and flat pavements, pocked with pans where rain lingers and streaked with quartz that is forever damp. Every exposed face weeps. The stone does not bleed so much as condense: pearls of water gather on its skin from a sky that seems too cold to give anything, but it does, always, drop by drop, thread by thread.

Everything else is plant.

Not plants as a collection of individuals, but plant as the floor itself, as the walls between air and stone. The substrate is a living mat meters thick, a spongy green-black carpet that yields underfoot and rebounds with a quiet, elastic insistence. Pressing a hand to it, you feel not a surface but layers of densely braided stems and veins, warm in pockets, cold elsewhere, pulsing very faintly. In this place, leaves are roofs. Ropes of lichen the color of old nickel hang in long beards from the rims of hollows, dragging the mist with them as they sway. Huge, low rosettes—over a hundred across the plateau, far between—compress and expand like breath, their inner ribs shining with dew. Between them are acres and acres of close-grown moss and heather-analogues, all fused together into one living sheet, pierced by slow wells that the mat keeps from freezing by its own chemistry.

The air is a saturated, dim choir. Cold fog moves in tides, ribbons slipping over the ground with a speed that catches you off guard, then standing inexplicably still around your knees while higher layers go racing past. The light has the character of late day even at noon—bluish, grainy, sifted through a ceiling of cloud only sometimes visible and more often palpable as a weight. Now and then that ceiling thins and a white disk shows where the sun should be, uncertain, indifferent, and then closes again as if a mouth pursed around it.

It is not quiet. The plateau is an engine hall without walls. Wind funnels through organ-pipe slots in the eroded rock, setting up droning notes that rise and fall as the fog density changes. In the mat, the vascular bundles answer: you hear the proximity of a valve-field long before you see it. It sounds like distant hooves at first, then like bellows, then like a roomful of people sleeping fitfully. Where the plant has taken advantage of a line of fracture in the bedrock, it has grown up a rank of tall, simple structures—pale cylinders six or seven meters high with their tops sawn off by time, each capped by a membrane that thumps with a careful regularity. On a gale their rhythm shifts to double-time and you can feel the faintest electrical hair-rise along your arms as they pump sap and ions uphill against the ordinary wisdom of physics. Sometimes, in a lull, they relax and sigh out fog so thick it falls in sheets, the sound a million umbrellas opening at once.

There are no roads, no fences, no wires. Nothing set here deliberately. It only looks industrial because the living mass has found industry’s moves and taught them to itself: pistons and plenums in leaf and fibrous pith, siphons that measure pressure on their own, turbine-crowns that drag power from the blunt wind, capacitor-nodes that store charge when the clouds spark their shallow lightning. When a storm crosses—and storms cross with a speed impossible to believe until you see it, a gray freight hurtling along the plateau’s length in a single roaring minute—the mat lies low, puts its valves into a low-gear thud, and dumps water into its rootrooms with a sound like loose stones rattling down a chute.

Water stands everywhere, but almost nowhere deep. It is a skin thing, coated on stone, soaked through plant, suspended in air. You will see a trick of this place: sound makes more of it. A shouted word leaves behind a lace of droplets in the shape of your breath that hangs for a heartbeat longer than it ought to. The louder the phenomenon—the thump of a collapsing rosette leaf, the concussion from a distant ice calving at the plateau’s lip—the thicker the veils that precipitate in its wake. The ecology has learned this rule. Some of the larger epiphytes clap their leaves in storms to farm their own fog. A flock of small, slate-colored fliers will pass, their wingtips vibrating at a specific pitch, and in the trail of their cry a new skein of mist threads the air like spun glass.

Life here is abundant and contradictory, obvious and spare. In the broad view the place feels open, almost empty, the significant forms few and far between. But in those spaces a thousand separate currents of living are going on. Long-legged grazers move like commas across the mat, five, ten in a day’s view, widely spaced, careful-footed on their spreading hooves as if walking wet pages. Their coats are moss-mottled not for camouflage but because the mat is in them and they are in it—mutualists with teeth only for pruning, their breath sweet with the same oils that the plants use to keep ice from forming. They stand to be cleaned by shoals of small velvet moths that move through the air in a constant, soft rush, drawn to a kind of static whisper produced by the animals’ fur.

In the valve-fields live the listeners: pale, blind amphibians with ear-plates along their flanks. They lie with their bodies pressed to the membranes, drinking vibration and the weak warmth of pumping sap. When a storm hits and the valves slap faster, they wake, yawn, and go arrowing into the drains where the mat keeps its pools liquid, to hunt the glass eels that coil there. The eels come up from cracks in the bedrock where the plant has excavated its own caves; their bodies flash with bioelectric scribbles that jump to the nearer membranes, and from there into the rest of the sheet, until in a dark moment the whole plain has a faint, shared glitter, like starlight inside soup.

Predation is often a matter of timing rather than teeth. The low-rosette meadows periodically take in and let go of their rims; when they relax a little, tiny shrimplike browsers flow in, legs high, to harvest the mucilage that the plant extrudes where frost abrades its skin. When the rosette tightens again it does so with such courtly slowness that nothing panics. Things only find themselves no longer out but in, and the plant’s digestive hairs remove them from the problem of further decisions. At the other end of scale, something not unlike a bird but built on the plan of a kite will hover in the venting breath of a warm crack for hours, barely moving, until the geyser of fog suddenly inverts and pours downward; with the roaring implosion the kite drops bonelessly, riding the vacuum-created pull, and scoops the suddenly-exposed pool with its flexible mouth. Its bones are marrowless tubes of striped cellulose with mineral rings like a tree.

Rules here feel negotiable, but not whimsical. Gravity is true enough that you do not float; it is gracious enough to let fog pool in concavities rather than disperse at once. Cold is strict in the open air but merciful under the mat where it never dips below what the plants have decided is life. Distance can be argued with if you are the right organism. The grazers never seem to cross the same ten meters of ground twice the same way, even when you watch carefully; the mat has very slightly rearranged its tension in the interval, bringing what they want into the path that their hooves desire. Time itself frays at the fog’s edge. There are moments when a patch of lichen seems to advance in weeks through twenty seconds of life, press out a fruiting body, breathe its spores, and then return to its slow before you have finished your breath. Watchful ravens—there are ravens here, or what local physics provides in answer to the word—throw stolen bones into the valve-fields to hear the echo they prefer, working out distances by tone, and in doing so call water out of the air: their games end in brief showers that they love for the insects it wakes.

Everything is accounted for and yet little is fixed. The bedrock that escaped the plant is papered with an alphabet of ice: rime that grows in angular filigrees, characters that repeat and then mutate as if a grammar were being tested and abandoned in a few degrees of frost. Sometimes those geometries lift under their own rules and slide across the pavement with the stately certainty of tectonics in miniature, leaving behind a ghost of themselves in moisture, like a tracer. Occasionally a pane will stop and stand upright in the air, supported on nothing obvious, as if insisting that it is in fact a page, and you are meant to read. What it says depends upon how close you are willing to be.

Beyond the flat heart, the plateau’s margins tilt down into cloud. There, whole knots of the living sheet have crept over the edge and hung themselves into the abyss as heavy curtains. Water plays them like instruments. Wind catches at the long drop and the plant responds by braiding itself into cords that thicken in storms as if to re-stitch the world to its own anchor. At intervals a curtain fails and the sound is a dull, continent-sized tear, followed by a shock of wind that runs the surface like a hand. In the torn place new growth already knits, pale, bristly, humming with tiny rotors of seed-case that set up a localized breeze to harvest stray fog. Old rock at the cliff foot takes the constant battery and smears into clay; that clay stands forever saturated, forming shelves of slow landslide where peculiar worms arrange the mud into lattice, and they will not be hurried even by gravity.

There is a smell—wet stone, iodine, metals, crushed ice, green after rain, a breath of mild electric. If you taste the fog it is surprisingly mineral, as if the place licks you back. Your voice does not carry far unless the mat desires it; sometimes a whisper travels, sometimes a shout falls flat. You become convinced that you could ask the plateau a question and have it choose not only whether to answer but in what format: a shift of pressure underfoot, the shape of frost on a slab, the behavior of a flock of small fliers turning as one.

In the dim, things glow. Thread-thin fungal partners lace the mat and light along the veins when the sap runs hard, blue-green, dim at noon and suddenly bright on a storm night as if the whole landscape has remembered a marine ancestry and chosen to be a reef for a while. The glow shows you the large housekeeping of the organism: a slow, riverine transfer of materials from the weeping tors to the lee of rosettes, a priority of repair over growth, and an unwavering devotion to low relief. Mountains may still dream of being mountains here, but the plateau dreams of being the last statement. Its forms are as old as anything can be and still change. It has settled on the shape of breathing.

The turbulence does not end, but it patterns. A rill that runs one way in the morning will, by afternoon, appear to run upslope, its water drawn into the thirsty mat by a pressure difference established a kilometer away. A bank of cloud will spend an hour unzipping into parallel stripes under the influence of song—there are, sometimes, long, subsonic moans that roll the length of the plain, and under them the fog granulates into waves. It is not random. It is only not your kind of order.

And in all that motion there is no made thing. No fencepost, no ruin. If a strut suggests architecture it is because the plant has taught itself engineering down to a fine art. If a noise suggests a mill it is because the bedrock and the air have discovered the same song that mills sing. You are alone with it, and not alone at all. Everything in you that can condense will do so: your breath on your collar, your ideas into named things as quickly as you can stop them, because it is easier than letting them be mist that sometimes gathers and sometimes goes backward up an invisible slope. If you stay, you learn to walk with your feet apart and your expectations loosened, to wait for your own words to emerge from the fog in the shape they choose, damp and perfect and ephemeral, before they drip back down into the mat, which uses everything.
