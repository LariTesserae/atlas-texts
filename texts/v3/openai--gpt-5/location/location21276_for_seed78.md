First you hear it, long before you can make out anything beyond the black-on-black of silhouette and star. The place is an instrument. Wind, thinned by altitude and honed by sharp ridgelines, threads itself through living structures and makes them speak. There’s a bass that is felt before it’s heard, a standing thrum running through the soles and into the teeth; above it rise long wavering vowels, then higher still a scatter of flutes and whistles, and, on gusts, the harsh drum of something massive striking something hollow. The sound never stops. It is as constant as gravity, as changeable as weather: you know morning from evening here by tone rather than light.

Out of this noise the land emerges in bits. A black rim with a harsh, new edge: raw stone, fractured into prisms and knifeblades, not yet scuffed down by ages of rain. The mountain is young in the way of mountains, all split planes and fresh fractures, the kind that slice fog into sheets. It doesn’t run to a single peak so much as to a comb of spurs and pinnacles, rising into the thin air until they vanish into a darker dark.

What stands on them—or rather, what grows out across them—is the world. Vegetation, but not as a floor. Think of it as a scaffolding thrown across void: pale cables as thick as rivers, ribs like fallen arches, lattices stretched taut from spur to spur until an entire sky is webbed. The “ground” here is the living mass itself, but it is not solid; it is a system of beams, membranes, and knotted nodes, a tremendous airy weave. You step onto braided mats of tendon-wood and look down between their strands to open black, open blue; sometimes, looking down through layer after layer of net and gap, you see the slow sparks of small life moving far below, and sometimes there is only cold darkness eating all light.

The plants: there are many kinds, but they behave as one edifice. Rigid anchors, mineral-studded and ribbed, fuse directly to the sharp rock where they can get a purchase. From these bloom tension members: long, hollow fibers capable of singing when the wind plucks them. Between anchors, membranes stretch—translucent, waxed, veined, sagging into broad scoops that fold and unfold like a bird’s throat. Nodes blossom along the spans, bulbous and faceted, their surfaces bristling with hairlike collectors. The membranes are not leaves in the bright-green sense; there is no “canopy” that casts shade, only skins tuned to wetness and wind. The colors, when you catch them by aurora or star-sheen, run to ghost: stone-pale, frost-green, resin-amber, the faintest bruised violet where a bruise truly is.

In this dark, where sunlight is scanted to a thin rain of photons most days and absent outright on many, plants do not live by light alone. They harvest motion. Every gust flexes the lattice. Every resonance pushes fluid along valves. There are switchback channels woven into the spans, slitted and fringed; wind pulses pump sap through them, and opening and closing shutters meter flow the way a heart does. The entire structure is a set of giant wind-mills without wheels, turning pressure into sugar. Microbes, cramped into warm niches in the nodes, finish the work: they oxidize what the plants exude and pay back nitrogen and faint, steady light. You never see bright glows, but sometimes the veins under your hand hold a dusk of their own, like moonlight trapped in water.

Water exists here as event and as storage. The air, even this high and cold, often carries a burden of faint vapor that the plants comb out. Fog-tide is the locals’ word: it rises from the lower canyons at predictable hours, a barely visible river of clouds that catches on every comb and hair. The collectors bead and fur with glassy droplets, then gather them along keels into runnels. The runnels feed the nodes. You hear it in the music when a node is full: there is a hollowing-out of tone as the weight pulls a note lower, and then a sudden quickening as a membrane bellies and dumps. Those are the moments you find dew-sills and drink. When storms do come, they arrive without pattern, streaming in with an animal’s speed—sleet first, then spicules of ice so small they hiss, then wet snow that slides in slabs off the skins and falls away in darkness, thudding on nodes and sending shuddering booms down the spans.

Despite the fullness of plant—the sheer weight of life strung above rock—space here is mostly emptiness. The gaps are not cracks between trees but chasms miles wide and dozens deep, with only a handful of taut members drawn across. The world breathes in cubic leagues. That emptiness carries sound like a bell-body, makes scents travel for great distances, and means there is very little between you and the weather. You learn, quickly, to read the weft and warp of the place, because the next stride might be five finger-breadths to solid or two steps to a thousand feet of nothing.

The air is hard and thin and bitter-dry until fog-tide, and yet it is thick with living things. Fauna are built for tension and glide. There are grazer-flocks with sails instead of wings: fine, many-ribbed patagia stretched between ankle and wrist, fanning wide to drift from membrane to membrane, nipping at the wax-bloom and the mats of filament-weed that the plant extrudes each night. They call constantly in soft hehes to keep contact in the wind, and they steer with the calls as much as with their legs, turning into or out of resonances like fish on a reef.

Where there are grazers there are hunters. The most obvious, in the dim, are the Scythes: they do not fly so much as cut. Long and narrow and black-matte, with a double-chinned jaw and a body like a thrown thorn, they hang by their rear claws from a high tension member, invert into the airflow, and release. In the moving air, their heads hum with a different frequency; they can feel the places where the weave thickens, and they aim for the zones where grazers will be pushed by sound as much as by current. When they strike it is not with a cry but with a sudden silence, the great-throated resonance broken for a heartbeat and then resumed. The hunters eat on the wing, flesh and wax and filament going down into warm guts, and then they climb again, all elbows, onto a singing span to rest in the shiver of the world.

Under the membranes, along the anchors and in the resin caves of drained nodes, slower creatures live. Bone-white, jointed like prayers, there are little resin-biters that scavenge and build: they carry nuggets of plant resin in their mouths like pearls and spit it to glaze their tunnels. You find whole neighborhoods of them, rooms as smooth as inside shells, and in their walls an embedded glitter of things that fell: mineral grit, meteoric nails of iron-nickel, the dropped stones of travelers. In winter (which is most seasons in this world) they heat their rooms chemically, burning the plant-sugars they have banked. Stand in a resin cave and put your ear to the wall and you will hear a sighing like a sleeping lung; put your fingers there and you feel a warmth so small you’d miss it if you breathed too hard.

This is a loud land, but it is not a loudness of chaos. There are rules in the sound. Each membrane has a register; each span has a fundamental; each anchor, by its mass, drags the whole chorus fractionally lower. When fog coats the hair-collectors, high harmonics are damped. When a storm front rides the combs, the lowest notes go beating, two giants arguing under the earth. Inhabitants—because there are people here, few and little, the size of foxes and corded with tendons, with teeth of translucent resin and clothes of plaited fibers—live by that music. They carry handheld harps whose only purpose is to find the quiet lines, the narrow notches in the chord where a human body can stand without shaking apart. They build where the music tells them to: small huts resin-glued to the lee of nodes, face to the flow, with slatted porches where fog is combed into pots. They rarely cut the plant; they tune it. A child learns early how to shift a span’s tone by tying a basket here, a stone there; how to twine a hair-collector to favor a slow drip over a splash; how to drop a wind-weight to thicken a note and keep predators off.

Tools here are hands and fiber and stone more than flame. There’s little fuel and less oxygen to spare for big fires, and the resin smokes badly. The brightest lights they make are coaxes for the resident fungi, fed with drips of sugar-water until the nodes they live in radiate a blue so weak it might also be imagined. Bones from grazers and Scythes, split and polished, become knives and needles. Obsidian from the young rock, knapped to wicked edges, does for the cuts that must be cuts. They weave their ladders from the same cords the plant weave makes and anchor them in a way that looks like prayer: many tight knots, no single point of failure. When they need metal—grit from the sky more often than from earth—they hammer it cold and smooth, build a bell small enough to sing to a membrane without frightening it, scratch it into mirrors to find other villages miles away by star-glimmer.

The world’s weather is its lifeblood. Apart from fog-tide, there are other cycles. There is the slow drift of auroral sheets across the high dark, the only “day” some weeks, green and violet flowing over the membranes and turning ice to transient mirrors. There are the heatless lightning storms that string themselves along the ridges, cracking air dry as bone and gifting the plant with nitrates that it tastes and saves. There are migrations as orderly as winter: the big herds of sail-grazers moving in loops that match the long beats of wind from the western basins; the Scythes’ broods learning the map of resonances like learning a forest’s elk-trails. After a time even the rock shifts: shallow quakes run through the young ranges, and the anchors groan, and the whole sky-web takes a different set. You can wake with the same stars overhead and need to learn your world again.

Where the weave is dense enough, you feel something that is perhaps closest to safety. The membranes overlap and double over into something like flooring. Snow finds hold there in thin veneers; you step in it, and the cold climbs through your bootless feet and you curse and laugh, because what else do you do under a choir of plant-skin singing to wind. Things grow here that grow nowhere else: mats of ice-lichens, feathery and phosphorescent when bruised; short-stemmed, pale-stemmed mushroom-clusters that exist to eat the hair of the membranes when they are old and give back salt; funny little birds that are not birds that hop and hunch like embers and steal your resin thread and look at you like you owe them something.

Rules of this world, rough as truths:
- Life here is tensile. Everything that lives knows how to hang, how to pull, how to sing back when the wind insists. Roots are as much ropes as probes; paws are as much hands as feet.
- Water is a question of capture. Surfaces are all combs, salts catch and hold as currency, and a full node is a richer lord than a pile of gold. A dry week makes the whole place an instrument in a higher key; a wet day drags it to a lullaby.
- Light is errand, not premise. Plants take what little they can when the sky permits, but mostly they know a dark economy: the trade of motion for sweetness, of noise for metabolite, of bacterial breath for the faintest shine. Your eyes, if you stay long, broaden their pupils to the size of coins and learn to love contrasts: frost-edge, star-edge, ivory on black.
- Distance is hollow. The way between two things is mostly nothing, and so travel is rope to rope, node to node, in short brave darts over long ruins of space. Voices carry unnervingly, and whispers can go a mile if you aim them into the right note.
- Time is layered. There is the beat of wind, seconds and fractions of seconds; there are the hours of fog; there are the weeks of stormless hunger when the membranes are dull and the grazers thin with waiting; there are the seasons of set, when the plant swells new spans into old voids and throws a bridge over a chasm and a whole new world arrives one day where yesterday there was none.

It is not silent; it is never still; it is not bright; it is terribly, almost beautifully empty. You live among a cathedral of plant, whose stone is fiber and resin, whose windows are thin wax skins that wink to let in fog, whose bells are stretched across the sky its full width. The cold is an honest cold, a cold that keeps rot away, that rings the ice on collectors into small chimes when the gust shakes them loose. The dampness is the moment when a drop leaves one world for another, a small river every hour that those who know it walk out with cups to catch.

And sometimes the place gives you spectacle. You may be up high on a narrow web in absolute dark, your breath a tiny private cloud, when the whole chord of the world shifts, and for a handful of seconds, the full-scale thing shows itself: aurora hovers in a shallow bowl of cloud, showing you span after span written across an abyss, anchor after anchor buried in fresh stone, sails of big grazers drifting like pageants through it, and tracers of fungal blue like candles in nodes below, and your own small knot of resin and rope tiny under one gleaming hair of a skin. In that moment you appreciate the only measurement that matters here: not height or width, not cold or wet, but tension, between everything that wants to fall and everything that learned to hold.
