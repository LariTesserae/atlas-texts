You come down into it by a ramp of raw, pale clay that still remembers the sea. The slope is sharp-edged, as if the ground were cut yesterday with a giant knife and not yet rounded by rain. Every step dents the surface; stand too long and your footprint becomes a cup, a slow bright oval blooming with water from below, as if the earth has lungs and your weight is a breath pressed out of it.

The hollow is not large—hamlet-small, a single neighborhood pressed into a shallow bowl. Around it rise the young faces of the land, quick to slough and gleam: chalky marl and bands of darker shale, lacquer-dark when wet. After any passing shower the walls seem to weep; even without rain, at dawn a cool film draws itself out of the shaded stone and beads along edges and carvings. The people here have built to that habit. The first thing you see is the shade.

They have hung a ceiling of fabric above the bowl,”room-sized skies” of pale, oiled cloth drawn on ropes among tall poles driven into the mud. They let the cloths move a little with the wind; they drag them by hand to walk the shade across the weeping walls. Where a panel passes and the stone cools, a bloom of droplets forms almost at once—an outline of the shade, made of water. Below those bands the people nail thin lips of fired clay and wood into the rock to catch the runnels and guide them into jars. The jars are heavy-bellied stoneware, patched, their necks bound with fiber. The whole quarter breathes with the slow tilt of these canopies, a mild industry of shadow.

Between the cliffs and the sump lie the dwellings, packed so close their eaves overlap, close enough that fire is a rumor. These are not tall houses; two low stories at most, more often a swelling of reed and timber on a splay-legged platform. The posts—driftwood, salvaged beams, cut alder—are smeared with pitch and bound with wet cordage that never dries. Such lashings are freshened daily. Ladders climb to every door, but the ground floor is rarely for people. It is for water and storage; it is where cisterns cool and glisten, where the earth presses damp against the boards, where root traces try for a space and fail.

Overhead, strung from pole to pole, from ridge to ridge, is a second sky: lines. You notice them only after your eyes adjust to the moving shade. The quarter is webbed with taut cords of fiber rubbed with some grease that keeps the water from soaking in. Before dawn, fog from the lowlands comes in soft and white, its first touch beading on these lines. By the time the light has come even, hundreds of bright droplets pend from every span, each bead fattening in place. And then a hand reaches up and plucks a line.

The drop does not fall. It treads.

The string hums without much sound—a felt, private vibration—and small beads quicken, lifting and running along the line toward the nearest jar-lip, as if persuaded. Larger beads take a slower road on thicker cords. All over, men and women and children stand on platforms and roofs and reach up with hooked sticks to tap this one and that, chasing constellations of water in motion. Some lines are tuned by knots spaced at regular distances, guiding the beads in a curious start-stop procession like little pilgrims. When two beads touch they merge delicately and move with new intention. In this way the city drinks the sky and does it quietly, with a plucked murmur the ear feels as a pressure more than a tone.

The air is cool here even in afternoon, fed by the sump—a black, still-sided rectangle with a lip lined in stone chips laid like fish scales, edged to break small waves. The water in it is brackish, more memory of the old sea than a present taste. Thin, brave plants rise at its corners, sour rushes, stonewort, a thin flex of willow whose leaves are stunted and pale. Most of the growth in this place is low: moss tumbling in dark velvet on the shaded side of a step, black-green slimings that slick the gutters, a little lichen map-pale on the dry side of the poles. The wetness is not kind to trees; roots find no deep rest in this new earth.

The walking here has rules. You do not cross the earthen floor if you can help it—every footprint is a theft. The mud remembers impressions too well and fills them with water, and someone owns that thin pool. Property is measured in drips, in the shade one can cast without trespass. There are paths upon paths: high boardwalks of split plank, railed with saplings and threaded with those water-strings like beadwork. Where two walks meet, there is a knot-stone set into clay, carved with the pattern of the webs overhead, so that travelers can read their way by cords as one reads by stars. The lowest alleys are not used, except by the eel-men and children; they are narrow and wet, twisty cuts through fresh white clay that will not hold any shape long.

Tools are simple and kept out of sight. Bronze is rare and takes pride of place where it appears: a knife with a greenish edge used to shape gutter-lips, a handful of small rivets on a jar’s ring. Most else is wood: mallets, pegs, wedges to jack a leaning sill and make it true, combs for splitting reed stems. There are fired tiles, porous but mended with pitch; there are baskets stiffened with something resinous, that gleam like beetle backs. The shade canopies are oiled with fish fat, their seams crosshatched with plant fiber thread. The people dress in layered cloth that dries quickly; their sandals are wide and soft-soled to disturb the skin of the mud as little as possible.

Quiet is the first law, and not only by temperament. Sound travels strangely in the bowl. A sharp blow on one side—the drop of a jar, the slap of a plank—and on the other side moss will shed its held water as if shaken by the same hand. The shade-makers favor gliding motions, even in speech; conversation is low, rolled in the throat. Soft calls carry. When the wind comes, it is a soft under-sound, lifting threads of fabric, making the lines quiver. The drip of the weeping walls is intermittent, an arrhythmic tapping like rain that cannot commit. There are bells, but small ones of clay hung near the slumps, to chime when the wall moves. After rain the chime may sound thrice in a day. No one runs when it does; they unpin and pull the shade-braces to new positions and restack jars.

Animals are few and singular. A narrow heron that knows its way along the sump’s stones comes some mornings, a grey, ungossipy presence. Once in a long while a vole there is talk of, print-only, because no one has seen it and the children build small shrines of rush at the mouth of likely tunnels to lure it. There are eels, glass strings in the black water, that come up the gutters in rainy nights; their brief arrival makes a market day of it for those who know how to lay smooth stones so that the eels choose to rest, and can be lifted quickly out into wicker with a twist of the wrist. Otherwise it is insects in careful measure: spiders that have adopted the dew-lines, light and untroubling; caddisflies that find no river to belong to and make do with jars.

The place is young, and it knows the shape of youth: growth spurts and sudden sagging. On one side of the hollow a crisp crack runs where it did not yesterday; a sheet of marl has moved forward by a hand’s span, tilted a gutter so that it mis-pours. The edges of this city are traded daily with gravity. Men with stained hands measure with a rod and a knotted string; they press a wedge. Women tilt jars with cord and a foot to bring a lip level to a fall that just now finds a new course. Overnight, a thin notch cut by someone’s gutter can be a little canyon, white and fresh, with a small rattle of pebbles as it explores the deeper clay. The rawness is everywhere: unweathered edges that catch the light too cleanly; the smell of split earth, which is salt and chalk and a bitter iron that comes green onto the tongue when you lick it.

There are days when the sun returns, tilts into the bowl like a coin dropped into a cup. On those days the canopies are wound tight and stacked, shade is rationed and sold, water rides along the lines from what is left in the morning harvest, and the walls do not bleed at all. The air warms a little, just enough for one to remember warmth, but the damp takes it back from your skin at once. In high summer a rare wind blows from the outer flats; it smells of distant sedge and something sweet like rotting fruit that never actually appears here. That wind finds every sheet, every line, and sets the whole quarter humming like a low instrument. You feel it through your feet on the boards.

At the edge of the hollow where the ramp comes down there is a standing stone, not taller than a person, with its top scored in notches. Each notch marks a season’s strongest slump, they say, or the highest the water rose in a storm. The top is new-cut. The bottom is old-crusted with salts that look like frost. People touch the stone with wet fingers when they pass, painting it dark in stripes where the shade falls. Children are measured against it by elders who carry a bit of charcoal for the marks. The stone holds—things must.

There are small rituals for water here that make sense in the body. Before lifting a jar you press your palm to the clay just below its neck and wait until the chill digs into the meat of your hand. That is how you remember you can carry it without breaking the skin of your grip. Before you push a bowl across a table you wet the rim so the bowl slides, does not chatter. When you step from one board to another and your heel creaks the join, you press the place with your toe and listen until the sound leaves it.

At night the lines are taken down low, so they do not sing too much to the wind and keep the quarter awake. The canopies are furled and bound so their oils do not drain away. Jars are capped with clay lids that fit with a scraped-in breath around the neck. Lights are few: a stoved ember, a pot-glow, a wick that burns fish-tallow and whispers. The sump becomes a bigger idea in the dark than it is by day. The walls bruise with shadow again and begin their weeping in fine glazes, a quiet industry in the company of sleeping. You can hear the first beadlets form if you lie awake—an inward clicking like small teeth, like frost deciding where to begin. By dawn, the lines will be fat again, and the hands will go up, and somewhere a child will be learning which string to pluck to walk a small bead home.
