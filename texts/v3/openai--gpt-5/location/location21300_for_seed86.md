The rooms under the plateau do not sleep. They breathe; they hold a temperature that never seems to shift from cool; they drink and exhale the light at the same set of cracks each day, and otherwise remain a world of dimness knit together by sound.

You step down through a fracture in stone that looks as if it happened yesterday. The edges are still crisp where a block tore away, leaving a negative of itself on the ceiling—ribs and teeth of broken bedding planes, not yet softened by flowstone or rounded by time. The rock is pale, hardly stained, with runnels like frozen water on a wall, thin scallops where air once scoured and now only slides. Whole rooms are new to themselves: collapsed galleries, raw-sided corridors, ledges cut as if with a straight chisel left by the earth. This youth is everywhere—angular talus underfoot, splinters you can cut a palm on, shelves that look too sharp to belong underground.

Moisture is a film more than a flood. The place takes its water by breath. At certain hours a slow river of air moves from one end of the system to the other, drawn by weather far away. Where that draft narrows into slots, the stone sings—a vowel stretched and held—the pitch changing from chamber to chamber depending on the width of the crack. The airflow cools, takes on weight, and leaves dew behind. On some ceilings droplets stand in perfect beads, bristling along fault lines like a second, upside-down stalagmite field made of glass. They thickly string from the tips of fracture teeth and collect in shallow trays in the rock. Elsewhere, wind-flung grit has carved little basins that hold the pooled distillate of a night’s condensation, clear and unmoving, rimmed with a film so thin that it’s more rumor than surface.

Light falls in thin, slanted sheets. Skylights are not grand; they are slits in the roof, chimneys where a block settled a finger-width aside, round mouths of collapsed joints. Under each is a halo of the upper world—a pale oval at the bottom of a well—where color is permitted. Here, strings of wiry ferns grip the dampest cracks. Lichen constellations crust the windward side of a boulder as if someone had salted it and forgot. In deeper rooms, green withdraws to gray, and then to the chalk-white of mineral films on walls, and then to black. The dimness is enough to sketch the shape of the space and to show motion, nothing else.

Vegetation is reluctant and local. Fragments of grasses lie wind-blown in drifts at the foot of skylights, dried beyond recognition. On the walls where a perpetual drip maps a vertical river, a nap of slimy algae lays down a starved strip of green a hand-span wide. There are no trees. There is no sod. The places that eat light are small and do not own the cavern; they survive off what the air gives them and what the stone condenses for them, and they stop when the light does.

Animals are another story. The place belongs to wings, legs, and blind hunger. Ledges under the skylights are latticed with twigs pressed together by generations of swifts, the structure so old and so continually repaired that you can’t tell the present from the past. Farther in, galleries of bats hang from high ceilings like dark fruit, swaying slightly in the pressure of the cave wind; tens of thousands packed shoulder to shoulder in the dim, their combined shrill fizzing into a constant band of sound that has no beginning or end. Where the ceiling dips to within a jump of the floor, jeweled sheets of cave crickets cling, clustered around seams where dew pearls; when they shift, they do it in soft waves and the sound is dry rain.

Underfoot, their droppings make dunes—rounded, low, and springy to the sole. Those slow slopes feed an economy of bright isopods, ghost-pale cockroaches with garnet eyes that flash, millipedes with translucent rings. Spiders stake territories with triangulated thread in the mouths of passages where the wind delivers inattentive flyers. Whip scorpions flatten themselves under ledges and wave their long feelers through the draft like anglers in a river. Ringtails and foxes from the surface move in the penumbra around the light shafts to take what’s careless, never straying far into true dark. In pools, a monastery of pale fish, eyeless and nursing on the fall of microscopic things, ghosts around in slow circuits. Amphipods thicken the water like falling snow if you kneel and let your headlamp drag the beam.

It is loud, and the loudness is layered. Closest is the whisper that isn’t a whisper—the rushing of air through a thousand constrictions, every narrow turned into a flute. Over that, a ragged surf of wings: swifts chittering in volleys, bats pouring out and returning in continuous trance, each ripple from one colony smoothing into another a kilometer away so that there is never a pause, only a rise here matched by a fall there. In the high range, sleigh-bell rain when crickets engage their pegs, a field’s worth of stridulation held aloft by stone. The drop of water is a metronome. In a room where the floor is a slate of thick, still pools, the plink repeats in the same place for years; shallow cups ringed with a pale mineral lip record the exact target of the leak, and nothing shifts.

The rules here are set by the air and the dark. Where the wind accelerates, things that fly learn the currents and will ride them as reliably as a river bend; paths of air become highways, and you feel them cross your face when you stand in the right archway and let your hand get almost pulled. Sound is how space is known. Every resident has a map of echo angles, a sense of how long a shout would take to come back and the shape of the room it would be born in. That internal cartography changes so slowly—if at all—that chicks grow into the same echo their parents used.

Erosion writes in a young hand. What speleothems there are are only beginning—milk-white teardrops frozen into points, a curtain here that hasn’t yet learned to ripple, a fragile rime of gypsum needles furred along a seam where air and vapor are just right for crystal. The rest is structure laid bare: cross-bedded layers you could trace with your finger until they vanish into shadow, a cube of stone still resting where it fell from the ceiling some season no one noticed. Grains that the wind plucks from one face collect across from it as a faint dust on a sister wall, a quiet and perpetual exchange that has not yet—will not soon—dull the break.

Space is generous in its scale. You can walk for a long hour through tilted rooms without repeating the angle of your back. A hall will open into a nave you could hang a small weather in, its ceiling lost even to your idea of height. Then a crack will narrow to a slot you pass sideways through, the stone at your chest cold and damp, and on the other side it will release you to a basin so broad that even the constant chorus sounds very far away. The world under the plateau is not a cellar but a valley folded into blackout, a network more than a cave, braided for kilometers by faults and laced with wells to the sky. The emptiness is enormous between presences; animals press into their zones like storms on a map, leaving whole countries of stillness between.

Nothing is made in here but by the place itself or by the creatures that live in it. There are no cut steps, no marks except claw-scrape and wing-scuff, no ash splotches or lines. You will not find metal, only stone, bone, chitin, and the bright occasional jewel of a beetle’s back. Tools are beaks and mandibles and the cracked corner of a boulder that a bird has learned to use as an anvil for drying snail shells in the draft.

Smell collects and is almost physical. Guano has a high, ammoniac brightness, sweet and acrid at once. The rock adds a limey, wet-dust mouthfeel, something you taste more than smell. In some cuts there is a trace of the upper world—the sun-pitched scent of dried grass and cold soil—washed thin as it threads down the chimneys. Touch is untrustworthy: the stone is smooth where fog rides it daily and suddenly, unpredictably, harsh as a rasp at the corner of a fallen block.

For all the motion, it feels constant. Generations of nests adhere to ledges polished by the same wings that built their grandparents’ homes. The chorus never really falls silent; it changes shape but not volume, turns its head but keeps its gaze. The dew beads form in the same places and fall at the same slow intervals. Trails of small feet find the same arches, because the wind is still behaving like the same long muscle flexing. If a chunk detaches from above, it is as if it had been always on the floor where it now lies; it fits the grammar of the place and doesn’t alter the sentence.

You leave with the same drafts sliding along your skin, with the low tone still trembling in your ribs, with a brightness at the edge of your eyes where daylight collects in a distant hole. Above, the plateau looks empty—low scrub pulled thin across stone, the sky open and wind-fretted. Below, everything continues: a loud, dim equilibrium of rock, breath, water-bead, and creatures that have found a way to be many in the nearly nothing.
