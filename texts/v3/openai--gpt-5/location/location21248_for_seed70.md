Light arrives here after a long swim. It is ordinary daylight that has been sifted through warm meters of water until it is grainy and soft, a clear green-blue that finds its way into everything. Bodies and stones throw faint shadows that drift with the slow current. There is no sky to look up to; even when you tilt your head and peer upward, there is only more light, and the vague ceilingless suggestion that somewhere above—far, far above—this column of water will meet wind.

The land underneath is high, sharper than it has any right to be under water. Ridges step and knife like the backbones of sleeping animals. Basalt appears in prisms and edges, cooling features that in air would have flashed with heat not long ago; cut faces still show glassy skins and knife-thin ledges that catch on draping fronds. In the lee of each promontory, a little dune of fresh grit feathers out, the beginnings of sediment—a hint of wear, not yet a history. Where the plateau ends, it does not ease; it falls. The escarpments plunge in clean planes that vanish into blueness, and your stomach understands “high place” even though you and the stones are held in water.

The water is warm in a way that relaxes the joints. It carries the taste of new minerals and plant-sap. It is busy without being turbulent. Everywhere you look there is something growing. Plants have taken the stone the way fog takes cold glass—immediately and completely, but in thin layers at first, then in pads and folds. Hairs of emerald moss and bronze filaments throw lace against the rock; wider fans unfurl like the pages of huge wet books; long ribbons as wide as your wrist run for meters and meters, sagging in arcs and swaying as if they were tired flags. Bubble-vines twine through everything, making airy strings of silver beads that skitter upward and catch in the forked hands of other plants; knots of jelly-like roots press into cracks and fissures, pumping acids, softening the new rock to something they can drink. There are whole groves of floaters that never attach, quilted bladders of plant-stuff that live off the light alone, brushing the edges of the ridges as they pass and leaving threads of themselves behind.

The forest has more layers than you can count. On the surface of a broad, leathery leaf, a skin of finer leaves grows like frost. Within those filigrees, little gardens of fuzz and lace and grit-bacteria set up house. Everything is almost touching everything else; the space is vivid with the idea of contact. Weight is different here: you can press a finger into a mat and not quite know where the plant stops and the water begins. Roots are not single throws into soil; they are webs of gel and fiber and stone slivers assembled by patient excretion. In places the plants have built shelves—baffles really—that catch the silt that does appear, new terraces like a city of tongues.

Sounds do not come far. They collect around you in a bubble that moves as you move. The loudest noise is your own wake on the ferny stuff. Every few heartbeats there is a soft click like a mouth opening—a gas pocket shifting, a seed pod cracking, a tiny implosion where the pressure inside something has found a slick out. Now and then the wall of the escarpment mutters, a dry under-note transmitted through the rock as a knife-edge warms, expands, and then remembers its intention to become separate. You could call this place quiet and mean that there are no shouts, no calls, nothing to alarm—but it is not silence; it is a thickening. The plants whisper, but slower than whispers usually go.

Fauna is rare enough to be an event. You spend an hour with plants and stones and moving light, and then one slow animal passes and becomes the day. Sometimes it is a ray of a thing, not quite a fish and not quite a leaf, a translucent mantle wider than you are tall that glides low over the edges of the ridges as if searching for the islands of texture the way fingers search for braille. Its mouth is a long slot it presses against the mats, drawing a thin veneer of life into it, and you can see the plants relax after it leaves, re-inflating around the path of its eating. Another time it is nothing more than a cylinder—pale skin laminated with amber plates like the articulation of a fern stem—threading through vertical meadows, sampling the tips without hurry. You do not see predators; if they exist, they are too rare, too far between to belong to the day. Small things do exist in quantity, but so small you have to change how you look: comma-marks that hop at the edges of leaves; grains that flash and vanish; little starbursts that unfold when you overshadow them and retreat when you leave. They are not showy. They attend to their work.

Temperature is consistent, a warm hand held indefinitely. The current here is a concept more than a fact. If anything moves on a grand scale, you measure it by the way the bubble chains lean over more in the afternoon than at noon, or by the angle of frond-stems when a distant tide shifts the pressure. Drift is the rule: a ribbon of plant that touches a new rock and is content to stay; then its children fill a ledge; then the ledge is a shelf; then the shelf is a table; and in a few human lifetimes, the gullies become ambers of captured grains, the sharp edges softened to tongues. But today the basalt is still angular, and most things that touch it must choose: wrap tight or be sawed.

This is a highland ocean. That is the rule that feels strangest and most certain after awhile. Down the cliff, down through blue into blue, there is more water; above is more water; and still you are on a mountain. You can tell in the way bubbles behave—they are large here, lazy and bright, as if slightly inebriated by the thinness of the world this high up. The gas the plants give off accumulates in pendent garlands, too buoyant to get stuck for long, not buoyant enough to sprint away; eventually, every pearl finds its path upward in infinitesimal increments. There are hours when a slow rain falls inside the water, the only version of rain that makes sense here: microscopic droplets condensing along a thermocline that wandered in from elsewhere, coalescing into veils that settle through you with the sound of a sigh. The plants drink that too. There is no surface in sight, but sometimes a blaze of brighter blue passes overhead and everything stiffens slightly, and you realize a clear window in the upper layers has drifted past, a skin lensed just right that brought the idea of noon closer before going on its way.

Edge-places are theaters. Along the brink of the great drop, where light fractures down and down, stands a rank of vegetative sculptures: spiraling chimneys built by mats that learned to climb their own excretions; tall candelabra where bubble-vines have stiffened into bone with mineral salts; curtains of tape-leaves that push out over nothing and then drape back, sucking their own edges and growing from both ends at once. The farthest tips hang in a slow perpetual fall, never quite choosing to tear free. Once in many hours a leviathan shape will come shouldering up from the bluer dark, uncurious and quiet, brushing the pendant forests with a side that feels like warm glass. It brings with it the smell of distance and a scattering of other waters.

Rules of the world here are simple enough to be unnerving. Water climbs hills and stays there, because it is not the kind that needs low ground. Plants are the engineers, and their tools are acids, gels, gas, and time. Rock has only just arrived and has not yet learned its manners; it still startles itself with a flake here, a split there. Light enters and is eaten slowly. Sound is a local thing, polite and close. Movement is made of patience. From time to time, along the plateau’s flat back, a wall of whiteness appears in the far midwater and moves like weather, a slow procession of cloud within liquid—condensation along a boundary you cannot map, cooling or salting by some distant cause. It passes through the forest with less force than a thoughtful hand brushes through hair, and yet the plants lean for hours afterward as if remembering.

If you stay long enough, you start to recognize the place not by scenery but by its habits. This ledge that always exhales a little more in the afternoon; that grove whose fans fold by degrees when a certain shade comes into the water; the escarpment’s habit of dropping a plate every hundredth day. These are slow cycles, the tempo of a region that has been remade recently and intends to keep to itself. When you finally rise along one of the brighter corridors and the light swells, the plateau slides away beneath you like a thought you were trying to hold, clean-edged, newly covered, dense with quiet things working, far above the world and drowned.
