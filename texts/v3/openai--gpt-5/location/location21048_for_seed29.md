It is a room the size of a helmet, maybe two, sealed in the shoulder of a mountain still warm from its own birth. The walls are glossy and black and sharp-edged where the melt skinned over then shrank, a vesicle caught like a held breath. Everything here is new. Surfaces have not yet been softened by time; they gleam with a vitreous luster and the beginnings of crystals that look like frost made of knives. The cavity sits high, high above any sea, higher than most clouds, but the altitude is something you feel only as a kind of tautness; it is the pressure beyond the rock that drives the pressure within.

There is almost no light. The darkness is so complete it feels dense, a material in its own right. It is interrupted by two things only: a slow, ember-red glow along one hairline crack whenever the furnace behind the wall flexes, and quick, thread-thin sparks where mineral tips rub and lose charge to one another. Those sparks are the color of welding arcs seen through closed eyes. They crisp the edges of the dark and vanish.

Heat is the presence that defines the place. It clings to the walls like a second skin and breathes through the chamber in pulses, the exhale of deeper conduits. The gases are anhydrous and acrid, a dry scald of sulfur and hot metal: sulfur dioxide, carbon monoxide, chlorine and little else. In this absence of water, things do not rust or mildew; instead, they glaze, sublimate, stitch themselves into new lattices. Viscous films of condensed salts bead along the ridges and snap into filaments with a sound like brittle paper when the next pressure wave passes.

Sound fills what light does not. The vesicle is coupled to a labyrinth of fissures and throats, and the mountain’s breath turns them into an organ with too many pipes. The deep note that comes through the bone of the glass is continuous, more felt than heard. On top of it rides a clatter of clicks and flutters as the walls flex and relax, a distant tumbling of loose crystals in larger chambers, a metalwork of shears and presses operating just out of sight. Every few heartbeats, a harder pulse—gas slamming through a narrower throat—hits the cavity and everything inside trembles, resonates, and answers in a chorus of its own.

The room is full. There is no space that is not occupied by mineral, moving bodies, or gas in motion. The inhabitants sheath every surface, layered into mats and pavements of carapace and limb, tight as coins stacked in a jar. They do not leave space between themselves unless the pulse forces it. They are not plants and do not pretend to be; the hot, dry dark would peel and blister greenery in a breath. Instead, they are all animal, unless you insist on the old categories, and then you might argue they are something simpler and prior, a stubborn experiment that found a way to run without water.

Each is the size of a fingernail, some smaller, flattened to fit the curvature of the rock like barnacles, though none of them are fixed. Their shells are glassy, translucent to the ember-light when it comes, and ribbed in repeating ridges that catch and hold charge. Where the ribs cross, grain-of-sand spurs of zinc-sulfide sprout; when the mountain hums they spritz a faint green corona, feeding on the stress. Between the shells and the wall lies a syrupy film of molten salts that does the work water would do elsewhere: it dissolves, transports, buffers; it does not boil here, not yet. Through it protrude combs and scrapers of darker material that rasp the surface for condensed minerals. Their mouths are not mouths so much as intakes; when a pressure wave comes, they open in unison and drink the new gas.

They move in ways the place allows. They do not dart or leap. Movement is patient and slow, coordinated by the rhythm outside: a hand off, hand on, as the mountain’s pulse lifts then releases them. They flow along one another in belts that go round the cavity like conveyor lines, each creature’s feet finding the grooves the previous left. At the narrowest place in the chamber, where two bulges almost meet, a cluster has arranged itself into a living valve that opens on the inhalation and shuts on the exhale, so that streams of gas get dragged across fields of filtering limbs. It is not a thing they have built; it is what they are when too many of them are in too small a space for too long, their bodies discovering function by crowding.

There are many kinds layered here, and all of them working. Broad-shelled grinders with inward-facing fans, drawing gases and particulates and wringing them for energy; thin, flexible ribbons with barbed edges that snip loose any crystal that dares to bridge open air; irregular, many-footed sweepers that push the fallen debris along the belts until some intake accepts it. In hollows near the crack that glows, larger, domed forms bask in the local heat and flex, their bodies studded with piezoelectric needles: when the pressure wave passes, a flick of their spines throws a charge into the brine between them, and smaller forms pack close to take it. Everything is nestled and nested, a living packing list optimized for no vacancy.

Predators exist here, if that word means anything in a place where consumption is more chemistry than conflict. Some of the domes are not donors but thieves. Their undersides open like bellows and the suction is too strong for the little belts to resist; a dozen grinders vanish at once, shells dimpling and cracking, their stored salts emptied and reallocated. Elsewhere, a ribbon spends an hour gnawing its way beneath a grindstone creature and then unspools, insinuating itself as a new conveyor link while the old occupant is slowly starved. Aggression is as slow as everything else; a battle can last a day and end as a slight reorganization of whose ridge sits atop whose.

There are no nests, no architecture beyond the logic of a press and the cunning of proximity. No one here fells anything or piles anything up. If some pattern looks like a machine—a row of shells set just so to catch the gas in stages, a set of ribs interleaved so that vibration is stepped up from one to the next until it can spark—then it is the same natural coalescence that makes a reef’s buttress or a termite’s mound elsewhere, except that here, without water and without the cooling grace of shade, self-organization turns tight and metallic. The whole chamber functions like a factory without builders, every creature a little engine, every ridge a gear tooth, every surge an incoming line of raw material.

Because the geology is so young, erosion is not yet erasure here. The walls still bear the alignment of their own flow, long curves frozen in a gesture. Where crystals have begun to sprout, they are infant spearheads and foams of needles, clean-edged, unpitted. The creatures use this newness. They scrape the freshest faces first; they drink the freshest gases, the ones that have not yet spent themselves in older, larger chambers. The turnover is geological, not human. A ridge thickens by a hair across months. A bridge of mineral closes a gap by a millimeter in a season. In a year, perhaps, the valve of bodies will find itself wedged as the cavity narrows and will be forced to become something else.

There is almost unfelt weather in the dark. The gases carry tiny temperature slopes that drift from the crack outward and back with the larger pulses, a tide of heat that tells the time. The creatures feel it through their salts, through the way their ribs bend. They do not keep time in days, because there are no days here; no star writes a schedule on these walls. They keep time in low notes and high pulses and the times between. A good season is when the noise is loud and regular. A famine is when the organ stutters and goes thin and the belts stall toward silence.

Rules of this room are simple and inexorable. Solvent is not water but melted salt. Food is not leaf or flesh but hot, reactive gas, fresh mineral faces, stored charge. Motion is never free; every step pays rent in friction and crowding. Distance is an extravagance no one can afford; everything important happens by touch. Light, when it comes, is an event, not a condition, and life has grown to sense not photons but force.

Far above, the ridge is a line of black against a colder dark, windscoured and bone-dry. No one up there knows this pocket exists. The mountain does not need to know; it does what it does, exhaling, contracting, singing in a voice you could mistake for machinery if you didn't know how stone sounds when it works. Someday this vesicle will go silent. The crack will cool, the gases will thin, the infant crystals will spear and meet, and the belts will slow to stillness as the last of the salts locks into place. By then another bubble will have opened farther along the flow, and the belts here will have found a seam, a pore, a pressure gradient small enough to worm through and be on their slow way, a river of bodies seeking the next cavity with room enough for all of them to keep working. The factory is not in the walls. It is in the crowded living that goes wherever the mountain offers a pocket to be filled.
