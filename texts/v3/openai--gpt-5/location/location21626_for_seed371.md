You come into it by sound long before you come into it by sight: a pressure in the ribs, a continuous iron breath rattling through stone. The air is thin and cold enough to make teeth sing; it smells like wet mineral and the faint metallic sweetness of ozone. Darkness occupies everything, but it’s a busy darkness, the kind full of motion you can feel in your skin.

You’re in a high notch carved by no hand, uplifted quick in geological terms, a fault-tear through a young volcanic ridge. The place is small by mountain standards—cathedral-sized, maybe a long nave nested between black stone blades. Walls rise close on both sides, less a canyon than a slot with only a suggestion of a sky. That suggestion is almost always a smear of cloud shouldering its way across the gap, the seam where weather is pinched.

The rock shows its youth: sharp planes sheared so clean they still reflect the scant light, glassy faces of andesite and basalt that flake like broken plates. Columnar joints stand as hexagonal ribs in some recesses, and in others the stone has been split into fins, stacked almost like the pipes of an organ. The edges are unsoftened; a gloved hand will find them keen enough to tug at leather. Where stone meets stone, no long time has yet gone by to grind them to comfort.

All of it is sealed up in greenness. Lush in the tightest possible way: every surface with a little purchase is wrapped, layered, dethroned and enthroned again by moss. The moss is many things here—cushions like packed velvet, fronds so fine they look like fog made solid, mats tremulous with water when the clouds are heavy. Filmy ferns paint the verticals, their laminae as thin as a breath on glass, trembling at the slightest draught. Clubmosses stand upright in their miniature cypress groves, sporangia fat on their tips. Sedges wedge themselves into cracks, blades scissored narrow, edges beaded. Flat rosettes—alpine houseleeks and their near—pack shallow bowls in the rock; they’ve learned to be their own roofs and gutters, combing vapour out of the air and dripping it toward their cores.

Water here is always made, rarely given. Rain does happen, but not on a schedule; it comes walking in with the cloud now and then and spends itself quickly, a hard, brief pour that drums the moss and runs off in silver threads. More often it’s the air surrendering to the surfaces, a steady sweat beading on leaves and stone whenever wind and temperature and the minute textures are right. It collects in hairline scallops along the underside of rock lips, falls as a countable series of drops while the gale keens, then it is gone again, drunk by plant and pore and the capillaries in the mats. Pockets in the floor—the rare bowl the young rock allows—hold water for a time, smoked grey in this dimness, little worlds perhaps the size of a helmet, laced at the rims with green beards.

The light is almost never bright enough to make a cast shadow. It’s that peculiar upland dark you get in thick cloud: the sky is there and not there, like a lid of milk. On some days everything is only a series of slightly different blacks and greens. When there is a break somewhere far above or far off, what light does trickle in comes cold and bluish. The most honest illumination you meet is in storms. Then the entire notch, for a breathless fraction of a second, is drawn in silver by lightning, every wet facet blazing. And in some blows—those with charge so pent the hair lifts on your arms—there’s St. Elmo’s fire walking the edges: a quiet corona, violet-blue, licking from the spear-points of sedge blades, from the driest ends of broken twigs, from the needle-fine terminations of rock. It is not much to see; it is enough to say the place is electrified.

The sound is industrial without being made. Wind is forced through the bottleneck in pulses you can set a clock by if there were any clock here to keep. It stacks up on the upwind side of the ridge until the pressure finds its sluices; then the whole notch inhales and exhales. In one organ of stone pipes the air answers in tone—low, grainy notes as if some buried turbine were spooling up and down. Elsewhere it’s percussive, hammers on an anvil-face. Water joins in like an assembly line that starts and stops: drips become drumming become rills that slap and chatter, then vanish to single tick-ticks as the air dries. In freeze-prone nights or days at the exact edge, rime plates build outward until weight breaks them all at once, and a local avalanche of sugar-ice cascades off a lip with a sound like a pallet of glass beads poured onto a metal floor.

Everything is close. Dense in the visual ways—planes of wet leaf over moss over lichen over stone—and dense in the way the air is stacked with presences: spore dust, mist, fine chips and soot of volcanic origin. You can reach both walls at once in some corridors, and touch more living things with two hands than you could name in an hour, none of them moving, all of them busy. There is a continuous tautness in the vegetation, the leaf-edges constantly worrying at the air as if trying the lock to see if it will open into rain.

Despite all this lushness, there is no animal sound. Not a wingbeat from a bird sailing the high winds outside; not the scratch of a beetle under bark. No midges snowing in the few scraps of light, no web gloved over a corner, no lizard’s scribble in damp grit. Leaves go unmarred by bites; spores have all the sky to themselves. The only motion not wind’s or water’s is the slow creep of green. Pollination is a matter of chance and breath: ferns and clubmosses release their powders when pressures shift; sedges let their dust fall in the gusts. Everything that eats does so without a mouth. Bacteria stain the film of wet on stone, tannin-brown in places. Fungi in the mats thread and unthread themselves with the sureness of things that keep their own time—should you pull a tuft back you’d find the mineral fur of mycelium binding the place together, bright as hoar.

The world outside the notch is harsher, skinned with raw rock and alpine plain. Up here the elevation is so much that the horizon is a dark serration under a very close sky. Weather is near, as if there’s less of it between you and whatever the atmosphere is doing. The notch is an accident that concentrates this nearness; it is a mill that runs on gradients. Wind is its fuel; vapor is its feedstock; it produces water for the room and charge for the air. Every cycle is stepped-down, mechanized by form. Venturi and eddy do their work wherever the stone shapes them. Capillarity and adhesion route the moisture as if along pipes, and you could follow a damp line with a finger without breaking it for the span of a man’s height. Roots are wires as much as they are anchors, linking mats so that when one face is wet, the whole wall is briefly bright.

The geology has barely had time to invent soils. Where detritus gathers it is a black gel in hollows, a centimeter deep if that, and packed with the remains of centuries of short lives that grew and failed to hang on. Out of that gel rise the greener, thicker things: the saxifrages and their like, cushion plants that make their own windbreaks by being their own landscape, their domes tight against the atmosphere’s hands. They trap heat by millimeters, make rain by hairs, and split each crevice wider by small insistences so that their daughters have someplace to put a rootlet. It is succession happening at high speed and very slowly, both. A new flake of stone splits; three wet seasons later it grows a fur; a generation after, a seed tries the ledge; a decade after that the ledge thickens by a leaf’s width.

Change is the constant state. In an hour the notch can go from dry enough to squeak underfoot to a sluice where every step wrings sound. Thunder migrates along the ridge and then is gone, audio of the machine powered down, plants lifting again from the bow they made. The cycle writes its record in ephemeral stains: salt prints where water retreated, pollen smears where a gust hit during a particular bloom, the thin glass of re-frozen drip where the previous night was a fraction colder. In one corner, a fresh scar bright as tooth enamel where a plate of wall the size of a door just went, torn free by ice perhaps, dropping to break and scatter into a new geometry of edges and catchments.

There are places within the place. A cul-de-sac behind a fallen column where the wind only touches on high-power days; here the moss mounds into pillows and, if you kneel, you can hear nothing but the soft rasp of air through its own gates. A hanging ledge with a curtain of sedge blades that now and then light up blue for a heartbeat when a cloud above rubs itself raw into a bolt. A narrow throat where the tone of the organ pipes insists on a note so low it is felt in the sinuses more than heard, the pitch changing with the standing water level in a pocket somewhere you never quite catch sight of. The boundaries of the notch are fingers rather than lines, and each finger composes its own weather from the general supply.

Rules of this world:
- Nothing is finished. The rock is still what it was a moment ago and is always becoming something else—edges remain edges until they aren’t, and then they’re edges of another sort.
- Water exists by arrangement. There’s no lake, no stream that stays put; there’s only what the forms agree to collect and what they allow to pass.
- Life here is mostly laminar. Layers upon layers, sheets of function stack tight: photosynthesis, then storage, then structure, then grip; all lungless, all busy.
- Electricity is ordinary. Charge lives in the wind and leaks along points. Sparks, here, are as common as stars are elsewhere.
- Loud is not the same as alive. The absence of animal must is an emptiness you notice only after a while, when you realize that none of the sounds dodge around anything.
- The space is a machine by virtue of shape, not by parts. Wind is the engine, cloud is the belt, stone is the chassis, moss the moving carpet that carries water, dust, and nutrients from intake to where they can be held.

If you leave and step out onto the ridge proper, the noise collapses into plain wind and distance. Looking back, you can’t see the notch at all—it disappears into the geometry and the weather. But it goes on running whether anyone stands inside it or not, running like a plant or a plant like a factory, tuned to a set of narrow, cool circumstances: dense with green, loud with air, dark as a lung, high as a decision, young enough to be sharp.
