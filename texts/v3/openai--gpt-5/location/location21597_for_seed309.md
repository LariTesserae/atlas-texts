You step into the green’s interior and the first thing is the voice of it: a long, slow thunder that is not thunder, a breath as big as a district taken through diaphragms of living tissue. Every surface hums back. It’s hard to tell which part is plant and which part is machine—their rhythms have found each other—until the second sound arrives, sharper and layered, a stacked clatter of chitin and wing, a rain of legs across bark. The hall is warm and damp the way a breath is damp: moisture beading and pulling itself into a drop here and there, hanging, falling, ticking. When the plant exhales, you taste copper and leaf sap in your mouth.

It is below ground in the technical sense, below whatever passes for surface here—a pale, sun-glossed skin of leaf-plate somewhere far above—but the interior is not a cave. It is a nave. It is a chest cavity. You could call it a station if stations were grown. Columns thicker than houses curve up in slow helices, roots the size of towers braided and fused by pressure and time, and between them a volume large enough to let a wind build its own weather. The plant has learned to bear its own weight gracefully; long ago its tissues settled into ribs and vaults, hollows where gas moves and heat bleeds, galleries full of air to keep the rest from drowning itself.

This place has known itself for a long time. The lines of growth are old and then older, overlain with polish where generations of mouths have rasped and tongues have cleaned and bellies have slid. Nothing has a sharp edge for long. Sap once bled down a wall and made a runnel; now that runnel is a groove, and the groove is a path followed by the clicking millions. Resin sealed a fracture decades ago; the scar is domed and glossy, like the beat of a drum captured in amber. There are no true corners, only the softened memory of them.

The light refuses to commit. It is generally dim, like late afternoon behind two curtains, and every brightness is local, a source with a halo. Pale bands crawl up the columns—algal skins that learned to glow at the frequencies the plant can’t see—so that the pillars read as slow comets. Where the breathing-ducts constrict there are membranes of translucent tissue webbed with veins; as pressures shift they flex and let through hints of the world above: a muted square of daylight that slides, then fades, then opens again like an eye. Industrial lamps are slung from cables in fixtures built to mock fruit, heavy and narrow, sodium orange and humming, often battered, sometimes broken—when a lamp goes, the shatter draws moths with glassy wings that fling themselves into its heat until the heat is gone and the darkness is a churning cloud. In a pinch, people smear the walls with sugar resin to wake the lichens; a bare handprint will glow for a short while, a covenant signed in green.

Wetness is episodic. The air wears its water like a shawl, close and fine, then drops it all at once. The plant exhales warmth and vapor; a change in the outside pressure—or in here, when the Works stoke their heat—condenses that vapor on cooler root and bark. Dew grows on everything. It gathers along cable runs and leaf-curl eaves, in the scooped bowls the sap-eaters leave. When it swells to mass it lets go, and there is a sifting rain for ten minutes, or thirty, or sometimes an hour if the breath-cycle and the machines’ schedule align. Pools appear in sockets, fringe themselves with a living scum in seconds, begin to be busy. You can watch fish happen: thin as needle-thread, silver as a lie, they assemble out of nowhere, become bodies all at once, flick their lives in the pool until the last droplet is swallowed and they click shut into dry things like seeds, waiting. Some cisterns are engineered—broad pans of finned metal that the Works have coaxed up into the air on plant-ropes, trays tilted to a gutter that feeds a tank. Water is harvest here; it is never free for long.

The plant is most of the world. It is the world. The ground is not ground but skin and cambium and fiber-thick mat, crisp here, spongy there, warm in patches where the sap runs close. The floor bows a little underfoot. There are perforated plates where the aerenchyma opens into foam, and beneath that the wet lung of the plant, and beneath that whatever heat engine all this was built to serve. Where the roots meet the wall, the texture changes: frayed rope into varnished bone, lamellae peeling like book-pages. Every surface is, if not actively growing, then at least in conversation with growth. If you leave something set against the bark for long—the barrel you meant to move, a corpse, a promise—thin white threads thread it. The plant tests it for edges. The Works’ rule is to leave a sleeve of raw fiber between any machine and any living surface, a buffer the plant can remake rather than invade.

The city lives here as a suspicion. All the signs of a settlement are present—work-lamps in rows, railings and gantries, safety paint on the rungs, a block where workshops run into cookshops, towers of fiberboard crates strapped with resin—and yet all of it is strung thin across the volume, pulled out like a spider wants it. Streets are catwalks. Rooms are platforms that lean out into the hollow, one here on this column, another two hundred paces along and three body-lengths above, one more halfway across the void where a cable burdened with baskets sags. The Works have no facades to face. They bolt iron vine into plant rib, lash bridge to bridge, roll pipe along whatever path provides. The heavy engines are mounted where the breath is strongest: big ducted turbines squatting in vent mouths, their vanes matched to the plant’s pulse, their shafts singing as they take from the exhale and give back on the draw. There is a beating heart of a mill that presses fiber into sheets the size of doors, a row of vats where resin is cooked down to pitch and glue, a shed of looms that take the plant’s long cellulose and spin it into rope and ribbon. Motors drone. Belts slur. Pressure vessels hiss and buck their valves. You can hear all the functions at once, like standing too close to a bad orchestra, and yet the plant is louder still.

Electricity comes by barter. The turbines throw current into a web of cables, and the plant throws something back: a steadiness of pressure when the people need it to run their cutters, a long sleep to settle the swarms in the shift hiss of night. The Operators swear the plant likes regularity. There is a sense that somebody is counting with you.

Fauna is the overstory even in a place without a sky. Everything moves with mouths and legs if you stare long enough. There are the outright swarms: clouds of gnats that drink fungus tears and turn them into a fine dust that smells a little like coriander, carpets of little back-first beetles that hoover the dust before it ferments and fouls the breath-channels, whip-thin shrimp that walk tiptoes on the meniscus when the dew is up and go glass-pale when it leaves. There are the grazers of meat—that is, of plant, which is the only meat here: white and blind and sinuous things that rasp with round mouths the way a man grinds coffee, leaving right-circular patches of raw where the cambium shows, their bellies braided with glow-cord gut full of living algae. There are the pollinators of absence, the ones that seek wounds and seal them: off-balance flies with manganese eyes that swarm any breach in the bark and spit hardening foam until the stimulus passes, nursemaids of the skin.

Predators come not as individuals but as strategies. Nothing hunts by itself. There are thread-wolves you may never see, stringing silk across the emptier reaches and plucking themselves into chords; anything that vibrates wrong is stunned by the resonance and reeled in. There are hangers-on you will see far too close: fin-arrow mantids that flatten against the bark like a paint-scratch until the right kind of shadow crosses them, and then half the air is a scissor and something small is gone. There are fist shrimps the Works call hammerhands that live in the dew-pans and hit the water so hard it makes a cavity; the concussion knocks prey silly and several days’ dinners float up. Once a month, coordinated without a signal anyone can name, the bats come up the long ducts from a deeper chamber—velvet knots of them, soft as the inside of the plant itself—and pour like water from one interstice to the next, turning the orange lamps into comet trains with their milling. The Operators nail down everything light on those nights, and still the bats carry off lengths of cord and spoons and once a man’s hat, which came back three days later layered with guano and the beginning of moss.

Beneath the sound of the machines there is the clicking and sliding and gauzy thrumming of bodies. If you press your ear to the bark you can hear the slow rasp of the root-grazers’ teeth. If you stand under a cable at dewfall, the tiny rain on your face is insect bodies bouncing to clear the lane. Voices fight it. People who live here learn to aim their words like thrown bolts; even lovers speak like foremen calling orders in a high shop. It would be unbearable if it changed. The Works runs on schedules the plant keeps and the animals confirm. You sleep in the two hours after the long exhale, when the air is clear and the breath is drunk, when the swarms settle themselves in their nap-places and even the hammerhands go still. You work when the lungs fill, because the big turbines spin best on the draw and the belts won’t slip.

Rules arise and harden. Don’t bring bright white light; it confuses the membranes and calls the glasswings, and they will cut their own wings to shreds a hundred times rather than stop, and then you have a storm of death and bare flesh exposed to the little fathers. Never cut live bark without someone posted with a sealing pole and a bucket of nurse fly eggs. When the plant is hot to the touch, do not open a tank; wait. Do not scratch the glow-lichens into letters; your graffiti teaches predators to listen. Drink on schedule: the dew will come, but not when you most want it.

Erosion here is not water and time in the rock sense. It is behavior for long enough. The flows of creatures carve valleys through possibility, and then those valleys constrain what arrives next. The footing is good where many have gone and the plant has given up trying to make it otherwise, turning footpath into bare subdermal wood that takes a nail well. The footing is bad where things don’t equalize: a single set of boot-prints becomes a place where the plant grows thumb-wood to hold your weight, and then never stops. The Works learn it, and sometimes they lie, dragging ropes to tell the plant false places to thicken, leading its grown supports to where a walkway will be in six months rather than where it is now.

The strangest things are simply things that have been true for long enough that the plant incorporates them without comment. The oldest turbine is no longer bolted; it is cuffed by wood, its mount swallowed, its intake skirted by growth that seems designed to prevent backflow. That’s a story the engineers used to tell as a caution, then as a joke, and now as a blessing; the old one is the steadiest, irons out a hiccup in the air as deftly as a hand on a throat. The Operators keep a score of pressures and flows on a wall slate and then strike lines through half, because the plant began to keep its own invariants. Once in a while a dendrite of darker bark creeps across a wall, seam-stitching two hollows; the stitch vibrates like a string for weeks, and the thread-wolves move their webs to it and become a permanent feature. The Works run communications on the phloem: tap two iron fish-hooks into a sap vein and you can hear what the same vein says a hundred paces away, a knack stolen from beetles that learned it first. The city’s telegraph is a rack of raspers worked by hand. The messages are felt more than heard, but you do hear them, in your sternum, over the machines.

Space lies in wait between presences. For every nest under the gantries there is a stretch of bark wall the size of a field where nothing stands, no platform is hung, and only the patterns of algae and the slow creep of repair flies break the plane. The emptiness is a relief. It soaks the noise and lets your eyes rest, and it is where the big ones pass. Once in a long while the whole hall thickens with a shadow that is not the loss of light but the presence of more air, and a thing like a wing with no body crosses from one duct to another, pushing a wind of smell behind it: rutabaga, cough syrup, the inside of a book. You don’t look straight at it; other senses register it, then deny. These are the plant’s ventilators, you’re told, reflex knots of tissue the size of storefronts that open and close to balance loads. Maybe. They come on the same long timescale as the floods of bats. The Works schedule nothing in those hours.

Past the engine platforms, down where the dew pools when it is most generous, there is a grove that is not a grove, it’s simply where the plant has grown stalactites instead of stalagmites. The drips accrete sugars into icicle shapes. Long as your arm, some of them, weighted with insects drunk on sweetness pressed into the cane like fossils. Children break them off and suck them and go into a white sleep and wake with their tongues sanded. There are always teeth marks. The grooves are a calendar more honest than any chalked on slate.

You live here by counting cycles and respecting pace. You live here by accepting that plant and animal and machine are exchangeable categories some of the time. The pressure waves that drive the turbines also carry scent and spore and sound; they lift the dew into being and crush it back inside and rock the bats in their knots and, probably, convey news the plant shares between itself and itself. You can stand on a catwalk and feel it all rising through your bootsole: the Works throwing sparks, the plant taking sparks and smoothing them inside its green arithmetic, the animals editing the making down to what can survive.

This room—call it a hall, a hollow, a chamber, a lung—has edges that you can reach in an hour’s careful walking. A cable leads to a sister place where the pressure is a fraction higher and the algae are a fraction bluer. It is a single feature area with boundaries and yet it is not discrete. Nothing in it seems to admit of separation. The plant has worked here a very long while and arrived at a balance that looks like mess until you mistake it for noise. If you fall silent and let the breath have you, the logic stands up.

It is warm enough that your fingertips prune if you spend too long working a wet line. It is dim enough that even lit work happens in halos and guesses. It is loud enough that you will never again think of silence without also thinking of extermination. It is damp enough some of the time that a fish can live eighty heartbeats, and dry enough others that the ash from last season’s lamps is still powder at the base of the pillar. It is strange, but only the way a machine you haven’t seen before is strange when you are a mechanic and know that gears are gears, even if these gears are teeth in a blind mouth making food that looks like another mouth for something else.

You take your hat off when you cross the old turbine’s walkway, because the Operators do, and because the wind is steady there and it has a way of cleaning your ears of the noise long enough that you can hear the rivers in the tissue underfoot. You touch the bark with the back of your hand to ask if it is a good time to move a beam; if it’s too hot you don’t, if it’s too cold you don’t, and in between you hoist. Machine, plant, animal, person: here, each is a way the place continues to say itself. You will learn to hear it. It is already loud.
