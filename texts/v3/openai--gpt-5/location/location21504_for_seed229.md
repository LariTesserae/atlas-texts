At the height where the air thins to song, a shallow bowl has been carved by centuries of abrasive wind. It is not large—one could walk its rim in an hour without hurrying—but it is complete in itself, an organ built of stone and living sail. The light here is always evening, filtered through a permanent haze of pale amber dust that eats the glare and leaves everything with a bruised sheen. There is no water. Nothing glistens except polished mineral and the occasional bluish corona as charged grains discharge against a frond. Dryness is the prime law: salts bloom instead of dew, crystals spread where on other worlds damp would gather, and the very idea of wet is distant and dangerous.

The ground is a mosaic of ancient scours and soft sounds. Yardangs lean like sleeping animals, their flanks fluted, their noses hunched into the prevailing flow. Fields of needles—ventifacts only a finger high—stand bristling, humming together as gusts comb through them. The soil is not soil but a friable cake of chlorides, nitrides, light metals fused into crusts by lightning and time. When you step it is like stepping on old sugar glass: a brittle crackling, a fine perfume of resins and tangs released by breakage. In the bowl’s center, a saucer of paler salt shines matte under the dim, the result of a thousand cycles of deposition and drift—no basin of water, but a congregation place for solvents that never boil or freeze in the human sense, only thicken and thin and soak into what lives here.

Vegetation rises sparingly but decisively, each presence given room, as if each plant were its own weather. The sail-trees—if trees they are—root by drilling ceramic tap-threads deep into the mineral mass. Their trunks are not wood; they are a composite of chitin-like polymer glazed with silica, banded with vanes. From these trunks unfurl great, ribbed paddles of glossy graphite-black, veined with coppery filaments. They turn with the wind, but also against it, tasting charge and current, sifting for ions the way a mouth might taste wine. Where the paddles split and curl, muffled thunder pours out; their edges flicker with tiny discharges, and sometimes a whole tree lifts a harmonic that seems too large for it, a human-voiced note you feel in the ribcage. Between these giants are lower growths: mats of crackle-lichen that look like brown frost, pink tangles of nerveweed that flex in little stuttering spasms as if irritated by invisible gnats, and ripple-bushes whose leaves are shutters that slam and relax in waves to pump the bowl’s thin gases through their flavors of chemistry.

Without water their saps are different. Their inner seas are eutectic brines of metals and organics, slurries of molten salt and oil-thick solvents that would set skin alight if they touched it. Their pores do not open for vapor; their skins are not wax against water loss but insulators against charge. They drink from wind and dust, gulping electron and cation, trading mineral bite for energy. Where they scrape the ground, new crusts form not from evaporated ponds but from exhaled salts and captured aerosols, ringed like tree-shadow halos around them.

Life is loud. The bowl focuses the planet’s breath, and every being has learned to make use of the noise. The sail-trees boom and shear and sigh. The ripple-bushes clap. Near the salt saucer, the ground itself sometimes sings—a sheet of tiny crystals rippling all at once, throwing off a low, throat-thrilling moan as a gust passes. Layered through all this are the animals. They are abundant to the point of astonishment, though never a swarm that blocks the sky; there is much empty air against which they define themselves.

There are gliders, translucent and lanterned, stitched of silica film and tendon, who catch the bowl’s standing waves and hang there like kites, cilia combing ions, a faint blue gas gathered in their sacs for buoyancy and chemistry. There are stilt-gnashers, quick-footed as birds, with six limbs and a glittering beak that chews crusts and excretes neat little bricks that roll down to visitors like gifts. There are drum-moths large as hands, bright with static, that clap their shell-lamellae to keep position and find mates in the roar; their bodies smell like pepper and cold metal. Up among the sail-trees live tether-backs, squat mammals-or-something with plated hides and many eyes, who carry in their spines the copper tethers they wind around a vane in the morning and unwind at dusk to glow softly from gathered charge. There are burrowers that never touch light: oval plates of moving tesserae that raft under the crust like ripples under skin, mining for slow pools of liquid solvent secreted by roots. When they surface, their backs break into applause under the dust. Each creature has some porch of its body extended to the air—fans, vanes, whips, ladders of vibrating hairs—to sense and drink the electricity that is as food here as mineral and breath.

The air smells not of rain but of bitten coin, of resin smoke, of ozonelike sweetness that is not ozone. It flickers. Dust grains hang like constellations within arm’s reach, and each time the wind slackens for a heartbeat you can hear a thousand minute chippings, the lathery crackle of ions settling, the patter of sand that is not sand but cast-off spicules from lichen crusts. Then the breath comes back hard, and everything leans: the sails flatten briefly, the gliders bank, a stump of rock screams like a bowed saw. Turbulence is the ordinary state; calm is the aberration. Standing waves of pressure slide like invisible fish through the bowl. Now and then an updraft amplifies through a pumice-vent on the rim and a white body of dust leaps up like a geyser, shearing into dirty banners that course downwind, painting transient shadows over everything. The plants are made for this, the animals play in it, and even the rocks answer.

Built things exist, but they hide in the lee of larger lives, unwilling to challenge the wind. On the north inner rim, a clutch of huts crouches in a sheltered crease—shells of local salt-sinter and resin, roofs weighted with smooth ventifact stones that glow faintly at night with trapped charge. Their doors are tight and their seams sealed with the gummy distillate of ripple-bush seeds. From tall poles sprout harps of gut and chitin that hum in any breath; some sing purposefully—warning tones, measures of change in the wind; some are prayer or play. Tethers run from these poles to favored sail-trees, tricky braids that can be hitched and hauled by hand to adjust a vane’s pitch when the bowl wants to scream too hard. Elsewhere, half-buried in the salt, sit boxes of pebbled black glass that warm in the rare sun and are used to render plant resins into useful shapes. Implements lie stacked in a shade trench: ceramic blades with serrated shells fused into their edges, fiber ropes spun from nerveweed filaments, neat piles of stilt-gnasher bricks now fired by lightning strikes into tiles. Everything touched by hands is small-scale, low and quick to be dragged into holes when a blow arrives.

Erosion has had time to finish its sentences. The rim is not jagged but scalloped, its back slopes gentle in the way of old mountains. The ventifacts have their faces exactly where they want them, turned to the angle of pressure that will smooth rather than shatter. Down along the saucer the crystals sort themselves into bands by size and density, rippling in paler and dimmer arcs that shift only slightly season to season, as if the whole ground were a breathing creature dozing in place. In fissures you can see ancient flow-tracks of some long-evaporated solvent, not water but something oily and brown that left a varnish of organics shining darkly in the dim. The world does not erode by flood here but by a million continual licks, by scratch and song. Even the lightning prefers to spread rather than pierce, spidering its way along sail and cable to ground itself in little kisses that char rather than blast.

If there are rules here, they are these. Water is absent and unwelcome; where a traveler once carried a bead of it in cupped hands, plants on its touch went pale and flaked away, their salts turning to powder. Energy is in motion and imbalance; stillness is death. Life has learned to eat difference: pressure differences, charge differences, the rich stew of reactive ions scraped from air and crust. Structure is an agreement between the living and the wind; break it too much and the bowl will instruct you, quickly and without malice, that agreements go both ways. Light is not the main currency. Things that need more of it make their own: the dust often glows at night, as if the bowl inhaled lightning and exhaled a quiet aurora. Animals carry slow lanterns in their sacs when the haze thickens. Trees hum themselves visible.

And then, there are the days when something new appears. The bowl is noisy but it is not chaotic; patterns repeat. Yet sometimes, out of heat and charge and spin, the standing waves in the air trap a veil of dust and ions into something with edges. A mound heaves itself into existence on the saucer, a hill the color of tea, topped with a ripple-bush leaf that did not exist yesterday. It persists for an hour, or a day, throws off a cloud of drum-moths with a timbre they did not have before, and then collapses, its salts traveling as a rust-red stain across the field. The tribes who dwell in the huts uncoil their tethers and step aside, their tools small in all that voice, because to live here is to be included in the chemistry, never to manage it. They set new chimes in the lee of the new stump, listen for what the bowl will sing through them.

At the rim, you can see past the organ’s mouth to a horizon the color of old bronze. You can feel the earth’s bone through the soles of your feet, thrumming. Lushness here is not leaf upon leaf but presence upon presence, each event fat and filled, spaced enough so the in-between can act. There is room for the wind to run and the thunder to walk and for a single sail-tree to throw its entire body into a note that vibrates down your spine and names the place again.
