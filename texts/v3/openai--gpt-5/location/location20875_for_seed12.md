The water has weight here and warmth, a slow, close-breathed hush that turns distance to green. Light arrives tired, a dim pewter sheen rippling down from far above where storms comb the surface into sheets. It is day, but the day is held in suspension. You feel the height of the place as a kind of relief in your bones—the water thins upward, your own bubbles climb readily, and yet there is ocean still on every side, a ceiling too far to touch and a floor that drops, clean and sudden, away.

The land—such as it is—rises as a spine. New stone, unrubbed by time. Basalt has heaved itself into fans and fins and stacked plates that intersect like the ribs of some leviathan, their edges still catching a faint glassy sheen. Pillow-lava bulges, arrested mid-swim, are stitched with contractive cracks that whisper now and then as they cool. There is almost no sand, no comfortable skin of sediment; the few drifts that exist are newly minted, a scatter of brittle shards slipped from cliff-edges, tiny avalanches of black confetti that make a sound like distant rain when they settle.

Across this high crest, water falls inside water. Above, storms pour their load in curtains; here, where salt meets sudden sweetness, sheets of clearer, lighter water roll over the ridge like traveling glass. Haloclines. They slide down gullies as if gravity had been reversed and turned inside out. The falling freshwater makes veils and corridors, windows of brighter clarity slung between darknesses, and where it touches warm vents it smokes into lace. Walk through one and the world re-bends—a visible boundary you can put your hand into, a skin that resists for the length of a breath and then gives. The taste on the tongue changes. The pressure changes by the slenderest hair, enough to make bubbles surge.

Things grow wherever a crack will hold them. The rock is crowded with root-mats and gripping discs, with tough, dark ribbons of kelp that pinwheel slowly in the long-drawn current. Bladder-fronds rise in braided cords, each pale bulb a store of sweetness and buoyancy, each cord weighted down with fist-sized stones netted in fiber to keep the whole from floating to the cloud-bright ceiling. Between the big growths, a pelt of shorter lives: feathery filters splayed open like green moths; seamosses that creep in scales and stitch rubble together; broad parasols of translucent tissue the color of old jade, turned up to drink the dimness in. Some fronds hold seams of living chalk—milky bacterial films laid along their midribs—that turn water to stone grain by grain, accreting pale beards on the sharp rock. Wherever a warm crack sighs, there is a fringe of silver threads—chemic-eaters—swirling together like hair caught in a drain.

The settlement is not something laid upon this place so much as something caught into it. You come on it by the change in the mesh around you. Nets begin to appear, at first as single lines bridging trivial gaps, then thickening into catenary roads slung from knifeblades of stone to iron-dark ridges, then into whole neighborhoods of interlaced fiber. The work is all tension and patience. Every span settles into the curve that water and weight agree upon; every knot is reefed a dozen ways against long soak and slow pull. Fields are hung as vertical gardens inside fresher veils, big loose baskets of cord and vine stuffed with soil-substitute—the shredded pith of old bladders, the pulvered mats of retired moss—so that tender greens can root in sweetness before their feet find salt.

Halls are grown-walled: hollow stems swollen by careful wrapping and bracing, longrooms braided from tough kelp-skin and stiffened with withies of cured vine. Under tension, these spaces ring very faintly when a long wave passes and the ridge hums. There are plazas that are nets underfoot, plazas that are nets overhead, nets that are woven around columns of warmer water so that the skin of a gathering stays temperate and the elders’ joints do not ache. The city’s spine is stitched straight through with a procession of baskets, each one a globed membrane full of clear fall-water. The baskets are tethered to counterweights carved from light pumice and shell, so that when a belly fills, the weight climbs slowly, when a belly empties it descends. There is no wheel in the dry sense; movement here is a negotiation with buoyancy and salinity, a dance with the sliding of densities.

Tools are hand-born and close to the body. Knife-edges flaked from obsidian-like glass thrown by the ridge, so sharp that a careless cut burns with cold. Drills are stingray spines set in wrapped handles. Rope is the city’s genius: twined from kelp fiber and hair-moss, tarred with resins smelted in little heat-baskets over vents, proofed and pressed under knotted stones while the current does the slow labor of curing. Hooks are bone; adzes are clamshell; needles are thorn. When the people want walls of stone where there is none, they make it to grow: they seed lattices with chalk and milk their own algal chalkers to leave behind ribs of carbonate; over seasons the weirs take flesh and heft.

Sound carries little and slow. The city is mostly quiet by nature and by discipline. There is the soft, continuous talk of water through mesh. There is a hiss where a fall-crossing breaks apart into pearls. There is the low plank-moan of a long net in a long swell. Rock makes its own punctuation: a popping note like a drumstick struck on an empty clay pot when a sheet cools past some remembered tension; a deep, occluded thud when a plate lets go and a thousand knives tumble into a darker pocket. Once in a long time, a wider noise drifts from outside the local air: the rumble of a storm’s foot on the surface turned into a far thunder; the groan of the deep slope when an old pillow gives way. Voices are close-range things, finger-talk and touch-sign carried by rope, clack and holler muted to handwork and eye.

Animals are few. A grazer with a puckered mouth and stone-sleek back comes through once in a day or two to rasp at the chalk-beards and move on, patient as tide. A single slow disk of jelly, webbed with blue veins like a stained-glass window, climbs sometimes through the warmer drafts, its bell so big you feel the water’s bulk sway with each contraction, and then it is gone. Most days it is all small work: a scissor-click of amphipods in a moss; the flip of a rare, pale fish threading a little river where two veils meet; an empty eye peering out from a new crack and deciding nothing is there to see. Life here is plant-led. It is the nets and the greenwork that make the density, that turn raw height into a held place.

The rules of the ridge are simple and everything builds upon them. There is no flame. Heat is a place, not a tool: you go to it, you bend it into your habitations, you trap bits of it as a lizard traps sun, by placing your skin where warmth goes. Weight is malleable, not fixed. Pull a bladder taut and you make lifting. Let it slack and you accept that downward is a suggestion. Time is what happens to knots. Weather is not what crosses from west to east, but how fresh the veils fall and how far they reach, the taste of the halocline today compared to last week, the glitter in a fall that tells you the storm above had lightning to spare.

Beyond the immediate webs the ridge runs on, a provincial grandeur. Fins make avenues. Parallel spines enclose pale valleys of waving grass, the leaves so long that they tangle and mat under their own abundance, lifting and subsiding like breath. Here and there, other cities have staked themselves to the high rock: faint geometries of net and bladder and sway, a little chain of dim lanterns when night-sickness clouds the shallows and even the bacterial chalkers dim. Between these strongholds, the slopes fall with very little pretense. Past the last fingerferns and chalk-beards, a ledge; past the ledge, a simple drop into ink-blue and then black. The first steps down are already trodden, hacked with patient, dangerous work into stair-roughness along a fin. They are angular, without comfort. Erosion has not yet learned to round everything into forgetting. Every season a few more steps are added. Every season the steps below shift a little and someone must go redraw them.

All of it is new enough to remember its own making. The rock still sweats in places. The mosses are only just figuring out how to speak the stone’s language. The nets learn by sagging, and where they have sagged for years there are now catenary streets so familiar your hand goes out without looking and meets exactly the right rope. Things won’t move quickly here, even if they could. Warmth and water and height conspire to make patience the only speed. Even the rainfalls that look so urgent from below are slow when you walk with them: a long drift of sweet down coiling through this newborn country, draping it in green and cord and a quiet human geometry, as if to teach the ridge what a place is.
