At first you think it is evening, because the light is dim and olive and close, but it never brightens. Day, here, is a condition of the air. Warmth rises from the living floor and the floor breathes out mist; the mist finds the leaves and condenses; the leaves grip the droplets and relay them down, and the whole place is a huge, soft machine for making water out of the sky’s damp.

You are high—your ears tell you by how sound behaves, by the way distant drips arrive late and faint, by the thin weight of the world pressing lightly at your temples. When the fog shreds, now and then, you can just see how high: a bluff of living buttresses and velvet-green ledges hanging over a pale void of cloud. The edge of this upland is a cliff that never quite resolves, because the clouds that fall over it move like very slow water, folding and recombining, and what drops does not hit a ground you can see. It is a continent up in the warm parts of the air, a wide, settled crown that makes its own weather.

Underfoot, the ground is not ground. It is an interwoven architecture of plants made old enough to be a substrate, a thickness of living and once-living that could count as geology if it were not still faintly flexing. Loops of root as thick as a waist, spongy with age, braid into each other until the braiding becomes mesh and the mesh becomes a mat, meters deep, holding in black wet humus with the memory of trees. The mat is springy, almost elastic. If you stare at a place long enough, you can watch it recover from a footstep—the little depressions fill with returning water and the tangled fibers ease their strain and level. In cuts and animal-bored tunnels you can see the cross-section: the uppermost is a crowded tangle of green veins and glossy leaves that wander without soil; below, a russet wool of old roots threaded with white fungal ropes; lower still, strata of peat compressed to a half-stone toughness that weeps when you touch it.

The water here does not run in streams much. It beads. It weeps. It hangs. It gathers in shallow saucers where leaves have remembered the curve of a million drops. It threads along the hairs of moss like silver insects migrating. You can hear it, always, in the quiet: the clock-slow pattering of thickened air becoming liquid and deciding where to go next. Every surface owns a tiny hydrology. There are places where the bedrock shows itself in rounded humps and low domes, flattened and scoured to their final shapes ages ago, long before the plants learned to grow into architecture. These rocks gleam black-green with a perennial sheen. They call the water out of the air—cool enough, compared to the breath-warm mat, to pull mist onto their skins. They never run with it; they weep. In the shallow bowls chiseled badly by some old hand or by frost that came once to this height and never again, a clear rime of water collects, not exactly a pool, more a constant state of becoming a pool.

The vegetation is not a forest in the way of trunks and crowns separated by air. Air is rare between things. There are trunks, to be certain, but many of them have turned into walls. Buttressed trees arch and meet and accept vines that girdle them not as parasites but as tension members. Branches think like rafters. Sheets of bromeliad rosette interconnect to form corbelled galleries across null spaces. There are living bridges everywhere, and not a single one was made with haste: a vine throws itself across a gap, lives, thickens, accretes companions, and a century later it is as trustworthy as stone. Even the epiphytes that grow on the epiphytes begin to act like panels and braces. If flocks of birds used to fly here, they would not, now; they would have to thread needle-holes and bounce their bellies along moss.

The light that reaches you is filtered through thousands of surfaces, every surface moving slowly as it drinks and exhales. It changes from olive to tea to cold green over the russet and black. There are patches where pale lichens wash a leaf-stack with milk-color brightness, and luminous fungi lining the undersides of vaults that pretend at starlight, barely enough to make silhouettes out of snail horns and fern tips. A few species use light like speech—you see them sometimes around dusk, slow flickers and throbbings in the veins of a sheet-plant, as if it were reading a patient code to the things touching it.

Heat lingers. Nights are warm as a hand cupped around a bowl. The day does not sharpen into heat the way lower lands do; the thick green mass stores it and gives it back gently. In this warmth, decay is a careful craft. Rot here is not a quick ruin but an ordered, old economy; the things that break down are broken down into precise feeds, and the plants take them back with deliberate slowness. That slowness governs everything. Buds unfurl in hours that feel like days. Flowering events can feel like arguments between one century and the next. Even the rare falls—the slumps of mat when a root fails or the slow failure of a wall of rhizome—announce themselves in low complaints for weeks before anything gives. When it goes, it is not a crash so much as a rearrangement, a sighing avalanche of damp matter that gives you time to step aside.

It is quiet, but not empty of sound. The quiet is a property of the place: so much surface on everything soaks up echoes. The air itself has a little viscosity to it; leaves shed a soft drag onto the sounds that pass over them. You hear water make decisions. You hear the little percussive plink of a drop letting go. You hear a low creak that is a buttress resettling into the compiled memory of its load. You hear an occasional thin thread of song, so attenuated by moss and distance that it feels like hearing the memory of a song rather than the thing itself.

Animals are abundant but intimate. There are small browsers that look like a smooth, wet idea of a deer and a frog spliced, each no bigger than a hand, draping their tongues over algal gloss and tasting the mineral that the rock-browsers have licked to the surface. There are stone-lickers, too, long-headed, soft-toothed amphibians with pads that can adhere to bare boulder, worshippers of the weeping domes, always nostrils-deep in the condensate, pulling at the diluted salts. Whole guilds of creatures filter the air. They raise fans like old library borrowers’ screens and harvest spores and plankton-fine pollen from the always-falling green snow. You see gliders sometimes, not because they move quickly but because their membranes catch a glimmer. They tip and drift rich as seedpods. Nothing here seems in a hurry to get from one place to another, and there is no far away to get to; the place is all corridors and rooms that bleed into one another, a house without a floor plan.

Some animals build, shyly. There are basket-reefs of woven petioles erected by invisible handspiders around the vents where warm, wet breathers sleep—an economy, not a technology. Piles of purplish pebbles ring the mouths of seepage wells, arranged by some beak with a taste for order. Over the centuries, larger herbivores that you never see directly, only by the polish on their trails and the faded drumming of their footfalls, have trained living bridges where they wish; you can read their routes in the way the vines have taken on corners and the moss has been taught not to grow on certain lines. Those are the nearest things to roads.

And then there are the other remains, older and stranger to this place. Scattered through the living mass, at the caps of weeping stones and at the few wind-cleared knolls, there are signs of decision too purposeful to be animal convenience. Shallow basins in bedrock with lips ground smooth beyond what water could manage. Steps cut into a tor that leads nowhere now, each step cupping a halo of green that tells you it holds damp for days after the rest has dried in some brief bright. A ring of low pillars with their edges rounded fat as beans by ages of fog and fingers of moss, the stone so old that it has learned to be kind to the touch. These are partial, not ruins because they never were whole in the way cities are whole—devices for catching water from air, for leading feet across soft ground, for doing with stone what the plants already do with more grace. They are nearly gone. Roots have claimed them, and not destructively but with that same slow adoption that turns a vine into a wall. Whatever hands made them are not here now; perhaps they left when they understood the better tools were green.

There are rules here that grew out of time and damp. Nothing trails far into emptiness; everything touches and is touched in return. Shapes are negotiated, not declared. Movement leaves a mark and, if patient enough, the marks stiffen into scaffolds and galleries; a single repeated pathway over a generation becomes a permanent ligament of the place. Water, being constant and abundant, is less a commodity than a constant conversation between rock, plant, air, and animal. Gravity is softened by hair and leaf; things still fall, but not cleanly—caught and passed hand to hand. Even the sky is not free; the canopy and the forever-fog make of it a soluble dome that grows from underneath. Light does not so much descend as seep, and then it is taken apart. Photosynthesis feels like whispers between close faces.

If you wait, you discover the more patient cycles. There is a daily breathing where the whole crown seems to lift and settle a finger’s breadth as water loads and then empties the mats; the animals sleep to the rhythm of the drip-lag, not the sun. There are weekly releases: the pale smoking of spores at dusk from under-arches that sends a quiet hail of green through the air and finds its way into every fur, the way snow finds a cuff. There are century-flowers big as canoes that unfurl in the windless antechambers and summon all the slow, sticky-mouthed things to suffer joy and go away marked. Erosion, that hungry god of elsewhere, is retired here to priesthood; it officiates in the weeping of the domes, in the final rounding of exposed shoulders, in the lowing sigh when a peat wall relaxes into a new geometry. Bedrock has given up ambition and lies as flatly as a thought at the end of a conversation.

Sometimes a path opens uncharacteristically, and you can stand where the place gives you something like a view: a soft escarpment where curtains of fern peel back and you can look across a living plain that rolls for days. It is not even, exactly; it is a quilt of vaults and recesses, pale lichens making continents of milk; here and there, columns like ribs show where a great structure rose as if to fight down gravity and, in the end, negotiated a truce. Far, far from you, a cloudfall drapes itself over the rim and hangs there like a frozen waterfall. If there is wind above, it does not reach you except as an intuition in the way new dew forms on the west sides of leaves.

Nothing leaps. Everything leans. The animals that fly do so with a decorum that makes them look tethered, even when they are wild. The plants that want to climb do so by laying down their ladders a millimeter at a time and waiting for the world to accept. The rare bright things—bursts of flower-amber, bird throats that glow a faint iron-red from the fungus they court—are shy fireworks under blankets. If danger comes, it is mostly from damp and patience: the ground that decides you weigh enough to become a story in it, the slick rock that asks how much of you you will leave on it. Predators exist, of course; you hear their talk in the faint snapping of membranous traps and the small, stopped squeak of a spore-feeder taken by a vine that has learned to move. But even that is almost polite; the place eats and is eaten in the tone of a family meal without speech.

Edges of the plateau breed their own oddities. There are “falls” where the plants have learned to drink upward. The mist that slides over the rim is caught by combs of hair and directed along veins that yield no drop to gravity. You can put a finger to a particular fern and feel the faint heat of liquid pumped up against the world’s pull, the way a heart speaks. These combs are the inventions of those rules that have been running long enough to think. They make of the upper air a river.

Leave a handprint on a weeping stone and come back a week later; you will find moss practicing the shape of your palm, learning your gesture. Set down a stick to mark a turn and by the time you return, it has uptaken; a handful of leaves have apprenticed themselves to it and the path curves a little more easily. The place rewards what you repeat, which is another way of saying it asks you to be patient, to say what you mean many times and softly. It is possible to get lost here and be found by the landscape itself—your habits become railings.

At night, the dimness deepens in a way that is more a change in texture than in brightness. The breath of the mat cools very slightly; fog makes pearls on everything; and the luminous things speak more freely, quiet matrices pulsing news along undersides. There is no sky to look up into, only a ceiling of things that are alive and generous with their secrets. You fall asleep to the sound of the world condensing and wake to the same. In dreams, if the place grants them, you descend very slowly through the thickness of green, and you do not touch the ground because there is no ground; there is only the patient roof of the planet, floating where weather learns to be soft.
