You come to the edge of height as if arriving at an ocean and discovering it stands upright. The world falls away in a single gesture, a continuous cliff whose faces recede in pale tiers until they become a braided haze. Sunlight lays out strong and ordinary, the color of bone and dust, and the air between you and the endless walls trembles softly with heat.

Nothing wet lives here. The dryness is an element you can taste: baked mineral, a faint bite of ozone, the distant sweetness of hot resin from sealed machines. Sound does not travel far—your foot scuffs and the grit answers only for a meter or two; further out, a slow clack of chain, the faintest hiss of drifting grain, a clatter so distant it could be imagined. Wind moves, but it is mannered and old, drawn tight to the faces of the stone where it slides instead of buffets. Sometimes it feels like all this height has taught air to behave.

The terrain is not simple wall. It is layer upon layer of ledge and rib: mile-wide shelves stitched into steepness, galleries cut by old collapses, suspended valleys that hang like terraces of a lost river. It is as if the planet’s crust once swelled and froze in place, and then was abraded for a million calm years by something that was never fluid in the way water is. The materials speak to that history. The rock is not one rock but a composite of fused salts and enamelled basalts, oxide panes and ceramic-laminate ribs that shear along old planes with the polish of a split gemstone. Some faces are the color of ash in sunlight, others copper-scarred, others like unfired clay. There are places where thin, vitreous skins craze and shed, forming flakefall taluses that creep down-slope inch by inch, day by day, in an almost-not-motion that is somehow continuous. You can set a pebble on such a skin and come back in a season to find it has migrated a hand’s breadth along a line that every other pebble follows, a river of solid things.

Erosion here is measured not in gullies carved by torrents but in the settling of thermal fractures, in the long travels of dry grains under steady field and calendar wind, in the warping of strata with a patience that makes centuries feel brief. The shelves all have an angle they prefer, a long-solved truce between gravity and grain size, between the cohesion of glass and the crumbling of salt. Nothing hurries. Even the pebbles seem to have adopted the world’s pace.

And yet there is life, drawn to edges and gradients like iron filings to a magnet. Nothing green grows out in the light; there is no plant to collect the sun with leaves, nothing that drinks. In the shade inside cracks and under the lip of rock, sparse patches of mat—thin, piezo-bright skeins the color of oxidized coins—quietly thread themselves into fractures. On certain dawns, when the faces shed their night charge and the shelves breathe off trace vapors of heavier gases, those mats bloom a mineral fuzz you can only see at certain angles. Animals come to rasp it. Others come for the animals, and for the tiny crystals the walls weep when warmed.

Fauna is this place’s language. On a midday ledge you can watch entire dramas play out in silence. Clingrills—broad-bellied, many-thumbed, with plates like porcelain tea-cups set along their spines—move like patient magnets over impossible faces, their toes tapping and holding with an audible tick only a few steps away. Bands of gliders, each a rigid delta of cartilaginous sail laced with coppery filaments, rise from shadow to sun in the persistent updrafts that hug the cliffs, banking along invisible rivers of lift that repeat every afternoon. They are not birds; their mouths are soft rasps meant for combing salt bloom from rock, and they land against the wall like thrown cloth.

Closer, on the warm stone at your feet, bead-worms coil and uncoil—strings of glossy segments like a child’s counting beads—sensing the lightest charge with fine hairs and depositing each segment against the last to form temporary circuits. When they find a vent strand humming with piezoelectric stress, they stack themselves into a column as high as their courage will allow and shiver, lit from within by the faintest biolum of stored energy being passed along. Fat, low-bodied grazers with telescope eyes (they look like a compromise between a pillow and an anvil) cross the smoother shelves in lines that make their own faint tracks, drawn to where the cliffs “sweat” a festival of micron-scale crystals at dusk. And high above, inverted underhangs are dotted with chandelier creatures—calcareous cages hung from filamentous anchors—that open themselves to the warm light, rocking in place and filtering what the wind brings: grains of silica with a living rind, fragments of dry spore that may not be spore at all but the next state of some animal’s life.

Nothing here wastes moisture because there is none to waste. Fluids, when they appear, are heavy and rare—a breath-thin film of complex organics condensing like an oil of the world along shade lines before it flashes off again in the sun. Beaks, tongues, and the soft underside of glider-wings are all engineered to take advantage of that moment. Many mouths here drink not with capillary action but with charge: tongues are fringed combs that harvest ions; taste is a current.

The city holds like a barnacle in a fold of the great face, spread over levels you could mistake for geology. It occupies a shelf as broad as a province, with further tiers sunk into the wall above and pendant works suspended below. Nothing juts; everything that can be recessed is recessed. The architecture is all hex-socket and slot, built to take strain in the same directions the rock already tolerates. Openings are few and deep, like the eyeholes of caves. You notice early the ritual of touchstones at every threshold—a hand of metal standing proud from the wall—because here static is a substance. Before entering any place full of people or machines you ground yourself; sparks bite, blue and friendly.

Industry breathes slowly. Vast chainbeds crawl up and down the face like patient caterpillars, carrying ore carcasses and finished ceramic along their own grooves. They barely murmur. The lifting halls are sealed; inside, the air is denser and still faintly chemical, and quiet again except for the low speech of machinery. Rotary furnaces turn behind layered shutters, bringing you their light as thin slivers along seams. Induction reefs—great combs of thin metal sunk into the wall—hum under almost inaudible loads, concentrating the daily electrical sigh of the cliff until it will run a kiln, a spinner, an extruder. Fiberworks draw glass straight from the wall’s veins, spooling it into cable that leaves the room on soft shoulders and disappears along the face into distance, humming. Water does not flow through these pipes; instead a diversity of other circulations thread the city: solvent loops for cleaning, gas lines for the breathing spaces, heat buses that take the day’s collected warmth below and leave it for the night shift.

Between such functions, there is emptiness. The intervals between towers are long walks. Bridgeways cross the great, slow drifts of glassflake talus at long, conservative arcs, their suspensions tuned not for gusts—there are few—but for the rare deep sigh of the wall itself when a pocket far below settles and the face rethinks itself across kilometers. The streets you find are a series of rooms open to sky, separated by curtains of air rather than doors. In them, people or things move with the reserve of creatures who understand that every stray motion can stir grains that might take a century to come to rest.

Rules arise from physics as customs. You learn to look for sheen: it tells you where footfalls travel and where they do not. You learn to keep your skin covered when the afternoon lift comes on, because even the soft rain of dust will draw a lacework of current on you. You learn to listen for a single sound in the generally quiet that means you should seal a shutter—the high hiss of a descending flow, when a bank of plate-glass flakes chooses to go and slides off as a shining river that will cross the shelf over hours, unstoppable in its patience. You learn that nothing that cannot anchor well will be made or allowed near an edge.

At night the temperature stays generous; the wall radiates back all the heat it took in, and the face becomes a boulevard for things that shun the glare. Thin-legged hunters with prisms for eyes and notches in their head-plates follow the shallow thermal winds hand-span by hand-span, pausing to set their antennae against rock and feel for the sizzle of a circuit. The chandelier creatures fold, the gliders thread their way to pockets of still air and go to sleep head-down. From deep within the built places you might hear, now and then, a body-length cough of steam as a sealed process vents to an intake baffle, but the sound is brief, tactful. The city does not throw its noise away, any more than it throws its heat or charge.

Time scales differently here. Weather happens, but it has a local character; this canyon wall and the next, separated by miles of depth, can be under wholly different rules. A lenticular of dust will park over a buttress for a season, sheathing it; when it moves on, the buttress is the same shape but the light finds it changed. Every few years a cliff-lip far up-shelf fails by a meter and the new angle makes a new itinerary for a long caravan of pebble and plate. Every couple of lifetimes, the slow, predictable charge of the wall hitches and a “blue year” comes when induction reefs sing loud enough to be heard in the narrow streets, and even the bead-worms stand taller.

For all its steepness, the world is generous if you consent to its pace and direction. It gives you height and lift, charge and glass, ledge and ledge. It offers animals ordered by the cliff’s patience, adapted to inhabit intervals rather than places—riders of gradients, readers of grain, eaters of bloom. It offers a city that hums beneath speech, industrial by the yard and by the century, tuned to harvest the energy of a dry planet without insulting its quiet.

And in the morning after the sun takes its first long, warm look, the rim makes a thin, brittle music as a million invisible fissures tell the light they’ve noticed. Then the day begins rolling down the wall again, and for hours you could think that nothing moves at all, and be wrong.
