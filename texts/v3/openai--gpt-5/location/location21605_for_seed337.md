From above, nothing announces the place except a scatter of dark mouths in the ground—narrow wells like blown-out candles, some no wider than a person’s shoulders, some the size of threshing floors, rimmed with pale stone that cuts the sun into white, square-edged shafts. The air that rises from them in the early morning smells of wet dust and a little of ash. If you lie over a rim and listen you hear almost nothing: a faint hollowing, a bead of water falling somewhere, the occasional creak of wood as if a house is turning in its sleep.

The city lives just under the skin, in a band of hollows that run for miles like a shelf inside the earth. You can walk the surface for an hour and keep glimpsing the same whiteness below, the same thin suns set into the dark. The roof over it all is young rock, a pale welded ash riddled with seam lines and long, clean fractures that haven’t yet been rounded by time. The stone takes a fingernail; it is soft enough to be shaped, brittle enough to betray you if you ask too much from a span. In many rooms the place still smells like fresh-broken cliff; if you drag your hand along a wall, dust glows on your skin.

Light falls in matching the day above. It is normal light, not the blue of glows or the red of embers: simple daylight driven down through shafts and cracks. In rooms close to a well the sun is a column you can step into. It warms the rock, dries ropes, coaxes a little green. Deeper it is a careful gray. People place pale slabs against the far walls to carry the spill a little farther, prop up thin sheets of mica to shiver a glow into a sleeping recess. Fires are rare and small and fed with thought; the rock sweats when it is heated and heat too fast brings flakes down like rain.

The ground here has only just learned to be hollow. There are no sagging bellies to the ceilings, no flows of old stone hanging like teeth. The floors are flaked and unriven; a corner looks like a corner. When rain comes above, you hear the childhood of a river begin: thin threads that awaken, gather, and stitch themselves across the rock, joining and parting without a path worn for them yet by habit. On dry days moisture appears as breath: beads lining a crack like silver seeds, fog that pools under a ledge before the sun finds it. People hang cloths where the air chills at dawn and shake dew into basins at breakfast. To own a good place for catching condensation is almost as fine as owning a field.

Vegetation makes a sparse attempt at life. Where daylight drops straight into a room you will sometimes see a green haze—mosses, tight rosettes of ferns no taller than a hand, a scrim of leaflings that live on the roof’s weeping and then go pale again by noon. More often the botany is upside down: the dangling worlds of roots that have found a seam and reached down from the shrubs above. Their hair-fine ends keep the walls stained with a dark brushwork. People harvest these for fibers, bind them into rope and mat, tuck their felted lengths into gaps as chinking. In wide wells with a proper sun there are little gardens, not many, hunched and patient. They grow bulbs that forgive short days, vines that can accept their sun like a vertical ribbon, the tops turning to catch the light.

Everything is close. There is no empty. A span of wall that is not needed for shoring is a shelf. The ceiling is a ceiling and also an attic and also a loft for sleeping. Narrow galleries become markets: baskets of clay beads, bundles of rush cord, wedges of white salt scraped from a dry sumphole. In the main runs of the city the wooden ways are bound together so tightly that you could cross half a mile without touching rock, following the boards like riverwater finds its level. The boards are dark and damp, sprung from simple beams set into mortises chiseled into the ash. Every third pace some person has left a touch—a tie, a peg with a loop on it, a trussed joint smoothed by hands. The place feels inhabited to saturation, but the sound of it is low. Conversation is in hand-distances and chin-tilts. Tools have leather sleeves where they bump a wall. Shoes are soft and soles are wrapped. Children learn to swallow cries as easily as they learn to climb. Sound is a force here, not a pastime; you listen to the stone and the stone listens back.

The city was not made at once but threaded through a dozen long seams. One seam is all households and cookfires, a place of steam and sliced roots and fermented jars and the small bright chatter of knives touching stone. Another is wide enough to be a square and is laced with crossing ropes—the ropewalk—where fibers are spun into heavier lines. One seam is all clay: vats of wet, a canal with hand-laid bed for the crude barges that slide when the rains run, kilns cupped under a roof vent so that smoke will not loaf along the ceilings. Bricks harden in stacks the color of bread heels. There are rules about them: fire them slow, turn them between days, never stack the new ones where the water will run. The city learns its own laws of material as the place evolves.

Water is encountered in moods, not as a single thing. There are pockets that don’t dry through a full summer—eyes like coins in bowls of stone. There are suddens: trickles that wake and then flood narrow, forcing a board to lift like a boat. There is rain that says “not yet river,” and reheats to air right before your fingers. People have made gutters with gentle intentions, curbs of brick that suggest a path for water rather than command one. Here and there a whole well becomes a throat, a proper sink whose water sings for days after a storm. Those rooms are not for houses; they are for listening, for cups, for the nets they lower that condense fog as it drifts through the cool. A faint, metallic taste tells you that the water ran over new stone; a chalk on the teeth tells you it had time to think.

Fauna are sparse and cautious. In the dampest rooms live thin salamanders the color of bone. In the bright wells swallows sometimes fall and then rise again, skimming the warm pillar and stitching it with their voices, brief spells of noise in a city that shies from it. Once in a while a lizard wakes on a board and stares with the empty wisdom of things that don’t choose their rooms. There are always the insects: crickets that tick against your ankle, pale flies that find dew, beetles that troop along the lines of fungus gardens kept in the dim places. No one would call the city rich in animals. There is a hunger for animal fat here that never quite goes away.

Techniques are older than metal, or metal is a luxury reserved for tokens and edges. Axes are stone pegged to wood with twisted root. Chisels are bone strengthened with rawhide. Pottery shoulders the real work: big bellies for water, thinner walls for catch-basins. Mica is split like pastry and used to lead light into a room around a corner. A few households have blades that flash like river scales—bronze or copper smithed elsewhere, cherished here until they’re more mended than blade. The craft is in fitting things to this young world that still changes. No one builds long without leaving little places for the place itself to move. Ropes are tied so the knot can be shrugged by a beam that swells in a damp wind. Planks are laid with gaps for water to find. You do not chisel a line down the middle of the room; you let it run in the shadow where it wanted to run.

Because everything is new, everything is also risky. The laws here are practical and near to the surface. You never drink from a pool that has no way out—you can smell trapped water if you learn it, but better to watch. If the rock pings, you leave the way you came by. If a seam grows dark on a dry day, you tell someone that lives under it; a dark line is a sign of gathering. Wood is a neighbor: when it shakes, you put a shoulder to it; when it whispers, you listen. Loud music is for topside. Dancing stamped down here once, and people still speak of that as a year of bad luck when the roof remembered it was only thin.

The climate in the rooms is a pulse. By dawn there are beads along the undersides of beams—dewfall that coats a fingertip like oil. Morning fills the light wells with fog; you can watch it climb and thin like a creature learning to stand. By midday the air dries, the pale mosses crisp a little, and the cloths hung out for catch become sifting shades for the plants under them. Afternoon air flows down the wells; you feel a draught at your ankles and see the cloths fill their bellies. After sunset everything chills in a rush and prisms sketch over the lips of bowls, faint skins that crack at the edge of a ladle. The city is in motion without ever going anywhere: moisture moving, crowds shifting toward and away from light, wood changing voice, ropes tightening and letting go, slow growth that you notice only when you compare it to your memory.

The open-ness of the place is an open-ness in distance, not in empty air. It is a belt of livable rooms that runs like a town runs along a river. You can follow it for hours: room after room, some silent, some choked with tenants and their basins and rope and a set of drying fish caught topside, others only trussed and waiting, ribs and props in place, plaster a new white that has not yet darkened with hands. At intervals the ground above subsides and a new skylight appears with a sound like a calf’s first cough; an old man hears it and marks another day in his bone-tally, and the next morning there is new brightness somewhere, and three families move. The city is built with the patience of nesting birds and the speed of landslides. It is both under construction and fully inhabited; there is no gap between those two truths.

Markets open when the light arrives and close when it goes. The quiet is busiest in midmorning. Women cut moss into square pillows for tender feet and lay them on a board where everyone must pass. Men carry long wet cloths toward the fog and come back laughing, their arms dark to the elbow. Children invent games you cannot shout. Noises pool in certain places—under the ropewalk, footsteps become thunder; above the clay vats, a word becomes a proclamation. Elsewhere, a whisper will carry fifty yards and return to you in your own ear. Sound engineers itself here out of caution and curiosity.

Ecology is a thin but insistent net. The people farm fungus in troughs where the light fails and the air stays moist, and they do not call it a plant but it is food, and it paints its ghosts up the walls in the faintest of browns. A beetle that loves fungus argues with a spider that loves beetles; sometimes a child watches longer than any adult can afford to. Salt feathers where the water disappears; people scrape it with shells and that’s their wage for a day’s patience. Above them shrubs stand in the wind and send their roots down like pilgrims. Bats come once in the year’s warmest week and hang in a vented room and then are gone; you would think you dreamt them if guano didn’t burn the nose. The rare predator arrives by mistake: a snake fallen through a skylight coils in a corner and becomes a story for a season.

Out in the rooms that no one has claimed yet, the rocks speak in here-and-there collapses as they find a better angle for themselves; a slab lets go and a new chamber exists, sharp and bright, edges like paper. There is an ethic about these openings. The first person to find one calls it out, and everyone stands and listens. If the stone is still moving you mark it with a cross of two thin sticks and walk away. If it is thinking about stopping, you bring beams and friends and put your weight into its new geometry. There are worse ways to spend a day than leaning under a ceiling with a whole street’s shoulder against a buttress and feeling the world around you agree to persist.

Rules of the world here are specific as proverbs, often phrased like jokes. A plank is a promise but a rope is a hope. Water takes the path you would take if you knew better. A shout makes walls curious. Never put a bed where a beetle chooses to die. If it’s bright and cold it will be wet by noon. If it’s dim and warm don’t trust it with your house. The old women tell you to carry your breath in your pocket during the festival because someone young will want it. They mean: pace yourself. They mean: the stone is new to us and we are new to it. It is still deciding how to be a place and we are still learning how to be the people of such a place.

It seems an unlikeliness at first—a considerable town tucked under a barely-there ceiling, with sun piercing down in columns and the rest composed of quiet and intent. But the more you walk it the more it becomes its own logic. The small pains of damp wood and soft dust, the cold underfoot before noon, the way voices find a path only if permitted one, the masked exertion of carrying water uphill in bowls, the manuscript of sweepings on every sill—that all convenes into a society like a stacked nest. Beyond the city, the seams keep going. Some are empty. Some hold only old drips frozen in perfect beads for months without a drop made. Some, miles away, cough mist in the same hour your ropes swell. A place in its first decades has that generosity: anything not yet true might still be, and the scale of it—the running seam like a long, lit street beneath rock—favors continuations. The people down here rise with the light, pause with the pings of settling stone, and believe the next room is already waiting. They will make it and it will, in its own time, make them back.
