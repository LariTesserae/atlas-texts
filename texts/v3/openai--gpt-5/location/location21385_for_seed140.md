You come into it as into a syllable held in the mouth of the mountain: an o-shaped hollow on a high, worn-down tableland, its rim leveled by epochs of wind, its floor softened by the slow swamping of time. The very first boundary is sound. Out on the open stone the night seems quiet; step down into the bowl and a wall of noise rushes up to meet you—layered, living, continuous. It is dark here, darkness with weight to it, a darkness that lifts the hairs on your arms not from cold but from nearness.

The air is warm, almost body-warm, and wet enough that your breath condenses sooner than your thoughts. The hollowness holds humidity: a microclimate pinned in place by geometry and old intent. Water is everywhere and nowhere—the rocks don’t pour, exactly, but they sweat. Every ledge glistens. The ribs of ancient quartzite are slick with a film that is never quite a stream, endlessly almost about to flow, and at intervals the nearly becomes actual and a runnel gurgles across polished stone. There are drip-notes everywhere, distinct pitches as different pools accept their drops; together they make a scale the chorus uses for tuning.

The stone itself has the look of something that has finished becoming what it was going to be. Primordial flutings—rillenkarren given geologic patience—score the bedrock, rounded to tenderness. Old pans, shallow and round, cradle peat the color of oxblood and starlight you cannot see. The basin’s shape feels settled into a final form: not a crater from violence, not a bowl carved by a single insistence, but a place thinned and lowered, thickened and lifted, until this was the easiest way for it to exist. The rim is not sharp. You cross into it almost by accident, tripping on a slight lip, and suddenly the world falls away inside itself.

Vegetation is lush, but not jammed. Things are spaced, as if the room of the bowl is as important as its contents. A handful of cloud-trees stand wide apart, each on a buttressed pedestal, each holding up its own sky of vines and orchid baskets and bromeliad cisterns that have never known full sun. Between them are meadows of moss that accept the shape of your foot and rise again quick as breath. Ferns mottle the lower stone, glossy and short-spaced; mats of liverwort write the wet with greens you can taste. Lianas run like taut lines between trunks, bearded with filaments that wick dew to the ground where the roots can drink. Epiphytes have found every height where a drop pauses before falling. The whole fabric is dense when you kneel to it, populous and intricate, and then open when you stand—the vault of the basin is mostly air.

It is so dark that color is an idea. The bowl draws down any glow. You hold a hand up and see nothing of it until some shadow movement translates to touch against your knuckles. Not that light cannot exist here—elsewhere it must, because the plants have made bargains with the day—but the present condition is complete night. Above, there may be a lid of cloud or only the smudged hint of sky; either way, you cannot tell. Even the phosphors that many nights offer—moss sparks, fungus threads—seem suppressed, as if the place is a pact: not now, not here.

At the rim, almost invisible unless your fingers find them, are things that are not rock or root. Slender arcs planted into the bedrock at broad intervals, as if marking an old procession route. They are warmer than the night when you press face to them, but dry, almost powder-dry in their touch. Once in a while one of them hums—a subsonic pulse felt in your kneecaps more than heard. Fog beads along their length in moments, then drops off, as if coaxed. You can smell cold in that moment, a thinness that wasn’t there before, and then the drip finds one of the little stone bowls and hits it with its note. These arcs don’t rust or slick over. Dew gathers to them preferentially. It is easy to think of them as fog-harvesters and too easy to imagine you know how. The people who put them here—if “people” is even the right name—understood shapes of air and the way gravity can be persuaded to prefer one path over another. Somewhere here, in this darkness, is a small house made mostly of absence and a few planes of something that feels like paper and stone at once. Its door is round, its windows are slots that admit no view. Inside, it is all listening.

Because the bowl listens, everything here speaks. The loudness is a fact of habitat. Frogs abundant enough to be weather—multitudes of throats, each on its own tempo, each adding a grain of noise that, by being added, becomes a beach of sound along which everything else breaks. Some calls click like pebbles tapped under the tongue; some are elastic chirrs that stretch and snap; some are hollow wooden booms that belong in larger animals and yet issue from slick bodies the size of your thumb. Crickets here make a sound so fine it’s like the edge of silk; katydids saw their names into the night; beetles work their wings until the air, already warm, seems warmed by friction. Bats pass through the soundfield as though moving through tall grass: you hear their invisible scissors of echolocation, little sparks that only instruments and moths usually notice. A nightjar or something like one takes advantage of that; it skims, mouth agape, through currents of moving wings.

In the moss are smaller livings dense as thought. Millipedes in lacquer and matte push through the miniature, feeling everything in slow touch. Flatworms reflect nothing, but leave a coolness on the skin that you can track without sight. Snails drag histories across the sheen. Salamanders, glossy, take turns in the fern roots. Spiders get by without webbing light, weaving tripwire in the lanes between roots where rodents have taught themselves the edges of place. There is no scarcity here, only spacing. Every presence is set back from the next by a stretch of empty where sound travels and scent edits itself and a drop can complete its fall.

The warm of the night does not make the highness less true. You feel, when you climb the rim again to orient yourself, the trick drop in pressure that a deep breath must manage. The sky out there is close, pressed onto your face by thinness. Back down in the bowl the air is a little denser, as if pooled, as if persuaded to linger. Rules here are simple and slow. No one hurries moisture; it is taken out of air by persuasion and suggestion, not force. No one chases light; plants have arranged themselves to sip and hoard by day, to close down and listen by night. Everyone speaks in pulses rather than edges, because pulses travel here and edges drop.

Time behaves like water creeping. Everything that changes does so almost imperceptibly: leaves grow toward guidelines of scent and drip; buttresses advance by etching their own rain shadows a finger’s breadth a decade; the arcs at the rim adjust their pitch to shifts in humidity that only a century can make; the peat bowl gains imperceptible depth from the soft green companies it keeps. Creatures wear and re-wear the same lanes until those paths become physical laws as real as gravity. If you step across one of those invisible roads, you will be noticed, and not by eye. Your boot will enter the place as an added instrument. The bowl will assign you a note, and you will carry it the length of your visit.

What emerges in the ears—the part of the landscape that cannot be mapped by sight—are the repeating patterns. Every twenty heartbeats, a deeper boom goes under the chorus, likely some vesicle in the rock answering the night’s condensation with an exhale. Every few minutes, there is a wingstorm that might be bats trading for higher ground or something larger testing the edge of the bowl; you feel it in the cool wash it leaves. At irregular intervals, and only when you’ve begun to forget to wait for them, you feel the resonance of a rim-arc drop a fraction of its tone, as if it had listened to everything and decided to favor a slightly different future. The mountain is old and almost still, but within the almost stillness is a complicated patience, and everything here has taken the patience into itself.

The scattered marks of former hands are few but consequential. A row of blackened stepping stones trails into a thicket and then stops at a wall of air; the rest of the path exists only in the memory of root swellings that once arched for it. A pole thin as a finger climbs out of the peat near the center, a line of minute vanes turning by movements you cannot feel; the pole tastes the wind so the arcs can sing to it. In a tree, a wound has been sealed with something that is not sap, and from that healed mark a hive of flying things now moves in columns so straight they look like math deciding briefly to become animals. Everything built here has learned to end quietly, into use.

If there are rules for you, they are not written but are easy to understand:

- Don’t bring light. The bowl makes a currency out of dark and sound; you will be resented if you pay in glare.

- Don’t rush. The longest-lived things here know you only if you behave like weather.

- Don’t try to count. Abundance is a sensation, not a statistic, and it is fulfilled by chorus, not headcount.

- Notice emptiness. Space is part of the abundance; it is what feeds it.

In the morning—there is a morning, though the bowl won’t offer you the sunrise—the arcs will change their hum. The fog will shift into something less like wall and more like tissue; sunlight will touch the rim as if uncertain of permission; in the high places of the canopy a few leaves will discover they are green. Drips will continue because drips are the nearest this place ever gets to rain. The animals will quiet in knots and clusters, and then the quiet will make a sound as broad as last night’s noise. Nothing big will move. The ancient plateau will continue to be almost flat, almost tired, almost ended. And here in its mouth, this bowl will go on listening, lush and spare at once, wet enough to shine without showing you its shine, alive in a way that needs your whole body to recognize it.
