The place is all dark throat and empty air, a single hollow folded inside a greater mass. You don’t see it so much as stand inside the pressure of its voice. Lowland, in the sense that everything around it falls toward here: a final basin, settled long ago into curves that no longer change. The void is large enough to feel like weather but small enough to belong to a room. Your breath finds nothing wet. Tongue sticks to teeth. The air is cool and meanly dry, salted with a bitterness like old resin and cold smoke.

What passes for ground is not ground but a taut and ancient lattice of plant, thinned by ages into a drumhead spanning a shallow sink. Beneath that skin is more of the same—layer under layer of living structure—yet in here the primary sensation is absence. You could stretch your arms and not touch anything, then take a step and brush some hanging rib or taut cable that shivers and hums. The mass that makes the city is everywhere, but it has given the volume back to the air. Density here is almost a rumor; the place is built of small things that make big quiet spaces.

Quiet, except for the sound. The whole chamber sings. Wind—if a slow, patient migration of gas can be called wind—drives through a series of living tubes and valleys, and they answer in standing tones that go through bone. The voices are low, some almost below hearing, and several high peers ride them, thin as wires. They don’t come in gusts; they have been here longer than memory, modulating a little with pressure tides you cannot feel, like an organ that plays itself by the difference between now and then. The walls have been edited by sound. Every edge is rounded, every corner found a radius that pleases the air.

All structure is grown. The chamber’s boundaries are a layered flesh-wood, a composite of stiffened fiber and fine silica and salts that ring under a knuckle. It isn’t a tree, exactly—trees dream of water—and this organism uses none. Its sap is a smell: a faint petroleum tang caught in lattices of resin, light oils that never bead as dew and move instead as invisible films along internal capillaries and charged meshes. It farms its chemistry on dryness. It wicks gases, chews minerals, trades ions under tiny voltages coaxed along the same strands that serve as beams and bridges. Touch a strut and feel cold, brittle living; withdraw your hand and hear it continue to hum.

What you call a city is what it has been taught to become. The plant learned an urban plan once, pressed into a thousand iterations by the hands of a culture content with darkness and span-line craft. You run a palm along a rib and find notches worn into it at knee and shoulder height, shallow saddles where ropes looped for centuries. Fibers are braided into long straps, petrified into a glossy mineral-skin by the plant’s own exudates. Platforms hang from three cords each, triangulated, skinned in tension, now empty and slightly canted, swaying in the slow traffics of the chamber’s breath. Steps exist, but they are insinuations of step: a local thickening of vein, a shallow cuff bulged from wall to wall at a rhythm that matches a human span.

Where the void narrows, louvers grown from living laminae admit and refuse the flow. Their edges are tongued; some are tuned, scored with crosshatch marks and pegged in place with wedges of glassy plant-ceramic. Someone set them once with palm-sized stones knapped to sharp, and you can still trace the rippled signature of flake scars on job-worn tools left to hang where the straps meet the frame. Everything hand-wrought is primitive, and precise in that simplicity: lashings pitched with resin, dowels whittled and wedged through splayed joints, powders pressed into chalky tiles and fitted into grooves to narrow a throat just so. No nails, no wire of metal. No hinges with teeth. Every device uses the flex of a living band or the lean of a lever against a compliant beam.

It is a city because it organizes itself around routes, and you feel them in the way the tones lean into one another. Long galleries that are nothing but connected pockets, each with a favored pitch; the chain of them implies a street. There are niches near the “ground”—bays sponged into the wall by growth and patient hand-planing—large enough to lie in one, shallow enough that your shoulder blades would find purchase. Above, far above by touch, are broader alveoli, once strung with hammocks of ribboned tissue and now only ghosts in the slick where loops bit in for years. A balcony is no more than a brace grown to tie three buttresses and then planed to a breadth of two palms, perfectly smooth from traffic that has long fallen silent.

Fauna, in the sense of scurries and wings and blinking companion things, are absent. No scrape, no whisk. Only the plant and the very small—films and dust-motes of living function, too far from animal to call animal. There is pollen here, but it is not for flight; it passes along wetted charges from one conductive seam to another. Flowers never were. Instead there are vents—tiny ones, hundreds concealed within folds—and each opens and closes by the slow differential swelling of fibers that taste the chemistry of the air. The organism seeds by fracture; a section of rib, cured to glass and shock, will some century detach on a resonant day, fall shivering through the emptiness, and break along pre-made seams into light and patient shards that will latch elsewhere and begin again.

Rules of this place begin with darkness. Orientation is by hand and by tone. Surfaces tell you what they are willing to do when you touch them: hard enough to be walked; spring enough to be sat; skin enough to be left alone. No eye ever ruled this map. Those who made their homes here sculpted the pitch because it carried further than lantern ever could, and lantern was dangerous in this air—fire would wander without water to tame it and the oils would drink greedily instead of burn clean. So they spoke in valves. Above you, a pipe opens, and the lowest note in the gallery drops a little; far away, something answers with a tremor. The plant’s metabolism is inseparable from this weather. It moves its solvents on these sighs and sucks its food in and out at the cadence of the void. When the tones shift for a season, the growing edges follow, laying down more silica here, more resin there, to tune the room again to the work of its life.

Erosion has already had its will. The city’s growth long ago asymptoted to these curvatures, and the places where hands planed are polished to final forms that no longer change. Salt dust takes a century to cross the room. Where the air scrapes persistently, it has etched hair-thin grooves into the louvers and then stopped. No crumb of water arrives to pluck or swell. The only drifts are of crystal flour that gathers in shallow cups like frost without cold, whispering underfoot when you move. You cannot smell rot because rot needs wet. Things break and become quieter versions of themselves.

Every so long, something more dramatic happens, though dramatic here is measured against a clock of lifetimes. Once in a while, the low tones beat and beat until a seam finally releases—an old tie loosens, a knuckle cracks with a report that will ring in this space for hours. A piece descends, grazing nothing, and lands far below in a place that eats sound. When it does, the chamber’s music changes minutely. Whoever once kept it corrected, re-stiffened what relaxed, brought a louver two finger-widths closer and restored the known chords. No one answers now. The sound drifts onward anyway, slow river of pressure, self-tending within a tolerance that suits survival if not memory.

You could call this lowland an organ, a lung, a plaza; each is partly right, and all are provisional. It is a city because it has addresses and an economy—of motion, of tone, of chemistry—that has outlasted its people. It is all plant, though the plant wears the work of those people in its very body. It is desertlike without sand, cool without comfort, ancient without ruin. And it is loud in the way of a sea you will never see: a patient, thunder-soft insistence, declaring the emptiness inside as a thing that is kept, curated, and alive.
