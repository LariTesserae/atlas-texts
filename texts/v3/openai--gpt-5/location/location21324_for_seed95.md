High country, but not the gaudy kind with saw-tooth ridges and tantrum cliffs. This is height worn down to its memory: a broad, pale back of stone that has settled into the shape it will keep long after everything else has moved on. The horizon is something you can draw with a thumb: long, level, faintly bowed. From a distance the place reads as empty. Up close it is very busy.

The bedrock lies exposed in sheets and low swells, a skin of quartzite and old sandstone that has been licked smooth by ages. Where the rock is soft it has been hollowed into bowls and honeycombs, the alveoli of tafoni opening like breathing pores along the wind-shadowed faces. Across the open flats the surface is scalloped into miniature rivers of stone—rills and flutes no higher than a hand, all oriented the same way, telling you which way water goes when it is allowed to exist. In the deeper hollows there are pans, gnammas like black coins, each with a dark line around their rim showing how high the water once rose in some long-ago storm, and how high it usually climbs in a good night of condensation.

Most days are warm from late morning until dusk. The air is thinner than it wants you to believe, and so it carries your breath away and leaves you a little light. Sunlight comes down ordinary and honest, not magnified, not filtered—the kind that draws hard shadows and shows even the fine dust motes when you stoop near the ground. Heat pools in the stone and lifts away in quivering sheets. Out on the middle distances, mirage scrapes hold false lakes you learn to ignore.

Moisture is a habit here, not a condition. At night the sky bleeds cold into the stone and the high plain sweats. Dew beads on every mineral hairline, collects in the micro-troughs, scales downward in long chains. The pans fill from below, from the idea of water rather than the act of rain. Storms do come—rare, warm, shouldering up from a lower valley or pushed this high by heat somewhere you can’t see—but they are events, not patterns. Mostly it is the breath of the plateau that waters it. You wake to little glass worlds on lichen crusts and the smell of damp iron.

Vegetation plays the long game. It is spare by choice and necessity, taking shape only where stone has flaked to a thumb’s depth of grit and the night-wet lingers past breakfast. There are cushion plants crouched over their own shade like animals pretending to be rocks; low spiny rosettes with fleshy leaves that catch mist on tiny backward-facing teeth; a handful of gray-barked shrubs whose branches are bent and trained by the prevailing wind into single arcs, each sprig furred with fine hairs that drink the passing vapor. Every crack is a ledger full of lichens, each an older calendar than the last, writing slow circles and patches in sulfur yellow, ash white, near-invisible black. After one of those rare rains, sudden lawns of needle-thin annuals pop loose from the seedbank, exhaust themselves in a week, and leave brittle confetti to sift into the next crack.

It is quiet in the register of lungs and ears. Wind is a presence but not a voice; over such a level plane it skims rather than sings. There is no canopy to talk, no grasses to gossip. Sound finds few taut surfaces to carry it. You learn to listen instead to the tiny things: a chain of soft ticks as cooling stone relieves itself of heat, the hairline crackles of salts blooming in a pan, the minute rasp of many bodies moving over rock. Water doesn’t run, it counts—drip into pan, drip into hollow, one two three until the sun rises and arithmetic becomes geometry again as everything curls dry into itself.

For all the hush, the place is crowded. This is where lives have learned to stand close. The stone is veiled in living film. Cyanobacterial skins grip the shaded sides of the flutes; the brighter faces are a jigsaw of crusts; there are places you can lay a fingertip and feel warmth that is not the sun but the lifting metabolism of an entire district of organisms layered so tightly they might as well be one thing. At predawn there is a migration measured in millimeters as the refuge-seeking multitudes move up out of crevices to drink: beetles with domed backs that bead dew and run it down to a waiting mouth; blind pale isopods that have traded eyes for humidity-sensing whisk; snails with shells that are more chalk than stone and that remember drought as a season rather than an emergency. Even the few reptiles—flat-bodied geckoes with toe pads like suede—move mouth-first, tasting the tracks laid by salt and water.

They all have found ways to be quiet. The buzzing habit has been selected out; there’s no point advertising on a plain where anything with a mouth can crowd you in. Communication is a ground-borne art. Feel the faint tap-tap near your heel? Some six-legged neighbor is drumming a message into the bedrock with a pair of thickened forelegs; another hears it half a field away, not as sound but as a touch. Large and small, everything seems to understand that the open stone is a single instrument. Each dusk, a bigger resident comes to a patch of rock polished concave by generations of use and plays it: a fat-bodied herbivore the size of a child, toes broadened into pads, tapping slow patterns that travel through the stone far beyond your ability to follow. Their heads are small, mouths gentle, teeth made for rasping at microbial crusts; they lift them to test the air but talk to the ground.

Predators flourish not by speed but by attention. Long-bodied things hug the shade lines and do not blink until they cannot help it. A mantis-colored poverty of forms: every hunter here has thinned itself to a shadow that can wait. The few birds that visit drop out of the enormous sky on hard wings, land silent, and move with the precise movements of animals that live on the clock of dew. Some nest here in the dry months, gluing cups of fiber and dried lichen into the mouths of tafoni with saliva; when the wet is real they ride it downslope to louder valleys. Echolocation is employed but not for conversation—you do not hear it. You see its mark in the blink of a bat’s ear, in the sudden midair correction of a night glide.

There are structures, but they are life grown into form rather than plan. Every few hundred strides you find a ring of stones the size of fists and thumbs arranged around the lip of a pan, cemented in place with resin and dust—dew fences that slow the wind and trip the night’s breath into droplets. A family of ants has layered silk over the mouth of a crack so thin you think it is just a shadow until it beads along its entire length at dawn and then gives that harvest back down to an underground farm. There are a few places where resinous shrubs have been trained by the same animal toes that drum: branches bent and woven into shade-blind hollows—cool in the day, warm with life in the dark—that smell of green and musk, a compromise between refuge and ambush. Here and there stand spires of stone that aren’t strictly natural, either: calcited heaps of exuviae and mineral-sucked droppings, built up over generations around a smooth-faced standing rock until the whole column channels mist into a slow weep. These are the scattered marks of doing rather than making. No levers, no cuts. Only additions and polish.

Rules assert themselves slowly but completely. Moisture moves toward depth, not distance, and everything is oriented that way. Flat is wealth; shade is a kind of currency; contact is the way speech and hunger and fear are transmitted. The bedrock is old enough to have moods. At dawn it calls—very low, too low to hear but easy to feel if you rest your jawbone to the ground as some do; a hum as it warms unevenly and the pieces pressed together for a quarter of a billion years remember to press again. At noon it retreats into itself. At dusk it gives up its saved heat and you can watch, if you have the patience, thin veins of warmth crawl away from the ridges and down into pockets where the night’s life will be.

The open scale reads in the way light pools, in how your eye can run unimpeded to the climb line of a distant inselberg, in the late-day procession of very small moving dots thrown tall by low sun. There is a town-worth of presences within the sound radius of your own breath. Lift a stone, there is a city. Sweep your hand along a yard of crust, there is a migration timed to that motion. Put your ear to a polished depression and hear nothing, but feel a coolness on your cheek as a slow exhalation from the crack below tells you that a chambered nest full of pale larvae is breathing.

Change comes here as rearrangement of emphasis. The pan grows a grain deeper each decade as dust, caught by a rim of resin and lichen, turns into soil. The dew-drinker’s tapping ground wears a shallow saucer over lifetimes. A shrub’s bent back begins to aim a little more to the south as the wind shifts a hair over a century. The old planet’s most patient agents—frostless, but present in their way—keep doing the work: saline crystals grow and fade, press and slacken, and stone crumbles to a sand you can barely see moving. There’s an old river in the distance, its cut nearly healed, its path recalled by a line of greener-gray because the cracks there still run in its direction. No one pushes; the place has already mostly decided what it will be.

Stand at midmorning on the lip of a pan with its ring of tiny dew-catchers, and the air is almost blank. A single midge lifts and falls like an anxiety. The lichen is a stippled photograph of a slower day. Somewhere a mouth takes a long drink from a hollow you cannot see. If you wait long enough, warm enough, quiet enough, the open plain resolves into its millions. Everything is almost touching. Everything is listening to the ground. And in the great, gentle, settled height of it, water finds its way down not by flowing but by remembering where it belongs.
