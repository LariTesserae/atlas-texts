At first you think it is ocean, because the horizon is a long, low gleam and the air carries a salt-heavy breath, and everything moves. But the ground does not give way. It is stone, planed and burned into smoothness by epochs of patient water, a continent pressed so flat that the smallest rises announce themselves as doctrine. The stone is dark and ferric, a duricrust like an old scar, and it weeps. Every outcrop, every low knuckle of bedrock, gleams with a skin of water as if the planet were forever condensing out of the air back onto itself. Springs are not points here; they are surfaces. You pass a wall of rock no taller than your waist, and the entire face is crying, thousands of eyes, a billion tears beading, streaming, vaporizing mid-fall. The tears are warm. Everything is warm enough that the wet feels alive against your skin.

The sky is a lid. You can tell where the sun should be by a paler patch wandering behind the clouds, but the light never sharpens. It is a diluted, sallow twilight between storm and storm. There is a sense the light has not been extinguished but packed, crowded into the vapors, forced to leak laterally rather than fall. In the glareless dimness, colors flatten to metal, algae-green, ochre. Far and low, the horizon is a strip of bright pewter that slides like a blade as you move your head, an illusion from the rank upon rank of thin fog-bands stacked to the edge of sight.

The air is a substance thicker than it should be—dense not with cold but with presence. Warm fogs gang together and mass and then shear across the plain with the slow menace of a river in flood. Clouds tear on unseen ridges of temperature, unreeling into long, glistening streamers. Mist beads on your eyelashes and does not fall off because the droplets are charged with a small insistence; you have to blink them away, or the wind will peel them off in a shiver.

You cannot hear yourself breathe. You cannot hear the inside of your own skull. This land has its own weather and its weather has its own voice, and that voice is never still. There is a recorded-surf roar at the edge of audibility, a vastness that presses on the bones of your chest. There is a mid-pitch thunder without lightning, the sound of whole strata of saturated air sliding over each other, the enormous, frictional moan of humidity. And above it all, in feverish lattices, there are the whistles.

The stone is not solid through. Once, in younger epochs, it was honeycombed by roots and channels and old vapors. As the world aged and its guts were boiled to this flat pan, those pores sealed to thin throats. Air pushes and is dragged in those throats as the fog-rivers go past. Millions upon millions of small vents sing and hiss and pipe in whatever key the hour demands—flute-tones, reed-squeals, a panicked keening when a gust combs downslope. Every low rise is a great mouthful of organs and birdbones, and the whole continent is breath. When the damp wind hardens and picks up, the plain becomes a stadium of flutes so loud the sound becomes a pressure mask. Speech has no purchase. Gesture and endurance become your language.

Where there are breaks in the rock, the water has laid itself thin over kilometers, a sheen as shallow as a finger’s breadth, and it slides. It slides in organized panes, skimming east today, then north tomorrow, not because of gradient—you could not swear where down is—but because the fog-rivers drag it. The land has settled into its final form, a last answer of gravity, and so water has learned to read the sky. It obeys wind. It obeys pressure. It obeys the long, unhurried pulses that seem to come from below, where the planet remembers tides.

When rain falls, it does not patter. It condenses to a single bright film on any upturned plane; it arcs into the air in hushed sheets and is turned sideways; it appears already as trickle and stream, as ripple in another place than where you feel the drop. After a moment’s rain, every surface is set buzzing with small explosions as droplets meet warmer film and scald to steam. The applause of this boils up into a hiss and melts back into the rest of the roar. After a long rain, the water has not deepened so much as moved. Whole kilometers of sheen pick up quietly and crawl away like a continent-length animal.

The ground is near-level to the end of the world, but it is not empty. It is saturated in another sense: not by features, but by presences that refuse to leave space between them. Microbial mats tile any persistent wet and throw a faint, green, sour light at night as if sulking. Salt and iron precipitate into wafer-thin crusts like lacquer on lacquer, which crack under your soles with a sound like desperate paper. Low ridges of manganese and calcite wander like the backs of schools of fish, inches high and miles long. Runnels repeat the same shapes, over and over, until you are certain you have walked past this exact glint-triplet of stones before—until you realize you have.

Something is wrong with distance. You move and the world moves you. The famous trick of this place is that space is crumpled like wet cloth. Not in the way of sinkholes or physics easy in a textbook, but in the way of obsession: the constant weather presses the world inward, and the world yields. In the moments when the roar rises a fraction, when the whistling assembles itself into a near-harmony, you will take a step meant to take you a meter and find yourself reappearing two field-lengths away, mid-stride, as if you had slipped through a pleat. And then you will spend an hour pushing through some elastic of time and fog trying to make headway on a straight line. Everything is packed so tight—vapor against sound against gravity against heat—that the seams show, and some of them unlatch.

Vegetation announces itself not as forest or field but as signatures in the margins of stone. There are reed-clusters like sparse needles where sheetwater is always on the move. They have blades tough enough to scratch steel and stems that ring faintly when pressed by the wind. There are mats of thin-leafed succulents that lie flat, doors closed the instant spray hits them, waiting until the noise dips to open and drink. There are shrubs, but ‘shrub’ here means a lattice of living drywood built to catch cloud, not foliage; their branches are silk-tough and woven to hair-fine nets that pull water out of wind, and then let the excess drip to a single root like a funnel. In hollows shielded from the highest whistles, there are lichens that stack themselves like shingles and peel, layer by layer, as if the air were a famine. The light is dim enough that all of it grows slow-Jesus slow. What you notice most are not green things, but the places green should be and isn’t: dim terraces of rock carrying only a mute sheen, kilometer after kilometer.

Animals, when they present themselves, do not comfort you. You will go days with only the company of worm-casts and the silver trails of a thing that swims in film-water with a double crest like a stitch unraveling. Then, one evening, the fog-panes quieten, and the organ-piping dips to a syrupy hymn, and there is something in the distance like a line of very tall, thin doors walking. It is only one creature, jointed like the inside of umbrellas, each segment a membrane catching mist. It crosses the plain with its own sound, a thrumming you feel in your teeth, and it opens and closes to pump fog through itself. If it sees you, it does not say. It does not return the next day. Once you see a plastic bag of air caught on a shrub-net, twisting, and then you realize it is an animal—more net than body—tightening like a fist as the wind changes, mouth hidden among the tangles. Another time, under a shelf of weeping stone, you kneel to drink and a flat disc lifts from the rock and glides away on the thin film, rafts across a whole pool, and at the far edge dissolves into mud. Threads return to the water from where it came, as if it only held shape while it needed to travel.

There are sounds made by animals too, you tell yourself. Somewhere between pipe and howl there is a call that comes only when the sky is particularly eager to move. It drifts like a buoy in the midst of the constant, deafening noise, and you doubt it until it repeats in a sequence that is almost, almost organized. Later you step on a field of small cone-heaps like the nests of wasps, and when the plain sings they vibrate in sympathy and you realize they are not heaps but homes. A whispering fog of near-invisible shrimp, lunging in place at the air, catching what’s not visible to you, the vibration itself like swarming pollen.

You begin to understand how anything lives here at all. Sound is not only punishment; it is food. Every flat, taut membrane in this world is an ear and a stomach. The umbrella-creature pumps the fog across mats of hairlike cilia tuned to harvest pressure spikes. The reed-blades ring, and the ring drives small systems inside their stems that bring water where light will not. The lichens sit in the lee of shallow cuts and let the plain’s infinite teeth gnaw at them, and their response to that touch is the small labor that keeps them going. Noise runs on circuits. Animals graze on it the way whales graze on krill.

Small constructions are scattered so rarely you doubt they are constructions at first. A curve of stones that is only different from the land by being too regular, too perfectly bent. A row of poles set into shallow pits and bound together with some old fiber, their top two palms above the everlasting sheen. Frames built not to stand against the wind but to listen to it; nets strung not to catch birds but to catch water. Cairns where there are no stones loose enough to make cairns. You run a hand over the topmost rock and find it limed with a thin cement of shell, and in the cement there are thumbtacks’ worth of fingernails darker than the rest—as if a generation once stood here and pressed and pressed to make the mark last. In the whistle of the ridge, the stones hum one particular note. It is possible these were instruments once, used to find safe passage through a day’s foldings in the plain; possible it is only accident. The architecture is so faint it verges on idea.

There are rules you learn by being flayed with them.

Rule one: Do not step when the plain sings in chords. The moment many whistles agree, the folds tighten. If you have to move, shuffle without lifting your soles. If you must lift your soles, know you may set them down somewhere another hour did not belong to.

Rule two: Walk on the dull. The lustrous surfaces are new skin—fresh precipitates, glazed water—that will tear and tumble like a upped tablecloth when the next fog-river comes. The matte, dusty stone has stood still long enough to be trustworthy.

Rule three: Never trust away. The horizon here is a perjurer. What looks far may be a single footfall through a seam. What looks near will go on all day until you learn something you needed to learn.

Rule four: Choose your whistle. Each ridge, each low-lobed outcrop, has its sound. One of those will become your ally. You will clasp it to your skull like a compass. Where it is loudest is not necessarily closest; learn instead to hear how the other whistles drag or tug against it. Once you know their tug you will find the gap you can safely pass through. Sound here is isobar map, pressure and pitch rather than arrows and words.

Erosion has gone past story into idiom. Rivers did their work and retired. Mountains thought themselves immortal until vapor taught them patience. Now the plain is the oldest possible thing that can still be called a landscape. Any new thing—pool, bar, thin island of silt—throws itself up dancing in the gales of this atmosphere, and throws itself down again in the next measure. The world wastes no time on pieces larger than a line’s width. The durability is in the pan itself, in a kilometer of fused suffering that has said all it will ever say about being ground down. Above it, life and water and air quarrel over micrometers.

At night, the dimness deepens to bruise. The light bleeding from the biofilms is a faint insult, enough to separate face from stone at your toes, not enough to find a path. The sky-lid lowers with the cooling and rests on everything until you feel you could stand on tiptoe and push it up with your palms. The roar changes timbre; when temperature layers invert the whistling becomes a breathless lowing like whales far away. The ground ticks as iron crusts expand and pop. Somewhere, pools of trapped gas release in a soft drumbeat. The animals, those that have made a practice of this venue, come out to sieve the thicker night for clean sound. There are glows further out, dull oranges, not fire—no fuel holds long enough here for flame—but electrets discharging along weeping mineral faces, a foxfire learned by stone.

Smell is salt and iron. Taste is metal and mildew and once, if you put your lips to the wet of a certain weeping ledge and sip what it offers, sweetness from some rare cluster of fallen pollen shredded from the lives that anchor out beyond where your eye has yet learned to recognize them. Touch is being covered. The plain sets itself on you. It will bead on your hair and eyebrows, it will lay film along your wrists and between your fingers, it will find a home in the hollows behind your knees. The sound uses your ribs as a drum. A day here fills you just by being there.

If there is a church, it is the places where sound cancels. You stumble into one by accident: a pond the depth of two hands in a scooped pocket of stone, dark as oil, under a roof that is really just a lip of crust leaning over emptiness. Stand in the exact middle and the whistles from both sides of the lip wind around each other and pluck themselves out of air. The roar is not absent—nothing is—but for three breaths, there is a silence the size of a palm on your chest, and in that space all the layers of presences give way at once. In there you can see the tiny paired prints in the film—slashes, dots—the marks of something that came and left every other night for a thousand cycles. In there, for a second, light is a little less dim.

What breaks reality here is not catastrophe. It is insistence. Everything has learned everyone else’s trick. Water learned wind. Wind learned stone. Stone learned how to sing. Sound learned bodies, bodies learned to eat sound, and all of them learned to press together until the rules between them got marked with thumbtacks and changed. You walk, and the plain walks you. You rest your hand on a weeping rock and it rests its weight on you, and out beyond, where the horizon lies like a blade, the whole old place breathes and hisses and shifts, again and again, until it seems to be thinning to its idea: a great, wet, warm, low, deafening lesson in how a world ends not in empty, but in full.
