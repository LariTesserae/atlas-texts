From far off, it looks like a continent somebody ironed flat and then lifted into the sky until the air thinned to a whisper. Up close, the flatness is a lie told at scale. The surface is old stone made hard by ages of abrasion—planed smooth in long swathes, crusted with desert varnish so dark it drinks light; studded with yardangs and low shark-fin ridges all leaning the same way, as if a single wind has been working them forever. Between the ridges, pavements of interlocking cobbles fit together like armor—no sand, no grit left loose; anything that could be carried away has been, anything that remained has learned to hold on.

It is cool up here. The sun has the strength to make your shadow sharp, but the warmth is thin, and as soon as a breeze moves past your skin it takes heat with it and never brings any back. You feel the temperature most when you step into a patch of shade and the stone underneath has already surrendered its warmth to the sky.

There is almost no water. That is a rule you feel before you know it. Your mouth dries and stays dry. Nothing moist gleams here. The few living films that dare the surface carry their solvents with them, sealed beneath wax and mineral crusts. They are not green in a way you recognize. They are charcoals and burnt umbers and metallic khakis that look like stains left by rain that never fell. If you crouch close in a place where the wind has paused, you can see their lacework—lichen-analogs that knit silica and thin organics into brittle, crackled sheets, edges lifted to the tiniest breeze to feed off charge. They lie low, hugging microtopography, fleeing prominence. A finger run across them would both kill and be cut.

Above the near-silence of the stone, the sky is all motion. Not with the rumbles and groans of familiar storms—there is too little air here for that—but with a kind of muscular grace. Bands of wind slide over the plateau in stacked rivers, laminar flows that part and curl as they cross the low relief. You see the currents by what they carry: curtains of pale dust rising from far away and sewn back into the rock as a fine polish; shimmering veils of charged ice crystals that never melt; occasional sheets of micro-needles, salt and silica flashing as they turn. Sometimes, far off yet all across, a blue-tinged discharge rips along a ridge in silence and then is gone, leaving the ground smelling—if any smell could cling in such quick air—of something acrid and clean.

The place is vast enough to raise its own weather in defiance of the planet’s broader patterns. The plateau’s rim—always far, always visible as a dim straightness on the horizon—generates a permanent tide of updrafts. Air climbs there and spills, and the long fetch across the top breeds standing waves that sit for days in one place, lensing light into crisp lines on the ground. The sky carries structures you learn to read: knife-thin lenticulars pointing like compass needles to regions of lesser gravity; faint luminescent braids that mark the paths where charge is easing from ground to rarified air along planted conductors you cannot yet see.

Once in a long while, a turbulence cell sprints across your view like a herd—dust devils without bodies, just the act of lifting and setting down translated into motion. They stitch nothing to nothing; they leave no mark you can put your hand on later. The ground is too old to care.

Age is the governing aesthetic. Every surface looks settled into its final compromise with abrasion. Ridges are sculpted to the same family of curves, voids are rounded to bottoms so uniform you suspect an intention. There is intention here—some of it human or human-kin. Scattered across the pavement are devices and the ghosts of devices that do not belong to any mundane history. They are few and far between, so much so that you can go days across the stone and see nothing but scoured geology—and then a thing turns the light wrong ahead of you, and you know it is a remain.

A survivor: a needle-thin mast, no thicker than your wrist, rises from a base you cannot name, perfectly vertical in winds that have leaned everything else. Touch it and you would find no seam, no fastener, only a temperature that is not the stone’s and a hum you feel at your bones rather than hear. A thread leaps from its tip a hundred meters to a twin you cannot see at first, and dust passing between them fluoresces faintly as if remembering a color. Once this was part of a net—field modulators that tuned the weight of the air close to the ground, shepherding its flows. Now it remains as an archipelago of operant pins: a line that steers a wave, a row that lifts a frost sheet, a lone anchor that keeps the local gravity a whisper looser so the pavement outlasts itself.

Another survivor is a bowl you can walk down into, so shallow and smooth your feet forget you are descending until your ears pop slightly in the added pressure. It is lined with tiles that drink light until the bowl is a dark well in the day. Once, it listened to something—perhaps the thin thunder of discharges, perhaps the hiss of molecules—the sorted geometry of its interior too precise for pure art. Now insects made of metal and glare, each the size of a flake of ash, orbit the bowl inches above the surface, tasting the heat and distributing it evenly so that the tiles do not crack as the day snaps to night.

There are marks too faint to be sure of. On some ridges, a smear of old polymer as if something once adhered there and later left without a residue but that. In some flats, lines of glass finer than thread cross the stone and sing not at all when the wind slides over them, yet the dust they intercept stacks in arrow-straight versions of dunes. They say these webs once hung fields that bent the edge of the world, making corridors of easier stride across hundreds of kilometers. Those fields are gone. The web remembers shape without function. Walk long enough in one geodesic and you feel you are retracing the bones of a creature long dead and immense.

There are occupants. Scarce, yes, and you can go a week and see none. But sometimes in a standing wave, a glider hangs—something thin as an eyelid and yards across, black as space except where the edge glows corona-blue. It is an animal if structure that patient and spare can be called one; it eats the varieties of potential difference the wind brings, and once sated it folds down toward the stone like a fallen scrap of the old devices and lies there indistinguishable from varnish until the wave returns. Once, skimming your horizon, a pale, many-vaned thing moves with no visible means, tacking across air in a geometry that agrees with nothing you practiced in lower places. It drops to a patch of mineral film and scrapes it for chemistry with cilia that leave trace glyphs in the varnish. You watch its mouth-parts worrying at an idea and it leaves, and the glyphs begin to flow—not quickly but with mineral patience—closing back to the stain.

Sound is what you bring with you: the click of boot on harder cobble, the whisper of fabric against fabric, the softest creak from your pack when you turn. The world gives small offerings. When a charge jumps between two ridges while you are near, there is a sound like a cat’s toenail on wood, a single tiny tic that is not attached to pressure in your ears. Occasionally, a brittle fan of silica the size of a hand, undercut by ages and now balanced at last beyond its center, leans into nothing and drops. It lands without echo, as if the ground refuses to take the note.

Even the abyss at the rim, when you finally reach it, is quiet. You know the wind is a river there. You see it in the way a pall of particles streams off and down and blooms in widening curtains far below. You cannot hear it. The drop is severe—kilometers, a wall of hard stone wrinkled with the fossil curves of waves of air themselves, pressure fossils rather than those of life. Along the rim, scattered at intervals that begin to feel ritual the longer you walk, stand pallets of smooth black material like altars. On each, the air feels the slightest bit heavier; you can tell by the ease with which you inhale. They are field sinks, still doing something to the fall. In their lee, some living films grow higher—thin cresses that make delicate veinwork forms, their surfaces a corrugation tuned to a resonance not of sound but of charge. In the lee of those cresses, something that looks like a dried hand on stilts, jointed bones of translucent calcite, shuffles forward one night over the course of hours and harvests the cress with patient, exacting snaps, then folds itself down into its own shadow for another season.

The rules of this place discover themselves in your muscles and lungs and the way the hair on your forearms behaves. Rule one: anything reaching above shoulder height accepts ablation as a way of being. Everything prominent arcs itself toward the wind, and even the devices that stand resist by appearing to be nothing but line. Rule two: charge is a currency as nutritive as light. Life lies where fields are complicated, where old tech still leaks a potential from stone to sky. Rule three: surfaces decide fates. The stone is a membrane stretched over time; step where it glosses differently and you may find yourself on a sink where gravity is thinner by a fraction and your step is longer, or thicker and your step bites too quickly. Rule four: water’s absence is filled with other lubricants. Things here exude oils that don’t name themselves readily—siloxanes, fluorocarbons—enough to let a hinge remember it knows how to move.

Travel is measured by effects rather than miles. You chase the shadow of a lenticular cloud for a day and find at dusk that you have arrived in cooler air that tastes cleaner in your chest. You follow a line of vents—no breath of air comes from them, for there is no underground convecting—but you learn that above each vent the local gravity buckles slightly and traps microdust into visible halos. You sleep in one of those halos and dream of flight because your body knows in sleep that it weighs less.

At night, the sky tightens. The plateau drops its heat like a discipline, and all things harden. Stars arrive in vulgar numbers, unsoftened by vapor. You see the world’s magnetic skeleton: curtains of green and violet that touch down at the ridges that are oldest, the ones that have learned to be lightning rods without burning. The devices wake a little in this clarity. Threads hum—not in air but in grit. If you scatter a pinch of powdered stone, it lines itself up along a direction that is not quite north. Far off, where you thought there was nothing, a vertical line of points brightens and then dims; a message to itself, some remnant network counting days.

In the morning, frost forms. Not water—never water—but carbonates and nitrates, minute spikes that rise from the varnish like fur. The first light knocks them down in a tinkling you do not hear but think you do. Where they fall and sublimate they leave behind the faintest dusting of something reactive that feeds the films. For an hour after dawn, the place smells, if smell can be invoked here, like a struck flint.

There are buried things. If you camp where the pavement buckles to make a natural shelter and you press your ear to the stone—useless gesture in this air but you do it anyway—you may feel pulsed madness beneath you. Microtremors not quite rhythmic, the sleeping heartbeats of the plateau’s own infrastructure. Long ago, before erosion drove everything to its final angles, before the built threads broke into lines and points, some intention hoisted this shelf and spread a lattice through it that manages stresses in ways unfathomed by mere rock. It braced this lid against downthrown mantle, bled heat away so it did not buckle, tuned gravity so rivers of air did what rivers of water once did elsewhere. The lattice mostly sleeps now. When it stirs, the sky burred by it muscles in answer. The turbulence you see is partly its pulse.

You can, if you like, believe it will go on like this indefinitely. That is the particular promise of such age married to such movement. The forms are at their final form and yet the place is never still. Seams are endlessly polished, new crusts laid and lifted, films creep their millimeters, animals rise for a wave and go flat for a year, a mast hums and then does not and then hums again. Basins continue to collect light and give it back into a network that is a rumor across the stone. Any mark you make will be smoothed. Any song you shout will die an inch out from your mouth. Your breath will go farther than your voice.

On days when the wind is particularly clean, when the bands high in the sky have aligned so that the air above you is not fighting itself, you can see other plateaus in the far clarity—perhaps mirages, perhaps the true ghosts of highlands beyond the drop. Between them, in air too thin for wing but rich with potential, something is traveling along a line strung out of field alone, a speed of thought masquerading as a thing. A migratory engine. A remnant of a way to cross emptiness that never needed air. It is as private as any animal. It passes. Dust catches light and floats in its wake.

Somewhere, at the far end of a day's walk, you find a door, if a door can be a piece of stone that does not quite ring like the rest when you tap it. It opens not into a room but into thicker air held back by cleverness. Inside is a pressure high enough for a whisper to carry. Inside is water—not liquid in any way familiar, but a gel that grows itself, that remembers shapes when you ask it politely with a field you cannot generate. Life inside is thick and opalescent and busy in a way the outside has chosen not to be. The chamber feeds off a still-functioning part of the lattice. It has gone on too long to be an emergency cache and too long to be maintained. It is a lingering.

You step back out because the high quiet makes more sense. Out here the rules are simple and forensic. Everything that exists has earned each atom of itself by the courtesy of time and a steady wind. Out here, if you lie down on the old stone with your cheek on the varnish and look sideways, the plateau slopes so gently you cannot be sure it slopes at all, and yet the whole sky is leaning. And if you hold still long enough, long enough to let your own noise die, you can feel, very faintly, the place turning under larger weathers than air, under a gravity tuned and retuned to old purposes, under electric tides that scratch their initials in frost.

It has the curious beauty of end states: level and finished and always in the act of being made.
