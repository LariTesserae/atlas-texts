The land rises until sky seems a neighbor. A continent of uplift, cold and clean-edged, thrown up fast in the measures of stone and slow in the measures of people. From above, it would read like a table rimmed with teeth—new cliffs, knife-ridges, miles long gullies that cut as if made with a single precise gouge. From within, you never step into an emptiness; something is always near: a buttress of rock, a hanging lip of corniced snow, a wall of dry-laid stone, a person, an animal. The altitude packs things together—not closeness in warmth, but in presence.

Air is thin enough that breath is a thing you think about. The light is untroubled. On clear days the sun lays a hard line on anything that can take a shadow. There are days of cloud that the plateau makes for itself: tide-like flows of vapour shouldering up the escarpments, folding, snagging on the new-risen edges. When those slow grey rivers arrive, everything gathers a skin of water or ice. You can watch frost bloom in a matter of minutes on the blackened capture-boards, watch bead-lines of melt creep toward a gutter’s lip. In the rest of the year, rain is an event with its own rumors—the kind of phenomenon people stop to watch, held by its rare percussion.

Water here is a practice. It does not sprawl; it collects and clings. There are pools at the bottoms of faults, narrow and deep as a throat. Chasms carry thread-rivers—clear to the bones of the earth, moving so fast and cold they burn the tongue. On certain mornings the entire rim of the world hisses faintly as cloud shoulders are torn on cliff and caught by webbed fabrics. The people set their cloth across the wind like fishing, angled to the usual drift. Rime slicks over the cords before the sun burns through and the nets begin to drip. It is a soft sound, barely anything: the edited end of a syllable, repeated, a city-long sentence of glottal release.

The city hugs the break where rock, sky, and cloud have been arguing for ages. It comes in layers, packed tight to cliff and pressed around the gullies. You climb: steps pecked into stone, steps in mortar, steps in earth, and sometimes steps made of bolstered wood, black with age and polish. Houses kneel into the rock to be small in the wind. Walls are thick—not a statement, a necessity—and they are left rough so frost has something to grip. The roofs are stone, sod, and hide; they take happenstance footprints as part of their design, because rooftops are streets.

Between houses are runnels—gutters that accept water from any angle. They are everyone’s business. Everything falls toward a cistern. The cisterns are the city’s plazas. Lidded, tucked under lattice, they are also the places where people meet without talking loudly: exchange of net-fiber, bone needles, peat; where children learn the knots that hold the world together; where the dry routes of rumor are the only fast-flowing thing. Every opening is baffled against the air with a hide-curtain or a woven screen. You can see the old repairs in every line: rope splices worn shiny by hand; stones re-seated after frost-heave, a patchwork of thoughtful work.

There are few metals here. A glint means someone’s grandfather traded well or carried it in on his back. Most edges are stone—polished basalt, horn, bone—driven down patient into the world, persuaded from it. If you come to a balcony or a ring of posts hurled off into a void, the binds are of skin and hair. Nets use fiber teased from shrubs that scrape a living from the lee of rocks; they use rope made from the combed undercoat of the herds. Knots matter. Knots are the alphabet and the law. On windless days a street crew will unmoor a dew-net from its forward irons, carry it in a long careful fold over their heads, like pallbearers for time itself, to patchwork it with a square from the deadcoat of a goat, to coil it clean again.

The noise of the place is the sound of not-quite-silence. Boots on grit. The clink of someone’s small bell, not rung but answering each step. Leather whispering on stone. The city breathes with its animals. Herds move through it and along it, their bodies filling alleys as a tide fills a narrow, their dense heat the only warmth felt as they pass. They are shaggy and black-shouldered; they wear the wind like a garment and grow ice beads on their muzzles in night-weather. There are goats in places that no one else would go but them. Their tracks are second maps. People shape their days around the herds’ hunger and manure and heat. Dung dries on racks, a permanent line in the skyline of any back street. The smell is everywhere, not offensive because there is so little else to spend on it; it’s a ledger entry.

Birds have found an empire here. Ravens live as close to people as people live to rock. They nest in those rough walls and sweep through narrow streets on wings that don’t quite fit. Choughs wheel in groups like black punctuation when a cloud-sheet lifts. Vultures congregate on updraft posts outside the abattoir quarter and rise with the day’s learning in their eyes. Pigeons, rock-doves, snow-finches—they are threads stitched into the city. The city wants them. Ledges are stepped so that a certain rope of droppings begins and continues; the chalk-white smear that seems to mean filth to an outsider is fertility here, scraped and diluted and carried to the terraced beds.

Vegetation is what stubbornness looks like when green. Most of the plateau is stone and a pale grey livingness that needs neither leaf nor sympathy. Lichens sheath the windward faces and turn them a color that varies with season and moisture—from soot to silver to a pale green that might just be a trick of eyes. Cushion plants make little domes where melt will sit for an hour. There are shrubs that keep themselves to the contour of a palm pressed flat against a wall. Here and there, in the city’s inner courtyards and terraces, are earthboxes—beds built from gullied silt and bone and the powder of rock, held in with good stone, covered when dry days go on too long so they don’t spend their water back into the air. In them: hardy barley, a blue-flowering pea, a tuber that tastes like sun-warmed stone until you cook it forever and then it tastes like sweetness saved over. You can watch someone skim off the night frost from a black roof with a thin bone blade and carry it to the beds in a pan like a priest carrying light.

If there is water that moves of its own accord above ground, the city binds it gently and immediately. Channels are cut but never smooth, because water is averse to being hurried here: it will slip away along the grain of any smoothness. There are flap-gates of leather to shut when the cisterns have their fill, shade-screens deployed without words as the sun climbs, an entire grammar of hands that say: lift that stone, turn that lid, attend that drip. Children grow up with a game of listening for water, a feral quiet that leaves them with a talent for hearing it in the presence of wind.

The plateau is young in its making. Rocks sound different when they are new like this; they ring. There are raw breaks where faces have come down in the night maybe last year, or last week, cutting through a path that has existed for five generations and obliterating it as if it had never been. Over these, people have learned an architecture of dodging. Walls lean in not just to shed snow but to catch stray stone. Streets turn inward before ravines so that a rockfall can spend itself along a sacrificial wall. There are watch-places not for enemies but for movement: watchers with their backs to wind or under a stone lip, looking for knife-lines that have opened a finger-width where yesterday that seam was closed. “You bake bread slow but look at rock fast,” someone says, and everyone knows it.

Rules grow out of those practices as naturally as shadows out of walls. You don’t run here unless it’s for fire or falling, because thin air punishes hurry with a scalded chest and a taste of iron in the mouth. You don’t waste a yell, either: sound dies in this air faster than in thick air; it drops short and rolls in the gullies unexpectedly; it startles populations that share your walls and time. Bells are not for announcement. They are worn small on ankle and strap to say only: a body is here. When the weather makes body-century fogs, you walk lines strung waist-high across the open yards and terraces, and you keep a hand in its loops as if your own name could smudge away.

Fire is a domestic thing, not a show. There are communal rooms where embers are kept, fed with pressed dung, fed with a shrub’s slow death. Smoke hugs the floors, for the air is stingy and you must learn to bend to it, to sit and move as if the world were a tent that would cave if you stood too quickly. Tea boils at the wrong number and all recipes are poetry about this. The best kitchens don’t boil; they keep stone pots buried in the warm dust around a hearth and use time instead of violence. Fat is a currency and a sacrament.

Beyond the city’s stacked neighborhoods are the slopes that the city keeps, not to own them, but to listen. People have put posts into the rocks all along the rim not to tie things, but to speak to wind. Each is carved and notched so that air makes a different sound around it. A row of them will tell a trained ear whether the cloud-sea will reach the nets by midday or will lie below sulking and yet not climb. Sound is weather here; it is read off the edges and then folded into the day’s making. If the vultures leave their posts early and the choughs go low, everyone knows to spread black cloth on the high roofs and hurry the frost-melt before dark.

Fauna come and go in cycles too patient for any one family to keep in a head, but the elders know: a year of longer fog brings the little rodents into houses by the basketsful; a year of scant rain sees the snow-leopards padding down the lanes at dawn, quiet as a thinking, leaving prints like bowls in the dust. There are insects in such sudden conscriptions—minute flies like ash under the eaves, a single day in late high-summer when the air shines with wings and anything that eats eats forever. The fish in the Thread River below the fault have eyes like stones and bodies thick with ice-cold cunning. You catch them with a net dropped deep and you haul up handfuls of life that taste like tin and river. It is work to carry wet up the cliff, even when it is inside a body already expressing its own cooperation; it is worth it.

The built things answer the land’s newness in another way: nothing is finished. Even the largest walls—massive tumbles held against the world with a truth of their own weight—show from the side a seam here, a new stone there, today’s work a shade lighter than last decade’s. Ramps get turned away from any line where water tried to speak or stone tried to move. There are plankways along certain faces, hung on pegs driven into slots cut at dawn when the rock is contractured and keeps a grip. In winter, much moves inside. Rooms stretch into galleries and then into tunnels, except tunnels can’t be called tunnels when they are more like the bodies of houses learning to be subterranean, skin pulling inward around a source of heat. Air is led along slits and tubes of clay and slate so that breath costs less. In such rooms the city is closest to itself—huddled, attentive, a nested animal gathering for patience.

What looks like harshness carries an austere generosity. The city leaves ladders down to the narrow ledges where wild goats come to lamb. It scratches tiny channels to lead frost-drip to certain shrubs so that their seeds will next year hold on with all their courage. It builds platforms that look like altars but are just places to rest loads at the height of a tired shoulder. Out on windswept bare, someone has made a niche with a single smooth stone in it, and into that stone a hollow, and the hollow is blackly polished by a hundred thousand fingertips. The stone is at the edge of a marked turning where any traveler stops because they have come far and will go farther, and stopping there marks the passage. No one says you have to touch it. No one doesn’t.

From some heights the scale breaks whatever part of your mind used to measure. The plateau runs out as if the world were a blade’s back, and then it falls in a single impossible drop into a sea of cloud. On certain cold bright afternoons you can see other cities along the far rim, dots and smudges with vertical threads like harp-strings—more dew-nets hiked to the wind, more lives clinging hard in abundance to a place that would seem to offer very little. Between them and you move the herds and the birds, the same species, the same stories, a circulation as slow and as sure as the creep of the rock under everything.

When night comes, it comes with both clarity and caution. Nothing in the sky is hidden by moisture except when the plateau builds its own roof. Stars are a multiplicity that embarrasses thought. The city’s lights are small holes in the dark where smoke finds a way. The rules adjust: pathways are hooked with small lamps made from cups of fat and wick, shaded so their spillage goes down, not up and out to confuse a migration or drain a cistern with unnecessary melting. There is no drumming. There is a late song, low, with no part of it that might bring stone down. By morning, the nets have furred over in white again and the bells begin to answer the steps of animals who don’t believe in hurry and have no need to.

Seasons happen, but they are thickened. Winter is king but never a tyrant; it rules by consistency. Spring is the rumor of noise: water that speaks during the day and then closes its mouth again at night. Summer is thin as a shawl flung over bones—useful, surprising. Autumn is a counting ritual: of fuel, of fiber, of the number of spare ceramic lids you have for the cisterns, of how long the herds will be high. The land itself is still new enough to remember its own shaping. The people are old enough to have made themselves at home not in defiance of that shaping but by listening to how it continues.

If there is a law above others, it might be this: leave the wind its way. Don’t make it be what it isn’t; ask it, invite it, give it a rope to pluck and it will pay you with direction. The city’s survival hangs on the crossing of such invitations: to water, to animals, to rock. It is as dense as honeycomb and as patient as ice. It speaks softly. It endures.
