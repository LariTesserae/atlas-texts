The light is ordinary and unwavering, as if noon has been fixed here for a very long time. It clarifies rather than warms. The air is cool without bite, the kind of cold you know mostly by the way your breath feels shallow. It carries almost no scent. If you listen long enough you will hear how little there is to hear: a thin, papery rasp when you move; a single grain settling somewhere a valley away; the slow internal work of the stone.

Everything is very high. The whole region is a system of ancient steps that climb and climb until the horizon loses track of them, each terrace a mile or ten wide, each scarp a clean, pale drop with wind-worked flutes. From above, it would look like the back of some behemoth that lay down and let its ridges wear flat. From within, it is a succession of platforms, basins, and old cliff-faces that feel inevitable. Erosion happened here until there was nothing left for erosion to learn. The sharp ridges long ago slumped to aprons. The aprons splayed into tables. The tables planed themselves to almost-levels cut by fathomless, vertical slots where the rock refused, for reasons no longer visible, to be rounded. Nothing new forms; the place has reached the habit it intends to keep.

Water does not live here. The white sheen you see from afar is salt and fused dust, a mineral skin that catches the light like bone and glass. In the lee of boulders, there are drifts of powder the consistency of sifted ash, and hardpans of pale silica where something, once, ran hot enough to melt the ground and then never ran again. When the air moves, it does not bring wetness, only a very small subtraction of heat and a very small rearrangement of fines. You can lick anything you like and you will not taste water.

Vegetation exists in the way a signature does: decisively, sparsely, elaborately cursive in a land of blocks. There are wirebrushes the color of iron filings that grow where cracks widen just enough to offer shade to roots that are more like needles than fibers. There are crusts, somewhere between lichen and mineral bloom, that trace the shadow-lines of rocks, turning toward light with a patience that must be measured in centuries. Once in a long while you will find a stalk the thickness of a finger, bristled, silvery, its leaves reduced to thorns that comb the air for mounted dust and heat. They do not grow in clumps. Each plant is a decision made alone, and the ground between them is an ethics of emptiness.

If that were all, the steps would feel almost deserted. They are not. Life has gathered so thickly here that it has found a way not to make a sound. The surfaces are encrusted with things that might be animals if animals could be built from porcelain and resolve. There are ribbed, half-spiral plates cemented to the stone, their mouths the shape of a Euclidean notch, their lids opening a fraction of a degree when the sun slides past noon. There are stacks of wafers, as thin as fingernails, that shiver microscopically in patterns aligned with fractures you cannot see. Between them creep jointed pale insects that are not truly insects, whose feet are hooklets of glass and whose abdomens dangle feathery sieves. They graze not on plants but on discontinuities: they brush along seams in the world where angles don’t quite add to a circle, and sweep in what abrades off there.

In the gullies too steep for gravel, there are curtains of web, grey as old smoke. They look abandoned until a slow wave runs from top to bottom, taking a year to pass, and something living is revealed in the weft only because it is now a hair’s breadth to the left of where it was. Above the terraces, under the constant, gentle light, float creatures like dried leaves pinned to balloons you can’t see. Each is tethered to a stone with a filament you could cut with breath, if breath counted. They are packed in the sky like a silent snowfall that never lands; from some angles they occlude each other so densely that they form dim ceilings between the terraces. Every so often one goes dark inside, like a hand cupped around a flame, and then light again—signaling some business that has all the urgency of tectonics.

The ground crunches, but what crunches are not pebbles. They are shells, plates, mute forms accumulated in such numbers that they have become a kind of terrain. In niches and under the salt overhangs there is a carpet of mites—stone-colored, shaped like hex nuts—that turn as one to face you as you pass and then turn away, a courtesy so restrained it almost counts as denial. In some places the animals have compiled themselves into walls and rings and velvets of their own bodies, the way mussels make reefs. In others, life is a scum on each planar surface, a very fine sand of living cells that feels like flour between your fingers and recoils with impossible slowness.

You might expect the air to be loud with this much aliveness. Instead, sound travels here as if the world has been padded from within. You can speak and hear yourself in your skull but the air loses it after a short arm’s length. Clap twice and the second hand arrives to meet an absence, the echo surrendered sideways into cracks in space where acoustics go to be eaten by rules that don’t favor them. The creatures have found this, or made this, and adapted to it. Their communications are by shadow, by position, by the lay of hairs in the all-but-imperceptible breeze. They drum on time, but their time is not ours.

The distances are correct until they are not. You can walk the length of a terrace counting steps and return by a different path with the same count and a different sun. Ascents can be level; descents can leave your ears unpopped. The steps are steep but also contiguous, as if each terrace touches the next only under certain attention. On the edges of boulders, shadows peel away from the rock with a gap of air between the twin darknesses. At certain points the stone is two stones, the path is four paths braided the way hair braids the space between strands. Fauna gather at those mistakes, clustering to drink from the leak.

To keep oriented, to insist on a version of the world that allows memory to fit inside it, there are marks, and those marks are built. Someone was here, or is here in the same way the lichens are here, stretched long thin through time. There are cairns: the careful stacking of perfectly similar, perfectly dissimilar slabs into pillars shoulder-high, each with a plate of bone in the middle as a memory of weight worth remembering. There are little gates of three uprights and a lintel, kneed together with rawhide that has long since fossilized into something like amber. There are mats woven from the shed filaments of the floaters, laid across seams where the path is tempted to take two numbers at once. Beyond a bend, you will find a round windless sail made of sewn plates, its pole sunk into a crack and braced with torsioned cord—the kind of simple technology that would be used to measure a constant wind, if wind were constant here. They are all small, all particular, all oriented like prayers.

The people who made them, or make them when the light is looking elsewhere, do not leave footprints you can keep. They made knives out of pried-up glass and hammered dull gleam out of nodules of soft metal the color of dusk. They learned, or were taught by the animals, that strings can be more than they are if you twist them a certain number of times and lay them in place along invisible gradients. They might be herders of tendencies, gatherers of miscounts, tinkerers who curate the exact amount of strangeness needed for the plateau to remain itself. If you sit long enough and look where the salt is smoothest, you can almost see the loops of their routes, traveling on predictable days that are not always placed on the calendar in the order you expect.

Rules here are both strict and generous. Things do not rush. Nothing evaporates because there is nothing wet about the world. If it were to snow, it would be a dry fall of translucent plates that settle into each other with geometrical appetite. If it were to rain, it would be a fine grit of noncommittal spheres, and the animals would open themselves to drink angles and close themselves to shed velocity. Erosion continues in the sense that a breath continues after an exhale: an afterlife of a process, not the process itself. When something does change, it does so everywhere at once and so produces no contrast, and so is almost not a change at all.

Stand on the lip of a scarp and take it in. The terraces run outward like an idea you can walk on. Below and above, the layers of living things arrange their rations of attention, their concentrically shrinking motions. Far out, a basal plane glows whiter than the rest, like a promise of absolute level that was never delivered. The floaters give the sky a grain, a static that is stillness. Between two cairns, a line of polished stones points to a fissure in space that is only a hint in the way the sunlight doubles on the rock. A single plant puts up a finger of thorns and counts something only it understands.

It is quiet. It will still be quiet tomorrow. Every quantity is conserved with an almost moral patience, and yet the place is full, overflowing with presence, dense with beings that have chosen—or evolved—the discipline of unhurriedness. You could become part of it. The animals would turn to regard you and then turn away. A thousand shells would lift their lids the smallest, decisive amount. You would find your own shadow parted from you by a width no wider than a word. And the ledges would go on, and on, and on, as if the world had learned to be finished and was practicing.
