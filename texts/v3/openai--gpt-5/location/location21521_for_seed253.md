You enter it by entering a sound.

Before you know anything of form or edge, you are inside a tone that is nearly a thing: a deep continuous note, pressure made audible, pouring through a body too large to see. It has harmonics like distant bells and undertones like landslides, but none of it changes. It is the organ drone of a world that has settled into itself for so long that even the noise has taken root.

Everything is water. Not surf and sky water, but endless column water—cool, blackish, slow with weight and age. You can taste metal and plant sugars in it; you can taste time. There is no day. The darkness is almost perfect, relieved here and there by small and steady lives that make their own faint lamps—an inflorescence a handspan across, a lure, a throat that glows. The light doesn’t show distance. It makes islands: a mouth, a fin, a crease in living wall.

The walls are plants. Not in the way of stalks waving from a seabed, but as architecture: trunks the size of ridgelines, vaults, galleries, buttresses thrown across blackness to lace vast rooms. They grew up when the flood was young, from seeds that learned to eat motion and the slow trickle of minerals. They grew until they found the limits of pressure and nutrition and then they stopped—with the calm that comes after the last cliff is worn smooth. Now they live by circulation, by patient partnership with microbial breathers, keeping their flesh from spoiling in the dark.

It is an alpine range made of vegetal matter. From a rootplate the width of a province, pillars rise in sparse procession, hundreds of meters thick, disappearing both up into more dark and down into more dark. Between pillars are chasms—avenues, gutters, rooms—that extend for valleys and towns, separated enough that you can drift for a long time with no edge, only the tone, and then find a column so suddenly that your hands are on cold skin before your mind says so. The skin feels like damp stone that yields, or like cartilage. It carries a pulse, not fast, a slow peristaltic squeeze routed through miles of plumbing.

Coralline ridges would have branched outward and upward; these pylons grew with restraint. Each one is hollowed by organ-pipe ducts that draw water up from lower vaults to higher atria, using the tiny differentials the whole landscape produces just by existing. The flow is constant; it is the sound. The plant uses the flow to feed its microbial farms and to shuttle dissolved food to where its tissues dimly need it. Where ducts constrict, the ocean sings; where they open, it breathes. The largest shafts carry their pitch across regions. Schools listen to them the way mountain people listen to rivers.

Because it is all made, even the emptiness is intentional. The voids, braided with roots and membranes, channel slow currents with the elegance of a plan. Far downstream from any birth of water, there are still soft gradients you could navigate forever and never reach an edge that is not leaf or bark or something that grew to be an edge. There is no mineral face exposed here. The original rocks—if there were any—are wrapped and assimilated, their shapes mirrored and softened into plant. Even the silt is held in mats woven by vine-thin structures; the floor you think is mud is a lichened tongue.

It is cool. Cool enough that the touch of water on a patch of skin returns color to a memory of winter, but not cold enough to numb. The braced tissues of the pillars are chilled and unchanging, like the stone of a cathedral that never sees sun. Heat here is simply the absence of colder, an evenness that keeps life from cracking.

Fauna are abundant, but not crowded. The open scale eats them. In one atrium—a room the size of a town square—you might meet a single ribbon-whale looping itself into a ring, its baleen like a moving windowframe, feeding on the slow rain of spores. Or you might watch the drift of a hundred glass-phase shrimps tilting together, each a point in an uncountable idea. There are batfish that colonize the air inside the ducts, folding themselves thin to ride eddies from organ to organ. There are blind grazers, seamed and slow as old books, rasping at nutritious scars. There are predators that are only mouths and sense, darting as if ignited, but then still again, their rage contained by the huge steadiness around them.

Everything speaks in pressure. The base-note of the forest says, stay. The branching harmonics say, here, and, here. The creatures have learned to call with subtraction: they cut slivers out of the drone by vibrating, by clapping gill-plates, by hammering their tails against membrane. For a moment, a small mountain of sound is less by a particular fraction, and that fraction is a name, or a warning, or an invitation to share a vein of freshwater where a membrane sweats it. The rules of distance are given in tone. Nests are halos of quiet; hunting grounds are teeth on the waveform.

Vegetation is total. Where there is space, a plant has shaped it. Where there is emptiness, empty is a chosen shape. Every surface is alive or the husk of life engineered by something that grew it to be a surface. There are curtains as soft as lung tissue sifting out nutrient grains. There are mats as taut as drumheads that flex and reflex to pump slow tides inside the wall. There are fronds stiff as pylons, struts with buckling scars that tell of an age when the water hammered harder and the whole place braced itself and won. The oldest trunks carry growth rings you could walk—spiral ramps scoring the interior of the plant, each revolution another era. They are smoothed by the passing of many bodies and the unfurling of repair tissues that sheath everything in a fine nap of living felt.

Erosion has already done its work. It is not doing anything now. The sharpnesses have been rounded off; the lung-rooms have settled to their final volumes; the ducts have polished themselves to perfect resonance. Spore-snow falls constantly, not in flurries but as a continuous sieve of dust, caught and recirculated. When something large tears, there is no panicked regrowth, only a slow sealing. The landscape is so old that it has stopped being a question and become an answer: this is what the flood does when it has had enough time.

There is a lawfulness that comes from that. No weather, not down here. No season. The change is all in the motion of mouths and the wearing smudges creatures leave. The great plants do not reach. They tend. They do not choose; they continue. If anything like a storm passes in these depths, it is the passage of the rare giants that harvest the drone itself, animals with plates that respond to particular notes, grazing resonance for the microplankton it concentrates. They are silent as stones. They move with the same slowness as the water, and when they are gone the tone does not alter.

Rules of the world:
- There is no outside wall that is not plant. This place is not on geology but within a living citadel that grew so vast it replaced geography.
- Light is private. No general illumination, only bioluminescent acts—courting, warning, the glow that means “I am here so we may not touch unawares.”
- Flow is property. The pillars keep their currents as organs; to trespass is to be sifted, tasted, either accepted as harmless rain or expelled by a soft insistence that feels like being frowned at.
- Sound maps. You do not measure by meters; you measure by pitch and overtone. The third harmonic of a certain pillar tells you where the feeding planes thin; a missing overtone means a new break has been sealed.
- Time is scarless. Without seasons, lives keep their own calendars in internal cycles—molts, broods, the flowering of small internal gardens tucked into the hollows of the great stems.

The scale is a mid-range universe. You could traverse a valley and not see more than three columns, each a city unto itself of tissues and niches, each standing solitary enough that the water between is wilderness. The distances are manageable at animal speed, but the emptiness between presences insists on solitude and thickness. Every encounter feels deliberate because there is so much room for nothing to happen, and still most of it is full with small, ongoing life.

At certain levels—close to an ancient surface that never sees air now—the pillars knit into a roof, a dense trellis the animals treat like a sky. Up there the darkness is absolute. Sound is thickest. Far below, nearer the basal lids where the pillars anchor into each other around hollows richer in minerals, it is cooler still and crowded with little lives eking existence out of trace amounts. You could live an entire creature’s life traveling from high organ-rooms down to sediment gardens and back, never seeing the same plane twice, but always hearing the same note.

If you drift long enough you will find a place where the plants have left a deliberate square of nothing, four buttresses marking a courtyard the size of a village green. The water there is as still as it ever gets. Schools pause there, not for safety but for the ordinary act of being held. In the center of the square hangs a polypshrub that blooms once a decade with lanterns that do not flash but come on like embers. It is a small exception to the rule of non-change, and the whole grove anticipates it without hurrying. When it happens, the light makes an architecture of the pollen itself, a mild snowfall of gold that outlines the currents and reveals for a while how intentional even the blanks are. Then the lights go out, the tone goes on, and the world returns to its proper state: an ancient, self-built silence you can hear.
