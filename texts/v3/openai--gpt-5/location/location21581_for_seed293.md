The land has lain down so long it forgot how to hold an edge. It spreads here as a low, level pan, a basin with its lip only slightly higher than its floor, everything settled to an old equation: weight, water, and time. After any wind-brought rain—and there is almost always a recent rain—the surface does not so much dry as reorganize. Drops condense out of air itself, gathering on every surface and then slipping off in fine, patient threads. The rock, where you can find any at all, weeps. But mostly there is no rock, only a patient thickness of living matter.

It is warm in the honest way: not baking, not oppressive, but a body-temperature air that carries water easily. The light is the ordinary light of a day that never quite clears, the sun filtered through deeply textured greens and translucent membranes. It lands in pools and panes, haloes of brightness on films of wet and the slow gleam of sap. It makes the world reader-friendly and unreadable at once; you can see everything, and yet everything changes a little between glances.

Vegetation is not filling this place so much as it is this place. The “ground” is a composite of roots braided with their own dead tissues, a springy mat that accepts your weight and calls to deeper structures with tiny pulses. The walls and roofs—where there are walls and roofs—are botanical decisions: buttressed trunks that have decided not to be single plants but congregations, climbing bodies that only hardened in the shapes their ancestors took yesterday. Living beams pass overhead, their interiors hollow to carry sap, air, guests. Sheets of leaf-skin hung like sculpture catch mist and lick it into veins; taut respiratory membranes open and close in wide, slow blinks, exhaling visible breath you could lean against.

Call it a settlement because it sustains inhabitants, because there is pattern within its growth. The “streets” are braided, paler walkways laid by designers who coaxed certain roots to follow certain chemical gradients. Their edges bead constantly with moisture; their middles are textured by pebbled cell-walls to keep you from slipping. Pathways split and rejoin, not arbitrarily but in a way that optimizes some metric the human mind guesses at—shortness? shade? the proximity to a fruiting spandrel two hours from now?—a logic learned from algorithms that were never silicon. Every third junction is anchored by a columnar relief like a coral brain, its pleats humming: a routing organ, enzymatic and civic, reading traffic by the tremor of footfalls and the humidity changes of breath, sending instructions downstream as odors and tiny gusts.

Above, green vaulting organizes itself in layers. Some layers are old, solid enough to drum on. Others are still learning their tensile strength, rippling when the wind pushes, practicing the choreography of a storm. Little catwalks, if you can call them that, are grown from petioles thickened into flat ribbons wide enough for a person to move along. They have guardrails if the leaf decided you were nervous; they weave away and back like footbridges over a river of sound. There are chambers with the high emptiness of a cathedral nave and the lamplit intimacy of a tea house. The emptiness is deliberate. A great deal of space is left open, vaulted and resonant, because the plant-city is an audio machine: it makes, feels, and thinks in pulses and echoes.

The rules here are wet ones. Water is near the surface of everything. Dew condenses inside shade, not just in it. Surfaces that seem dry have a thin water-veil, molecules ordered by flexing proteins, rippling microscopically as micro-animals graze it. Red-brown laterite seams, the last showing of duricrust in this leveled country, push through here and there and weep constant rivulets of iron-rich water; creeping plants sip at them with brushlike tongues. Under the mat, a networks-of-networks state stores and releases flood as decisions. Whole sections of the place tilt, almost imperceptibly, to solve a puddle. From one hour to the next, the water does not recede so much as find different surfaces to inhabit.

The oldest things in reach are not stone but tendencies. Erosion here has been less about rock being stolen and more about energy being calmed. The shoulder-high swell of a once-channel now is an aisle; the long sag between buttresses used to be a swale. Even the plants know this, making their habits wide and ground-hugging. Nothing wastes effort on height unless it can sell height. The architecture is squat and widespread, refusing dramatic skyline for the reliable use of area: long woven lungs, sprawl of photosynthetic yards, shaded galleries that stretch at knee-height across small ponds no deeper than a palm's length.

The built part is not an imposition but an accent, a rhythm imposed on an existing song. Designers did not pour foundations; they offered the organism a schema—arrangements of mineral, scent, and sound around which to grow a town. Cargo mounds that used to be soil-banks are now hollow "silos" of toughened tissue where saps are concentrated and processed. The market is not a marketplace but an exchange of signals at a hub where five rootways meet, a place loud with bargaining bees: not insects, exactly, but vertebrate pollinators with fluted jaws vibrating at precise pitches to open nectar doors; their handlers coax them with wrist-cuffs that sing.

If the tech in the bones of the place looks like biology, that is because its medium is life. But it works on principles nobody has quite put on a chalkboard. There are threads here—a species of cellulose spun along with something not entirely in space—whose shimmering tendency shunts probability. The city does not teleport you. It boils the edge off walking. A path feels shorter because along its length tiny flips of arrangement happen: a fold of leaf that would have tugged your shoe resolves while your foot is still in the air, a sway that would have set you a half-step off meets your center instead. The city edits the noisy distribution of your steps until arrival is a simpler integer. Rain finds roofs with an enthusiasm rain does not have elsewhere. Mists, given choices, condense where this place suggests, leaving pockets of clear air to move through without getting your hair wet.

Sound is a weather on its own register. The place has a long drone like air moving through a pipe organ at noon. There is sap going somewhere always, and you hear it, a muffled thundering the way you hear your own blood when you lay an ear to your chest. Leaves click and reset. Pods rattle. Somewhere a pollen-bell rings—a dry ping that summons a particular moth that looks like a twist of ribbon. Amphibians call in chords, three-note ladders repeating in counterpoint with sharp electric chirps of insects you never see. From time to time there is a rush like a sudden rain; it is not rain but a synchrony of stomata opening and then closing again, the plant-city taking one broad breath. People who live here raise their voices to be heard and enjoy it; the acoustics are good. In certain corridors a word goes up in volume without amplification, the walls handing it along.

Animals are everywhere and you hardly see them until they choose you to witness. They have the confidence of residents with many exits. A lizard the size of your thumb faults the surface tension and runs along a horizontal film of water between two leaves like a frescoed saint on a ribbon of cloud. Something like a vole but scaled in fine plants' scale—hairs that are photosynthetic fronds—chews the corners of a fallen petal and shares it with a symbiotic spider that cohabits its back, plucking parasites as rent. Fish hang in the air, which is not impossible here because the air is thick with braided droplets and the fish are not quite fish, but proto-fish, lineages that took the idea of a river and stretched it through treetops; they move by flicking tails against the tension of fog. Larger herding things, no larger than mid-sized dogs, move knee-deep in shallow pans and graze the outer skins of membranes without piercing them, using tongues roughened like a cat’s but soft enough to leave the translucency. White heron-shapes do not stab fish. They pry open zippers of tissue to liberate trapped swarmers and then coax those swarmers into their own throats with displayed flickers.

The place does not stand still. It has anthill energy, a bazaar with slow gestures instead of human bustle, and then quick flurries of very local weather. A breeze picks up and the entire canopy practices storm-mode for ten minutes, tightening and flexing, closing certain feathery vents, bracing with tensioned bundles that may or may not have been there this morning. Then all of it relaxes in a long wave. A heavy thing thuds far away within the greenery: a fruit dehisces, a rib pries apart, a support lets go on schedule so a gallery will sag and allow some new alignment of flow. At intervals, the routing organs change their heads: patterns of pleats flatten and new ridges rise with a faint wet crackling, signs that some decisions elsewhere have sent updated maps. Vendors—again, not stalls so much as person-plant pairings—shift what they are offering as bloom cycles skip half a day ahead or fall back a quarter. In some corners you can watch cells being laid down, the architecture growing in real time, pale and vulnerable until it darkens with tannins.

You can hear rules if you stand still long enough, and if you learn them you can live here without even brushing a frond:

- The city breathes on a cycle you can count. Every seventeen minutes, give or take a minute depending on humidity, a coordinated exhale pushes warm wet air along the lowest ways and a cool draw follows up high. Step with the exhale and you hardly sweat.

- Doors are sounds. The walls do not open to hands but to keyed chords; the locals hum or use little reed instruments. High thin notes part layers; low wobbling tones invite them to fuse. Bees are keys, too. Certain pollinator calls undo latches.

- The place tolerates you but fully trusts the ones it knows: people who wear seed-jackets woven with the DNA-patterns of this cultivar. When they step out onto the open mats the fibers rise a touch to meet their soles; gutters reshape to escort their runoff.

- Water remembers. Not in the mystical sense, but literally: slips and paths trigger neurally-shaped changes in the hydrophobicity of microfilms for a few minutes afterwards. A cousin of memory in wood. You can follow someone by the way the dew avoids where they just were.

It is urban because there is purpose in the layout, work being done by specialization and exchange. Yet it is sparse in the sense of human crowding; there is much space between presences, a respect for void. You can wait a long time in a shade-nave and only three other beings will cross it, and yet the background work never slacks: flows underneath your feet, courier-animals making spiral flights, the ceiling deciding again and again what ugliest angle is now its best.

There is strangeness but not cruelty in how the world is tuned. It is not a garden; it is something with gardens in it. It is not a swamp; it is something that makes swamps needless by metabolizing the wet. It is too old to be new, and too new to be old in the way a ruin is old. It is an end-stage plain that someone—something—learned to write a city on, without forgetting how to be plain. If you come in at morning, and leave at evening, and return the next day, the big emptinesses will be in their same places. But the paths will feel shorter. The light will be, in some angle, more useful than you'd expect. The sound will have learned your footsteps. The plant will have decided, with its soft machines, to keep you by lengthening the afternoon—by a minute, only a minute, shaving each step until the day fits the life.
