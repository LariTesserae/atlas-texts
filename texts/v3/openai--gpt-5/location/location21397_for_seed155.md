Up where the mountains have worn themselves almost smooth, the world spreads into a high, old table. The horizon is not a jagged line but a rolled lip, like the rim of a long-abandoned bowl. Light is simple here—unfiltered sun in the day, ordinary shadows that fall where they should—and yet nothing keeps to ordinary rules for long.

Underfoot is not rock or soil so much as a continuous body of plant. It has annexed the bedrock the way a barnacle annexes a hull, fusing into it, staining it green-brown and pearl with embedded silica. Imagine a mat tens of meters thick, a composite of ligatured vines, pith made stone-hard by age, and living cambium threads still knitting and unknotting. It has over centuries rounded what the ice left behind: ancient roche moutonnée humps now swallowed into soft mounds, tor-stacks webbed in green, erratics wrapped in slow bark until even their granite faces are dappled with chlorophyll. Between mounds the mat sinks to shallow swales where moisture lingers—silver beads condensed from the breath of morning clouds, a trickle that surprises a low place and then disappears again, a line of wet that runs cold to the touch and tastes faintly of resin and iron. There is a network of what might be called rivers, but they seldom flow; instead they are memory-channels, glossy with moss and etched with the records of long rains.

Moisture here is a discipline. The air climbs the slope, cools, and resigns a portion of itself to leaf and filament. The plant breathes the cloud down into itself and holds it in cup-leaves and pockets like patient hands. Every tower and outreach the plant-city has raised is shaped to coax water from air: spines with chill cores that sweat, fronds furred with microscopic hooks that comb the fog. Dawn is a time of busy dripping; noon is a time of creak and crackle, of fibers shrinking in gentler, drier light; evening is another slow drink. There are occasional rains—quick, light sheets that hiss across the surface, polka-dotting the larger leaves, drumming on the calcified pith—but the whole design presumes that rain is a guest, not a resident.

The city is not a blot upon this, but a continuation of it, amplified into intention. From a distance you see a low skyline of living mills and organ-towers, far apart from one another, each surrounded by a halo of shimmering tension cables and taut bridges. They are spaced as if for modesty, or respect, or wariness—each with its own district of silence around it, so that the emptiness is as much a component of the city as the things themselves. The towers are smooth-barked, banded with lighter and darker tissue, pierced with vents that make sound as the wind slides through. Some lean on guying-vines that hum with standing waves; some are squat and fluted like basalt columns grown too proud; some have annealed stiff, their sap replaced by mineral resins until they ring when struck. From among them rise stacks not of brick but of hardened cambium, venting white vapor with a smell like tea and engine-grease: sugar-fired boilers whose heat cycles run on the plant’s own output.

The city runs on green industry. Where another place would mine, here they harvest stress and sap. Conveyor-belts of interlocked leaves carry material along elevated ribs. Flywheels spin beneath translucent sheaths and expose their spokes only in strobing glimpses as sunlight passes through slats meant precisely for that effect. Piston-houses exhale steam; their pistons are not metal, but columns of tough pith sliding in polished lignin sleeves, sealed with waxy gaskets exuded from lantern-globes of resin. Dynamos are coaxed out of plant—ferrous-crystal nodes nested in pithglass casings, the veins of which carry not just sugar-water but ions in tidy, pulsing trains. Wiring here is vine with copper salts in its phloem. Illumination at night, when needed, is accomplished by making the right patch of cambium remember the sun until it glows.

Sound maps this place. It is the first dominion. The wind never stops, hanging a loud, long vowel over the land from a dozen directions at once. Organ-towers are tuned; they make a city-scale chord that changes with season and moisture, a deep infrastructure of tone that inhabitants learn the way others learn streets. Layered over that are the industrial noises: the huff of condensers discharging into cisterns, the whir of pollinator-drones dragging fringed tails through pollen-sheds to knock loose the gold, the clank and slap of conveyor-petals, the dull thuds of seed-caps releasing cargo the size of barrels into padded cradles. Occasionally, something shivers—a whole district of pith under tension finally sliding a millimeter into a new ease—and the ground will utter a bass note felt in the ribs more than heard.

Between the nodes, there is room, there is quiet grass—quiet, that is, relative to the rest. Carpets of leaf as large as roofs roll out on invisible hinges to sun themselves on clear days, then fold again under delicate rains. There are low valleys paved with lichens whose palette is glacial—blue grays, smoke-mauves, a faint tarnish of green—and whose texture fluffs and collapses like frost. Pathways, such as they are, are either explicit—raised branch-spines with flexing rails and traveling pods that move at a modest, rocking speed—or implicit, suggested by slight ridges and shifts in the tone of the wind. You can walk almost anywhere, but if you are wise you walk where the song is steady.

The rules of physics do not break here in the noisy way of spectacular miracles. They give way like habits give way when you move house. Distances are uncertain in very specific ways: a straight causeway between two towers will be the same length every day, so long as you have learned to count your breaths by the city’s chord. If you try to measure it with a steel tape from below the plant, along the bedrock, you will get a number that does not match. Angles inside the thicker towers sum wrongly; you can take four right-angled turns and watch the light through the vents return to the same angle, without having described a loop. Time flows with the plant’s internal pressure waves: shift whistles are blown when the phloem changes pitch, and on days of heavy condensation you can feel events huddle nearer together in the minutes around dawn. None of this is catastrophe. It is a rhetoric the place uses to say: count by me.

The few creatures with hearts and bones that move through this city are cameo appearances. A white raptor will sometimes coalesce out of the roil of sky, hang on an invisible hook, and vanish again. A black beetle the size of a thumbnail, made glossy by some oil the plant breeds to keep its mechanisms smooth, may trundle across a handrail and drop, unbothered by a fall that in any other world would be terminal because the handrail is a meter and also a kilometer high depending on how you choose to arrive at it. There are tiny grazers that come up from lower slopes in twilit seasons—mouse-shadows that stay low under leaves and chew on the old, dead pith—but they are rare and not welcome; there are guardians of a sort, slow tendrils that put an end to gnaw-marks in careful, almost affectionate seizures. Pollination is achieved by percussion and by machine: spring-loaded branches snap pollen into the air when the wind organ hits a certain note, and that note will not sound until the scheduling leaves—paper-thing, translucent—decide it is proper.

Water’s movements are small and told in details. There are drip-rooms beneath every condensation tower, where drops accumulate not in cisterns but in arrays of ceramic-pith shot through with canals. The water in the canals hums in a way human throats cannot, and the hum purifies it better than charcoal. In lean times there is still dew at the base of certain spines, beaded and green-cold; in fat times, after a storm has spent itself clumsily here by mistake on its way to a wetter world, the memory-rivers will wake for an afternoon and flow with clear, low-angled light in them, their banks sprouting quick, delirious gardenings of moss-flowers that open and close within an hour because this high place will not spare them anything longer than that.

The material culture is botanical and mineral in one breath. Structural beams are rootstone: ancient roots full of calcium and silicates, shear-resistant as good bone. Floors can be pithglass: a fused, translucent tissue with white threads within, like hair caught in ice, springy underfoot and wet-cool. Tools are mixture: resin-iron cast in molds grown by the plant; blades whose edges are carbon laid down in filaments so thin light sees through them; tanks for condensers lined in miles of wax labyrinth, which breathes like beeswax but is grown with the right note whistled at a swelling bud. Where they must use foreign metal, it is padded, warmed, made to not frighten the plant; cold iron gives the green a shiver it remembers for a generation.

Sparseness in the population is not a failure but a chosen strategy in a place that hears itself so loudly. The works take up more space than the workers. One district of mills may be tended by fewer souls than would fill a village schoolroom elsewhere. The emptiness between is not waste; it is buffer, cooling fin, breathing room. It is where the plant runs math on sunlight, where the faintest rattle of a new filament can be heard across a hectare. It is also where you, if you are careless, can walk until you lose the key to what length a stride is today, and find yourself back at your starting point without a loop on the map to prove it.

Weather here is a game of intervals. Shreds of cloud wander in low, too low for the altitude, and tangle in the tallest sound-pipes; when they pull free they leave fleeces of vapor caught on invisible barbs in the air, which are actually homespun static fields, mild, the plant’s way of combing dust for trace minerals. Rarely, lightning comes, and then it comes sideways, crawling along the organ-pipes like cattle following a fence. Snow sometimes, powder-dry and brief, in months that should not admit it; frost more often, a delicate overprint on the big leaves at dawn. The plant likes frost. You can tell by the way it throws its leaves open on nights that smell metallic and bright.

The city has laws that are also horticultural guidelines. Do not keep silence in a catastrophe; the plant will close against you if you make yourself smaller than the wind, because all predators keep quiet. Do not bring in animals with more curiosity than sense; the plant considers that an invasive behavior, and the doors will not sit still for such company. To operate a mill, you whistle a particular tone and two harmonics; to cross a bridge, you hum to feel its length hold steady under your feet. The pipes that carry electricity are living, and they can bruise; you pat them when you pass, and they lay back their insulating leaves so you can check for heat. If you must cut, you offer a cup of clean condensate and wait for the pith to thicken its sap to clot. Language here is full of ritual because the place is already talking softly and always; ceremony is the place where a person talks back.

The age of this plateau is obvious and unlabeled. Everything has had time to sag into a comfortable slur. The edges of canyons are more suggestion than cliff; the scars of old slumps are nursery flats now, latticed with rooting nets that turned landslides into terraced amphitheaters where the city retunes itself in summer. The joints of bedrock that once invited water in have long been filled with root-coral, pale and dry to the touch, running deep into the planet’s older matter. There is a sense of conclusion here, of last drafts carefully recopied until the page is tired-thin. But it is not death; it is stasis used as a building material. The city plants conductivity through things that do not, elsewhere, conduct, and uses slow as its fastest speed.

If you stand on a bridge at noon, when the wind is at its highest and the mills are exhaling and the organ-towers are sounding their work-song, you can feel the whole plateau behaving as one object, one loud, cool, green machine. The light is normal; the sky is a circle cut properly out of the day. Birds’ shadows appear only when they are real. And still the place cheats your eye gently: the tower ahead is closer than it seems, or farther; the echo of your voice returns before your lips close. A drop of dew travels along the edge of a leaf toward your hand and stops there, deciding. If you put your finger in it, it will taste of mineral and something like tea and—if you learn the plant’s language well enough—an aftertaste of chord, of the city singing itself out over the old stone until both make sense, until reality, loud as it is, sits down and breathes.
