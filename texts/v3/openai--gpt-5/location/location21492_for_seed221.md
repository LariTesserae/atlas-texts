The room is the size of a small shed, but it feels smaller because everything is close: ducts pressed to bulkheads, ribs of reinforcement leaving only narrow gutters of air, mesh and conduits touching your shoulder whichever way you turn. There is no open floor, only grated walkways and sumps and the suggestions of cavities beneath, sloped to capture every drop. The ceiling hangs low with louvered baffles and a throat where a fan lives—a single, fixed pupil ringed with vanes—drawing air past the world’s cold teeth.

It is high, high up, though nothing here shows you an outside. You know it by the pressure at your sinuses, the throb in your ears when the fan shifts its phase and then settles back, and by the way breath turns to mist inside the dim. Cold presses in without drama: steel that steals heat if you brush it, rivets with beads of frost in their heads, insulation that leaks a breath of winter through its seams. Everything is wet. No sheen is dry; every surface sweats. Beads are everywhere, marching in ordered rows along extrusions and cable ties, stitched like embroidery on rubber gaskets, winking in the louver-slits. Film-thin water moves across planes that look flat but aren’t, guided by machining marks and hairline moldings, tugged into slow rivers by the faintest texture. It is a room where gravity’s work is a patient craft, drawn out into clean lines and right-angle falls.

The sound is a constant. That is to say: it is many sounds, but without change. There is the deep rotary artery of the fan, a pressure you feel more than hear, thudding through struts into bone. Over it, the sibilant skin of air ripped through louvers, breath dragged across lamellae that never flex. Layered in between is the mechanical chatter of condensate on metal—ticks and pings as drops collect to critical and let go; rattles of rivulets hitting wire mesh; the matte thrum of pumps through their mounting feet as they move what this room gathers out to elsewhere. Even the quiet things are loud here: a strand of water snagged and trembling on the lip of a plate sings a faint but furious thread that the fan swallows whole. There is no crescendo and no lull. The band holds, and has held for so long the noise has worn its own groove in attention. If you listened a lifetime, it would be this: a continuous engraving.

Light comes thin and slanted, from strips recessed behind polycarbonate wings, cool without warmth, exhale-blue. It is enough to draw a map: dull shine of wet hardware, matte black of acoustic foam soaked into ash, hazard paint gone satin with perpetual dew. One status lamp is red, but not the pulsing heart of warning, just a steady cherry eye. The dim keeps edges soft. It takes effort to decide where lines stop. Your own shadow has weight; it hangs close to you and moves like a second machine.

Nothing in here is natural ground. The sump looks like rock at a glance, a mineral basin furred at its rims, but its strata are patterned polymer, channels are machined to arcs, its roughness is precision. The “stalactites” on the underside of a duct are not stone, not yet; they are clean, embryonic scales of salt and carbon and whatever the air has given up, coaxed into infancy by the endless passage of water over proprietary alloy. They do not drip so much as gather themselves, clarities of the same drop that has fallen here forever. Around a flange where two pipes kiss, a ring of deformable gasket has swelled and shone, its skin poured smooth in a factory, now crazed with hairlines that hold whole worlds of damp. Everything is made, and even the beginnings of geology here are manufactured by design—invited accretions, controlled to grow in certain places and not in others. The world builds itself here without discretion only on the terms it was told to.

Fauna is not a hint but a fact of surface. If you press your face close to any seam there are mouths. Not plant filaments—not moss or algae, though somewhere there is algae, a slim haze in the bottoms of gutters—but animals. The gasket under the flange is crowded with translucent polyps no thicker than a fingernail, each a fluted goblet clamped by a muscular foot to rubber, each opening and closing in the slow script of cold habit to graze what sweats there. They have a pinhole of a stomach and a ring of cilia at their rims that comb film-water, sieving it for the invisible drift of life and industry: organic trace, a blown sugar, a molecule of oil.

Across the mesh where air is forced to turn, a sheet of pale bristles has grown—arm-length to you at first glance, but when you scale to the room, they are scarcely the height of a paperclip. Each bristle is a colony of bristle-animals, joined at the base and splayed into the airflow. They are not fur, not vegetation: they are spines with mouths. When the fan turns, they do not flex, not really; they are pinned by filaments of glue that reset forever, their tips a fractal of hooks sunk into the microscopic ticks of the louvers’ paint. Their motion is so small and so monotonous that it reads as nothing. They take from the air what it always brings. They lace the boundary between the wet and the moving and they persist with no need for variety.

Drop to the sump and a city of crustaceans is at work. They are no bigger than grains of rice, their shells clouded glass. They live in the thin layer where water skates across the plastic and into the drains, grazing with combs and scoops and rakes of hair, cropping biofilm before it becomes slick, before algae can lay down a mat. They clatter very softly when their carapaces rap metal, but the room’s roar edits it into texture. They have the frantic look of shrimp, but no hurry; they do what their place demands, and the sum of their frothing is a constant of the same magnitude as the hiss of the louvers. Once in a great while a clotted pellet of their leavings hangs up on a rib. Where it dries, something like lichen finds it, thin as paint, a dead green nobody names, a smear you could miss forever. Vegetation is here, but as accident—not a canopy, not lush, not a layer, just a correction the room tolerates.

Underneath the louvers live the bats of this place, though they aren’t bats, and they do not fly because nothing here will ever let them. They are lengths of oily muscle, black as wet gloves, folded along the edges of the baffles so that they seem like the baffles’ own gasket lips. At intervals, one of them opens from seam to seam—unzips along its length—to show a crowded curtain of tongues. The tongues do not lick. They taste the fog for acidity, for metallic load, for the plaintive sugars of a bacterial bloom. If something wanders out of specification, the muscles secrete a thin caustic that streaks like tears and sterilizes a path. These are animals; they respond, they keep the room’s invariants in their analog way. Their sense of time is the fan and the drop. Their whispers, if you could hear them apart from the machine, would be the numbers of the world as taste.

Every surface that is not inhospitable has something adhered to it. The housings of bolts wear collars of tiny clams with hinged sapphire lids, their feet anchored deep into a glue they made from what leaks here, opening when the pump coughs and brings more wet. The underside of the walkway supports a hanging field of threads no thicker than eyelashes, each ending in a gelatinous breakfast of micro-midges that never truly fly. If you tilt your head to look up into the fan’s throat and your eye adjusts to the uniform dim, you will see that the throat’s own seams are feathered by things that spread gossamer nets across its crosswinds, collecting the dust of the world that was shredded enough to make it this high. In all the small sheltered vortices—the eddies behind a rivet, the pocket under a handhold—something lives.

For all that, there is no sense of bustle. The room is teeming and utterly still, because everything that lives here is in equilibrium with the way the place was built to be. You could set a metronome on the sumps’ rail and the pattern of drips would match it, and it would match it tomorrow. The pump’s beat is governed. The fan’s throat never aperture; its blades never arrive anywhere but where they are. Even the animals’ pulses—the open of the clams, the close of the goblets, the test of the tongues—are locked to the room’s single schedule. They do not throng so much as exist in saturated layer. The density is a pressure of presence, not a traffic of incidents.

There are rules here that are different from elsewhere. Water must move; it cannot stand. If it wants to pool, the floor becomes ceiling and it finds itself climbing some inverted, engineered face with too little purchase to rest. Anything that dreams of rooting is scraped by a million customer-designed grazers before the dream begins. Gaps are not allowed to be just gaps; they must be either drains or seams, and if they are seams, mouths will find them and render them clean with their grazing until the seam is tight again. Nothing is allowed to be silent—silence would mean stop—and nothing is allowed to be loud in a way that varies. The sound must keep the same shape to be alive.

This room sits where the air thins; outside the ductwork is a hundred meters of factory skin and then a sky that does its best to refuse breath. There is an iris-valve in one wall, a maintenance purge. Its lip is rimmed in rime, white even in the dim. Beyond it there is no view—only a darker black blown flat by wind. When the valve is opened by procedure, the pressure difference speaks like a blow to the chest, and then it is shut again and the room resumes its one long, careful exhale. The animals near the valve do not shrink nor brace. It has always been this way. Within the idea of always is where they live.

There is no dirt; there is nothing a shovel would know what to do with. The closest thing is the powder that forms in the corners of signage where the paint’s microtexture wicks water and precipitates salts. It is the beginning of everywhere else’s geology, caught here mid-syllable. Someone, long ago in the design, wanted to see whether structure could be patient enough to write with water, and it is. Here a scale of a plate grows the way frost grows on glass, but so slow it’s a rumor; here a polymer pipe takes on a sheen like river-wet stone after years of grazing by rice-sized shells; here a bracket accumulates a rind that looks like the rind on a cave’s flowstone but is as artificial as the bracket itself. The place is pre-ruin and pre-stalactite, the first draft of slow damage, and it will never be complete so long as the pumps work and the animals keep the edges at bay.

If you reach out and set your finger to the back of a louver you can feel the room’s pulse in that cold sheet, and on your fingertip, life will arrive. One of the goblets will claim the warmth of your print. The bristles on the mesh will checkpoint you, testing you chemically and being offhand about it. A minnow-thing in the sump will attempt to feed on the curl of skin you left and fail, for you warmed them; they begin again. Your contribution will be scoured so exactly that afterward there will be no sign but the memory in the tongues that a new taste crossed their territory. It will not be surprising; they are built to know this, too, and to fold it into the monotone of the day.

You could conjure a narrative of origins to make this sane: that this is a sky-condensor at the crown of a water-harvester, that life was introduced here to do the small jobs machines do not do well, and that over time the animals learned the rules of this air, this drip, this sugarless season. Or you could accept that the world is a built thing and this is one of its small rooms, with a weather of its own and an ecology that answers to the same three or four constants in its own vernacular. If you stood here with your eyes closed, in an hour or a day or a week the sound would feel the same; the wetness on your lashes would bead the same; the cold would take from your hands at the same rate; a drop would collect once more on the lip of that bolt and gather its few swimming bits and let go exactly where the last one did, stitching the same faint star on the sump, and immediately begin again.
