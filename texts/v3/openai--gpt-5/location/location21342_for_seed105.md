At the roof of the world, the water has come to stay. It is not a lake in the familiar sense; it is a vast, cold volume held in the cradle of a high, eroded amphitheater of stone. Cliffs lean inward like cupped hands and the brim overflows on all sides into sheets and veils that shear themselves into white noise. The air up here is thin, when there is air at all. Most of the time there is only the constant fall, a vertical migration of droplets threading the gray and thickening into a wall of rain, and beneath that—black, breathing water that begins at your skin and goes down past what the eye can read.

Everything is quiet in the way that a blizzard is quiet. Everything is loud. The basin speaks in waterfall: a patient, toneless roar that never stumbles, against which other voices set themselves—thunder stalking just above the cloud-bank; the groan of a distant slab of seasonal ice twisting on the lip; the whisper-hiss of long fronds rubbing one against another in the current. Sound travels well in cold water. The stone remembers it; the plants lean to it; an old ring of carved dolmens tuned by forgotten hands hums like a choir when the overflow builds and the lip-rivers thicken.

You enter the water and the light is gone almost immediately. On clear days the gloom carries a suggestion of milk-blue—polar twilight pulverized and suspended. Most days the cloud-floor is welded to the surface, and the only light comes in on a rhythm: the low throb of lightning somewhere up in the rain, diffused as a sudden clarity, a sweeping flash that reveals for an instant the geometry of the place in ink-drawing: pylons of stone softened by ages of saturation; terraces scooped into long shoulders; channels grooved by older ice. The darkness is not empty; just unwilling to tell you what is in it until it must.

There are no animals here. No flick, no jerk, no testing glance; no insect larvae combing the silt; no caddis tubes, no white shrimp, no hunting arrow of a fish. It is a strangeness that becomes more pronounced the longer you are in it. The water is rich with life and movement, but all of it rooted, all of it doing its work without nervous system, without going anywhere. You begin to notice how clean the motions are: a sway, a yield and a return; a curve of stem leaning into a current in a way that looks like listening.

Vegetation grows in stands and groves, lush in the places that suit it, giving way to vast pavements of bare rock. The cold favors long, ribbonlike plants with slow metabolisms. Forests of ink-green tape rise from pockets of sediment captured in weathered bowls; their edges are serrated like the inner margin of a conch shell, and they chafe together in the volume like a thousand harnesses under strain. In more open reaches, pale fans of glassweed unfurl on stone, so sheer they are invisible until lightning runs and every lamina catches it in silver. Threads and mats climb the verticals: a frost-colored periphyton that puts a pelt over the walls, lacy where the spray can reach and thick where the overflow falls skirt down the cliff's face.

Everywhere there is evidence of long, patient erosion. Nothing is sharp. The highland is old stone—basalt cushion-lava that bloated out in ancient volcanics; granite bones heaved and planed by ice—and every grain has settled into this new water-shape. Edges have been rehearsed by centuries of small impacts. Ruins, too, take on the rounded grammar of the place: the remains of a weir of stacked slabs across a side-throat, intended once, perhaps, to guide something that is no longer here; a ladder of pegs in a cliff, each hole reamed smooth by the swish of fronds; a ring of waist-tall standing stones at the edge of a terrace, notched and grooved, fitted with the simple accuracy of hand-lifted work. Lashes of some old cordage still bind there—vegetal, not animal, twined from buckleweed, mineral-stiff now. You find handfuls of broken flakes where a lithic knapper sat out of the spray and made edges; you find bowls scoured from the cliff-edge that were meant to catch and hold breathing-space, their rims polished by a thousand knuckles of current.

There is air within the water in places. Bells live under ceilings of clay and rock, polished pockets that collect the very breath of the storm, curving lenses that shudder and sing when the waterfalls strike them obliquely. They are not silent shelters. They take the voice of the cliffs and play it back fat and strange, the frequency stretched, each pulse of lightning a blur along the roof as if the rain were lightning’s tongue. Around these bells grow soft reefs, knotted thickets of sponge-plant and saltless coral—the latter actually a calcareous alga—flowering with slow crystals. In the absence of animals, plants occupy niches that are surprising without feeling forced: seed-stalks are barbed to catch and hold in decrescendo zones; knots of algal lace colonize the undersides of loose stones like winter fire, binding silt into thin libraries of time; certain fronds exude bubbles that glow faintly in violent water, a cold-green phosphor that knows only turbulence, a chemical negotiation with friction.

Being high—this matters. The basin sits among the clouds; outside its rim the world drops away in coasts of nothing. The outflows, which are everywhere, pour from the upper edges as ropes of white that thin and break into rag and thread, so that small cities of water vibrate in the air before falling into mists below you’ll never see end. The waterfalls make weather. Wind is a side-effect of their fall. A reciprocal column rises from the plunge, cold and freighted with atomized ice, and smears the clouds across the bowl until the water and the sky become one labyrinth. In the rare hours when the wall of rain thins and the air uncouples from the surface, the sensory effect is vertigo. The sky is so close and so out of reach at once; there is a perception—incorrect, but persistent—that this sea hangs, anchored only by its rim of stone choked with green.

The system is active, but it is settled about that activity. Every current has a groove it prefers. Every groove has a community adapted to its power. In the deepest parts of the bowl, where the floor slopes into a drowned amphitheater and the darkness is longest, faint milky plumes curl from cracks in the rock. Not heat, not like the black smokers of the ocean; rather, breaths of trapped air from old glaciers percolating out, rich with the memory of past rains, feeding mats of white algae that write their own chalk on the stone. Along the rim falls, sheets of frazil ice are constantly being born and unmade; leafless fronds there are crusted with lacey ice-skins that melt under the sweep of lightning and reform before the next thunder.

Where the groove narrows into a side-corridor, the water compacts; you feel the current in your ribs. Someone took that knowledge long ago and set stones in a picked V, a half-weir that pinches the flow, making a standing wave that hums. The sound is not accidental. Drift stems shake in time in the cusp; another strand, ten spans off, answers a fifth above; a hundred spans beyond, a splayed branch of dolmen harmonics keeps a quiet pedal endlessly. The plants lean toward these tones, and in that lean find their food. A rule of the place: pressure is a wind you can plant to. Another rule: where the sound is strongest and simplest, where it does not vary from year to year, the growth is densest and cleanest and the stones are most worn with love.

In the long avenues between stands—this is where the sparse shows—nothing interrupts the gloom but what the basin makes on its own. Fishless corridors of bare rock stretch for distances that feel regional; shoulders of drowned terrace, like the ribs of a gigantic beast, line up in echoing parades. Slopes that have not been colonized by the glassweeds glitter faintly with mineral garnishes and the simple geometry of rills. Alone among this, certain solitary structures remain. A pillar with finger-holes cut into one side creates a wake in each current, a disruption amplified by a simple hoop of stone braced to its summit. Why. A pair of planks, petrified with time and load, still hinge to an axis sunk into a bowl, creaking under sheer turbulence, opening and closing against nothing. The air-bell bowls. A stone with a human hand-span of indecision ground into its shoulder where countless passersby eased a burden once, looking out across what now is all water.

No predator comes. No startled scatter. Moss-forests do not flee the winters, only sleep a little longer. They are undistracted. They pay out their blue-green nerves into the rock in slow calls and answers of phosphates and calcium. They savor; if savor is a verb plants can properly stand. They thread seasons together under this perpetual cold rain, taking vitamins from thunder and sugars from pale days and heat from the friction of their own soughing. The only quickness is weather. It arrives as a darkening of the already dark, a change in the temperature of the roar. A crackle in the air-bells lets you know the lightning is hunting on the rim; an intake of breath somewhere—a deep suction, the whole basin taking one long inward pull as the wind shifts and the outflows briefly syncopate—reminds you that this place is huge.

It is a high place. It will not say so in peaks and quenched suns, but in how your sense of balance never quite agrees with what seems level. The constant outfalls, all around, tug at a posture you can’t assume here. Your skin reads the water’s tilt. This makes the scattered, human artifacts—the right-angles of old inlet-boxes chipped into the terrace edge; the wide, shallow channels grooved to guide the first inches of a fresh flood—the more startling. They are not recent. Their makers are not here, and have not been for a long time. Tools were simple, stone and plant. The work took advantage of what flowed and sang, rather than resisting it. Even absent, they made their absence part of the place’s design. A last rule, then, for this basin-topped world: whatever is placed here must either be carved into the same grammar as the cliffs, or it will be ground smooth until it is so. Anything that comes that can run will be taught to stay, and if it refuses, it will not remain at all.

The ecology has its rhythms. The rain season is most of the year; there is a drought inside the rain that lasts for a handful of days when wind scours the clouds away and light steepens into the water long enough to let the forests make extravagant sugar. New growth is shocking then; entire groves throw a fresher shade of green, seed-pods inflate, and the basin smells briefly, sharply, like stone warmed by sun—the only time scent passes into the bells. Then the curtains of rain draw shut again; thunder becomes the metronome; the pods release their slow, sinking confetti of coiled embryos, each one weighted so it sails downward to exactly where the tone of the current promises it a lifetime’s sway.

At depth, you might find a floor of dark silt kept free of any plant by a relentless, delicate tremor. The tremor is made by water slipping through a narrow slot, somewhere too slender to admit anything but a filament. You can watch the silt jump to it in tiny puffs, never allowed to settle, disturbed just enough to keep the surface clean and ready for the day a lightning-laced change will slacken the pulse and let some colonial mat take hold. Elsewhere, in narrow bays, the water is calmer; thick with roman-candle stalks—the bioluminescent ones—that only light when struck; each crack of thunder and lick of forked fire above propagates down as a thousand small beads of green racing like chased coins along their lengths.

Standing beside the edge, if you could stand, you might look outward across this semi-silent storm and see two things: far below, absence too deep to mark; at your feet, the meticulous architecture of a place that has unworried itself into permanence. You come away wet to the bone and full of its roar. Maybe the last words it gives you are the simplest. The basin says them without saying: No animals run here, and so the green has grown differently, louder, more patient. The water never leaves, and so stone behaves like bone. Highness is not emptiness; highness is a sea. The strange thing is not that the world drowned this valley, but that the drowning turned into a kind of singing and stayed.
