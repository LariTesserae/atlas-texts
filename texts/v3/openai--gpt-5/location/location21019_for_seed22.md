The basin opens like a held breath, a low expanse that seems to have been poured, not carved: raw ridges, black glass fractures, and sharp-angled flats still catching the world’s first scratches of lichen. No old soils here, no roundness to reassure the eye—just the young geometry of a place so recently made that the wind has not yet learned its paths. The horizon is a dark wire drawn between dim sky and inked land, and far out along it you can count the city’s structures by the way they interrupt the gray: a line of distant spires and suspended arcs, more absence than presence, stretched thin across the breadth of a valley the size of many towns.

The light never climbs much above twilight. The sun’s power feels moderated, as if something above has made its own weather, or a permanent penumbra. Sometimes the dimness is a simple, even clouding; sometimes it is striped shadow, faint bands sluicing across the sky as if cast by a colossal lattice hidden high above. Under those slow-drifting brakes on brightness the air becomes a machine for moisture. Warm and tendril-thick, it steeps water out of itself where stone cools and where leaf surfaces skew the temperature by a degree. You can hear it happen. The dimness makes dew: condensation falls in threads and in hushes from the undersides of bridges, from the lintels of basalt, from the hairline veining on the city’s filaments. When it decides to be rain, it is not skyborn sheets but long curtains called down by sudden pressure changes—brief, localized, drenching a square and leaving the next one crisp/dry.

There is a river, if you draw the definition tight. It is a black watercourse no wider than a street, too narrow and too sure of itself to be anything but deliberate. It spools lazy from an up-valley fracture, vanishes through cooling fans of rock, then resurfaces miles on where the basin’s bottom remembers to sag. Its lip is stitched with rushes and long-pored sedges that drink by night and merely breathe by noon. Around it, moisture collects in pockets: in the bowls of rosette-plants whose thick leaves tilt themselves at fractionally altered angles to catch the micro-drizzle; in the scraped depressions where animals have learned that thaw-sweat will bead on the cold morning stone; in cisterns the city has grown like fungus out of lattice. Water is never exactly present in the way of lakes, and never exactly absent either. Its body is missing, but its sweat is on everything.

As if responding to the concentrated damp with an extravagance born of hunger, the vegetation is lush, yet it learned to climb first and root second. The groundcover is a hurried patchwork—ferns that stitch themselves to fissures, glassy grasses that cut and glitter, plump mosses with teeth—but it is on the vertical that things get bold. There are cable-trees, little more than flexible black bundles up to thirty meters long, whose entire strategy is to get high, braid themselves around anything that offers, and then let down a sleeve of leaves the texture of velvet until the dew sifts and runs. There are hyssop towers, engineered a century ago and now naturalized, that act as living condenser arrays: their trunks are ribbed with cooling veins, and under every rib hang pitcher-like throats that taste faintly of metal where they trap the night’s breath. There are tapestries of epiphyte fairs strung between city-lintels, heavy with trumpets and tendrils and neural green; buckets of color tilt and spill pollen motes into the wind, trusting sound itself to deliver them. The foliage isn’t a canopy so much as a series of stacked curtains and balconies, with light seeping through green layers to pool cool around your ankles.

Fauna moves behind and between the leaves with a confidence that suggests there is more of it than you can see. If you flatten your hand on any surface here, the stone, the bark, the braided cable of the bridge you’re on, you feel the world as pressure first: a hundred micro-tremors telling some story you will never parse. Everywhere, creatures have made instruments of themselves. There are striders with skin that looks like dull brass; you only notice them by the way the pollen vortices shift as they pass, and by the long vowels they emit through columned throats when evening drops—calls that last so long you think they must be sustained by machines. There are cupshell amphibs camped in wet hollows whose songs are layered into stacks, low and then lower until you taste them through your teeth. There are gossamer things by the thousands that at first glance are only fluff, but they are not seeds—they are animals, each with a ring of vibrating cilia; swarms of them hang in the pressure-sinks like punctuation. There are manta-wings as wide as a room, leather-dark, that tilt to catch the infrasonic swells the valley generates and then surf them from one node of the city to the next, grazing as they go on the thickened brew of airborne spores and ultrafine plankton that condenses over every cool plane.

All those bodies do not make the place feel crowded. They are abundant in count but fugitive in contact; the architecture of this world is emptiness, curated space. Between each spire and its neighbor, a kilometer of clear air; between the lowest vine and the next plant’s highest aspiration, a tall story of nothing. The sound fills those vacancies like fluid water might, and the city’s design invites that flow to accelerate and deepen. It is organized construction that seems determined to erase its weight: towers with the tensile thinness of a spider’s machine, long traveling paths so slender you doubt yourself on them, blown-glass galleries with a floor the thickness of fruit skin, infinities of line and filament and curve with little mass in them. The urban fabric is draped, not stacked. Its density is restraint: it exists in planes and threads and light nets, not in blocks.

A place like this could have been quiet—dim, low, dew-fed—but something in the material and in the will of those who settled here made it instead a machine for sound. The basin is tuned. The basalt organ-pipes that shoulder up from old lava at the northeastern rim are literal: dozens of vertical hexagonal columns, newly cooled and sharply jointed, nested by the city’s early engineers with banded collars of an unknown composite, and now blown by the prevailing winds to produce chords you can hear from twenty kilometers. The arc-bridges of the central ward narrow and widen in exact progressions; the air that passes through their latticed bones is squeezed and released enough to set up standing waves. There is a seeded aerosol here—very fine, invisible, not a dust you taste but something that drifts from place to place with a conscious obedience—full of nanostructured resonators. They are phononic crystals afloat, all different scales, devices no one born here even calls devices; and when the city hums, so does the sky.

The noise, therefore, is not accidental. It is both consequence and crop. To step into the open you must wear a hood or step into a personal hush; the locals fasten little black eating-mat-thin cloaks to the back of their skulls that shed counterphase in a narrow bubble. Outside that bubble the valley plays itself like a giant throat. The organ-pipes set the key; then come the infrastructures: the power-collectors that look like long, fluted sails and are in fact arrays that convert differential pressures into current; the watermakers that draw the night-dew into reservoirs by summoning it with a music of their own; the transport corridors, mostly invisible, that ride in resonance with the low machines and fling light carriages along on pressure crests. Some of that is audibly audible—the roar and whine and the chuck-chuck-chuck of the filaments as the wind plucks them—but much of it is a bottom-of-the-bones affair, infrasonics so heavy that they make your breath misbehave.

There are hours when the biology drowns the city out. At dusk by the river, a crescendo of stridulation from winged katydids whose elytra have grown into little keyboards: each press of wing on wing locks into the valley’s dominant harmonics, so a million of them can make metallic rain. When the hyssop towers open their auxiliary throats after a cool shadow has chased across and dropped the temperature by two degrees, there is the clear ping-ping of dew beads committing and falling, a glassy sound multiplied across the ward until it is one of the valley’s many versions of thunder. At night there is an intermittent hiss like surf—not water, but pollen being expressed from the cathedrals of fern above the placeholders, billions of grains rushing in turbulent clouds where the city’s sound corrals have trapped the air in articulate whorls. Predators use the noise too. Husher-cats come then, visible only as distortions in the active dust where their built-in null-fields carve bubbles of subaudible suddenly-still; within those canceled-out halos the ears are deadly again, and so are the pads that can hear the last footstep’s echo and pin it like a map.

An urbanism has to happen in such a place that does not always trust the day. People exchange names in sign or in rhythmic pulses tapped into the railing they share. Doors are as likely to be curtains of interference as they are to be literal. Every structure is nested in countermeasures. Material is layered for strength, then layered again for sound-reflection, then scored away until only the least remains. The materials themselves are past the naming of wood or metal or stone; there are new woods that are grown in crystal directions, one axis dense to splinter the wave, one axis hollow to pass the breath like a flute; there is stone poured like syrup and coaxed to set into a gradient of lattices; there are branch-glasses that drink the same dew as the hyssops and are vascular with films that shift their index as an answer to pressure. The city is not for show or a mere place to live, but an organ helping the valley’s larger organ to play, and at the same time harvesting the music for motion, for ease, for food.

Food here is not only grown in ranked terraces of wet leaf and vine; it is grown in the air. The sound-reefs—standing waveforms pinned in place by blackbox emitters far overhead—are veils that trap pollens, seed-spores, ultraphytoplankton. The gossamers feed there, and are fed upon, and are netted by liquid tongues from pitcher-bones grown up to kiss them from below. Along every path, small feeders are hung to harvest the “aeromass” caught this way; they taste like cold bread and kelp, and are what the migrants eat when they come down from the rock. Warmth helps: the suffusing heat is not tropical, but human-supple and always just enough to slick the upper lip. The exceptions come with the moving shadows. When a band passes and the basin hurries toward chill, the change is quick; that hurry and arrest make this place’s weather, more than any cloud.

There is a rumor that the dimness itself, the great overhead hand on the light, is a built object—a distributed array of shade-makers that ride the high winds and steer their own lay so that the valley will breathe in this rhythm forever. Watch the shadow stripes pause over a bridge and you’ll see them call a rainline: dew flecks beading and letting go in a row so straight it feels like human signage. Tell yourself it is the ring of a giant planet if you like, or the smoke of a thousand volcanoes, or the falling ash of a comet that was allowed to shed until it made a ceiling. The locals shrug. It doesn’t matter what gives you dimness if dimness is what lets your hyssop towers work and keeps your sound intact. If the world above the valley is technology, it is technology so integrated into climate that it has taken on the weight and right to be called weather.

Erosion is happening still, and it is misshapen by the sounds. Landslides fall now and then when a node is retuned; edges are sharp where last year they were daggy; paths you learn this month will shear anew when a thunder, carefully coaxed, unhooks a vein of brittle glass. The little river cuts deeper where the valley’s instrument booms gently; dust is urged into drifts like dunes, but not by wind—the infrasonic shapes it until you could swear you see the waves cushion and heap. A place as young as this can be kind and treacherous in the same day, and the city carries that awareness in its posture. Nothing is firmly staked but rather tuned and then retrained when the notes change.

There are rules here that are not written but known. Don’t whistle out of key where a bridge meets a cliff. Keep to the hush under your hood when the organ-pipes are fed, or you may vomit and fall. Trust the dew to appear where the shadow writes it, not where your map shows a cistern. Remember that soaring things have the right of way along the pressure corridors—they are multi-ton gliders and they do not stop. Accept that there are hours, sometimes days, when the valley chooses a new chord. In those times, the gossamers press themselves into the glitter of safety and everything with legs or rootlets grips, for the ground humbles and rises and sets a new pace for hearts. Double-tie your hammock then; shut your counterphase tight.

The first settlers did not mean for it to be so loud, the stories say. They came for warmth in a low place where a young planet’s light was weak, and where moisture was the gift of sky on cooled stone. They found a basin that sang when you flicked it, and flicked it often, and understood that there was an economy here: in shaping pressure, you could force water from air; in trapping the press of the wind, you could ride from one end of the empty city to the other and take nothing with you but a thin strip of shade and a few tools. That first urban line was wide-spaced because there was no need to huddle; there were no old neighbors whose rights you would harm. It remains wide-spaced now by principle, by preference. The emptiness is not just a quality but a law. If you fill this space, the sound would dull. If the sound dulls, the dew fails. If the dew fails, the hyssops gape with thirst like mouths.

So it is a city of distance and nearness both: your nearest neighbor is a kilometer away across roaring nothing; your nearest fellow thought is in your palm through bone-conduction taps on a railing; your nearest water is the next shift in shade angle; your nearest safety is in the human-sized world under your hood. You walk along a filament above an honest fall, and a glass block bird paces you two spans out, and you both tremble at the same deep beat. You reach a crossing where the phosphor moss stains the basalt like a map, and you touch a node and feel the schedule of the day vibrate into your fingers: this is when the ridge will thunder; this is when the dew will string from these two outriders; this is when the cats will hunt the quiet back into existence. Somewhere far, a maintenance spire adjusts its height by a breath, and the sound it makes is as brief and bright as the song of a needle cut loose.

In all that noise, the richest silences are a local art. Guards wear bands that explode a bubble of hush with a patient beat. Hospitals set up pockets the size of almonds that travel with the mouths of the sick. Lovers rent arches that sing the opposite of the world. Pilgrims kneel at the very edge of the basalt organ-pipes and push their fingers into the corrugations to say a prayer you cannot hear but only feel like a held vowel in your ribs. There are also accidents with silence: weird holes in sound that drift and pop; a caravan bridged across one collapsed once and fell as if gravity had remembered it all at once. Sound is as physical here as rock; absence of it has its own mass.

It is warm and dim and damp in a stingy way and wet in a generous one. It is lush without wildness at ground-level and wild without mess in the air. It is loud, so loud, and empty as a church lit only by weather. The fauna is not in your face but in your bones. The buildings are so light they seem a thought. The rules are written in pressure and the pressure is a kind of god. You can be alone in a way you have never been—alone with a million wings and called to by frequencies beyond your hearing, flattering you into your hooded, human-sized hush, walking a gloss-black ribbon with dew falling in a private rain from your own outstretched hand.
