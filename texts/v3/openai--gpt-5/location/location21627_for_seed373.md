At the top of the world the rock narrows into a throat and the sky becomes a room. It doesn’t look like much from the upslope—the last switchback of pale stone, a litter of grit, a few ribs of dark rock—but the instant you cross the saddle everything opens and everything is sound.

The pass pours into a high-walled amphitheater, steep as a bowl dropped and shattered and then worn smooth by centuries of wind. Columns and fins of ancient basalt stand like the ranks of an organ, honeycombed and fluted by abrasion until each surface gleams, each edge rounded to a singing lip. The floor is a crust of light minerals—lithic foam, silica, salts that don’t taste of anything because the air here has had the moisture shaken out of it a hundred kilometers back. Every grain skitters, not in drifts but in fleeting patterns, little snakes of dust that slither in and out of wake vortices and are gone, as if the ground is rehearsing the currents above it.

No water shows; there is nowhere for it to be. The light is high and clean and the shadows have edges. You can see for the length of the chamber and, beyond its rim, the duller blue of the lower air, yet the place feels contained. Not sealed, exactly—the wind says otherwise—but bounded, like a cathedral whose walls are pressure and habit rather than stone. The high elevation strips the mix to a thin, cool clarity that makes your teeth ring. Every breath is a measured labor. The skin of your lips cracks just standing there.

The geography explains part of the sound. This is a venturi: a wide plateau shrunk to a neck and then flaring again. Pressure says its piece, and the wind obeys. But the rest of the sound is bent and braided by things that are not just rock. There are structures in here, spare and scattered, many disguised by lichen-bright mineral accretions or by the forms of the living things that have colonized them. They aren’t large—no towers, no walls—just a field of devices no taller than a house and many no larger than the bones of a hand. Some are rings set on stalks, latticed with holes too small to see; some are slatted vanes of a material that is not metal and not stone, flexing almost imperceptibly in the gale. They have no obvious moving parts, and yet they effect movements that the terrain alone could not: pockets where the roar dies to a gnawing buzz like a swarm behind a door, corridors of pressured air fitted so tightly the dust snakes line up and march like soldiers, then spill and scatter where an invisible edge ends.

It is wind-lensing, of a kind you can sketch in equations and never solve. You stand in a blue-white day, watching zones of quiet drift like clouds without visible boundary. A handful of the rings throb at a pitch that’s more sensation than sound—your chest feels it, loose things on your pack feel it. Your hearing makes and unmakes phantom tones as your head turns and your bones and sinuses add their own resonances to the place. In a corner of the bowl, the skeleton of a failed device leans: a torus split and fused back together by the pressure of its own function long ago, its edge feathered into a thousand filaments that whisper a note all their own. It’s raining nothing, and yet the noise is like standing under a waterfall.

Strange, then, to use the word lush. But that is what the life here is. Not lush the way a rainforest is, with dripping leaves and rotting logs and the sweet, fond stink of damp. Lush like coral, elaborate and tight to the rock, using every angle and pocket, spread in velours and fans and the glossy sausage-shapes of organs grown for a purpose. Plants—call them that because they don’t go anywhere they can’t be shaken loose—root in the old vesicles of the basalt and the accidental shelves of the built things. They are dry to the touch; some are even crisp, like delicate pastry, and break with a sigh. They pump ionic liquids under their surfaces just the same, saps that don’t mind how little water there is, that take their ions straight from air and stone. Their leaves are not leaves: they are drums.

Across the windward faces, membranes as taut as animal skin span frames of rod and foam. In the leeward shade the drums are looser, or layered into little stacks with slits between them. Each skin shivers under the pressure, beating a pulse faster than you can see. Where two membranes face one another a few fingers apart, you can hear the thwap of beats interacting, setting up moirés of tone that shift as the gusts turn. Close to your ear a disk stutters at the very edge of audibility and your hair lifts. The energy is there; it has to go somewhere. It goes into chemistry. The drums drive pumps, and the pumps make uphill reactions out of motion the way leaves make sugars out of light under other suns.

Tassels of flexible crystals hang below the drums, slick with ultrathin films of charge, combing ions from the air. Fan-forms like overlapping shields narrow and widen in wind: their undersides are pale greens shot through with metals, reflecting normal daylight with a matte, non-reflective hunger that reads as thirst for something other than water. Towers of calcic foam rise where a device’s quiet pocket sits reliably; there the membranes can wind down and the fluids can sort themselves, and under your hand the foam is warmer than the air, a gentle exotherm from chemical work.

The fauna are clay and chitin and bone analogs, not fur and flesh. They have had to be light—so much air moving in so much space—and so much of them is made of sail. The smallest are everywhere, an insect-density that the emptiness of the chamber absorbs: specks and threads and vanes, bright in the light like metal shavings in oil. They hang in the lee of anything that projects, swarm into the tuned corridors of the built devices and take what the coherent wind gives them: pressure differentials and steady beats. They ride, and their small parts vibrate; their diets are ions and dust and that mysterious energy that is the difference between standing in a gale and letting it pass through you. They scream, and their voices are lost in the general scream and also part of it. The bigger ones glide, their frames all rib and ring, thin as the ribs of a kite, edged in rigid trailing fringes so that each banking turn bites a new note out of the place. When they rest they are just outlines against rock, silence painted into a clamor-painted wall, and then—some shift in the standing wave, some around-corner gust—they are away again on angle and nerve and memory.

Others don’t leave their perches at all. They grow on the drums like barnacles, stacks of horns that open and shut with a clack to harvest flow; with every opening the horn’s rim flashes metallic and then dull. Some of them are grazers. They creep across the membranes like patient scissors, trimming the edges of drums and peeling films, returning ions to stone in tags of chalk. Predators exist, here, in the way lines can cross. A glider with a mouth like a shutter drops into a vein of coherent pressure and opens; in the instantaneous kink that follows, the drum downstream bursts with a sound bigger than all the rest for a heartbeat and then there is a hole where it was, a lacy fringe where its rim used to be.

Rules emerge. Everything here, and every thing that works, works with or against pattern. Life listens not for voices but for beats. Plants reject gusts that are not on-mesh with their own cycles; their membranes stiffen, and you can almost imagine them frowning. Animals read phase the way a hunter reads prints. The built structures—old, unattended, still adjusting themselves—remember settings of a climate older than our models and prefer to keep those chords alive, so the whole amphitheater plays the same few notes every day and different ones in different seasons, and the living fittings have learned that too. Predators don’t chase so much as sabotage: a body placed in the throat of a horn at the right time, an interference pattern thrown at a gliding sheet to take the lift out of one wing while leaving the other alone. There is no water to drink. There is a flow to match, or to disrupt.

Because the place is perched so high, the pressure differentials can be brutal. Every now and then, a downramp forms: a pathway of dense, chilled air scraped from a higher layer and hurled through the throat into the bowl. You can see it coming; the dust shapes change, the light shivers, and the sound goes from one chord stack to another. Membranes bow, horns clap, the smallest things become confetti. Some of the rings in the floor ignite a sheen, a lacework of faint blue that might be corona discharge and might be something we do not yet name, and for a half-breath the noises shift into a register that your inner ear hates. Out of that impact comes a riot. For three minutes everything is eating and being eaten and fixing and seeding, and then the flows return to the basal state and everything smooths. The shapes have done this for so long they bear the work like muscles. That edge of horn? The shine at a drum’s rim? That’s not fragility; that’s where the tissue grows fastest because the chord demands it.

The built things have been here longer than any of that life, or else time is playing a game. Their skins are weathered to the silkiness of river stones in a place that has never known a river. They can be read as the bones of a tuning, a set of notes and counter-notes whereby sound is made into structure. One arch is webbed with a stuffing of fiber that isn’t dead and isn’t alive in a way biology would approve of; under magnification its weave is too regular, too knotted in clever four-dimensional ways to be just accretion, and yet lichens—the wrong word, but use it—lace it in patches like islands on a map. Another stanchion vibrates with the day and then with the night, flexing half a millimeter, and in every flex a cascade of nanogates inside it opens and closes and feeds or starves some pocket of air until the whole bowl breathes in a way that the weather outside does not require. There is an intelligence here, or an intention fossilized into habit. Engineering is not the right word either, but it is closer than art.

You can see where something once came through to tend all this. There is a notch in the rim that is not natural; it has been smoothed into an intake for fliers that are not the ones alive here now. In the lee of it sits a shed the wind cannot enter, or at least cannot enter as wind. Inside, the noise fades to a thrum you can hear as individual pulses, and the smell changes. There is no rot. There is dust, and the faint tang of hot mineral, and a smell that’s like a radiator after a rain in a world where no rain falls. Someone left a tool here, a length of that not-stone that hums faintly delayed to everything else you can hear. Lichens—or their equivalents—have colonized even this, sprouting a few tentative drums in the shadows, as if they were feeling their way into some new kind of chord.

Space is the strangest thing about the place. For all the abundance—the air busy with scatter, the organ-forest full of beating skins and clacking horns, the burst of a downramp launching a hundred creatures into motions like flags—the overall feel is emptiness. The scale of the room drinks motion in gulps and is never full. There are long stretches of bare wall between colonies, whole vaults of air that nothing crosses for minutes at a time because the currents there are chaotic, not patterned enough to be used. To cross those you would need an engine that makes its own beat, and that is not what this world trains. In that hollowness your eyes rest, and then they return to the teeming fringes and the tuned corridors and understand why life has gathered where it has. This is a place made by scarcity, not by lack but by too much of the wrong kind, tamed into habit by rock, by machines older than our certainties, by a biology that learned to turn noise into order.

Standing there, a creature that runs on water, you are surplus to requirements. Your breath steams and then is ripped away to no effect. Your ears ring like struck bowls and then find ways to listen in the tumult to the smaller tumults—the insect harpings, the odd lonely lowing of something big riding a pressure ladder on the far wall, the enormous soft clap of a drum giving way and being eaten, being replaced. The rules become ordinary as you stay: that sound has texture, that friction is food, that stillness is a note you have to pay to hear. The place is cool and dry and murderous and prosperous, all at once. It has been this for a very long time. It will be until the chords change.
