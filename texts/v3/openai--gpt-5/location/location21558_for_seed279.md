Before you see anything, the place introduces itself by breath. It is a chamber without a roof, broad as a valley, built of stone that inhales and exhales. Every few seconds a bass note swells underfoot, rolls through your knees, then breaks somewhere to the west in a coughing boom. Between those pulses a constant, crowded murmur lives: the pack-ice hiss of spray driven into fractures; a chorus of thin whistles that never quite find a pitch; deep, elastic groans traveling the ground like the memory of thunder. The air quivers with it. Even your clothes acquire a sound, a wet rasp as they move, because everything here—the rock, the air, your tongue—is wrapped in fine suspended water.

The land is low and recently made, as if it only just learned to be land. It spreads in black and lead-gray planes and ridges, a surface that was once liquid and still remembers the gesture of flow. Fresh edges are everywhere: broken lips where an old crust peeled back, glassy skins snapped into hex and wedge, ribs of cooler stone that stood fast while the rest sloughed away. Narrow corridors run between walls of columned rock, tight enough that your shoulders would brush both sides, then open into round courtyards, then pinch again into slots where the sound all at once grows toothy and close. The fractures are young and sharp, their facets catching what little light there is and throwing it back in damp glints. What’s level is never entirely level; every flat face beards out with capillary grooves and shallow pans, the marks of water that never got the chance to be a stream before a deeper crack stole it.

Water is near without being generous. The stone sweats. Fine beads stand out on every surface, fattening until they merge and slide into runnels. Where a ridge overhangs, threads of droplets unlatch and cue one another down in beads like counting. Pools gather in lint-clad bowls, their surfaces quivering from the percussion of the next boom through the substructure. There are faint, sketched rivers that begin in nothing but condensation and end in a disappearance through some circular mouth. Now and then, far out in the flatted west, a breath of sea arrives, flung up invisible through deep tubes; in some corridors the exhalation is cold and brackish, in others it is just cold. Salt rimes things in white seams and whiskers. In the corners of rock-chambers that never see direct sky, you find stilled mirrors of almost-fresh water, an arm’s length deep and black as ink, the topmost film torn now and then by drip crowns rising and collapsing in slow crowns upon crowns.

Everything you touch is mineral: basalt and glass, iron and manganese varnishes slicking the windward faces like a dark lacquer. The floor crunches lightly where it’s armored in new salt crystals. In places the black is interrupted by stains like bruises—purples, oilslick blues—where dissolved metals have bled from the stone and dried. Elsewhere you find batterings of froth that never quite dry, a fragile sugar-foam of silica trapped on ledges like snow that will not freeze. Hair-thin needles of salt grow in sheltered pockets, a frost of bristles that collapse to paste at the touch of your breath.

The light is not so much dim as withheld. There is a ceiling of cloud, close and undecorated, the color of unpolished tin. Occasionally it goes from charcoal to pewter and back, but never opens. You live by reflected light on wetness, by the pale, almost internal glow of certain mineral films that look like shine until you notice they’re in shadow. Your exhale appears as a pale flag and is taken apart at once by the next passing pressure wave. Smells live low: iron, a lick of sulfur in some corridors, a faint iodine sweetness, the hollow wet smell of caves even though you are outside.

Under the sounding skin, the place is an instrument. Caves, culverts, throatings, bellies of stone layered and branched under the plain—not voids you can walk into, but enclosed volumes that the sea or some larger, slower reservoir of water presses and releases. Each has its note. The black ridges and bundled columns are the lips and reeds of this anatomy. Air drives through one and it buzzes; it eddies in another and a whistle begins, too high to hear and yet your teeth hum with it. These tones set the rules for the water aboveground. Drips gather in antinodes, stringing themselves into regular necklaces in places where the pattern stands still; in other places, you see whole beaded curtains tilt because a hummed pressure just barely leans them. A pan two paces from an identical pan fills; its twin stays dry. A black wall is soaked on one half, chalk-dry on the other, the border crisp as a page-fold whose origin is in pressure, not slope.

There is life here, but not the green sort that breaks the light and throws it back as food. The builders and eaters are small. They lace the undersides of overhangs with translucent slime-coats that are more sophisticated than their nakedness suggests, woven of tubes and pockets that trade salts and gases without ever admitting something with a mouth. Where fresh rock has spilled its meal of metals, these films seize it, tasting the iron or manganese and leaving a duller stripe when they pass. In the shallow basins where sweet and brine brush in layers, delicately ribbed sheets assemble, like paper made of snot and ash, and crawl by a fingernail’s width each day, toward the most favorable chemistry. If there is any light-fed chemistry, it is marginal and gray, living on the borrowed photons that reach the bright rim of a drip before the next cloud thickens.

Animals, if you can call them that, are rare enough that seeing one is an event. There is a shell here—a single piece the size of two joined hands—grown in stacked spirals and thickened where the sound is richest. When the plain inhales, the creature opens a row of stiff, translucent vanes along the rim and trembles them, taking in a blasting of wet air and the dust of food that rides in it. Its foot is not made to glide, but to take purchase on angles and to grind a path across varnish; in passing it leaves a pale road of abraded black, a chalky record of where the sound once suited it. Deeper in, where the exhalations are salted and rhythmic, a slow ribbon of flesh hangs like laundry from a rock mouth during the exhale, then curls away into safety as the inhale sucks hard enough to seize. Its body is threaded with stones and hairs of some unknown mineral, so that it looks, when withdrawn, like a gnarled rope with thorns—more anchor than animal. In a flooded courtyard you notice movement at last: a lattice of spines like a collapsed umbrella lifts one sector at a time, sifts the water for floc and crystals, and then settles without haste, a machine made of nothing but cartilage and patience.

Not everything of shape is alive. Scattered across the plain you come upon arrangements—small, deliberate-seeming—a ring of fist-sized stones in a spot where the bass is dulled; a low berm leaned against a wall where the whistle softens to a burr; a row of wedge-shards placed like teeth across a narrow slot. They are old, many of them half-lacquered, half-sunk. Some have fallen and then been reset slightly to one side, as if the mind that arranged them comes rarely and works without language. The effect is subtle. Move a stone here and the corridor loses its nagging harmonic; pile enough there and the pulsed boom drifts from an eight-count to a nine. None of it speaks of tools, only hands or their equivalent, and time. You guess that the sharpened shells glean the extra calories that a changed tone brings, that the lacy sponges of mineral-and-flesh open most fully when the burr is just such, and that the scattered architecture is both food-garden and prayer.

There are rules you feel before you know them. Stand on the high, thin lip that looks safely dry and you are in the path of a not-quite-visible wet wind that will strip the warmth from your fingers in a minute; step down into the dark bowl that looks treacherous and you are out of it, in a pocket that is merely cold and dripping. Any edge will injure you; there is no softness unless it is alive, and then it may sting with metals. Anything that rises will be skinned by salt. Anything that is quiet will be made to speak: pebbles piled in a channel will begin to shake and buzz; a shallow dish will dimple into patterns under the pulse and leave a lace of salt as it dries. Hear first, then step. Watch what the drips are doing—and watch where the drips are not.

Distance here is a trick of saturation. You can see for a long way across this basin because nothing interrupts the low horizon. Yet every meter is crowded: with ribs, with beaded threads, with tiny courtyards and ledges, with the bodies of old surges trapped in rock. The place is saturated with presences even while being nearly empty of creatures. Aboveground, the density is of forms and droplets; in the stone, it is of volumes tuned to one another like bees in a wall.

Change is patient. The loudness is not a storm but a metronome. The sea, if this is the sea, is mostly absent, reaching inland through a thousand throats and then withdrawing. The sky is inert and heavy. Each dramatic sound—each cough of a blowhole, each rolling groan—is part of a cycle that took centuries to set and will take centuries to drift. Only the smallest things are quick: a vaneing shell gasps and closes; a drip joins; a bristle of salt grows and falls. Between thunders, the world is busy at sub-insect scales, knitting and unknitting films, oxidizing a stain by a shade. Seasons, if such things occur, would be marked by a different tone, a different balance of sweet and brine, a filmed pan that this month is alive and three months from now is glass.

If you listen long enough, the patterns sort themselves. The deep notes tell where the buried bodies of water are today; the shrills trace the cracks that do not reach the surface yet; the silence maps a place you can live through. The plain continues its respirations, its self-made weather of droplets, its blunted labors of chemistry. It has no greenery, yet something is always growing. It has little movement, yet it shouts. And over it all the dim lid of sky does nothing but gather the sound and send it back down, softer, as if the world were teaching itself how to ring.
