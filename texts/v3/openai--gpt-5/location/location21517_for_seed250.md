It is a roofworld under a falling ocean. The high country rises like a black-green knuckle into a sky so saturated that liquid sheets descend as if the air had liquefied. Not rain as drops, but walls — glassy planes that materialize from mist, fall in steady lamina, fracture, heal, and sweep sideways under the push of invisible gusts. Light filters through them as through moving panes of clear ice, daylight broken into tremulous bands. Between the curtains, the air is startlingly empty, a cool, clean void in which distance runs unresisted for kilometers until the next vertical river takes it away again.

The ground is new and sure of itself: raw-birthed rock, all sharpness and prismatic faces, a tessellation of fresh columns and plates where magma met a world still deciding what weather was. Basalt stands in hexagonal ranks, vitrified skins shining wet, obsidian wedges rimmed with immature rime. Pale silica fans bloom where steaming veins once exhaled; slick black dikes slice across paler flows like writing in a language of pressure. There has not been time enough for true valleys, only fast-made, just-as-fast-lost runnels, and ledges splintered by the ache of cooling. The plateau’s rim bevels into sudden space; beyond it, the world drops into a tropospheric sea, a textural abyss of layered cloud.

Water behaves with its own choreography here, half obeying gravity, half listening to forces that roam the stone. Sheets cling to cliffs long enough to become ice-glass skins, let go in plates that shatter to gravelly hiss. Narrow rills run uphill for a heartbeat under the shove of downdrafts. Microbursts step down from the underside of cloud in silver corduroy; where they hit, fog explodes sideways in white avalanches that never touch ground. Curtains collide and fuse, release rippling seams of cavitation that sprint down their lengths like lightning trapped in a pane. Channels appear where none were, crease the surface in perfect fluted symmetry, then blink to smoothness as a slab of hail skates through, melting as it goes.

Everything has a voice. The steady thunder of descending planes lays a floor of sound you feel in ribs and teeth. Over it, the columns sing — Aeolian organs stroked by crosswind, each pipe a different length, each note leaning into and away from its neighbors as gusts shift. Quartz seams, squeezed by tectonic breath and waterfall weight, give off thin, faintly electrical cries. From the high flat, infrasonic pulses roll out as if the massif were a slow heart; you sense them more than hear them, pressure moving through the voids between rain-walls, setting curtains to tremble. Lightning doesn’t fork; it unfurls as luminous membranes, blue-white sheets silently igniting inside the falling water and then speaking themselves aloud as the thunder integrates, a long-bodied roar rather than a crack. Sometimes the entire place becomes an instrument, and for a few seconds the rain modulates into standing waves, striated like the rings of a note.

Life has only just found purchase, and it keeps close to stone. Thin films of iron-green and smoke-blue stipple the lee sides of spires where diffuse light accumulates; they smooth the edges, micrometers at a time, eating at the glass with patient acids. In hairline cracks, mineral lichens build slow palisades of white grit, an outline of future soil. On the undersides of overhangs, translucent cords dangle an inch or two into the falling planes and harvest whatever touches them — dissolved carbon, stray nitrates knocked from the air by thunder — then retreat as flow strengthens, flexing like drawn breath. In rare, quiet windbreaks, a low mat like wet velvet catches pale light and drinks it, almost black, leaving a dark thumbprint that survives an hour longer than the surrounding sheen. There are no animals; nothing moves here that isn’t water, rock, or air.

The scale teaches its own rules. Gravity seems slightly reluctant, not because it is weaker, but because the distances hand it more to do; a droplet leaving the cloud edge falls for half a mountain before it learns to be spray. The curtains are not merely rain but waveguides, holding light and sound, bending both in interference you can see in the air — moiré bands walking across space, projected onto emptiness by nothing you can touch. Piezoelectric seams in the rising crust charge the water sheets; repelling charges keep curtains from coalescing until a certain pitch of thunder tells them to touch. The storm reads itself. Memory forms as spiral scars of spin within the falling planes, tiny vortical cords that live hours and migrate kilometers, carrying a record of where the mountain exhaled heat, where a crack sighed open. No mind designed this; the interplay of materials and forces builds a machine because that is how they like to fit, computation in flows, prediction written as resonance.

Weather has its own geography here. A permanent thunderhead tiles above the highest spur where updrafts and topography make a treaty; you can see, through breaks, the braided trunks of vapor climbing, woolen-gray, cooling to release more water to the planes below. Far off, a cold fall collapses, and a piece of the mountain disappears into cloud, not eroded but simply edited from sight by a pewter curtain three kilometers wide. Between systems, sun pushes through in angled corridors, warms stone enough to send up steam that becomes scaffolding for the next construction of sky. The hydrology is mostly vertical; there are no long rivers, only incidents of descent.

The cool is pervasive and clean. Breath would plume if there were mouths to make it. Surfaces glaze and unglaze as supercooled sheets brush them; a hair-thin ice veneer can appear on a sunlit ledge and hiss away an instant later under the touch of a denser fall. On some mornings — mornings only by the angle of light, because the roar makes all hours the same — the obsidian sweats a mist so fine it makes halos around nothing. In faultlines, warmth gathers: fumarolic damp that smells of stone misremembering the deep. Even here, even now, steam has nowhere to linger; it is taken into the machine of weather and given form as the next wall.

Erosion is a rumor. Edges are all first-draft, angular phrases awaiting a language of rounding. Hammer marks of planet-making are still visible: contraction joints like the scales of a stopping wave, glassy peels torn free where cooling rock wanted to shrink from itself. Where water runs, it carries a mealy suspension of brand-new grains; the paste gathers a few meters and then is pulled as a single skin, leaving the under-shape unchanged. Nothing yet has had time to be smooth, only wet, only loud.

Distance is mostly empty. You can walk your eyes a long way across nothing but clear air bounded by moving water and feel the austerity in it — volume without occupant, a cathedral whose pillars are vertical seas and whose vault is anvil cloud. Shift your attention, and the emptiness becomes event: a curtain folds, a seam lights, a low sound thickens until the stone answers it. Then all at once the world resets somewhere else and the large spaces are bare again.

If there is law here, it is that the fall makes and unmakes, and that the mountain is still deciding the shape of that contract. The place is embryonic but already articulate, a self-writing score scratched in basalt and water, performed in thunder. Untouched not because nothing could touch it, but because there has not yet been time for touch to matter. It will be different in a thousand storms. Today, it is this: a high, cool continent in the act of teaching an ocean to fall.
