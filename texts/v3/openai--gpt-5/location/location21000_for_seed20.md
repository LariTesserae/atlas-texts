High, where the spine of the range should be wind and sky, the sky has taken on the weight of water. A small amphitheater of stone opens like a throat to the drowned air, a single chamber carved by no mason and older than any hand, yet young as a scream: edges still glassy from the fracture, planes unsoftened, shards of black and milk-white rock fitting together at angles too crisp for weather to have learned them. The whole bowl is full—warm, at body temperature, a sea pressed into a room. The light is a green dusk with blue in it, a dimness that tastes like iron and salt.

Sound is not here as a thing you notice; it is the medium everything else floats in. There is a continuous, absolute roar, a presence so complete it crams itself into bone and tooth and the empty spaces behind the eyes. It is waterfall and surf and furnace and organ-pipe all at once, a band of pressure that never thins. Objects have shadows and they have wakes; here, everything also has a note. The stone ledges thrum with different pitches if you lay a hand on them. Clefts pulse with infrasonic heartbeats. The chamber itself is a single bell big as a church and every instant someone is striking it with both fists.

It is not a cavern but a notch, a scoop in the mountain's crest where the drowned atmosphere shunts itself downslope in endless chastening. Imagine a waterfall with no distinct edge, a wall of rain bulked to an ocean, pouring sideways. The amphitheater receives that horizontal fall and breaks it into ducts and blown plumes. There are planes within the water that cut and shear like blades, white lines where bubbles are scissored into powder; there are rounded, tumbling slugs of water the size of house-cats; there are curtains and clearings and, for the fraction of a breath, open rooms of thinner substance that smell like old wind before they collapse again.

This place is young. The fractures in the rock gleam like new scars. The floor is not a floor but a scatter of angular blocks keyed like teeth, with sharp clefts between them where the pressure-sanded silt has not yet found a resting place. On the right-hand wall a whole panel of pale stone has sloughed away in one piece not long ago, leaving the negative of ferns printed upon it from a time when this height was not licensed to hold water. Their shadows were already there when the drowning came. Now those ghosts watch algae comets stream past.

Vegetation is scarce and improvised. No forests wave here; no kelp cathedral hangs. Instead, sparse mats cling like tarnish to the lee of projections—bronze and petrol sheens of bacteria that eat what the water brings. A few tufts of something that wants to be a shrub still stand rooted where the ridge was once dry, their wood sealed in a clear glaze exuded against the flood; they are statues of their own past selves, branches cased in mucus, the tips sprouting new flexible, ribboned leaves that harvest motion instead of sun, trembling as the pulses pass. In one shaded corner a rope-thin vine has found a pressure-shadow that is almost a stillness. It has built itself into that stillness the way a spider builds into a draft, a few sparse fans of flesh flared between its internodes, folded and refolded like accordions so they will not tear.

Fauna are few and singular. A needlefish, all cartilage and wary eye, hangs like a twig in a window, angling itself so that the laminar skins of two crossing flows wrap it into a quiet pocket. A translucent bell drifts past and then is not a jellyfish but the internal geometry of the pressure itself, a standing wave thrown into form by the harmonic between shelf and splash—then gone when the beat shifts. Once in a great while an oar of something larger strokes just outside the curving mouth of the amphitheater and does not come in. The detritus tells the story more than the living: a single scale like hammered tin resettling among the stones; a coiled, transparent egg-case that took the shape of eddy and cylinder both as it hardened; a ribbon of cast skin from a creature not seen, caught on a spur and clicking.

Built things exist here but only the ones that grew from the same patience as lichens. On a shelf at shoulder height a ring of flat stones has been stacked into a low crescent, so carefully that the coursing cannot topple it. The stones are chosen for their overtones; together they subtract a narrow band of roar, and behind the crescent, in its lee, there is a pocket of almost-quiet the size of a cupped hand. Someone knew how to shape stillness from pressure. Not because they had devices, but because they had sat long enough in the roar to detect the chords of the place. Near the bowl's upward rim, incised grooves pecked into the glassy black lead water into channels thinner than a reed, creating white threads of microfalls. They do not carry water far; they exist to pull sound into a different braid. There is also a child of a cairn: three small blocks set one upon the other, each turned a half-turn from the last. Nothing else tells you anyone came, not paint or bone or rust. The texture on the top stone is smooth in the way of many patient hands.

The rules here are not the same as under sky. Gravity is not all in one direction; for a breath the concept seems approximate. Each beat of the place builds a scaffold of pressure surfaces you can lean against as if they were glass, then takes them back again. Edges are reliable only when you are in the habit of reading current as an alphabet. You do not go from point to point; you go from node to node where chords intersect and make less chaos. There is a sound-map instead of a road-map. The smallest creatures—thread-worms and pin-limbs—are born with the habit of listening, and you can see how they settle in specific harmonics the way bird flocks once settled into windless corners of cliffs. Time has small eddies, too. Bubbles that should fly straight upward will idle in loops before deciding which way is up in a world where "up" must be negotiated among pressures. An ascending thread of silt will pause in place when a deeper tone swells and will not fall or rise until that vowel has been uttered completely.

There is rainfall inside the water. This is not a metaphor. Between two stones, there is a sudden appearance of droplets finer than dust, a local wall of rain shouted into existence by the amplitude of a specific tone. The droplets fall sideways, hissing, and then they join larger bodies of water anew. In that later moment the stone they passed is frosted lightly in a scale of mineral the color of teeth. Precipitation is fast here—stains that should take years draw themselves in a day. Thin draperies of white carbonate furl under shelves like unfurling page-edges and then break away when a new pattern of turbulence arrives. So the place writes itself and un-writes itself all the time.

If you could pluck out the sea and leave only sound, the chamber would be an oculus and converging ribs. As it is, there are glimpses of architecture that are made of moving water as sure as columns are made of stone. There is a nave that runs from the lower left corner of vision to the upper right, consisting of a stable clearance where the turbulence refuses to enter for six or eight heartbeats at a time. There are flying buttresses of spray that arch from ledge to ledge and do not break, held in place by racking chords. There is a rose window of bubbles that spins like a coin about to settle. You can swim through it and come out without knowing if you've gone in a circle around a pillar that is not there or crossed the room. This is how the world fails to be ordinary here: it keeps deciding that form is negotiable, then makes that negotiation physical.

The temperature is kind. Warmth slides along the skin like a thought. It is the heat of a planet's breath, of crushed water rubbed against young rock. Between thunderings there are faint thread-thin streams of hotter water that stitch across the main body of current and vanish. They carry the taste of sulfur when they cross the tongue; for a moment afterward a band behind the teeth rings a different color. The warmth keeps everything slightly faster: the mineral invisible until it is not, the bacteria able to eat lightless sugars spun by pressure; even the stones know urgencies they did not know when the sky was empty.

Occasionally the roar resolves. For no reason at all, all the randomness lines up in a progression you could hum. The amphitheater plays a chord, holds it for the length of a thought, then drops it for a lower, rougher one. That is when the almost-quiet behind the crescent cairn becomes truly quiet, a bubble of stillness into which the noise will not enter. In there, a thing could make a sound and have it be its own, not a slave to the larger body. Perhaps that is what the crescent was for: a place to name things without the names being dragged away.

Elevation matters even if nobody breathes air anymore. Things here are thin in the way of uplands—fewer kinds of life, fewer contours to the noise, big spaces between presences. Outside the amphitheater the drowned ridge runs as a knife-edge into the green gloom. Snow-grit still rests in creases, transformed to a kind of curd by the warm flood. The pressure is not the crushing fathoms of deep seas; this is the upper ocean of a world whose air has fallen, a sea that ends in brightness somewhere above. Sometimes that brightness swells as if sun were wrestling to be relevant; the whole room goes olive. It dies again to dim. If you look up the gullet where the falling water comes from, you see not a source but a place where green turns to paler green and, once, the inverted silhouette of a bird that forgot itself and came too high, now a relic embedded in lime.

The chamfered rocks keep changing, growing beards of new stone overnight; the built crescent gains and loses its stones as flows rearrange their local truth, and yet it always finds its way back, as if the pattern of it were stored here along with the other patterns sound insists on. Perhaps that is another rule: memory is a function of resonance. Things that align with the place's tones are harder to erase.

If the roar stops for a heartbeat—if far thunder eats the closer thunder and there is nothing in its place—you will finally hear the small body of this chamber: the tick of grains, the complaint of a bubble corkscrewing along a groove, the whisper of a ribbon-leaf resettling. Then the larger flood's breath returns, and the world is once more a single voice pressed through a narrow throat, and all the downstream valleys receive what is given here without being asked.

It is a room made of water perched on the back of a mountain. It is a young wound in a world that has learned to be ocean wherever it once was not. It is a quiet scattered into the many forms of loudness, with pockets where quiet pretends it is safe. It is a place where distance is counted in chords, where shapes are a habit voices remember, where living things that might be many elsewhere are few and careful and turned to the grain of the pressure that passes through everything.
