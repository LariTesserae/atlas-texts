At the crown of the range the stone opens not to sky but to a throat. A narrow cleft falls away from a blade-edged ridge and fattens into a bell within, a cathedral of weathered limestone whose mouth is so tight that day itself gets filtered down to a suggestion. The first few steps from the lip are still mountain—lichen-scabbed ledges, a tuft of grass clenched in a crack, a shrub twisted like a fist around its own hunger. Then the rock turns inward, out of sun, and everything thereafter is governed by warm darkness.

The chamber has had a very long time to decide what it wants to be. The walls are not jagged but oldly smooth, scalloped by eras of mist and the rare, furious river of a cloudburst. Runnels braided the stone long ago and then softened their own edges. Blind pockets pecked into the ceiling hold the last glances of daylight as a thinned milk, but none of it reaches the floor except, on a few dry days a year, as a narrow blade of light that lays a cutting across a ledge and reveals one small country of photosynthesis: a skin of algae on a damp lip, a few pale mosses, a fern the size of your hand holding out a frond to the sun’s brief ceremony. Mostly the vegetation is that—the almost, the nearly—things that have adapted to take what little they can from the condensed breath of clouds.

The air is warmer than the mountain should allow. Put your hand to the stone and you feel a stored body-heat as if the whole place is an animal that has been sleeping here for centuries. Some of it is the insulation of depth; some of it is the constant metabolism of what lives here; some comes from the quiet combustion of damp rot. Warmth loosens the moisture from air; tiny beads form on every ripple and ledge; the chamber rains from itself even when the sky outside is a hard blue. In fog weather the mountain swallows the cloud and exhales drip. In dry spells, water still appears by stealth—sweat on stone, breath that condenses on the wings of roosting bodies and falls in a slow, interrupted fashion to rimstone pools. These are shallow dishes whose lips—dams built grain by grain by mineral-laden seeps—ladder the sloping floor. They fill without sound, empty in silence, and remember, when they are dry, the shape of all their previous brims.

There is a smell here you can almost see: acrid, animal, sweet rot, a mineral sting like the back of your throat in winter. There is a taste: saltpeter on the tongue, the metallic suggestion of stone dissolved in air. There is a texture: stone polished to a grease by thousands of points of contact; below that, the yielding crunch of old guano, anciently compacted into low dunes and buttresses, cut by the channels where the rare flood sluiced through and then smoothed again.

Darkness is not empty here; it is full. It has weight and layers. More precisely, it has wings. The roof and upper walls are shingled thick with bats. They are a mass with a temperature and a pulse, in the way that an ocean is both a sum of drops and a thing you can hear sweeping in one piece. They hang on each other like conifer cones, fur to fur; the older ones with scars in their ear-membranes hang deeper in the hollows; the new clamber at the boundaries, tiny fingers feeling for old holds. Warmth refines them. It rises from the colony in a slow draft and meets the cooler air higher up, and at that seam tiny eddies form that pull scent and dust and the rasp of sound into a constant, dense weather.

Sound rules here. The chamber is both bell and lung. Even when the colony is asleep or in that bat-state of listening-to-itself, a dozen other continuities stitch the air: the constant patter of condensation falling through space, a soft grit-sound of chitin upon chitin as beetles work the floor, the tiny rivet-tappings of cave crickets landing and springing, the legs of a thousand harvestmen ticking the damp varnish of stone, the whisper of blind millipedes moving in furred rivers. Most of the things that live on the floor cannot see; their world is a taste of nitre and the shape of tremors, and they are numerous enough that to stand still a minute is to feel them reconnoiter your boot.

At the times when the bats decide as one, the chamber is a different instrument. First there is the collective stirring like wind finding flags. Sheets of tiny, percussive chirps. Then a rush. The air thickens into bodies, and the space between you and the next outcrop becomes a liquid road of sound. Bats pour from the roof and flow down the tilt of the chamber, banking around protrusions, folding into the mouth of the cleft in a ribbon that tightens, tightens, tightens, and peels outward into the dusk beyond. For several minutes your ears belong to this. Silence returns not as quiet but as the intricate persistence of all the small things you had not been able to hear under the wing-roar.

The walls bloom with other lives. Fine threads hang in curtains across some runnels: glowworms have plaited their traps in the whoosh-paths of midges, and they shine with a pallid green hum that never quite becomes light, only the idea of it—a lot of the illumination here works like that, as if the stone itself were remembering daylight and repeating it to soothe you. On patches where the guano stays damp, thread-fungi lace the waste into a sponge-mat. In places the mat has the sheen of foxfire; in other places it is only plush with hyphae so fine your fingers will feel them before your eye names them. Micro-pools hold sculling ghost-things: amphipods with no pigment, a few planaria with a kind of dim internal moon about them, the occasional salamander with eyes like unlit coals that still track movement by the motion of current. Spiders take the ceiling fringes that the bats don’t claim; they hang long webs that collect the dust of wings, and sometimes, after a great exodus, you will see one web sagging with a dozen accidental prizes, each one the thrumming knot of a gnat’s short history.

Predation here is precise because space is tight. A pale gecko lives on a specific ledge and will not cross its own border; an owl has learned to cling just outside the cleft and snatch at the ribbon of departing bodies by sound alone; it leaves only pellet-ghosts that disintegrate and seed the lip with the smallest of grasses. There are scorpions that learn a particular drip-line and wait where drops hit a stone and spread, because that’s where the crickets land to drink. There are snakes that come up from lower, leafier valleys to breed under the belly of the mountain and then coil their eggs in the guano where it heats itself in long, patient smoulder. Nothing built; nothing arranged by hands. The place is crowded enough that each life makes a boundary out of crowdedness and the chamber can hold them all because their timings are offset, their hunger windows nested.

Water is intermittent but assured. It arrives as weather does, not schedule: a cloud that scrapes the ridge so close that water appears on the stone by miracle; a thunderstorm out on the plain whose pressure wave reaches up here a half-day later and the cave takes a long breath, and a vein in the ceiling that has held calm for weeks suddenly weeps. On one or two nights a season, a true rain will find the slit, and the careful, crusted edges of the rimstone will be rearranged by a pour like a silver rope. The proof of those rains is written high on the walls in a paler banding of stone where the mineral wash dried into memory, and in pockets near the foot of columns where pebbles the size of teeth have lodged and become kin to the rock.

The erosion is old enough to be gentle. What the water will cut, it has cut; what it will leave, it has taken the edge off. The chamber’s big forms—the bulged walls, the cave-bellies, the stone lips, the odd, almost architectural cornices—have been held so long they feel inevitable. Even the guano has become part of this geology, its old layers compacting into something like shale, its younger layers yielding to a thumb, their bony goods hidden until insect-time gets to them.

The elevation is felt only at the mouth. Step outside and the world opens into a sheet of cliff and a sky so near it can tilt you. Thin air that scrapes your chest if you forget not to try to run. Out there the mountain is clean, pale, severe, all edges and long, old talus. Inside, against all expectation, is warmth you cannot see a source for, and density that takes your breath in a different way. The two climates share a seam in that narrow slot, and the chamber is sustained by being precisely where they meet.

This place’s oddity is not dramatic but accumulating. The mountain breathes—not in a poetic way but in the factual way of pressure: on some days you can hear the wind drawn through the throat slow as a huge animal sleeping; on others you feel the exhale as a small, steady wind pushing damp against your face though outside the world is still. There is a stratification to the air here as delicate as a pastry: a layer near the floor rich in carbon dioxide from fungi and rot where mouse-hearts that wander into it will begin to race and stumble; above that, the good air of motion where bats launch and return; above that another cool band near the upper pockets where a minority of swiftlets have trained their own tiny clicks against the stone to map themselves, and return to cup-nests they cemented years ago from borrowed saliva and the patience of repetition.

The world operates slowly under the clamour. Generations of bats learn the same approach to the mouth by the flow of resonance; the colony shifts not because of a decision but because one old anchorage exfoliates and the lack of grip propagates outward until, over months, the roost moves a meter to the left and the stone under the new place becomes darker with oil. Pools remember floods by their hems; stone grows a millimeter downward a decade; the glowworms wane in a season of dry and then return after three fogs because that is how the midge-hatch happens up on the plateau. When there is a catastrophe—a rockfall exacted by a lightning-split far above that sends stone through the throat like a single hard cough—it becomes a feature in the next state of balance: a new shelf for lizards, a new line in the flood, a new lip to hold a rung of water.

If people come at all, they come lightly, and the cave keeps no record. A pair of highland gatherers may lower themselves on a braided rope when the fledglings are heavy and too new to fly—hands and fiber and a quick throat-song, no iron, no marks in the stone. They carry away a small sack of meat and the smell of the place, and by the next rain the only trace is washed into the lower matrix and becomes one more ingredient in the manured, miniature garden at the entrance where the shrub twists in its nutrient-wind.

Rules to move by here: go slow; go with sound. Set your feet on the slick in such a way that you are one more addition to an old rhythm, not a new noise. Do not trust your eyes to tell you where the edge is; a lot of the edges are warm-and-moving. Know that everything here is near something else; that the same square meter may be three dozen homes; that a hand meant to steady you will make a weather of tiny panics in the floor. Breathe higher if you need to; the lower air will take you if you get low and tired. If you must make light, make it small and let it float; the bats will read it as a new star and avoid it. There is nothing here that was put here by hands, and yet everything is arranged; inhabit that arrangement as a guest.

Step back to the lip and look in one last time. The glow threads smoulder like a community of sleeping embers. Drips make constellations in the pools. The colony has lifted and is a living rope leaving the mountain’s mouth, making a sound big enough to be weather, while, along the edges where the light of evening thins to nothing, a few hardy blades catch the undeposited pieces of the day and begin to drink. Beyond, the ridge is knife and air; within, a crowded, old dark warms itself and goes on.
