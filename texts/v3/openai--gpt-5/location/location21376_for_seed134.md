Light comes first, thick and ordinary as daylight through a clear window, fractured into a thousand moving bars by the water above. Then sound arrives as weight. The place lives inside a loudness you wear like a coat, a constant pressure-hum that enters bones and teeth, the roar of falling water translated into tremor.

You are inside a bowl cut from newborn stone. Columns of dark basalt rise and meet in an open crown, an amphitheater the size of a cathedral nave. The rock is young—edges keen, joints still sharp with glass, hexagonal prisms only just released from their cooling order. No slime of age yet, no rounded patience; the surfaces carry a dry-looking glitter even underwater. Pale crusts of silica collect where the flow rests for a moment and then moves on, like hoarfrost that cannot melt. Rust-brown lenses bloom on ledges where iron wakes in oxygen, and black manganese whiskers bristle along cracks as if the stone is growing a beard.

Over the crown, a river stands on its edge. A sheet of water pours down from a higher ledge—sky not as air but as a moving wall: a permanent, cold cataract stretched across the opening. It drops as a single element and also as filaments and rags, splits into cords, rejoins, scythes past the pillars in clear, hard wedges. Each ribbon’s edge is invisible until it intersects stone, and then it becomes white with air, boil, and purpose. The fall beats the amphitheater into a grammar of forces—laminar here, savage there, dead water in tiny rooms tucked behind ribs of rock. The bowl’s geography is nothing but pressure and release.

Nothing green grows. No leaf, no lace of algae. The base of life is gray and orange and black and taste-of-battery: thin felts of microbes that eat metal and stone, harvest charge from fresh basalt the way animals lift heat from another’s skin. They arrange themselves in contour lines of chemistry along stain and seam, where gradients are mild and reliable. Everything above them is animal.

Every surface that can be held is held. The place is packed, near-touching, intimate with bodies. There is a family resemblance to all of them: a love of leverage, a knack for taking hold of flow and making it work. Where plants might have made a forest, the animals make an engine room.

On the upstream faces of columns, broad, pale creatures flare themselves like turbines. They are living impellers: rings of stiffened fin-edge that can splay or fold, turning the force that strikes them into a slow, patient rotation along a central spine. Their flesh is cool and hard with ceramic salts; every few minutes a line of cilia along their rims will stutter and arc, a blue-white leak of charge as they bleed off the current their piezo-bones build under strain. In their depths, chambers hum—gut as dynamo—moving water across stacked membranes seeded with bacteria that fix carbon from dissolved gas using the stolen electrons. They ignore the light. They run on shear.

Between them work the belt-worms. Each is a many-strapped loop of muscle and tooth-plate that cinches around stone and scours its skin, tongues on conveyor belts, raking the new films into their long throats. They move not in inches but in ratchets. A series of little clicks like distant percussion marks their progress; each click is a pawl of bone finding its notch, holding against the draft, advancing again. They shed grit like stars shedding dust. Where they pass, they leave bare glass that hazes over in hours with chemistry and begins again.

In the side eddies, the drum shells wait and turn. They look like squat jars grown from layered nacre and iron salts, and on their lids sit patterned plates that tremble in the water like speaker cones. The fall breaks here into a wash of frequencies, and the shells tune themselves to the stable ones. They turn those notes into work, flexing and releasing in time. The sound you feel in your ribs is inside them more intimately: they wear the noise as a second skeleton. When they feed, their lids simply widen their resonant bandwidths and the microbe-rich soup shakes itself into their mouths.

Ribboned up and down the plunge lanes, hinge-mantas milk the high-speed water. Their wings are not for flight but for braking; each is perforated with holes that open and close like gills, setting up standing eddies within the wing. They angle themselves until their hinge rates settle into the right slow rhythm. Young ones cling to their undersides like spare parts learning their trade, feet splayed on the very pattern of the pressure field that keeps their elders fed and fixed.

Every few arm lengths a different craft: grinder-crabs with spiral molars set in their stomach walls, that swallow pumice and bark their bodies warm by working it down; wire eels that braid and unbraid the hair-fine magnetite filaments on the rock, harvesting and tasting the field; shoulder-shrimp with claw-pairs made for fitting into bolt-shaped cavities in the columns and locking there so that the current cannot tear them free. Between the larger forms, a permanent storm of smaller lives: valve spindles, bubble ticks, scale moths that never rise through the water but skate it, all of them riding boundary layers and curling around vortices you can see only by what clings to them.

Predation, here, is sabotage. No one swallows anyone whole. Instead, a throat is clogged, a membrane fouled, a notch jammed. A set of teeth built for raking will spring across a rival’s conveyor and introduce a pebble; a filament will be cut; a hinge will be salted with grit that will not wash. The combat is close, slow, and extremely technical. Territories are not patches of ground but arrangements of flow—slices of the cataract mapped into possession by bodies. Two neighbors can touch and never quite compete if their parts take different pulses.

Nothing has been built in the sense that a separate intelligence chose, cut, and assembled pieces. Yet everything works with the blunt clarity of machines. There are shops without walls. Workbenches are lips and arms and rims of shell. Assembly lines are the river lanes. Waste is clean. Slag is incorporated—what is ground off here becomes substrate there. The whole chamber is a mill that remembers. When you trace a ridge of polished glass on a column, you are touching a ledger of mouths.

The rules of this world are not laws written once but continuities felt. Life and stone conspire wherever a gradient is reliable and not too cruel. Chemical hungers find their profits where iron awakens in oxygen and gives electron after electron to whoever holds the better wire. Mechanical hungers find their measure where the beating water can be leaned against just enough to keep a hinge warm, a gut moving. Time is almost still. The cataract has not changed its pitch in generations. Columns split slowly, power-lines of current drift and reform over months, not minutes. Births are timed to cycles of discharge, to the long coolings and warmings of strange seasons in a drowned highland. Death tends to be a quiet suddenness—the misplacement of a grip, a column’s thin edge giving up its spare angle, a sound that changes pitch and starves someone a stone’s throw away.

You can tell this amphitheater is high because the light is full-bodied and the pressure is kind to land lungs. When the fall splinters itself into mist at the topmost rim, the mist is thin enough that bubbles keep their roundness for a time instead of crushing into solution. If you swim up toward the sheet, a final film feels almost like weather against your face, as if you could surface into another air. Above the lip, drowned uplands probably go on and on—ridges like knuckles, bowls like this one lined in newborn glass, each with its own arrangement of work.

And listen: beneath the roar is a series of steady notes you would only call human for lack of a better word—standing tones tuned by bodies. They do not chant; they meter. The place plays itself and the players live by the beat. If there is a language, it is in the rate at which a hinge opens, the way a ring closes, the pitch a lid prefers at the cool hour. At dusk-only-by-angle, when the light above goes to cream and then to lead, almost nothing changes. The belts ratchet on. The mantas hold their stance. The impellers glow intermittently with little underwater lightning and then hush. On some far span of time that no one here measures, the cataract will move or fail, and the mill will buckle and crawl away to the next sure gradient. But for now the room stays a room, bounded by rock and falling sky, dense with bodies that take the place into themselves and make it turn.
