There is no sky here, only a ceiling that never resolves. Above you—if above can be trusted—hangs a thickness that drinks light, a vault of living black felted with leaf upon leaf, membrane upon membrane, a quilt of plant that has outcompeted the idea of open air. Warmth holds steady in that absence. The air is almost a liquid; every inhalation tastes of resin and wet stone, faint iron, a memory of salt. Moisture beads on every edge and lip. It is always just about to rain, and always only just condensing.

The ground is not soil. Footing—if anything like a foot existed—is on a continent of braid and pad and rib. Vast buttresses of dark fiber rise and knit, meeting and parting with the crispness of something very recently made. No leaf litter, no rot in the way expected; the dead is folded in and rewritten by the living. Where the web thins, young rock shows through: newborn planes of mineral, sharp as unblunted tools, banded with fresh films of precipitated salts. They shine with their own dampness. Water does not fall here in streams so much as runnel along pre-made paths: along the undersides of arches, down the inner faces of vascular grooves, through the capillary-thin stairwells the plants have grown into themselves. It represents itself as a world of tears—stone that weeps, bark that sweats, condensate endlessly choosing the easiest way down.

Every surface is in use. Broad, cold-veined laminae, leaf only by analogy, draw heat from the close air and persuade it to bloom dew. Tongues of tissue flick outward—slowly, always slowly—to harvest the beads and pull them into thirsty canals. Thick cords hum without noise, filled with sap dense as syrup, trundling its own cargo along peristaltic belts. At longer intervals than a heartbeat, but with exactly the same confidence, the big conduits flex and release. You only know because a faint pressure finds your eardrums and retreats again, a pulse of the world’s hydraulics keeping time across a flatland so wide it keeps its own weather and refuses the general sky.

The plants are everything: wall, roof, road, refinery, stomach, lung. They have diversified past familiar shapes into organs of intent. Some drape like curtains of bristles that snag and sort, combing the passing vapor for dissolved metal ions to tuck into storage nodes. Others are plates the size of plazas, slick as eel-skin but cooler to the touch, their undersides etched with channels that taste of the atmosphere, bind what is rare, let go of what is not. There are pillars with porous hearts that low-key fizz with reactions we are not meant to see, whispering redox bargains into the dark—drawn from rock and brine, funded by the permanent difference between wet warmth below and minute coolness above. This is not photosynthesis. There is no light to catch. Carbon is fixed under muffling leaf-roofs by means of gradients kept meticulously alive: chemical, thermal, electrical.

Here the industrial is alive, and the alive is industrial, and nothing is manufactured except by growth. The broad faces of condensers bend themselves imperceptibly, generating tiny voltages on every flex; piezoelectric nerves carry these inland. Whole galleries of tissue act as electrodes, set in pairs in the low brine sumps where the ground plant-layer dips to kiss the old sea. A current creeps between them with planetary patience. You can trace the routes in the dark by a feeling like weather about to change, a prickle along the arms of every vine. The products of these circuits are used without waste: a little hydrogen for a reducing pocket deep in the rib-cage of the plain, a little acid to etch nutrients from a shard of extruded basalt, a lacing of metal threaded into the xylem to stiffen and to store.

No one built this. Nothing wears the signature of tool and plan. But everywhere the landscape behaves like a workshop staffed by nerves and valves. Long conveyors of tissue move starch pearls by the million, not tumbling but handed along by pads that kiss and pass, kiss and pass, their pace a sacrament to continuity. Distillation happens in columns that are also trees: sap lifts through gradients of pore-size and polarity, shedding salts at one ring, sugars at another, heavier isotopes in a laughingly thin sheen at a third, until each layer of the column tells the story of the flow it chose to keep. The curtain-hair that filters air also hushes it. Even breath here is muffled.

Animals are an old rumor. Nothing scurries. Nothing rasps. There are no eyes on you. Reproduction is internal: some long slow shuffle of genes through graft and seep, through whispering plasmodesmata that bridge between strangers until they are not strangers anymore. When a plant wishes to travel, it lays itself outward. It learns a different tensor, a cousin chemistry to make a new type of stiffness, and the continent expands by the width of a thought. Spore and seed are ideas from some brighter epoch; here kin recognition is plumbing and calculus, and newness is budgeted against the obligation to keep the circuits running.

Because the system runs, everything is quiet. Sound becomes geology. The largest voice belongs to the rain that never falls, only gathers and is moved. Close by, you hear it as a lacework of ticks all around—dew jumping the last millimeter into the safe mouth of a tube. Farther off, there is a feeling rather than a tone: infrasonic plosives that move through columns the size of city blocks because sometime in the last hour the great sump to the west needed to exhale by two atmospheres. The sharpest sounds are mineral: the hairline crack when a newborn carbonate sheet across a trickle of concentrated brine decides to grow a little and relieves the tension of its own youth. Even these do not echo. The plant walls absorb and forgive.

The plain is low, low as if trying to stay close to whatever is old and cool left in the planet. Beyond the felt forest’s faint rises and deliberate trenches, there are no highlands. What little mineral emerges does so with an arrogance that only youth allows: flinty shelves that have never seen a thousand years of water, edges of lava that met the sea yesterday and were immediately wrapped and worked by rooting tongues, still black glass under their lime-white rind. The text of erosion has not had time to be written on them; the plants write instead, wrapping, plating, waterproofing, incorporating. It is not that there are no canyons here, but that they are inward-facing: cathedrals hollowed in living mass to give certain flows their head, not carved by an exterior river. The geography is not quite exterior at all. You are in the organ of a world.

The dark does not lighten. Every hint of glow is a byproduct, not an overture: a blue margin where a membrane is stressed and a few volts go to phosphor, a breath of foxfire green at a crack where enzymes are burning off the last of the day’s acid to keep the pH where the pumps like it. Even these are scarce and shy, more meter than lamp. Most illumination is tactile and chemical. Direction is a pressure field; distance is latency on a pulse that took a day to return.

Equilibrium is not static but committed. The flows have names in the plant’s language—if a language of periodic amplitudes and staffing schedules could be called names—and they do not miss their appointments. Variability here has been negotiated away, bartered down until only what is necessary to hold gradients remains. A sheet of condensers always holds eleven drops per square span before wicking; a pressure trunk lifts against a head of six atmospheres and never seven; the columns remember the isotopic signature they are meant to prefer and never forget. This is a forever made of routines so long lived they might as well be laws of physics. A storm would only be a system error. The world has not made one in a very long time.

Strangeness arrives not as spectacle but as a sense that the rules are different under your hands. Touch an exposed rib and a long way off something answers, the way a very distant furnace answers with a sigh you feel through the floor and the soles of your teeth. Blink, and your eyelids come away saltier than when they closed. There are places where leaves become plates become panes become slumped glass without ever stopping being tissue. There are arches where the weight of a continent has been accounted for by the torque of a million braided cords, every one aligned to share that burden like a prayer passed down a line. You can find a surface that refuses to wet, where the dew will not sit and every drop is flicked away, not because a spider lives there to hunt but because the condenser above does not want to waste the cold of its own skin.

On a human day, the landscape is the same from beginning to end. On a human lifetime, it will still be the same. But if you could hear in the units this place prefers—one tide’s thermal difference, one century’s adjustment to a new trace gas, the slow tilt of a gradient like a tone bending flat—you would hear the plant-plain work, continuously and without pause, toward a balance it has nearly achieved. The great circuits hum, soundlessly. The young rock loses its sharpness one atom-layer at a time and is recycled at once into the braided mass. The ceiling remains a ceiling. The warm air remains wet. The silence lasts, and if there is a sky anywhere, it is under your hands, in the slick and the grain and the small quiet thunder of a world-sized organism keeping house.
