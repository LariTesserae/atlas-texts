Climb until the trees let go. The air thins, temperature slips to a crisp cool that keeps breath visible in shade, and the light—white, hard—arrives with a kind of authority. Up here the world has been planed wide and level by fire and time and not much else. The ground is new basalt and vesicular glass, but you almost never see it. The rock is swaddled in living matter: a continuous, springing skin of plant that has turned the plateau into something soft-edged and damp.

It is the sound you notice first, long before the line of the rim reveals itself. A continuous, body-filling roar bears in from the far side of the open land. Wind piles up against the escarpment and spills, and the water that has gathered up here falls off the world in ribbons and sheets. The noise is not an event. It is weather. It flattens conversation into pantomime and sifts grit through clothing. Everything else—footsteps, snapping twigs, the thin tick of cooling metal—is incidental against that steady, cavernous thunder.

The plain itself goes on and on, an undulation of living green-marrow and tannin browns and quick, bright films of water. Vegetation is not something on the land; it is the land. The mat, five, ten hands thick in places, is made of interleaved stems and leaves and dead plant turned peat, a weave of fibrous sponge. You walk and it yields, a damp resilience that slicks boots with plant oils. In hollows where water idles the mat domes into slow-growing cushions—close-packed, moss-like organisms with starry, glossy tips—each mound an island that knows its shape and keeps it year to year. Between them run bright threads of rill and tarn: narrow, shallow trickles that have not yet decided to be streams.

There is stone beneath, raw and stubborn, but its personality is only beginning to show. Erosion is still in rehearsal. Drainage lines are sketched more than carved. Polygonal cracks from old cooling breaks make faint, straight hints that align pools in odd, hexagonal arrangements. Where the plant skin thins, the rock is wet to the touch—sweating, weeping—cold beads of condensed cloud sliding out of hairline seams to be caught by root and rhizome. In places the mat draws those tears into neat, brown cisterns, a thousand tiny menisci trembling bright as fish scales under the full-bore light.

Water is everywhere and nowhere deep. You taste it in the air, on your sleeves, in the wool. It film-coats every surface and then, without much urgency, leans toward the edge. The plateau is high and blunt; storms freight themselves up its windward face and unburden here. When it is not raining, it is precipitating some other way: ground fog forming at ankle height, blown sideways by a low, insistent wind, then erased by sun glare into a glitter that hurts to look at.

Because it is high, the sky is very near and very large. The horizon is clean and far, and the distances between incidents—the lone boulder, a shallow lake, a jutted rib of unburied basalt, the distant, skeletal lattice of a structure—are generous. You could walk a long while and touch very little that can be circled with two arms. The density of tall things is low. Presences, when they occur, stand alone and throw long, hard shadows.

Plants take up the volume left vacant by trees. There are no trunks; architecture is horizontal. The mat heaves to make low parapets that turn the wind, funnels it into louder spells, and quiet eddies. Low, thickened arteries of living tissue—the equivalent of buttresses—trace faint spines across the plain, binding pools and hummocks into something with coherence. The materials are simple and total: cellulose and water and sunlight. In the glaring noon the green goes blue at the edges, glossy with a skin of condensed vapor; in late day the whole surface bronzes into satin.

Fauna are sparse and small. You see skittering arthropods that live in the film between leaf and light, black water beetles ticking in sheltered pans, sometimes a high hawk smoothed motionless above the rim, then a long time of nothing that moves on its own. In the piled noise most animals have elected to be elsewhere. The occasional line of hoof prints freezes mid-plain and resumes hours later as if the animal had lightened into air over the worst of the wet.

Edges write the rules up here. The rim is a bright burn of motion, a kilometers-long drop where the plateau ends clean and falls into a blue-gray nothing veiled by spray. Water goes over in hundreds of separate syllables: narrow ribbons, braided veils, sudden sheets that have trained grooves into the brink. From a distance each fall is a white stripe pinned to the cliff; up close, each is an atmosphere of its own, with a local weather of chill draft and wavering rainbows. Even on the plain, far back from the edge, the roar is present enough that people who work here carry flags and lights, and the few structures blink against the brightness to declare themselves.

Industry lives lightly and far apart. A trio of fog-harvesting fields stand like half-finished scrims against the sky, black meshes stretched between low, open frames that drink cloud out of the wind. You see them first as geometries, then as sheets with a dark shine of wet, the drip collected into shallow troughs that snake off through the mat in low, careful gradients. The noise the meshes make is a coarse hiss, a paper-tearing whisper that the great roar eats before you hear it from any distance. Elsewhere a single pumphouse, boxy and galvanized, knuckles into a rise, its door chocked with a stone; its interior hum is intimate compared to the outside, a pelvic vibration in the floor that you feel rather than hear. A narrow-bore pipeline runs from its concrete sump—laid on skids so as not to kill the living skin beneath—heading inland to a storage cistern that squats like a dull gray lozenge. A skeletal tramline runs straight as a rule toward the rim, more a drawing than a machine, its carts infrequent: they bring in mesh and take out saturated filters, ferry coils of cable and canisters of preservatives that keep the harvested water from carrying too much of the peat’s brown into town.

The presence of these things has made its own small conventions. Do not cut the mat deeper than your hand. Step on the old boot-dark paths where the fibers have already submitted; the bright, springy places are new and may sink. Never trust a fold that drains toward the rim. Secure every loose panel; the wind does not take— it rips. Anchor rigs in rock, not in the living weave. In high sun, watch for glare; in low sun, watch for frost, even in late season, even beside a shining puddle. Carry the red flags, because even under a sky like a split pearl, people can vanish into the brightness and the noise.

The place changes slowly. The mat bulks and subsides by measures that make sense only if you live here long enough to mark a horizon against a nail in a door frame. Pools creep, harden, grow an edge, then silt into lawns. The living buttresses inch, year by cold year, tugging the plain into softer curves, redistributing everything, patient as snow. The rock opens itself in new hairlines; water finds them and teaches them the lesson of gravity. The fog field supports gather pale halos of lichen that thicken ring by ring. The pumphouse door rusts along its bottom edge, then more, and men scuff a brighter spot into the threshold where boots always land.

There is a particular smell that becomes the ambient of memory: wet cellulose, a little sweet, a little sour; iron; stone; the thin ozone tang that comes when the sun is absurdly bright and the air is scrubbed empty of anything warm. Under it all, water: unshowy, everywhere. The mat’s breath is cool on your palms when you kneel to press them flat and feel what the ground is doing. The sound is a weight across the shoulders, a pressure through the bones. The light is an instruction that every edge, no matter how low, must throw a shadow. The world here is a plain fact: plant and water married for a long arrangement atop a high new rock, everything else—people, metal, rules—scaled down small to live inside it without leaving much of a mark.
