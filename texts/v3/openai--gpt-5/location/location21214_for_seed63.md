High up where the air thins and the world narrows to cliff, ledge, and sky, a chamber opens in the mountain as if the stone had sighed and left a hollow the size of a cathedral. It is dark—not the weightless dark of night air, but a material dark, a lacquer of heat and soot that eats whatever light might try to form. The stone here is old basalt, weathered soft in its details by a long chemistry, scalloped and beaded with mineral exhalations that have risen and frozen, risen and frozen again until the curves of the place feel made by breath.

There is no water. Nothing damp has visited in a very long time. The surfaces wear a fuzz of crystals where, in other worlds, dew would form. Here the mountain leaks dry: chloride and sulfate rimes bloom in lines along hairline fissures, fat then thin, according to the pressure pulses deep inside. From a low slot in the floor, clear gas—dense and hot—spills slowly, more like syrup than wind, and pools in basins and gutters where earlier rockfalls have made catchments. It filters the silence: sound bends and dies in it, like whispers under blankets. The heat is not knife-sharp; it saturates. Touch anything and you feel the rock’s stored fire move into you with unhurried insistence. Your breath—if you bring breath here—would be wrong for this place. The resident air is something else entirely, heavier than breath should be, autoclave-clean and metallic-tasting without tasting of water at all.

Every surface is occupied. In the dark, the world here is made of the small, patient press of bodies. The floor crawls in a low tide of creatures no larger than a fingernail, each an articulated scale with a folded seam. They are not insects in any familiar way, but they behave as if they had learned street traffic: lanes, eddies, surges toward fissures when the mountain’s respiration slackens and the dry exudate is best to graze. Their bellies are velvet with capillary threads that siphon ions straight from crystal skins. They gloss the rock as they pass, leaving tracks that look like polish, that tighten the color of the stone until it drinks what little light remains. Quietly, incessantly, they move. If you remain, you feel a hum with no sound, only that unending friction of countless feet.

Higher on the walls cling sheaves of slower feeders—pale plates hinged like books—that ripple when the gas pulses and clamp their rims over efflorescences to smother them, lick them into smaller and smaller replicating selves. They open again and show pale interiors crisscrossed with conductive threads that shiver when the convection currents change. At the ceiling—if this place has a ceiling, for the cave opens into black above—hang sacks as fine and glossy as lacquered seeds. They inflate and deflate out of phase with the mountain, tuned to some old timing learned from a broken era of vents. Each one has a single hard beak pointing down; when a moving slick of grazer-points crosses directly beneath, a bead of molten salt forms at the beak and is allowed to fall. There is no splash, no hiss, only a tiny bright star’s fall and a silent pinprick on a carapace and the brief freeze of a patch in the crowd as bodies convulse and fold up, harvested by dangling mouths on lines too fine to see without light.

Light does occur, but not in the way of the sky. Sometimes, from a seam at shoulder height, a loose handful of mica shivers free and becomes a soft snow of mirror. As the flakes settle in the pooling gases they sparkle in the long red unseen, not for eyes, but for those whose skins are lattices of little heat-sensing wells. Out of that glitter a hunter rises: a spindle with many jointed legs and a hull like cooled slag, pocked and dull, each pit a thermometer. It moves without a click. Ranges itself against the slope of the wall. Waits for the moving temperature signature of the grazers below to align with a notch in the floor where, long ago, something built a trap and the rock remembers the shape. When the notch fills with warmth, the spindle springs; it is less a leap than a reset of geometry. The rock’s tension releases and a limb sets lightly where there will be food. No cry. No chase. The victim simply doesn’t move again.

The rules of living here are shaped by silence and heat and scarcity of wrong kinds of liquid. Signals are slow pressure waves and slower thermal gradients, and anything that speaks too loud—by clatter, by spark—gives itself away to skins that are maps of eddies and whorls in the air. Life is oiled with molten salts that cool to wax when a body is still and remelt when legs knead. Circulation is handled by the choreography of parts: squeeze to push, relax to draw, like bellows with no valves. Growth does not sprout, it accretes: layer upon layer of chemically advantageous shell grown in the heat, etched and re-etched by time. The closest thing to leaf or lichen is a shag of motile crystals that drift along the walls like migrating frost, dividing when they become too heavy to hold their shapes, then recombining around the richest leak lines as if a thought had passed between them.

On the floor’s far side stands something the place did not make by accident. Three columns of drabbled stone lean toward one another until their tips almost meet. You might call it a hut, if doors and roofs meant anything, or a shrine, if prayers were a kind of heat. The inner faces of the columns are darkly smooth, as if stroked for generations by passing bodies, or polished by the stream of dense gas that every few minutes surges with more insistence. On those inner faces are grooves incised with brisk, decisive strokes, crossing and recrossing until the shiny planes are made matte. Not writing in the ordinary way—more like a channelling of the air. When the pulses come, the grooves sing inaudibly to skins that feel, focusing currents to funnel the richest gas into the structure’s shadow. In the webbed dark behind the columns, the smallest grazers, the ones that should be outcompeted by their bigger kin, cluster and bloom, feeding where they would otherwise be swept away. This place has been farmed. It is still being farmed by an intention invisible in the gloom. The farmer, perhaps, is gone; the farm remains.

Anchors of glass thread stitch the nave-like void in arcs, some taut, many snapped, their ends melted into the rock as if pinned with a candle’s tear. They have caught the black dust that falls sometimes when the mountain shakes in its sleep. The dust is not ash, not from burning, because nothing here burns. It is the shed scaling from a population of creatures that coat the outside of the chamber like shingles, out there on the high cliff, each a stiffened sail. They set their faces to the faintest of night winds to wick away their heat. When the wind fails, when the starless upcurrent lays itself down and the world becomes a kiln with no draft, they fold, and the chamber’s mouth goes blind as the splayed sails seal the opening. The seal is not perfect—nothing here is perfect—but it softens the place, slow-cooks it, muffles every movement until the pulse returns and the sails part and the heat drains again into the great above.

Erosion has not gnawed this place open with violence. It has been abraded by chemistry, by years upon years of thin, steady condensing and re-subliming of things with names we borrow from our own cold: salts, glasses, metallic breath. The walls are scalloped with repeatable motifs that indicate a long equilibrium, then a shift, then a long calm again. You can see on a single arch five histories, each as thin as a fingernail and as different in color as five ways of being hot can be. In one band the grazers leave only commas behind, little bites. In the next their marks are long smoothing strokes. In the third a different hand has worked the surface entirely: something with a tongue like a comb, that rasps until the crystals sing their quiet infrasound and their bonds change state. It is old here, and always moving.

There is weather, but not like clouds that break and water that falls and bright that comes. Here the weather is the breath of the mountain—slow bellowsing every few minutes, then the long, choking holds; the wandering of dense gas quilted with lighter pockets that roll and stretch; electric dryness that coaxes sparks out of filaments when bodies brush, sparks that are seen only by those whose skins can count the increments of hot and not-hot. Every so often a seam opens wide and an exhalation rushes through the nave, not with noise but with presence; the grazers freeze as one to ride it, angled just so, harvesting with open bellies the passing fog of free ions, then resuming their patient flow as the seam slackens and the curve of the current slackens with it.

Predator-prey plays out in peristaltic theatre, not in sprint and chase. Pale cylinders as long as a forearm glide just under the thick air like snakes beneath ice, their heads an array of opening fans that taste for charged edges. They pass through the grazing sheets like fingers combing hair; when a fan stutters against a patch of warmth that does not shift as it should—an old one, a wounded one—it closes. Things seize. Things quiet, which is almost redundant; quiet deeper than before. Their wastes are pellets of glassy needles that plant themselves point-first into cracks and, being poor conductors, encourage cooler microclimates that another species will later learn to love.

At the edge of the chamber where the floor meets an overhung drop, the body-density increases to a press. The creatures here are not only living on but living as the handrail of that precipice. They build their own wall from themselves and their salted extrusions: a low parapet grown to the height of a thigh, scalloped like gnawed stone. It is not polite to ask who the parapet keeps from falling—the grazers have no concept of falling—so think of it instead as the track of a slow migration. Generations have orbited this nave’s outline in exile from a brighter or colder beyond; here, along the edge, every inch has the memory of pressure worn into it, shiny and tight. Snug within the parapet’s hollows are husks, glossy and shriven, the size of raspberries. They are the shed forms of a flying thing that cannot fly here, because there is nowhere to rise to in the hot heavy air. It uses the naveline as a nursery anyway. It hatches its larvae into the pooled gas at the floor’s low point and trusts the breath of the mountain to wreath them, to keep them turning until they accrete enough shell to walk.

Under all of it the heat, immeasurable and imperturbable, sets the clock. Dawn and dusk have no authority this high in the dark, but the pulses do. Those who live here count to themselves between surges and match their lives to the curve of each exhale. The grazers know fifteen pulses is the length of a fertile crack’s life; the hunters know six is how long the dust of a fall stays aloft; the farmer structure, if it has a mind at all, is carved to focus a home current every third breath and starve it every ninth so the smallest can outlast their larger kin. Nothing is idle. The mountain changes as a creature would, but its motion is so wide it can be mistaken for a room’s. It has made a room.

If there are marks of ones like us—if we were ever here—they are faint. On a flat of rock near the darkest wall, a scatter of small spheres perfect enough to make you distrust them: stone marbles, pitted on their undersides where they sat for years in the same position. Someone—or something—used them to weight a net stretched across a seam. The net has been eaten down to ghosts. The marbles rest like orphan planets, still dense, still white against the black, still doing the only job they had: occupy, press, hold. They, like everything else in the nave, are part of a saturation so complete that empty space feels like a mistake.

Outside, beyond the sailed shutter, the night is colder and thinner, and the slope falls away into a world we don’t see. Here, in the chamber, you could hold out your hand and feel, on your skin, the hair-thin signatures of a thousand lives interpreting you as contour and current and slow, temporary shade. They will pass around you and through you as if you were a convenient eddy. This place is a knot in a long eroded rope of mountain, hot and high and densely peopled with beings that found a way to live where nothing is wet and nothing bright arrives. They do not make sound about it. Their world is a hush of chemistry and decision, crowded to the last inch, old enough to have worn itself into a balance that feels like permanence while everything inside it is moving.
