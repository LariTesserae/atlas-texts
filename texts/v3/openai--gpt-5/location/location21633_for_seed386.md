The wall rises before anything else registers: a continent of stone tipped up on edge, slick with its own sweat. In the heat, the cliff’s face glows a shade darker than day, striped by flow-lines like muscle. Every crease runs with water. It spills as veils, beads, and threads; it gathers into drapery you could put your arm through, and into coin-bright rivulets that cling to the vertical with the stubbornness of moss. The air at the base is cooler by a degree or two but denser; it presses on the eardrums with humidity. Drip rhythms tesselate the silence, close and far, so many points of falling that silence feels carved by them rather than interrupted.

The ground is as much root as soil, and as much sponge as root. Ferns taller than a person lean out, fronds freighted with condensate. The earth yields a little, not greedily like a bog but slow and reluctant, as if it has been pressed all its life and has learned to endure. The smell is tannin and ozone: black-tea waters steeped in leaf acids, underlit now and then by a high, metallic tang that comes on days when the sky is rehearsing lightning out over the basin beyond. A thousand individual small presences assemble into a single dense impression: palms layered with bromeliads layered with lichens, ants crossing colonies, darting lizards flicking dew from petals, small birds slipping in and out of the cool that gathers wherever the cliff throws a partial shade.

Climb, and the rock proves old and clever. It is built of stacked ledges and pockets sculpted by ages of warm rain, a karrenland that knows how to hold and how to let go. Handholds are all water-rounded; alcoves are glazed with iron-oxide varnish; weeping seams knit crusts of travertine that have been decades in the making. The stone is poor in nutrients, almost pure quartzite with only a whisper of impurity, which means everything that grows here has learned to catch the sky. Leaves curl into cups. Vines make nets that intercept mist. Even the beetles are dew-traps, their backs beaded before dawn; their first meal is the water they carry.

The top is not a dry plateau, and it is not level. It is a broad sponge lifted high into the heat, a quilt of shallow pools and heath and slickrock runnels all running to the edge. Step from the last ledge and your foot lands in a world that holds water close to the skin, not in lakes and rivers but as a film that moves. The velvet moss that coats the stone gives underfoot like wet felt; its resilience is treacherous if you stop trusting it. The pools are black with tannins and the dissolved tea of centuries of leaves; peer down and they appear depthless, but a hand dipped in finds bottom at a palm’s length and a second hand’s length of soft peat below that. Each saucer of water has its tenants: a lily no bigger than a coin and a frog no larger than a thumbnail that matches it; darts of water-boatmen; a patient, carnivorous cup leaf that has been slowly digesting moths since before you were born.

There is no building here in the way most landscapes are built by hands, but there is infrastructure, completely natural and tireless. The mountain runs its engine daily. In the morning the stone warms, and air, finding no shade, convects. Updrafts braid along the cliff faces; white threads of cloud snag on the highest, then bulk up. The vegetation opens its stomata with the sense of a shift whistle; millions upon millions of tiny valves breathe out water vapor in a single hour, and together they are an industrial evaporator field. By midday the cloud that has self-assembled over the crownlands is an anvil, bright-edged and opaque. The electricity of it is literal. When the cathedrals of cloud pass low enough that their bellies tangle with the high ridges, leaves begin to hum with a charge you can taste in your molars. On some days, at some edges, tufts of grass and the tips of pitcher plants wear cold blue halos of brush discharge—St. Elmo’s fire for the ground-anchored. The first lightning strike is not a sound; it is a flash that wrings a sound out of you.

The storms are hard where they happen and local: the mountain prints its own weather. This is a closed-loop plant working on solar input and gravity output. It feeds its dripping walls and its caves of rain behind the falls; it feeds the perched aquifers laced through the upper stone that leak month after month from a single hour’s downpour. After the violence of it, there is rinsed quiet. You hear the forest reset itself: water running in fifty different registers, drops pinging off fern-ribs, the low poured-honey sound of a sheet of wet slipping from a ledge, the tiny elastic clicks of quartz contracting as the skin of rock cools.

The economy of nutrients is strange and tight. So much is green, and so much of that green is a trap. Pitchers and sundews and butterworts make up for what the stone can’t give. Their hues are saturated enough to look like a different spectrum when a sunbeam lands: lacquered burgundy, milk-bottle green, petioles like licked glass. Between them run runners of wiry shrubs with small leathery leaves that have learned thrift. There are bromeliads everywhere, each the size of a kitchen basin, leaves clenched into rosettes, holding liters of water. In every one of those cups, a self-contained city: mosquito larvae, tadpoles that never see anything but that individual sky, threads of mineral film built by iron bacteria that feed on the dissolved whisper of the rock’s blood. Primary production by the square meter, scaled up to plains’ worth by repetition. If there is any hint of industry here, it is in the replication and scale: a process perfected and then multiplied until the entire surface is a facility.

Fauna find ways into this high, wet, hot stability. Tiny frogs chirp with metallic notes, a hundred distinct species so alike to your eye you doubt your senses until you hear how different they are. Dragonflies out of proportion to their insect prey patrol the still pools; when the sun comes through, their wings throw oil-slick colors in panes. A snake as slender as a vine works the margins of the pitchers, tasting the air for luckless insects that have overcommitted themselves to nectar. Hummingbirds learn private routes between flowering epiphytes and keep to them like train schedules. There are flights of swifts that live on the updrafts along the walls and are half rumor, the flicker of ink-dark arcs against lighter rock, and there are little mammals you meet only by sign: a fig with precise bite-marks, a soft-pressed path in moss the width of a wrist, the scatter of seed shells by a root. Nothing large lives long up here except the constant: the ants, present as weather. They do not have monumental hills, only veins through the sponge, neat and black, rearranging the forest floor in a quiet perpetual conveyance. When they cross water they make their own bridges with their bodies, bead-chains of motion drawn tight across an inch of blackwater.

Edges make rules. Here one of them is about charge. The plateau is a capacitor: dry stone under wet film, quartz tuned to store and release. On afternoons before a storm, if you linger unshaded at the highest point, the hairs on your knuckles will stand up. Birds know; they fly lower, and you watch them shear off away from the rim as if there is a pane of glass they can see and you cannot. Another rule is about footing. Runnels that look frictioned and safe have been given their polish by centuries and are treachery in the first fine rain; crackled patches of more matte rock are grippier even when soaked and know your weight. One more: never trust a black pool’s silence. It is an organ stop on the mountain’s instrument, and it may begin to speak—frogs calling up rain—just as you decide it is empty.

The scale is mid-range open: enough of the world is visible from any point that you can orient by large things. To the west, another table of rock stands, a hop at a vulture’s scale but a day’s thought at yours, its own umbrella of weather piling over it in the same shapes at a slightly different tempo. To the east, the cliff you came up reappears, but different now in reverse: scarps that curve with their own weight, fans of green where seeps are longest lived, iridescent threads where algae have found everywhere the water finds a new path. The horizon isn’t straight; it humps with islands of green set on black stone, cloud pinned over each like labels. You could look long enough and notice that their cycles are out of phase, that one is just now bright while another is already wreathed and muttering under its daily anvil. The rhythm of the region clicks like a room of looms, each running its bolt a little offset from the next.

Heat is fact. You feel it on skin as a firm hand; the sun is not cruel here so much as indisputable. Even when cloud covers it, there is no relief: the sky-skin is a lid, and the wet under it holds warmth close. It is a green heat, full of plant breathing and damp stone. The hottest places, paradoxically, are small, temporary patches: dark lichen crusts on south-facing lips where flies gather, the metal-rich streaks where a seam cuts daylight and flashes back more of it than the paler rock around. The coldest are always in motion: the line of a fall; the skin of a seep; a cave of scalloped stone two body-lengths back under a tottering roof of rain-dark blocks where the air never changes temperature because it is always giving it away with water.

There are the caves of reach and the caves of rumor. Behind the bigger veils of water, notches and halls have been carved where the flow throws itself sideways in heavy-weather seasons. They are wet rooms that echo only with their own small noises, where your breath smells like pennies and the undersides of ferns hold galaxies of spores. Deeper still, to which there is no easy way, there are rumors in the biology of footlong crabs that carry gardens of algae on their shells and taste only the microfilm of iron as it seeps. Armies of eyeless fish that live their whole lives in a single boulder’s interior, in the blackness of bottle-green stone, in the small but continuous slow river of water that moves through pores too tight to be seen but wide enough to wear down. You will never meet them, and they will never meet the sky that tunes everything up here to the same four notes: heat, wet, height, and time.

Time is the last presence, thick and near. The rock has been this way long enough to have found its balances: erosions slow enough to become architectures, vegetations that have iterated themselves into elegant redundancies, waters that know their courses by memory. The unusual lives in little margins: the way your hand tingles if you reach out into the air just ahead of a strike, the way some mosses glow gray-blue in that deep-sky light you get between thunderheads, the way a line of ants will stop moving, every third body lifting its abdomen briefly as if answering a pulse that only that caste can hear. It isn’t a place of spectacle unless you count its entire operation as spectacle. It is a plant, an engine, a green factory for water and sky, running on heat, making weather, making drip, making life in high density across a broad floor, its only noise the quiet complicated work of things that have been doing this forever.
