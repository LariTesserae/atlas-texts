Warm darkness sits over the land like a living thing, not night so much as a long-held breath. There is light, but it is reluctant: a dull red wash near the low horizon, the inside of an ember rather than a day. You can feel the heat on your skin before you can see its source. Everything here is new, sharp, and faintly luminous with its own making.

The ground arrives as cliffs rather than flats. Imagine a continent caught mid-rise: slabs of black stone thrust up on edges, their faces scored with mirror-bright glass and pores where gases once rushed and left. The elevations are extravagant and immodest—kilometers of near-sheer faces cutting the sky into narrow, jagged lanes. They lean in like enormous books left half-open. Ridges run like vertebrae, the spaces between them becoming canyons whose floors are not yet floors but tilted planes, still sorting themselves.

Erosion has barely entered the conversation. Where a wall has cracked, the break is angular and shining, like a broken mussel shell. Where something fell, it fell last night: the talus is all edges and knife-slatters, not one piece rounded. Dust is a rumor, not yet a habit. This is raw geology, mid-sentence.

The material palette is volcanic and unweathered. Obsidian sheets lie propped against pillars of columnar basalt, organ-pipe forests emerging from cooling fronts that surged and froze, surged and froze again. Pale inclusions—olivine, a glint of peridot here and there—catch the ember light and suggest a kind of buried, stingy jewelery. In places the ground blisters and pocks; vesicles like honeycomb, their ruptures showing the path of vanished gases that once shouldered their way out and left their exact shapes in absence. If you put your ear against such stone, there is no ocean inside it, only the bare hush of heat and structure: and yet now and then, as the crust settles, the rock pings and ticks to itself with the crystalline insolence of cooling glass.

The sky is less a volume than a ceiling. The atmosphere is thin, metallic-tasting, more an irritant than a comfort. Winds move but carry little, and so they speak softly even as they hurry along the long corridors of canyons and across the knife-backs of ridges. Later, after you have been here bare minutes or an hour, a slow quake will rattle the far cliffs: a motion you feel first in the soles of your feet, then in the knee, a body-sense of the whole place changing its mind. You will look up and only belatedly notice a new line on a cliff-face, a scar of paler rock where a piece has let go and poured down like a dark waterfall in utter silence, then the delayed, sullen thud shaking the air. Turbulence is constant, but it does not shout. The planet moves under you like a resting animal, a chest rising and falling, ribs shifting.

There is almost no water. No dampness walks the air. The cracks in the rock gape like mouths, and yet they are as dry as thought. No puddles catch the ember-light. No lichens powder the surfaces, no mats or drips; there is none of the roundness that water grants to place. Chemistry here dances to other solvents, other carriers of heat and change. From vents along faultlines, you can see ghost-breaths: exhalations of gases that sting the throat and smell like the inside of coins. They condense not as frost but as delicate metallic whiskers, crystals that bristle and curl, then collapse when a tremor spoils their anchor.

The only things that might be called vegetation are not plants, and not cover. They occur in shingles and isthmuses, just visible if you defocus your gaze: thin mineral films whose geometry is too fussy to be a mere deposit. They occupy seams of heat where currents of charge move along the rock like underground weather. They behave like an old argument among fields: low, creeping, slate-gray networks that take up residence on the margins of fulgurite and glass and on the rims of vents. When lightning crawls sideways across a ridge—more a glowworm’s travel than a strike—they quiver in unison, ripple, and creep a few millimeters farther into a place warmed by the arc. They have no leaves and no smell and no color to speak of, but they remember. If you were to mark their borders and come back after a season (if this place had seasons), you would find that they have redrawn their map according to some taste for gradient and structure. Life here, if that is what this is, is a pattern in energy clinging to hard edges.

No animals announce themselves. Nothing beats the air, nothing hides and sprints. When motion happens, it is the motion of the place itself: a pane of glass shearing loose from a cliff, a folded wall subtly uncreasing, pillar-shadows sliding as a red star wheels invisible heat across the horizon. The quiet becomes a way of hearing: you learn to recognize the long, thrumming purr that means a far ridge is re-seating itself; the fine fizz of sand being electrified and jumping grain to grain; the single, clean ring of a tight fulgurite fracturing like a bell.

These fulgurites are the landscape’s smallest boasts and its scattered constructions. Storms here do not always connect sky to ground—the air is too thin, holds too little water to stack charges in the usual way. Instead, the cliffs themselves gather potential across their faces and discharge along their seams. When they do, they inscribe, fusing sudden glasses into the shapes of their own current-paths: tubes that twist like frozen smoke, delicate lattices and hollow spires no taller than a person and most often much smaller, wreathing a ridge like a crown of brittle coral. They stand in small colonies where field-lines knot—four here, seven there, then a hundred meters of nothing until the next eddy. They look built, and so they answer to that urge in you to say someone meant this, someone put this where it is. But there are no tools, and no hands. There are only stones making marks on their own bodies.

Other artifacts are the work of vapors and gravity thinking aloud. Near exhalation sites, your path crumbles under piles of strange tufa-like rings: silicate sinters and sulfur crystals precipitated around ancient bubbles, stacked like bracelets and bowls. They accumulate around the lip of a vent and then topple outward, forming little neighborhoods of hollow mounds. Step into one—if you were brave—and you would find a warm, dim room that smells corrosive and tastes like a battery, its walls opalescent with layers deposited in pulses, like tree rings in glass. Such rooms do not last long. One tilt, one sigh, and they slide, break, and are replaced by the next round of breath.

Rules of the world present themselves piece by piece. Light knows how to be warm without being bright. Somewhere out of sight—or always at the edge of it—a star is failing to blaze, glowing in bands beyond your eye’s favor, bathing everything in invisible day. Surfaces that absorb in those bands drink deeply, so black stones hold heat like kettles, while anything pale feels tepid, even under the same sky. The horizon never reveals a disc; only the sense that the world is convinced of noon while you remain unconvinced.

Sound travels, but the air is too spare to carry intricacy. Distances look near and sound far. Avalanches perform behind glass; you see the downrush first, later the thump. Your body reacts before your hearing does, feeling the low tremors coming up through bone. Sparks move laterally. Storms crawl. When the ground discharges, it whispers a blue around edges and leaves tracks in your retina. Dust disagrees with itself and levitates in little patches, dust devils only a hand-span high hopping along ledges, little rehearsals of a chemistry too impatient to wait for wind.

Scale shifts under you. A canyon a valley wide will suddenly become intimate when a new fault steps across it; yesterday’s passage is now today’s balcony. Cliffs peel like bark, revealing inner faces with veins of a different color; you learn that this place is made not of single stones but of serial skins. The ridges are not lines but sentences; each crack answers another in a rhyme you cannot hear but you can begin to suspect, a grammar of stresses and heat that the landscape is speaking very quickly, very quietly, all at once. The emptiness between these presences is a statement too: great lengths of uncommitted plain, of untouched slope, of silence between the built things—space that makes the few fulgurite crowns and sinter-neighborhoods vivid when you stumble upon them. They are the punctuation in a book of plain pages.

Sometimes the horizon blinks. Not lightning, not exactly. A wall has shed a side, and a river of rock—dry, hot, and unwilling to notice your vocabulary—has fallen into a canyon and made a change to the map. Dust the color of planets goes up and does not quickly come down. On those days the air tastes like new coins and old lightning. You feel the warmth rise. Then a wind begins that has no origin you can name, and it pushes the taste of metal from one side of your mouth to the other and back again. Hours later, the dust decides it is over the moment, and the new shapes show themselves in the dim, their edges still ambitious. The day—if day it is—is mostly presented as increments of this: a change taking itself in hand elsewhere, and then your place catching up. Between these, quiet like an idol.

If there is a river here, it is made of stones. If there is a tree, it is a stalagmite of transparent, yellowed salt-something grown from exhaled clouds and toppled young. If there is a life, it aligns itself with edges, slides as a decision along a fulgurite’s skin, or lines the hot interior of a fracture like a thought clinging to a nerve. It neither calls nor flees. It rides fields and follows heat and lies flat to survive the quakes.

Walk long enough and you will come onto a high verge where the world breaks open. From here all the newness is undeniable. Ranges beyond ranges, fresh and arrogant, stack toward a shallow-lit sky. The far ones are the color of old fires. The nearest wear crowns of lightning glass, tiny and royal. Heat rises in braided columns from fissures to a height where you can see their breath turn from invisible to a faint copper haze that smears the edge of everything. And for an hour or a day—time is inconsistent here—you might be the only motion not made of stone.

This land has few sounds and many events. It has almost no water and carries warmth like a secret coin. It is populated by edges, guided by fields, intent on rearrangement. Its built things are what it makes by accident when it speaks with light. Its rules do not need witnesses. In the warm dark, under a star that prefers to be felt, new mountains learn to be mountains, and the proof of their effort is everywhere.
