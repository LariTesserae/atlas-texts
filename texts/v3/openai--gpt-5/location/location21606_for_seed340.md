Beneath a lid of pale mineral—salt, ash, and something that rings like glass when struck—the world opens into a warm, bright underland grown rather than excavated. There is no soil, no film of water on anything, no damp anywhere; the air is dry as rubbed amber, astringent with resins and hot dust. Yet everything is alive. Plant is not a separate presence here; it is the floor, the walls, the buttresses, the long vaults and interlaced bridges. The way opens for kilometers in all directions, a region-sized honeycomb of halls and chasms and pillared naves, steadily shifting, steadily remade.

Light arrives as columns. It is taken from the surface by pale trunks that pierce the crust and split into hard, translucent panes. Whole galleries glow like afternoon through sugar glass. Along each luminous rib, veins of crystalline leaf catch the light, thicken it, and pour it farther on; where these veins knot, the space suffuses into a level, shadowed day. The brightness isn’t pure—it is stained by amber and iron, greened here and there by pigments that are not chlorophyll but something deeper and more metallic. Close to the light-funnels it is hot; step aside into side chambers and the warmth becomes bearable, a comfort on the skin, if the skin can handle the dryness.

The plant’s substance is grid and muscle. It is fiber drawn from salts and silica and long-chain oils, a lattice that hums when it takes a load and rings like porcelain if you rap it. The floors are woven in wide mats, cords on cords, with spring underfoot; the walls are not smooth but plated in tessellations that can open into louvers or knit shut as if a zipper ran through them. The ceilings are catenary ribs, white to ochre, studded with needle spicules that grow and retract. You can see the growth happening. A seam on the floor fattens as tubes beneath peristaltically crawl; a new arch unfurls like a pressed flower brought into heat; a mouth you mistook for a niche tightens closed, its inner surface hazy with fresh mineral bloom. Nothing here is old. Erosion has not yet written its patience on these rooms; what “wear” exists happens by teeth and scraping tongues and rushes of hot dust.

The air moves in pulses, and the pulses are sound. Every few breaths, a deep percussive beat courses the vault—foamvalves in the plant’s heart walls slamming and opening, pressurizing a nonaqueous sap-gas that drags atmosphere along with it. The beat is percussion enough to feel in the ribs, but that is only the fundament; on it is laid the city’s mixed orchestra. Animals stridulate and drum, the plant itself rasps and chatters as panels rub and release, the fiber ribs sing at their own long resonant tones, and countless small, hard things scuttle and collide. It is deafening in the way a million cicadas are deafening; the sound has direction and current and topography. Step into a side hall and the dominant frequency will change, dropping from high hiss and crisp chitter to a velvet rotary thrum. Entering a chamber is like entering a sound’s body.

There is no water here—no liquid that runs in beads, no trickle, no dew—but there are other flows. Solvents with peppered, bitter smells vanish as quickly as they appear; a droplet falls from a seam with a sound like a pin striking stone, flashes coolness, and is gone as vapor. Bodies here do not drink; they wick. Mouths are felted and tongues are velvet mats; things lick the air, not to take moisture but to strip out resins and salts. Where a leak of actual water—alien, sweet-smelling—has been tried, the plant blackens and blisters away from it. A single drop is an injury. The rules are absolute: dryness is life. Moisture is caustic as an acid.

Follow the light-trunks and you find the great plazas, cathedral-broad, their floors stitched with ridges that branch and rejoin like dried river deltas. The ridges are conveyors. In their channels, a living meal of spore-dust and fine shards and aromatic crumbs rides the period of the percussive pumps. Grazers swarm over it very much like mammals and very much not: low, many-legged bodies plated in salt crystal scales, their undersides grown with abrasion pads that burnish the floor glossy as they pass. Behind them come raspers the size of cups and the size of carts, with beaks that throw off glitter with every bite. Overhead, thin things ride the breath—membranes stretched over ring-bones, pilot kites that tack from pressure wave to pressure wave, dipping to snatch the emanations from open plant pores. Predators detonated by air slam shoot like clay cones from hideouts in the walls, tumble into nets of their own filament, and are hauled back by coiling bands. Vermicular augers—living screws—vanish shoulder-deep into the plant’s softer joints, then reappear at a right angle six paces away. Between all this motion run rivers of minute life: whiskered mites each carrying a crystal shard like a monastic tablet; bead-bodied, chain-bodied, accordion-bodied things; every surface teems, so crowded that the line between animal and dust blurs.

The plant is not passive architecture. Its valves respond to touch, yes, but also to pattern. The cacophony is not only the incidental racket of feeding and fleeing; it is the control language of the place. Knots of creatures gather to drum on rib and louver in sequences that are echoed by other knots farther on, and after the third repetition a run of shutters along a wall unfurls to release a spurt of solvent, or a cascade of tendrils drops from the ceiling to ensnare paper-light seeds, or a whole section of mat-floor tightens and rises, presenting a new incline to divert a grazing flow. Chant a rhythm against a load-bearing arch and you can slow the pumps in your quarter hour by hour. Mis-sound, and a door clenches on you without malice. The world is built to be handled by sound and the shock of bodies; silence is interpreted as absence, as a lack of claim. Where things go quiet for long enough, the plant grows shut over the quiet.

Here and there, among the natural palaces and crawling markets of life, the signs of someone’s hand. Small, not many, and never intended to last. A low wall of knapped salt plates, each edge worked to a fine chisel-bright line. A trio of tuned ribs hammered into a floor seam, their tones forming a sequence that keeps an airway in a trickle-open state. Tethers braided from glassy fiber to make a safety from sudden wind; a ladder of hooked spicules set into a vertical shaft, barely a suggestion of a stair. In a recess, bowls from fired plant-ash clay, their interior facings rough to catch powder—no glaze, nothing that water would once have demanded. A mask of woven stiff filaments with layered baffles, an ear-shelter against the worst of the valves’ concussion. No iron, no copper shine; the rare metallic glints are pyrite and micaceous, secured into tools as teeth are set into a jaw. This is a hand-tech world: whittled from hard salt and bonelike plant, sewn with sinewy fibers drawn straight out of the city’s own ligaments, tuned with hammers, made to be played upon as much as climbed over.

Smell is almost taste here: hot, solvent, resinous; a pepper of things boiled off from the plant’s breath and the animals’ abrasion. The spark of static is constant. Brush a rib too fast and a violet arc whips your knuckles. Some animals culled it into defenses; you’ll see a lizard-slick thing pause, lift, and crackle its scales together until dust pops and drifts fall away. Others long-haired in electrostatic plumes that comb out the air of micron-thin food. One broad step more and you’re in a gallery where the plant is new, wet-shining with a slick that dries as you watch; here, the geology is embryonic, the ossature barely densified. You can push a finger into the floss of its fabric and leave a lasting dent. Farther along, a thunder of structure—arches thickened, floors tempered by passages of a thousand feet—tells where the old routes lay before they were closed and sealed. There are places where openings are being born. The plant’s pressure thrums, a seam buckles, then bursts—a door opening like a membrane peeled from fruit—and what had been a blank louvered panel is suddenly a throat with warm breath.

Though under the crust, there are weather events. Not clouds, not rain. Pressure storms. The pumps will synchronize and you will see motes and fragments lift in a slow boil; a low ululation builds, ribs begin to sing, and then the place goes over: a gust-front of warm dry air plows the hall; spider-kites bend until their frames creak; the grazers press low and lock their plates; you feel the weight of the air walk you backward. In that roar, above even the plant’s voice, the built pieces whistle and hum—someone placed them to hum—and a narrow pass that might have smashed bodies shut opens instead and takes the blow sideways. The sound has been carved to shelter.

Ecology collapses categories. Oscillators—creatures whose only trick is to keep a rhythm—thrive like bees in orchards. Drummers with beak-hammers and hollow chests claim territory on the basis of tone. Predators wear baffles and counterbaffles to pass as silence, then release their pent-up noise in a stunning clap. The plant’s reproductive structures are acoustic, too: cowl-flowers that are not flowers, but smooth megaphones; their seeds are barbed dust set loose only when the right multipulse lands. Visitors—the few there are—learn to walk with rags wrapped around their ankles so that friction doesn’t sing, and to make a small steady click with tongue against teeth so that the world knows to leave a gap.

The rules of the world are simple and strange:
- Nothing here is carved without being sounded. Sound is the hand; rhythm is law.
- Dryness is nourishment. Water is a toxin; even bodies are built to shun it, swapping it for oils, salts, ionic liquids held in felts and sponges.
- Growth is the default. Absence and quietness are invitations to close.
- Light is a trapped river: harvested at the surface, funneled deep, divided until even back-chambers have a slow day.
- Territory is not fences but standing waves. Where a tone holds, a boundary holds.

Stand for a moment on a balcony that was not built but grown to be one, scalloped edge beaded with electric thorn. Below, the mid-range opens—a thousand living bridges crossing and uncrossing, filters and pallets and drums, everything saturated with motion. You will not find a stream or a pool to mirror it, only unresting surfaces. Even the dust does not settle; it is grazed, it is combed, it is hammered into place and then freed again. Between the percussive heartbeats you will catch other things: a short-lived quiet on the brink of a new opening; a hand-sized cairn of knapped salt that says someone came here before you and tuned the world to keep a door ajar. A place under construction, by the plant and the multitudes and a few who learned to speak the same thunder.
