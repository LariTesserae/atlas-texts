A city the size of a lung, packed into a hollow inside the ice.

Everything is water here, but not the broad water of horizons and waves. This is intimate water, held in a pocket under pressures that keep edges sharp and gestures small. The chamber is roughly ovoid, three, maybe four body-lengths across; the roof is scalloped ice ribbed with young crystals, the floor is an uneven drift of salts and fines that have yet to decide whether they are sediment or scaffold. At the margins, the ice is granular, just-born; you can see the hex of crystal faces still learning one another, and within them, narrow veins of brine wander like capillaries. The whole pocket sits beneath thicker ice—beneath what used to be a surface and no longer is—so that above there is weight, and around there is the tender geometry of something that only recently became a place.

Cold hushes most things but motion. The water is near freezing, fingers prickling with salt if you could have fingers here. Yet it is busy. Brine falls in thin, almost-musical sheets from sill to sill, as if the pocket grew the habit of curtains. Haloclines hang like veils, denser ribbons slowly sinking and lighter ribbons rising, so that the chamber is divided by vertical walls of differing water that flex but do not quite break. They refract the dimness. A pale, steady glow emanates from patches of bacterial rind—cold-blue, greenish in corners—enough to make each veil of denser brine seem like smoked glass. Light from above barely reaches; when a long fissure opens far up the ice, a duskier silver dims across the ceiling then goes again. It is never bright. The inhabitants do not need brightness to read.

Built without tools, the city is nonetheless unmistakably organized. You can trace the avenues in the current. Long tongues of slow-moving water arc from the main inflow—a seam at the roof where warmer, salt-rich brine sighs into the room—down along the right wall, dividing and recombining around monuments: chimneys of ice grown from inverted stalactites; buttresses of carbonate secreted by encrusting colonies; a spiraling bench grown by tubes of living animals. Where the current makes an eddy, a plaza forms. Where two laminar sheets meet and shear, a gate. What you might call buildings are lidded chambers, clustered like barnacles, each one an architecture formed from flesh and excretion. No species here moves stone with hand or lever; everything extrudes, dissolves, precipitates, crystal by crystal.

A mat of pale, almost transparent filaments clings in a crescent to a shallow ledge close to what passes for a skylight: the vegetation, such as it is, sparse and precious. These filaments taste the rare photons, storing them in languid sugars and exhaling exactly enough bubble to dapple the ceiling with bead-bright lines; the beads accumulate in quiet pockets and pop with a sound so soft it is more remembered than heard. Elsewhere, the green is replaced by frost-colored bacterial pelts that feed on the brine’s minerals. A city cannot live on light here, only on gradients.

Fauna are abundance personified, but scaled to the place. Some are small to the sight and huge in intention: sheets of sponge-lattice extend along the floor, perforations sized to sort traffic, part filter, part fence. Within the lattice move the builders, white and jointed: little worm-like contractors that live inside tubes of their own making. Their bodies shine with the faint oil-sheen of secretions; with slow, measured motion they pipe saturated fluid against ice, growing a rib here, a crenellation there, marking corners with mineral tags that tell their kin where to continue. They are not alone. Thin shrimp, translucent, cherry-eyed, shuttle along the down-slope rivers of brine, bearing fine grains in their hind legs and dropping them into the mouths of waiting polyps. A pearled isopod, armored in scale-plates, lumbers like a porter through gaps too narrow for it, the plates clicking very faintly as its underside brushes the scalloped rock. Jellied things, only just more than membranes, hold position in the slacker water and turn their cilia to steer scavenged drift into their mouths: street-sweepers. Something eel-slim and reflective rides the tension lines between haloclines, a whisper of a predator that knows to use a curtain of dense water the way you or I would use a doorframe; it strikes, withdraws, and waits, more shadow than shape.

Each surface, and there are many surfaces, is claimed: not by fences, but by the continuous business of feeding and being fed upon. Pale, feathery appendages unfurl to comb the gentle current where it carries the finest food; behind them, tucked into niches, fleshy sacks pulse slowly, rhythmically, sluicing processed water—waste—to the periphery. Territory isn’t measured in lengths of stone, but in pieces of a gradient. A rich lick of iron taste along one seam? That is worth a fight. Two shrimps square up with their antennae in a brush of decisive touches, and one yields, back-pedaling into a darker cul-de-sac. Predators aren’t thunder here; they are edits, inserts, small subtractions. A flip of a fin; a kink in a line of cilia; a sudden vacancy in the traffic.

The traffic is heavy. Dense presences hover near-touching, layers upon layers, living on and inside each other. The roof groans sometimes, faint and long, more felt than heard, as distant stress in the over-ice becomes sound. Crystalline hairs of frost dendrites reach down and accrete, then are broken off by some slow pulse from the inflow, fall, and dissolve on warmer brine like snow on a tongue. The city responds. Down in the soft floor, a mound grows: a midden, glittering with the calcareous shards of old domiciles and the chitinous frass of those who have eaten those domiciles’ owners. The mound is not refuse so much as an archive. The next generation of tubes grows up through it, choosing lines of strength, avoiding, with a wisdom evolved from countless small collapses, the weak seam in the lower left wall that weeps fresh water and would undermine any weight.

Rules here are chemical and formal, and everybody knows them. Do not tear a halocline if you cannot rebuild it; that sheet is both a wall and a highway. Move upstream if you are laden, downstream if you are hunting. Touch first, then taste; sound is unreliable. When the inflow grows colder and the brine bites harder, retreat to the inner ledges where the warmth of bodies and the slow exothermy of growth keep the edge from killing. When the ceiling dims with the passing of a tide far away, expect the small storm: a brief, mean downdraft through the central chimney, enough to shear off the feeblest filaments. If a white column begins to descend from the ceiling like a candle of winter—brinicle forming—clear the floor, for it will freeze whatever it touches and shell a slow forest where there was a square.

This urbanity is not planned by a mind larger than a thumb’s nail, but it is planned. The city’s sewers are a trail of pressure lines, knotted and unknotting with small changes in density. Its streets have right-of-way written as patterns of cilia-beat that most agree to; dissolved pheromones lace these with tone, saying: this way, slow; this way, swift; here a nursery. Nurseries are alcoves deep in the carbonate spires, plugged with mucus on three sides to keep the hungry gradient out. Inside, float eggs the size of raindrops, each anchored to a wall by a sugar thread. When they hatch, the room fills for a time with flutter: new swimmers trying on their cilia, bruising themselves gently against the haloclines until instinct finds them the seam where the food runs thickest and the predators have slower eyes.

If you stay long enough, you feel the place inventing itself. Erosion is not yet dominant; what exists keeps persuading matter into its image. A wall begins nowhere, a tuft of mineral where a worm stopped and breathed; it grows into a buttress that will, a week or a year hence, divide a thoroughfare. A sugar film becomes a gathering place for soot-fine iron; iron gives a taste that calls a certain grazer; the grazer’s waste, sticky and alkaline, invites a calcareous bloom; the bloom becomes a loggia over which shrimp parade, and under which things that fear eels shelter. If a fissure opens—if you feel the soft crack of ice far above become a faint wind and a new trickle of fresher, colder water—you can watch as the color of the nearest bacterial pelt shifts, as the translucent filaments toward the skylight perk, as the city inclines ever so slightly that way. The map redraws. Gaps appear in the old traffic; new cut-throughs are learned. There is nothing still.

Silence, if you can call it that, is the thick quiet of hard water and bodies made to speak in other ways. There are sounds, but quiet ones: a grain ticking on a shell; two armored plates brushing as their owner squeezes between kelp that isn’t kelp; the soft shredding noise as a mouth of fine teeth erases a bacterial patch to shadow. Once in a while, a long, thin chord: the roof complaining to a pressure you will never meet. In those moments, in that suspended attention, the whole chamber seems to hold breath. But of course it does not. Gills beat. Cilia comb. Mouths open and shut in a rhythm no outsider can hear.

Weirdness is normal here. Rooms made of falling water. Doors that are gradients and can be opened with a waft of salt. Fountains that are not fountains but veins that flow even when your hand would insist nothing moves. Citizens that construct boulevards by changing the taste of a seam. Streetlights that grow in uneven arcs of cold fire, turned up and down not by switches but by the presence or absence of slow predators whose bellies shine. News carried not in words but in a change to the feel of the water: an acid blush that means collapse; a bitter edge that means hatching.

You could map it like a human city, if that pleased you. There is an old quarter: the first chimneys, now much eroded, where the population is tightest and the bones underfoot are thick. There is a market where the inflow fans and the bacteria, sleek and well-fed, make a golden pelt: many come to graze there, and many lurk. There are residential rings, each an increment deeper into the scaffolds, where the geometry is more private. There are civic works: a large, deliberate spiral of carbonate that tightens a current into laminar grace, its curve shaped by generations of builders who never saw the whole and didn’t need to.

If you leave and return, you will know it again, but not quite. The pocket will still be cold, submerged, hidden and dense. The haloclines will still hang, though not in the same places. The dim light will still stain the veils and sketch the ribs. The creatures will still touch first and taste next, and leave you out of their quiet aside from the wake you trail. But the plan of the city will have moved; it must, because it is always under construction, because it is as young as the last layer it laid down, because under the ceiling and above the soft floor, life here is an active verb with very little space to waste.
