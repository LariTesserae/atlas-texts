Light here is reluctant. Afternoon lingers, stretched thin, until it feels like a habit the sky can’t quite shake. Everything is seen through that softened dimness—edges dulled, colors turning toward blue-green and iron. It is cool enough that your breath hangs, briefly; not winter-cold, but a constant, high chill that never decides to warm. Things sweat instead of drip. Beads of condensation thread down cables in pauses, hang, then vanish into air or are gathered by the clever lips of moss. Water is a visitor more than an inhabitant. It arrives on scheduleless errands—fog rubbing its body against steel; a sudden hush where rain condenses all at once from a band of air that remembers being ocean; rime laid in neat combs on the windward faces of rails. There are cisterns the size of amphitheaters that never fill. And then, in hidden clefts, pools remain for years, the city’s quiet astonishments.

All of this is draw-stringed along an uplift that does not agree with the rest of the world. The ground went up here—hard, fast, and recently, if “recently” can be measured in how naked the rock feels, how unbruised by the chemistry of weather. Faces of stone keep a shine in the dim light, a glaze, as though they have not yet learned to be matte. There is almost no soil. There are shelves, planes, and blades: a cathedral of facets, slate-black and smoke-grey and sometimes a strange pearl when wet. Whole horizons are cut on a bias. The mountains do not properly meet the valley; they shear past it. In places, when you drop something, it will slide downhill without choosing a direction you believe in.

Erosion has not yet rehearsed itself here. Talus exists in the future tense. Scree is sharp and careful, stacked bead by bead along newdriven fractures. When a ledge fails, it does so with the sound of a word being pronounced for the first time. The city knows this and spreads itself thinly. It is urban in long strokes: neighborhoods clamped like barnacles to different faces of the same impossible ridge, wide emptiness between them stretched across by cable and rail. You move by tram and ladder, by baskets that cross gulfs on lines that hum. The platforms are numbered like stations along a coastline. There are lights, a low, thoughtful sodium amber that makes fog look edible, and underfoot lines of cold-blue bioluminescent paint that wake with footfall and forget you a few paces later.

Industry came with the anchors; without engines nothing would stay where it is. There are engine-houses like harbors, heavy with turbines that peel wind into electricity and warmth. There are condensing towers combing moisture out of air with electrostatic teeth: fog-catchers singing a faint high sustained note you feel in your fillings. There are processing sheds that take the raw, newly coherent rock and coax out veins of iron and magnesium while it is still undecided enough to give them up cheaply. A thousand gullies smoke with breath from exchangers and heat sinks. The mills and winches and cranes sound continuously, a low loudness that recedes only in your imagination. Cables speak. Bolts tick. Every so often a siren rises and falls so the city can listen to its own reflection off the faces and know if something has moved.

Plants have arrived as if by rumor but stay with conviction in the places water collects. In the cradle of a bolt-head big enough to sit on, a moss forest the size of your hand makes weather: dew at dawn, fog at late day, all green hairs beaded with unfinished rain. Cushions of stoneworts upheave their own weight to catch the combed fog, and miniature ferns nest in wind shadows behind rivets. Lichens paint complicated continents on the rock: herbs in a book you have to learn to read to know if they are edible, or sacred, or both. The city helps them without admitting to it. The cable-gardens—a skein of nets between pylons—catch fog and carry slow rivers of drips along ropes lined with pitcher plants and dew-snares, and in their shadow run troughs where dwarfed willows stand tight-budded even in summer. In the cracks sheltered from the eternal crosswinds, whole biomes happen—mats as lush as meadows you can lean down and hear, leaves whispering to one another about minute changes in pressure.

Animals are more numerous than your first glance suggests, and stranger. The obvious ones are the high birds, creatures with more wing than bone, their second pair hinged in oblique planes to ride currents you can’t see. They do well among the distilled winds of the ridges, making tessellations in the air as they hunt fogflies. The insects are legion: metallic skippers that emit a salt-sweet scent; moths with transparent tails that cut small holes in the mist; slow, fat-bodied beetles that feed on the iron-rich lichens and gleam like coins. There are cliff-runners that place their hooves where the slope isn’t, and lizards that will blink out of one crack and into another as if the ten centimeters of intervening stone didn’t count. The cable-sloths—their name a joke—are fast, six-limbed things with fur the color of weathered rope and mouths adapted to scrape sugar from the condensation lines. Owls here have dish-faces like instruments; they hunt by hearing the shape of a space, and their calls come back in chords because the cliffs keep more than one answer.

Some of the fauna are not negotiable with the old words. There are murmur-cats you never see, only a parallax in the ropes like a thought you’ve reconsidered. There are vapor-hares that coalesce where two reflected winds cross and disintegrate when pursued. Once every long while, migrations pass like errant weather systems: slow-soaring leviathans buoyed by differences in reality-pressure, gills filtering spore-thick air, their flanks inscribed by patterns of moss. The city rings the horns when those arrive, not for danger but out of courtesy, the way you would announce a ship making port under no flag you’ve ever flown.

Listen and the place instructs you. The wind here is not wind in the way you learned it. It is a long vowel moving through a throat a thousand meters tall. It knows its own stages; you can tell what hour it is by the key of the cables. The mountains themselves have a note. The engineers have measured it—their graphs look like lullabies—and will tell you that the tone drops a fraction of a fraction each year as the stone settles into its role as stone. The people tune their lives around it. Factory shifts end on the held breath between moans. Weddings take place when the rails sing in a certain interval that has proven, over time, generous for the binding of promises. Children know not to shout by a faultline; echo is a tool here, and careless sounds can worry a crack open into a problem.

There are rules that the place keeps and makes everyone else keep. Gravity skews along several polite directions; the city has handrails on “flat” floors, and you will see warning paint where down changes its mind without asking you. Carpentry is triple-plumbed, a matter of choosing which of the three rights you mean this morning and lining your spirit level accordingly. Water obeys its own history; it will find last year’s path again if it can, even if the stone is newly different, and in this way the channels first scratched by frost and drop now deepen along improbable routes. Light arrives late. Not that there is night all day—there is day, but a day that is tired by the time it gets here, long-bent by the altitude or by the seam in reality that rides the ridge like a forgotten seam in a garment. Shadows are palimpsests of each other. Sometimes you have two, or one and a half. A thrown ball will return to your hand through a slightly older air.

Urbanity here is not the crush of buildings but their insistence across a region. You can see the city from a long way off, because it is mostly negative space made articulate—bridges impossibly taut over distances you didn’t guess rock can span, stacks of scaffolds clamped to faces like a thought experiment, smoke writing cursive footnotes in the dim. Districts declare themselves by their noises. The Condensaries are a shimmer of fans and bead-rains. The Rail Yards chime in sleepsong any time someone in the valley attempts an experiment with timing. The Foundries breathe out chemical winter and chemical spring. In between there’s room. There are safety woods of emptiness, mandated gaps that serve the double duty of not overloading the new land and giving the animals their own lanes through the city. People tend to stand far apart when they talk, too, because sound carries and personality carries in sound, and there are places where the wrong proclamation will stick to a cliff and be heard again at the change of season.

The economy is what you would expect of a town that holds on to a brand-new mountain and asks it for a living without rushing it to ruin. They harvest moisture from fog as a craft, weaving good water out of cold air with skill and timing. They cut ore that is easier to ask for now than it will be in a hundred years, when chemistry has married rock to itself more firmly; they are careful with this, argue about quotas, rub their souls raw over whether they are hastening the future’s difficulties or responsibly translating a transient gift. Energy is wind and drop, and also the potential in misalignment: they have learned to lay blades into the places where reality slips against itself and leech a current off the friction. Markets spread over terraces. Fish arrive from the valley in insulated trains of air. Moss-sugar is sold in sheets like letters.

It is not a fast place. Once a month, a ledge moves the thickness of a coin and alarms burp and men and women in orange harnesses go to re-tension the city’s scarf. Once a year the tone of the ridge dips a tenth of a cent and the whole place recalibrates its sense of key. Once every few years, the slow daylight stumbles and noon is replaced, for a week, by something like moonrise. Nonurgent repairs are scheduled on the decade. People keep calendars not of days but of phenomena—the week the condensers sing raw, the season when the moth tides thicken, the fortnight every six years when the cable-sloths throw their fastness into courtship and catapault from line to line in a blur of pale rope and joy.

At street-level, you learn to touch things with your ear first. You don’t just cross an openwork bridge; you listen to its song for the presence of a new harmonic that means someone overloaded it yesterday. You don’t pick herbs from the lichen-commons without leaving a little sugar, because the beetles that defend that patch also keep the rust down on the bolts that bite into your district’s anchorstone. You never pour anything out without checking which down is in effect, and the children play games not of hopscotch but of angle-chase, hopping from one gravitational bias to another to learn them by body.

It gets properly dark for a few hours each day, some of the time. In those blacknesses, lamps bloom in spheres of tinted fog. The railcars pass like constellations. The high birds fold and hang from the underside of cables in great patterns, their backs breathing, their eyes reflecting the lines the city has drawn across the chasm. Sometimes, on those nights, you can hear the mountain tell itself a story of how it rose, and the air will thicken with a soft, collective exhalation as if all the plants and animals that live here have been hearing that same story all their lives and cannot help but answer. In the morning, the light will take its time getting here. The dew will decorate everything again. The liked places, the edges, will be wet. The center of things will remain, for reasons of its own, dry.

And if you stay, if you learn how to settle your weight into three kinds of down, if you learn to speak only as loud as necessary and to listen for what your steps wake up in the metal, you will feel the city and the mountain and the long, slow rules they wrote together. It is a place in the middle of becoming. The rock is still waking up to what it is. The engines hum like midwives. The animals stroll in from the edges that are not in the same place twice. It is damp without being wet, crowded without being close, loud in the kind of way that leaves the mind calm. It does not hurry. It holds you on its narrow lips and widens the world.
