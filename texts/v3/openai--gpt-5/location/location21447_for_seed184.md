You enter as if entering a hush: warm, weighty water that accepts you, slows your intentions, and fills the sickness of empty space with substance. There is no air to take in—only a broad, green-brown room that breathes with its own tide. The chamber is not large—cathedral to a quiet god—yet it is full to the corners and corners within corners, so dense you can touch three different histories with one slow reach: concrete rounded by time; a web of armored cable like the root of some blind tree; a frond of ribbon-weed shivering with its own private current.

This used to be an engine hall. Even drowned, the bones remember it: pairs of poured columns lift the low ceiling like shouldered arms; the floor, where there is one, disappears under a guttered plain of silt and plated walkways; bulkheads the size of town gates stand half-open, heavy with rust and marine calcification that drips into the stillness like stalactites. The water is warm enough to be an animal in itself, the temperature of a hand held forever. It smells—if smell can be tasted through a mask—of iron and leaf-rot, sweet in the back of the throat.

Light comes sparingly. Twelve, perhaps fifteen lamps burn behind thick glass portholes along one wall, sodium-honey in the gloom, each wrapped in a halo of algae that feeds on their waste heat and exudes the faintest milky luminescence in reply. Day on the surface is rumored rather than seen, a paler patch above the high vault where the ceiling’s glass blocks are now a green cataract of silted light. The place’s geometry is a map of dim gradients. Beneath each lamp an underwater understory of plants rises, paler in the glow: slow-leaved, broad-tissued things adapted to this permanent dusk. Between lamps roams a duskier forest: long bands of filamentous algae, kelp-analogues trailing like torn banners from lattice-girders, colonial mats of bead-weed crusting every seam of steel, freshwater sponge rounding the corners of valves into a meringue of living porcelain.

Everything here has been where it is for a long time. Paint used to be yellow. On one giant pipe’s flank you can still make out hazard striping softened into amber-and-olive bands under mineral bloom. The edges of every step and brace are smoothed by the work of water and the patience of organisms that do not care about ninety-degree thinking. Where welds once glittered bright, ferrofita—iron-eating greens—have colonized, their mats as plush as velvet, drawing filament-thin threads into the metal itself. Rust, here, is not only failure; it is soil.

Listen: it is not silent, but every sound has to cross a room the thickness of the world. The biggest sounds are breaths, and they belong to the hall. Somewhere beyond the columns a turbine turns so slowly it might just be a long thought. The current it sends is gentle, a direction almost indistinguishable from your own wishing, and it confides itself in tiny clues: a strand of hyaline algal hair drawn taut, a page of long-set posters on the far wall that very nearly stirs, the smooth peel of bubbles that detach from a cable sheath and rise with all the urgency of fall leaves. There is the click of something cooling, the furred chitter of an unseen gear train that hunts half a tooth and settles again, the soft scuff of silt cascading as a creature you didn’t see vanishes into darkness.

An urban idea lives here, scaled to the room. The city is not streets but flows. The broad passage at the room’s center is the river set by engineers to cross from intake to clarifier to digesters behind the bulkheads; under the next bench of columns you find the lower alleys where the maintenance rails run, still hung with winch-trolleys, their wheels now ovals of lime that algae have furred into hush. Machinery is present the way bedrock is present: pressurized housings like sarcophagi; a slow flywheel in a glass oil bell that turns in syrup under water; valve trees of cast iron, their handwheels thick-lipped with coral-like growth you can cut your glove on. Conduits run everywhere, armored and lead-bellied, carrying power in oil-jacketed cables from a humming, copper-green nest of dynamos. You can watch the needles of the old analog gauges through cloudy glass—faint movements, asymptotes reached hours later by instruments too stubborn to relax.

Between the iron and the light, the city has been planted and replanted. The walls have trellises, once for conduits, now for rope-plants that bloom without petals in shaggy clumps. Square frames dangle from chain-runs; they used to be filter screens and are now acres of moss-garden, lattice upon lattice, shaded and overhung, coaxed into tiers by the slow pulsing fans that direct currents with the minute variations of a lighthouse keeper. A conveyor, hooded in a sheath of perforated brass against the greens, creeps along the old service ways, gathering loops of cut weed like stitches. It moves whether anyone is here to watch it or not. It spills its harvest into a long, low mouth of a vat whose interior is alive with digesters—bacteria whose lantern-spatter glow can be seen through the seams at the vat’s base, like a far city seen from a train window at night. Industrial, yes. But slowed until the factory’s heartbeat is a day.

In all this fullness the animals are rare. You watch longer to witness them. A glass shrimp, impossible and almost not there, hangs above a copper plate and then is gone in a rectangle of displacement. A pale eel, slender as a ribbon and blind beyond the most convenient inches, threads a course by memory across the same spans of pipe at the same hour every day, rubbing its sides on the familiar beadings of mineral growth like a rosary. Where the current meets the corner of the biggest bulkhead, the water stacks and then peels away, and on that ledge there will sometimes be a little assembly of snails the color of machinery oil, chewing the stories of the place into their tracks. Larger fish avoid it. There is oxygen, but not much above what a leaf and a bacterium negotiate, and the city keeps a steady schedule that has no need of quick hungers.

It is warm because it is inside the earth’s hand. Somewhere above, or perhaps below—the directions keep softening—you can feel the slow seep of heat through concrete. The bulkheads and pillars sweat on very long timescales. On the undersides of beams there are curtains of condensed vapor rising into the greater water like reverse rain. It is pleasant, almost domestic, in a way that dismisses sky. Nothing here prepares for winter. Nothing here has a day to waste in haste.

Rules of the world are posted, long-since blurred on enamel plates, but the water knows them and you can learn them by practicing patience:

- Currents are the streets; stay with them and you will arrive where arrivals are meant; fight them and you will wedge yourself among the machines and have to back out slowly, embarrassed, like any stranger.
- Any iron left bare will become garden inside a week. If you need it again as iron, you will ask the place and the ferrofita will slough themselves off in great umber veils and then creep back when you are done.
- Noise unthreads things. No engine here works well to the quick rhythm. Everything is designed to prefer a hum, a throb at the tempo of a plant making sugar. Shout—and the lamps will dim a little, affronted. Whisper, and bioluminescent freckles tighten along certain wires in sympathetic constellations you will learn to read.
- What is straight outside is curved inside. Pressure and time bend all honest lines; plan around arcs. Pipe-runs sway like sea grass. Cables sink with handsome bellies between supports. Your ideas will do this, too.
- Light, even hung in the same place for a hundred years, belongs to the water that carries it. Lamps make their own weather. Gardens will arrange themselves with a logic that is slow but exact within those amber microclimates. If you move a lamp, you move neighborhoods.

There are places where you can tell someone—caretaker, diver, the kind of resident that belongs to schedules more than to faces—tends the room. Near the most diligent banks of lamps, lines have been trimmed with a polished, ceremonial neatness. Golden clips tie the vines back from the valves so that if a handwheel must be turned there will be no green tug-offer, no small wrestle. A jar, caught in a net of cable at the height of a human head, holds punctured shells like money; a long-handled knife with its blade chewed to lace by corrosion rides in a holster strapped to a pillar. The entrances to maintenance alcoves are curtained with chain weighted by iron seeds; when you part them, clouds of tan silt rise politely and then lower themselves again, ashamed to be seen.

The room is full to suffocation and yet it is restful, because nothing demands your immediate answer. The floor, where you can reach it, is soft with a cast of sediment that will show your step for days. Overhead, a run of ancient glazing bricks—the hall’s only skylight—imagines a surface world you hardly need. If something outside this chamber changes, it will take its time getting here: a long decline of voltage in a gauge; a slight cooling of the water as a valve opens an extra finger to an autumn reservoir; one of the lamps quitting with a tiny click and then, ten minutes later, the arrival of a pale caretaker fish, a machine or a creature or both, the size of a cat, its back armored in scale-stamped brass, its front bare and whiskered, to nose the housing and release the spare.

At the far end of the room, where the bulkhead is thickest, slow doors rise like the backs of whales and fall again by inches. Through the seam you can see other rooms repeating: pump naves, clarifier cloisters, fan halls where the air is also water, and miles of these modest cathedrals stepping downward to where a river once ran before the flood took it and made it useful in new ways. But the place you are in is enough. It is a whole city in one room. It has settled into itself, plants and pipes and silence fitting together so well that to remove one would feel like pulling a bone.

Everything moves and nothing hurries. If you dare let go and hang, all the pressures balance you without effort. Your breath becomes smaller, more aware of making ripples. The lamps make rain. The engines cross into sleep and back again, time not kept by clock but by the long arc of a needle aligning eventually with the faith of a painter’s mark. In the green gold dim, all your crisp, unrounded ideas dissolve and flow, and the room teaches you the last rule, the one it lives by: nothing can be empty when water is allowed to remember its shape.
