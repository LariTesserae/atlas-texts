The light never quite wakes here. It filters down as a muted cream through a lid of cloud and smoke, and the air, already warm, holds it at a distance like something dissolving in syrup. Every surface sweats. Steel sweats. Concrete sweats. Even the compacted earth of the levee gleams along its flanks, darkening as fat drops pull fine dust into brown beads and let them fall.

From the crown of the berm the land opens in long, low sheets: basins, pans, and shallow channels stretching away until the horizon takes them up in a dull line. It is all close to sea level, maybe only a little above it, and it all knows the sea’s habits. The water is never far. It stands in thin, mirror-flat layers over salt-crusted clay, it seeps from joints in retaining walls and beads on the undersides of catwalks, it whispers along culverts, sluices through grated throats, condenses under pipes and ticks from them, steady, a slow polite clock.

There are no plants to soften anything. No reeds at the channel margins, no weedy bravery between concrete slabs. Everywhere the baselines are mineral: dun clay, white rims of halite, hematite-orange scabs where iron-rich trickles spread like rust-bloom lichen across the flumes. In the old spillways the sediments have settled into a weary anatomy—rounded levy-toes, scalloped edges where repetitive overflow has nibbled a constant bite, faint terraces inside the basins where successive high-water marks have taught the mud a lesson in patience. When the wind presses, it raises a skin of motion on the shallow water, and you can watch it slide in bands like the faintest of muscles beneath translucent flesh.

The built thing here is a city, but it keeps its height low and its weight wide. It is a city of lines and planes more than towers. Long causeways ride high on armored embankments, pipe galleries run in parallel like bundled nerves, catwalk lattices stitch the intervals over rank upon rank of tanks, sumps, and settlers. Pump houses sit heavy on pilings, their walls stained with mineral tears that have learned their path and return to it every hot morning. A double-decked sluice-gate assembly spans the main channel like a pair of machine ribs, the lower deck shadowed dark even at noon, the upper freckled with salt blooms. Rail spurs reach and then stop at loading aprons. Gantries watch the basins, motionless until something rare calls them from rest. Power lines hang in long parabolas, dull in the low light, and when they hum it is so soft you wonder if it’s air moving along them or electricity moving inside.

All of it has lived long enough to make peace with this place. Edge protection is rounded from a thousand small overflows, bolts wear a coth of precipitate and ooze until their heads are unreadable with paint or stamped marks. Painted safety stripes go chalky and then subtle. Every measure taken against corrosion is itself a landscape: thick skirts of bitumen seamed and checked like dried lakebeds; sacrificial zincs half-eaten, their gnawed sides a fine, bright gray against the duller gray of steel. The balance is not perfect, but it is repetitive. Things fail in the same sequence, at familiar rates. There are spare parts in the pump house with chalk on their sides, dates and initials written like charms against panic.

Heat wraps the whole region in a shared recipe. Even with the dimness, you feel the ovens of the past sunlight. After rain—when the clouds crack and pour with tropical decisiveness—the heat does not leave but changes mood. The first hour is sound: hard-rushing gutters, sudden gurgles at the head of a culvert as it takes its first throatful, rain jabbing shallow pans into an ecstatic foam. Then a hush after, a steaming quiet full of drip-lines and the faint fizz of bubble films collapsing. Everything smells louder: wet steel, soluble salts, a limestone note from the concrete, sulfur rising cold and then warming from the sumps where purple slicks of bacteria catch what little light reaches them and eat it methodically.

That slick is one of the few colors. In places the water surface is wine-dark under an oily shine, elsewhere mother-of-pearl with bacterial colonies patterned by tiny currents. On the floor of a broad settling basin a patchwork of orange and black carpets marks where iron bacteria have colonized the flow. They are busy down there, and that industry—quiet, chemical, unfussy—anchors much of the rest.

Because there is life everywhere. It does not come as green softness, it comes as movement. In the hour the pumps throttle back, the open water shivers with it—threadlike midge larvae lifting and sinking under the films, turbidity flickering with amphipods as fine as eyelashes. Near the weirs, where the oxygen rises and falls with the breath of the gates, small fish with the complacent look of things that have seen everything hover in undulating layers, sliding as one when shadow presses. Eels arrow out from culvert mouths and then melt back, their skins the color of the oil-streaked light. Crabs as big as a child’s hand pick their way along the bolted base of a flow-splitter like cautious mechanics, tapping at barnacled debris, then scuttling sideways to avoid the bulging nose of a rat. The rats are legion and glossy, their runs worn into the salt with a precision that makes you think of habit more than hunger.

Above the surfaces, the air is thick with fractions. Mosquitoes gather in numbers so great that they become a quality of the light in certain corners, a gauze that hangs and breathes. Midges rise and settle in veils; dragonflies keep station in glittering patrols, pausing to tilt and adjust with the attentiveness of inspectors. Bats cling in the cool under-lips of the bridge decks all day because the world is dim enough to grant them small mercies; they launch in shy shoulders of shadow whenever a shoal of insects gets too confident. Swallows and martins overlap them, scribbling low arcs above the warm pans when the clouds thin for ten minutes and a little more brightness makes the gnats dance. Gulls perch in smug white commas along the short parapets of the pump station and discuss, with a patience learned from tides, which movement will yield fish. From the far side you hear the flat-bark calls of herons, a measured commentary from birds that have put their perfect feet down in unloved water for generations.

There is company under the surfaces too. In a deep sump where a working fluid from one part of the plant runs cool along the bottom before it is lifted to be treated, pink mounds of filamentous bacteria bob like slow roses whenever a backwash current twitches through. Between them, transparent shrimp jump in place like sparks. In a concrete gutter that never entirely dries, a gray snake lies folded in itself, drowsing in the lull of afternoon, tongue touching what flows as if taste might count the hours better than light does here.

The economy of it all runs not on leaves but on leakage and residue, on careful waste. Materials that arrive in trains or barges—grains turned for industrial starch, bone meal for feed, reagents for a process you never quite catch—shed what they always shed. Fine powders in corners, dribbles at joints, a little meat water where cold rooms cough up their melt and it travels to a grate. Everything becomes nutrient for something small, which becomes nutrient for something larger. The city’s operations write daily scripts, and the animals learn their lines. When a siren near the sluice house sounds its long, low warning—barely a sound at all compared to a city proper, more a rubbed throat of a note—the fish nosing at the bars move back of their own accord. When the afternoon’s main outfall reverses for backwash, shorebirds gather a few minutes early along the shallows and begin their sewing-machine treads as soon as the fresh water brings a light that the insect larvae cannot refuse.

Sound holds itself in restraint. The hot, moist air takes the aggression out of sharp noises and returns them warmer. Pumps work behind walls and make the floors vibrate faintly like a heartbeat; the rails, when they carry one slow train of covered hoppers, transfer a careful metal complaint into the long bones of the causeway. Most days you could hear a heron’s feet if it chose to take a few light steps on the pump-house roof. You hear the tamperings of small claws in the undersides of things. You hear the welling and shutting of water, miniature events repeated in a thousand drains. Sometimes, when a breeze pushes a line of heat down onto the wires, they sigh very softly, and the dragonflies don’t even flinch.

The rules here are plain and old and everywhere enforced, even by those who never knew them: water takes every chance, and metal pays for it; salt travels in the lungs of the air and grows teeth; anything vertical loosens and leans unless it is over-loved. Heat is not an incident but a climate—everything is designed to be left in it and remain itself: thick walls, fluids kept moving, hinges greased with graphites that don’t wash away. Maintenance is not drama but diet. The people who tend the place walk it like farmers, checking their known afflictions: one gate lip that always weeps, a feeder pipe with a joint no sealant has ever liked, a motor that draws half an amp more in August than in March. They leave behind small metal offerings: a replaced gasket, a new zinc button, a bead of sealant smoothed by a thumb. And things come to visit those little changes immediately; ants find the fresh resin and examine it with their dark heads; a wasp unsleeps to see the new shine; a gecko prints the bead with crescent toes in passing.

The emptiness is true, too. The volumes between structures are broad and calm, occupied by air and surface and the slow concatenation of shallow waters. The distances between pump house and rail apron, between one tank farm and the next, are large enough that the individual noises fade and each place can carry its own small weather. Heat stands in curtains over one pan while the next keeps a low skim of cloud a hand-span above it. In late day, the dimness deepens without drama and silhouettes arrange themselves—pipe racks, parapets, the long loping rhythm of towers’ shadows—and for a moment the whole region looks not like anything made but like a landscape that happened to go this way.

At night there are lights, but they don’t argue with the dark. Sodium glows keep their thoughts to themselves above doors and along service walks, and in their edges moths beat a speechless riot. The open basins hold the gray of the sky like ongoing thoughts. The bats become the main punctuation; fish knock at the undersides of scum as if asking, almost politely, for a step aside. Somewhere, water shifts level by inches. A gate makes its low decision and leans on it. Rust grows in a million damp crevices with the privacy of sleep. And by dawn, when the sky pales only enough to announce that the cloud has gone nowhere and the heat is already upon its feet, every surface gleams again—the city’s weeping resumed, the microbial color renewed, the animals re-assembling in quantities that make you count by their sound instead of their bodies.
