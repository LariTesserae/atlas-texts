The light comes first. It presses down like a white hand, hard and clean, burning detail into every surface. The air is thin enough that you can see it waver; the heat sets whole bands of the distance on a slow boil, mirage stacked on mirage. High country, but not cold—this is a plateau close to the sky and closer still to the sun.

Stone makes the land. Pale, porous stone in heavy shelves, honeycombed and fluted from a long arithmetic of wet and heat and time. The plateau’s edge breaks into curtain-cliffs that don’t so much fall as weep: not waterfalls, not exactly, but endless films of water mirroring the day, diverted along hairline channels the tufa grew for itself. Each notch is a memory; an old trickle, mineral-rich and patient, has drawn down a ledge, left a rib, shaped a gutter, filled a pan. The stone is always growing and always eroding, in balance. Some of the faces are banded in glassy crusts that look freshly iced, though what they record is an age of drips. The terraces hold shallow pans the color of old coins and salt-bone; basins walled by rimstone, scalloped as if by tongues. All along the brows, the cliff lips overhang like fossil waves about to break and never quite doing it.

Water is frequent but seldom deep. It is everywhere as a presence: sweating from the stone when clouds brush the escarpment; creasing down in transparent sheets; pearling on the undersides of ledges; hanging in drop-circles on the eaves of a cave mouth; gathering as fog in the gullies when the air cools. It moves the way ceremonies move, in repeated small gestures—the same rivulet returns every afternoon because the same slab finds the same dewpoint. When the sun is highest, vapor streams upslope from the lowlands and slams into the colder stone, and the walls darken and begin to cry. The voices of these waters, though, are quiet; the cliffs take sound the way they take wet. The rock here, light and vesicular, drinks vibration. A drop that would ring like glass on other days seems to arrive and vanish inside the face with barely a whisper.

Where drainage can collect, the pans load themselves with dissolved minerals. Some of them are sour, some are alkaline as soap. Your tongue would know but not enjoy the difference. The balance has held long enough that the features are settled in their habits. Sudden collapses have a way of leaving new shelves that line up with old ones, as if the place remembered its edges.

Life is hard to see at first because there is no green to find. Plants don’t take hold here; not much that we would call a plant. The light is too much, the chemistry too particular, the wet too thin and restless to root into. But let your eyes adjust from the spectacle of the stone, and everything begins to move.

The most obvious are the filters. They ride the warm upslope currents like kites, flat and flexible and pale as parchment, cross-braced with a delicate lattice that throws prismatic edges when they tilt. They look like paper fish, silent armadas tacking from one drip-line to the next, their ventral surfaces strung with fringes that bead and wick. Their mouths are the size of a pinhead and there are thousands; they harvest aerosols and the tiny bacterial scum that blooms and fades on the falling films. When they land, they become nothing at all—slip a little, adhere to wet stone, and drown themselves in condensation. They climb back into the air by shivering, squeezing dew out through pores along their rims that coalesce into flight again.

The grazers are sober little things, jointed and dull and ghost-colored, present in huge numbers but scattered enough that you watch a single one for a long while before you see another. Sand-grain to thumbnail in size, with mouths like felt disks, they move in slow sweeps over the faint opal gloss—the biofilm—taking what little is there and moving on. A pan’s lip will be ringed in a hair-fine fray where grazers have been. Sometimes a grazer will fall and find itself in a runnel; it will fold its limbs in and become streamlined and let the lamina carry it out into a quiet, where a surface tension thicker than you remember in other places holds it up like a coin between fingers. It will dry there or be gathered by something else.

The hunters do not pounce. In this thin, hot, wet air, speed is a kind of waste. Predation is patience. There are bands of stone so cold with condensed heat that they are almost blue, and from these margins long limbs unfold. You notice them by their shadow: a blade of darkness that moves when there is no wind. The hunters drink gradients. They place a digit near where a sun-warmed trickle crosses a cooled plane and feed on the shiver of temperature, the little flinches of moisture as film meets film. If a filter or a grazer blunders into the zone the hunter is skimming, the limb closes. There is no snap. There is simply possession.

Everything is quiet. Not hushed in the way a chapel is hushed, not for reverence—quiet for physics. The air takes less of the voice than you expect and the stone takes more; conversations happen with hands and cloth, with light thrown from polished tin and slate, with lines rigged between places to pull codes along. When there is a wind, you hear it far away, a fabric trembling that may be the city. The animals keep to silent manners because to make noise here is to find that sound wants to leave you.

The city moves along the weeping faces like a climber who will not let go. It is not dense—a line of presence more than a block. Platforms hang from anchor-pins set deep into the tough layers; the pins blossom to distribute weight into the forgiving stone. Walkways braid along, canted and decked in mesh that condenses night-fog into morning rills. Roofs are louvers, wide and pale, that can turn to throw or swallow light as needed, and their undersides are combed to wick and drip. Big plates of shadowglass glitter where the inhabitants harvest sun for heat and electricity in equal measure, and between them the stills sparkle, letting brine creep through long capillary paths until salts precipitate into neat trays. Toward the base are spillworks—low basins and channeled cascades that take in the city’s mineral-laden runoff and sort it by patience: the quick stains of iron, the white fans of carbonate, the delicate green of copper if you come at dusk when the glare lets go enough for color to be true.

Engines here all count in slow measures. Pumps hum in sealed beds beneath the platforms, diaphragms flexing on differential heat rather than pistons banging on combustion. Conveyors move like glaciers, with shoes of a felted mineral fiber that leaves no echo and can be composted as a mineral later. The hoists that carry ore and water and people up and down the curtain are counterweighted by brine; a weight sinks as it fills and rises again as it drains, pulled by valves you can thumb with no more noise than a breath. When the waste-gas from a leach-pit rises, it is caught in bell-hoods made of ceramic leaves; the reaction columns are strapped with quiet coils. If an alarm must be sounded, color shifts through the city in bands—a sequence of white-to-shadow ripples along facades—and the ground-line vibrates, a pulse you feel bare-footed.

Industry grew into geology here because geology is slow and sure. The city strips its chemicals from foul water, plates a micron of metal on a seed and then another and another, cooks ceramics in sunlight by focusing not heat but time. It grows its own anchors by colonizing a wet trickle with a thickening crust, inserting a template under a nascent tufa beard and leaving it for a season until the stone itself hardens into a hook.

There are caves behind the veils. Their lips are as bright and dry as a skull’s teeth; inside is a cool gray with its own sky of stalactites and delicate soda straws that could be humidity turned to solid. Air moves through them according to rules that feel architectural; sometimes you pass a cleft and a breath takes your hair from your forehead without a hint of a draft elsewhere. Fungus is there, if life without chlorophyll answers to fungus—whitish fans as thin as skin that spread under rock like maps, and knotted bodies that thrust into vents to drink the nightly fog. When a ceiling collapses and lets the sun into one of these rooms, animals raft out the pale mats in strips and the light dismembers them in a day.

At dawn, the plateau is a collector. Mist climbs the walls and is caught by tens of thousands of ridges, threads the flutes and fills the basins. Filters float flat over the drops like punctuation. The city opens slightly, louvers yawning, the decks going from shadow to shine. Tiny gyres spin along every runnel where pinpricks, too small to see until your eyes decide to see them, turn in place to chew at what the night made. Hunters crouch in their blue slots. From the lowlands below the cloud-line flashes sheet lightning; you feel it, a hand on the shoulder of the air rather than a sound.

At noon, the light is hardest. The heat draws water out of all that is horizontal; the walls sweat instead. The basins stipple with small organisms that can run an entire life today and be a smear on a stone tomorrow, their eggs locked to a crack until next similar day. Filters lift and become an idea of fish in a white river. The city’s plates turn their faces so that they are both furnace and shade. Heat storage stones underfoot drink the extra and promise it to evening. Persons in hooded veils move slowly and leave no clatter. Time is visible in the long slow shining lines where a trickle crosses a surface that is nearly, but not quite, too hot to look at.

At evening, the fat of the day comes back as rain that is not rain, a condensation that runs from air to rock instead of the other way. The city tightens its louvers and opens its faces toward the walls. Every bolt has a gutter built into it; every surface feeds the sumps. Drips add beats to silent measures. You cannot hear them from far. You must bring your ear to a thread in a corner where a pipe meets a beam—then you know that everything is busy.

What happens in storms is very strange and very ordinary here. The plateau snaps with electricity before you can catch a scent of it; the sizzle is something the stone eats and grows with. There is never a roar, only a tremor underfoot when a section of cliff furls away like a page and sails down out of the edge world into the low cloud’s anemia, the fall’s end unseen and unheard. The city shifts its weight by degrees and keeps on distilling.

Rules of this world are consistent even if they are not soft. Light is a food, but not for leaves. Motion is more efficient than voice. Water comes more surely to those who climb than to those who sink their wells. Stone can be taught to hold, if you teach slowly. Animals count on film and vapor more than they count on flesh, and the best predators have learned to eat difference—warmth sliding into cold, air folding into wet.

It is a grand quiet, a measured place as far as a valley goes and a valley turned on its side, strung with industry that is built to be the opposite of clumsy. It is hot and it is wet and high, though sometimes what feels highest here is the ceiling of cloud below the ledge, the sea that never sloshes, just receives. What you will remember is the way brightness drives detail to the surface and the way the world refuses to return any of that attention as echo; you will put your hand to the wall of a weeping house at noon and feel the cooling go past your skin into your bones, and you will understand why the animals say their names with color.
