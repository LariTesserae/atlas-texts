Heat stands here like a presence. It does not waver so much as inhabit, filling the air with a high, dry pressure that sits in the sinuses and on the tongue and in the thought. The light is straightforward—no tricks of twilight or color, just a stark, clean day that finds every surface and draws out its edges. From this elevation, the world spreads in a suite of muted metals and scorched minerals: a high, old plateau that has learned the long, slow grammar of wind and time.

The ground has the look of something melted and allowed to cool for centuries under a steady hand. It resolves into polygonal plates, each a few body-lengths across, joined by seams of paler material that crackle like frost but never hold moisture. Their faces are glass-black in places and satin-matte elsewhere, softening over ridges into a ceramic sheen. Between plates, the welded lines are crowded with minerals that have crept up from below and frozen out at the surface in thin, combed fans: needles of sublimed salt, rills of cauliflowered sulfide, skins of crystalline halide that flake like mica when touched by wind.

There is no water. It is not merely absent; it feels uninvited. Where shallow hollows collect things, what gathers is not liquid but slow vapor: low, opalescent fogs that remain inches above the ground, trembling with their own inner charges, each a permanent ghost-theatre of tiny ions changing allegiance. If they blow apart, they do not wet anything. They taste like a penny and a struck match.

The plateau’s relief is ancient and assured. Yardangs—those long-backed, wind-sculpted ridges—run in parallel like combed fur for kilometers, softened to muted ribs, no sharpness left to them except on the shadowed sides where cold holds on. Arches once carved by some vanished abrasion now exist only as their stubs, fatty with dense mineral that resisted. The few towers that puncture the horizon are not boulders but extrusions: pillars of sintered ore that have grown upward in patient rings like living things, only to be thinned and perforated over millennia by something other than rain. When the loose crust is broken, the fracture is not jagged; it peels, it delaminates along subtle planes, as if the whole plateau had been cured under tension.

Thin air, high sky. The horizon is far enough away to make the throat feel empty. Yet there is no quiet. The ground hums with a base note too low to name, more felt in long bones than heard, a slow, slow throb that arrives through the soles long before it resolves in the ear. Over it, a chorus of other sounds unfolds: a sustained pipe-organ sigh moving up and down the scale with the passing of invisible breezes; a rattling chatter like rain on tin, though no drop will ever fall; and, on some subtle cycle, a long, exhaled moan that comes from entire valleys, as if the plateau itself were a bellows.

The pillars explain part of this. Many of them are hollow, and their perforations, distributed not at all at random, act as stops in a great instrument the size of a county. Wind—if wind is the word for the slow migration of heated density from one basin to the next—pushes through them, and they answer. They do not just sing; they build. Mineral carried in the gas adheres to tone; only at certain frequencies does deposition occur. Over eras, each tower has tuned itself, tone by tone, to the stable voices of the plateau. Some open pores are rimmed in yellow salt that tastes like electricity. Some are bruised purple, heavy with halide. Around their bases, dunes of filamented crystals lay in drifts like frozen grasses leaning one way, then the other.

There is no plant life, nothing that uses light to coin sugars and wrap itself in green. What grows instead is a different patience: films of metallic whiskers that creep along shadow-side stone; patchy sheens that look like oil slicks but are in fact arrays of molecules locked into pumped rhythms, storing energy as arrangement rather than bond. If you look sidelong, the air over some flats shows a herringbone moiré where a standing wave of charge kisses dust and lifts it a finger’s width. These are the closest things to fields. They appear and vanish with the day-long drift of potentials the same way terrestrial grasses bend to wind.

From a distance, the place could pass, to a casual glance, as empty. In the mechanical heart of it, it is crowded to suffocation. Life here is not rare; it is simply spread out to fill the silence.

The first moving thing often shows itself as a mirage: a ripple on the horizon that refuses to resolve with approach. Then it is a shadow the size of a barn, and then an edge of chrome. Then it is upon you: an animal in the sense of animation and appetite, not in any familiar body-plan. It moves on many paired limbs that function less as legs than as pistons, lifting and placing with indecent slowness, each contact a meticulously controlled transfer of force that does not crack the plate beneath. It is a smelter-beast, a walking furnace in which the mouth is a maw of counter-rotating mineral grinders and the gut is a labyrinth of catalytic wind-boxes. Into the maw goes plate and seam, atomized to flourish inside at temperatures that would make sandstone flow. Out of ports along its flanks stream little fountains of glitter that collapse into ordered piles: ingots, or something that wants to be ingots, bars self-casting from molten salt blood and cooled on the beast’s own radiators. It breathes in noises, a hard drawn-in hoot that sets the towers nearby to sympathetic vibration. It breathes out a white scream that runs like a blade across the plain and then is gone.

They do not build foundries; they are foundries. Their skeletons resonate with the hum of the plateau and take from it the structure necessary to align their chemistry, and in return they modulate that hum by being. They gulf down the plate not so much to feed as to be tuned. Their trails are not gutters but lathing tracks, kilometers-long glosses where their feet have polished the glass. They move in migrations that last lifetimes, swinging in shallow arcs you could map by the sheen. If they die, they do not rot. They seize. The heat inside them retracts into stable configurations and holds there. In years a carapace-in-echo remains like a gently-slumped kiln, and out of it pours a tide of smaller lives that had lived inside them, each a thread in the long industrial ecology.

“Industrial” is not a metaphor here. Industry is a way of eating. Energy lies not as light but patterned into the deep fabric: into the slow standing waves set up by the shape of the plateau and the speed of sound in its bones. Primary consumers are not green; they are tuned. There are animals that husband tone like herders, coaxing the hum around their own geometries, building it into gradients they can drink. You hear them before you see them: ranks of low conchs fused to the leeward sides of towers, throats fluttering, drinking amplitude. Farther along will be the grazers in the strict mineral sense: carpets of micro-arthritic devices, the size of bread crumbs, walking lockstep on their own piezoelectric feet, tapping and tapping, lodging themselves briefly into place and then letting go, harvesting a handful of ions at each step. They look like a phase-change in the surface rather than a population. Their tapping is the rain-on-tin. Their cast-off grains—tiny ordered knots of metallic glass—are the richest ore of the place.

Above it all float things that do not even pretend to wings. They rise on the difference between what the air wants to do and what the field insists it must do. Thin, square frames of a substance that cannot decide whether it wants to conduct bounce in place like thoughts, edging toward towers and retreating when the tone shifts. Their bellies are crowded with coils that fatten the more they hum. When two meet, they do not fight—there is no hurry here for violence. They mesh their frames and hum together, exchanging what passes for gametes in the long roll of an interference pattern. In flickers, as heat ripples, their shadows appear where you would expect them to be only to be pulled sideways an inch, a foot, a meter—the light here takes small detours, consistently enough that you learn to plan where you step by it.

Predation exists, though the word wears a new shape. Apex bodies do not pursuit-run; they loom and lean. They are shapes like prayer-wheels the size of towers that spend a hundred years half-buried and then tilt up imperceptibly and roll one body-length down-slope, shearing coherence off anything unfortunate enough to touch the thin edge of their rims. Where they pass, the order in other things collapses. The smelter-beasts avoid them with the devotion of experience and will divert a migration by a valley’s width to go around the fraction of an inch of rim that is above ground. Little frames in the air will unmake themselves at a touch and settle to the ground as rain that will never be water: a glitter so fine it seems like probability.

Not all the sense here is continuous. Some rules skip. Between plates, in seams or down the throats of pillars, there are places where cause and effect have been sanded against each other for so long that the fit is not exact anymore. In such places, if you drop a crystalline needle, it may ring before it hits. You can clap and a mile away a tower will answer with your clap, and only then, close by, will your ears recognize the sound you made. A fat animal may put one piston-foot down and refuse to fall for several seconds, as if the idea of falling had misrouted, and then, when it does, the impact will show up in the wrong direction as a ripple in a dune. Shadows carry weight at certain hours. You shift your body to ease your knee and a line of glare across the plain tens of kilometers long will kink in sympathy.

This is not caprice; it is equilibrium. The plateau has been patinated by its own physics until the physics has learned to run along the same old grooves. The animals that live here take advantage of that. They carry inside them pockets where a tone will stick for an extra heartbeat, and that stolen beat used as a battery to move a foot. The air frames have edges that slide through the pores in cause like paper knives. The tapping carpet has a rhythm that forces the future and past of their taps to blur, so that one set pays for the other in an accounting no one off-world would recognize as metabolism.

Notice the density. For all that life, there is so much room. Between towers, kilometers of polished plate lie without feature but haze. The smelter-beasts, larger than houses, may cross the visible horizon at the rate a hand moves across a table. Whole migrations pass in a day in the sense that their leading edges enter a valley in the morning and their trailing edges may leave at dusk, but any single body in the stream has moved only enough to change what it is touching. At night—if you choose to measure the pause between day’s light and the next day’s light and name it a night—the hum cools a few tones down, and out of the now-slightly-deeper quiet roll other voices. Somewhere underfoot, something the length of a town turns in its sleep. It has been turning here since before the towers were tall, since before the plates were plates. Every century it breaks the surface with a ringed spine, polishing away one of the sintered organ-pipes to a nub and leaving a tang of hot chlorine and metal in the air. Over its vents, crystals grow in plumes so dense they look like smoke frozen in place, down which, for a day or a week, a new ecology shivers into being and is gone, all of it having never recognized the concept of wet.

It is hot, but not brutally so; the heat here is cultivated. It moves in currents that life can plan around. Lighter air drifts upslope at exactly the rates that the coiled frames require to keep aloft; hotter seams light at the seasons when the carpets are ready to push; the smelter-beasts time their exhalations so that their own out-breaths feed the towers that tune the field that tunes their bones. Everything is large and slow and terrible in the original sense of that word, and everything balances on the edge of a quiet that never arrives.

There are rules that newcomers learn or they go away.

- A tone at the right pitch will call matter to it. You can hum at a seam and make it grow a beard. You can shout and make a rain of needle salt. The locals do not sing without reason. Some of them have chambers that they cork with their own lips when the wrong note blows so their insides don’t plate themselves.

- Shadows have consequence. Step in your own too often at midday and your soles will begin to stick as if to tar, because the place where light has decided not to go is a place where other exchanges become easy. Predators lurk in shadow because it is simply a cheaper place to be complicated.

- Touch is dangerous across plates. They are not at rest with respect to each other in causality. Many beings keep a foot on two at all times to average themselves and avoid being split into lives led at different minutes.

- The air is a field as well as a gas. Bring in something that conducts unexpectedly, and you will find yourself followed by a string of thin frames drawn to your wake the way fish linger behind the prow.

- Nothing hurries. If you force anything, it will come apart. The plateau does not reward speed. It has worn away every sharp edge with time and expects its lives to flow around one another in the same slow negotiation. Even the apex wheels roll in their century-long way, gathering the drift, never lunging.

In this, the world is gentle to itself. The violence it knows is the violence of adjustment, the long working of a high place along its faults to come to terms with its own weight and heat. The built absence is complete: there are no walls, no tools set aside from bodies, no marks arranged to mean anything outside of what they accomplish in chemistry and motion. Yet the whole region has the affect of a works-yard. Piles stand. Trails of gloss arc. Scaffolds of bone accept tone to lay down new layers. Boilers move, their steam invisible but screaming. Conveyors of chitinous footpads ring the horizon, and underfoot the entire plateau runs on slow bearings.

You could walk a long way and not meet a thing you recognized, but that is because recognition here must stretch to include engines that ate their sparks and decided to crawl; weather that finds vowels and says them; a geology that hums, not as omen but as act. The day will go on, white and candid. The heat will keep, as if stored in the composition of the very rocks. The towers will pour out their chords. Down in the seams, in the tiny cools, carpets will tap on, the closest analogue to a breeze within this sealed pact of dryness and stone.

In the far distance, perhaps, a line where the plateau steps down. There the hum drops like a bottoming note and the tone of the world changes. But here, in this high, thinned place, everything is already itself and has been so for longer than the word “age.” It is hot, and it is loud, and life is overwhelming because it has filled every possible groove with bodies that are also functions, bodies that do not need water or leaf but only patience and tone. If you are careful—if you account for where your shadow falls; if you hum nothing you do not mean; if you touch with both feet, always—you can stand and listen and feel the humming of the long machines that were never built and know that you are on a world where cause and effect have been domesticated by geology and eaten by animals, and that all of it is in balance, and that the balance is heavy, and that it is not yours to move.
