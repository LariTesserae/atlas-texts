At altitude the heat does not thin. It rises with you and spreads, a wet lung pressed against the sky. The first impression is steam and sound: a constant organ of air through hollow stems, the slosh of water along channelled bark, the rattling applause of leaf-plates slapped by convective gusts. Light is ordinary daylight, yet everything that receives it is alive and building.

There is no exposed stone. What looks like cliff and strata is living fabric stiffened with crystals it secreted for itself. Underfoot, the “ground” is a wide, pale braid – layers of hardwood-scaled liana fused into sheets, the grain of growth showing as ripples and ridges. It isn’t especially crowded. For long stretches the surface opens into empty aprons and terraces, nothing rising above knee-height for a hundred paces, then abruptly a buttress or a tower will rear up from the plain with an edge so sharp it throws a knifed shadow. Youth shows in that edgework. Surfaces are not smoothed; they are new, bright where torn, glossy where sap still dries, fluted where the last growth pulse hardened in a hurry.

Water is everywhere without collecting into ocean or lake. It weeps from pores, runs as glassy veils over glossy bark, leaps in threads across the mouths of vertical shafts and vanishes into leaf-made basins. The air is saturated and hot. Walk a while, and your shoulders are glazed in condensation, as if the habitat itself is settling on you. In the distance, on a scale of valleys, white sheets fall from green ledges: not rock waterfalls but overflows from giant condensers. Those condensers present like cliffs: miles-long steps of overlapping frond that stack into a slanted wall. Warm drafts striking them are milked, led inward by the geometry of their veins into hidden cisterns. The whole upland hums with that intake. The plants have pulled the sky close and are drinking it.

Between the condensers and the empty aprons, the architecture repeats in a few slow, colossal chords. There are the walls – living buttresses that curve from the plain and meet high, forming vaults. There are the aqueducts – tight, parallel vines fused into gutters, running from crest to crest. There are the runners – long cords at the edges of things, always seeking, rimmed in tender growth, probing out over sky-chasms like a hand Groping for the next abutment. These are sparse. For stretches of several valley-widths, nothing interrupts the level but the faint pile of a vein or the blister of a nascent node. Then suddenly the world narrows as a vault throws shade over you, wet and resonant like the belly of an instrument.

No animals cross the terraces. No midges stain the light. No wing shadows pass. Movement belongs to the plants and to the water and to the air they stir. You can see it directly: an edge unfurls a fraction in one wind-trough, recoils in the next; a field of thumbnail-scale blades lifts their faces in a gust, then smooths flat as the pressure changes; a vine’s tip jerks and digs a new bite, then holds as the cambium behind it hardens with a crackle you can hear. There is a schedule to these motions – pulses of pressure and transpiration that run on the slope of the day – but it is not slow. By dusk, this morning's horizon will be edited.

The rules here are not domestic ones. This place is made down to its substrate, but nothing has used a tool. No hand drew these spans; no hinges hang upon them. Instead thousands upon thousands of meristems and stomata act together across distance, coordinated by pressure, light, scent, the throb of sap in conduits big as aqueducts and capillaries trickling through tissue-foam. Where the updrafts are steady and the light regular, edges extend; where the heat pools and water beads, sinusoidal walls of frond roll out to catch the fog; where stress concentrates, fibers lay on more fiber, and a rib-beam suddenly has ribs of its own. The world is built like a choir builds its own cathedral while singing.

Storm is local. You watch it assemble, high and personal. Over the span of an hour, the condensers tint their pale surfaces dark as their vessels load. The air ahead of them stripes with vapor; distant veils of rain appear in the air, going nowhere and then abruptly everywhere as a convective cell closes like a lung. It is loud. Drums of rain on plant-skin merge into waterfalls into a tearing, upholstery-rip sound as a fresh seam fails and sloughs down the flank of a vault into a vat below. Thunder folds into that noise, born not from far sky-strata but down low, from charge that builds in the churning mist balls and discharges along hairline filaments that the plants raise in expectation. In the storm-cycles the architecture flexes. Beams bow, recover, bow farther, and then snap back new-stiff, the broken place immediately rimmed in green as little growing faces take their cue.

Water here does not just fall. It is pumped. Under the aprons you can feel, through knees and palms, the steady push of sap. Some of the broadest runnels under the surface act like valves. With each loud, leaf-stirring gust the platform exhales; with each quieting it sucks. The syncopation is felt as a tremor, seen as a shifting gloss as films of water on the floor reverse direction and hurry the other way. It keeps nutrients in circulation without help from any creature that moves of its own accord. Where you might expect rot and the patient work of a decomposer guild, there are small, blind hollows curtained with rootlet hair. Anything fallen and dead is carried there by surface tension and the tug of the pulses. The hollows are warm and sweet-smelling, faintly acid. Green mouths with no leaves – smooth swales of tissue – open within them and drink the digest. This is the only place where the sound quiets. The mouths make a faint kissing noise, a sound of careful thrift.

Tall features are far between, but when one rises into view it is felt in the composition of the sky. The biggest columns sprout apical crowns worthy of weather maps: fans of thick, blue-green planes fluted into gills, so hot with sun they cook rain from the air. Each crown is its own local climate. Beneath one, the light is milky and stays that way even on clear days. Under another, everything is beaded; steps grow slick; the catchment gutters overflow and flick water outwards in silver arcs that fog before they fall. Those crowns are in conversation. Bands of warm air braid between them; you can see the paths where aerial threads once latched and the skin has not yet caught up its own tornness. The gaps are edged with new comb, scalloped, promising to close the distance soon. Valleys size the space between these presences; each valley is its own system, with its own taste of resin on the air, its own mix of sugar-in-bloom or tannin or salt. The emptiness between keeps them discrete, and gives you distances flat enough that a runner can draw a straight edge for a while before the world starts correcting it.

The material palette is vegetal but mineralized. Under fingers, the dry ridges are not purely woody; they are gritty, silica-larded, hard enough to nick your palm. Translucent inclusions fleck the living sheets, as if frost were forever embedded in them. Some places feel like horn. Others are tight-laced as wicker, springy when you jump. All of it moves water. The plants have made themselves not only walls and roofs but pipes, valves, sieves, flutes, resonators. They do not let the world be quiet. Air drawn through the slit-vents of the vaults becomes a tuned moan; sudden flare-offs of vapor from a sunlit burner-leaf pop like knucklebones; long, long fibers taught across spans sing a thin, almost metallic note that fades and reappears as humidity changes. On clear days there is the constant sound of leaves as cloth. On wet ones it is rainfall’s roar. Between, the big, breathy hush of updraft.

The strangeness is more in what the system is willing to change than in any impossible law. Space is not fixed. A span you crossed by balance-walking a narrow, lightly arched rib will, a day later, be a deep foam-floored throat; a court open to the sky will be canopied by the convergence of two plates that were separate in the morning. The living surfaces treat geometry as a negotiable pact with flow and force. They remember useful forms and return to them, but not forever. Give them heat and water and they will try another line, experiment with another fold. The horizon is an argument, and every edge sharper than you expect it to be. Few things are permanent enough to grow moss. Nothing sits long enough to be colonized by what is not itself a plant. Even dust doesn’t last; pollen falls in curtains and is either drunk or glued into the next layer.

There are no trails made by tread. Where something has the look of a path, it is because the plant wanted a path there: a lingering ribbon of untextured, wetted skin that invites footfall but was extruded to speed water or light, not you. Where something looks like a building, with rooms and courtyards, that is because those shapes do what the builder needs – catch light at the correct angles, abrade wind into informative turbulence, herd rain to where thick, hungry mouths wait – not because anyone meant to sleep there. The absence of any other minds playing at construction is felt in how unapologetic the place is about suddenness. The edge of a terrace will buckle into a new rib mid-step; a sheet will form a lip and then a beard of new growth off that lip as easily as a field grows a shadow-cloud. It is not hostile; it is motivated.

Night cools it but not to silence. Heat leaves reluctantly, sliding from the crowns and eddies under the vaults, coiling in the hollows. The plant’s breath slows and deepens; the organ-notes drop a register. Where a day breeze shook bright shivers from filmwater, the dark condenses it into proper beads. It drips. It drips from the same point precisely, long enough to notch and darken the living ledge under it, and then the notch is healed and the drip has to find a new seam. Stars appear, legible in torn slots between crowns. They strobe a little when a thread of vapor passes quickly. Sometimes a thin line of distant discharge pulls between two high condensers – a vein of light so faint you doubt it – and the next morning the air there will smell of iron and the tissue under your hand will feel newly armored.

The ecology is entirely green-minded and extraordinarily opportunistic. Kilometers of plain are empty of verticals not because nothing can grow there, but because there is a map that says the verticals belong elsewhere, feed better, function better. Every presence you see is at work. The low mats creep where the pressure gradient tells them the next good seam will be. The absorbers open and close their doors to catch the angle of a sun that is just far enough away to seem ordinary. The digester mouths are sealed or sipping. The fibers lay on or rest; valves are open or half-closed to keep the warm-shock from breaking the next curve. Growth doesn’t come from a single crown lording over a plain but from a distributed wanting, and it is satisfied in fits and starts with an energy that feels like weather.

There are rules you learn between the roars. Trust color: gloss and paleness mean youth and slip, old surfaces varnish into matte. Stay off the leading lip of anything beautiful; lips are invitations to edges to roll. Treat distances skeptically; the far side of that emptiness might not exist by the time you cross the intervening plain. Listen for the deep beat; it tells you when the sap-pulse is coming, when the exhaling will slick every surface underfoot and the inhaling will tug mist into the hollows and make the walls sweat. In the spaces between presences, stay long enough and you can feel the plateau draft itself. What is absent is as deliberately placed as what is present.

Everything is made, but made the way bone makes itself, the way frost makes a fern on glass – by the patient insistence of a material that has grammar enough to articulate itself. High, hot, and newly hard, the plant-architecture is still settling into what it is. It does not need anything that isn’t it. It breathes all the presence this air can hold and spends it loud. It climbs the sky and milks the clouds and drops its own rain back down. It is a young, wet engine built out of leaves, and it has miles yet to invent.
