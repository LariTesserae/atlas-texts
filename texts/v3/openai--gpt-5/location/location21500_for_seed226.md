The path climbs until there is less world than weather. Air thins. Everything you touch is cold to the skin—the rock, the ropes, the rain that isn’t rain yet but a slow collection of beads. Moss paints the stone in velvets and slicks underfoot, then breaks to patches where the mountain shows its new face: unscarred planes of gray-green bedrock like plates of a newborn shell. Edges are sharp. Fractures run ruler-straight, as if the ridge had just been lifted and the lines of stress were still drawn upon it: chalk marks not yet rubbed into river.

You crest the ridge and the ground falls away into a round absence. A bowl about the size of a small village has been taken out of the highland—a clean, vertical mouth with stone lips. The walls are sheer and sweating. Even in hard daylight the interior is threaded with pale veils as mist catches itself on things and lets go again. A single rope bridge arcs across the void, a fine brushstroke in a volume mostly made of air. The absence is not quiet. Wind pulled over the ridge dives into the mouth and is given voice by what the inhabitants have made of the rim.

They have ringed the sink with works: terraces that do not grow out over the drop like brag but keep a respectful arm’s length from it, scalloped layers of laced poles, mortised timbers, and stone ledges, each wet-edged with dripping ferns. Their joinery is patient and strong—no nails, no iron. Pegs and knots, woven splice, bark rope gone dark with weather. Between the terraces stand frames stretched with mesh—hair-fine cords and mats of netted fiber that billow and shudder in the gusts. Drops gather on every thread. They race along taut lines toward clay-lipped funnels and tilt into buried cisterns. The place does not have a river; this is its river, conjured from air.

It smells of resin and mineral. It tastes of penny and leaf if you lick your lips while you stand here. Your ears, though—your ears belong to the mountain. The air organ is always sounding. Someone, or perhaps several someones over generations, carved flutes into the inner lip of the bowl and lashed bamboo and bone tubes in ports all around the rim. They are not a single instrument but a distributed one in layers: long throats that moan a note that sits in the belly, shorter reeds that keen and overblow, shallow slots that hiss. Together they make weather audible in a language that changes as slowly as a cloud crossing the sun. Today the wind is high and cold, and you have to pitch your thoughts above a constant antiphon of vowels—the A of fear, the O of recognition, sometimes thin I-sounds that are like the edge of glass.

The ring quarters—a hamlet’s worth—are built close but not thick. Empty reaches are part of the plan; you can walk twenty paces along a deck and have no one within a hundred of you in any direction that isn’t along the boards. What populates the middle of your sight is not people but space, and the busy pass of droplets. Here and there someone crosses a span with a basket of cut moss. A child runs a hand down a rope to watch her palm bead silver. A trio of elders in hide-wrapped boots leans over the lip where a new crack has opened, measuring it with knotted strings and patient eyes. They do not shout; you do not need to be told it would be useless to try.

Beyond those immediate figures everything is plant and shape. Lush is the first word that arrives. Lush in a place without soil depths: green drawn out of rock and wind. Ferns unroll in galleries where the spray is strongest. Epiphytes grow their own loam from the air, stacking bones of their dead into cradles for the next leaf. Bromeliads cradle elbows of water that bead and spill, bead and spill. Moss is everywhere it can be, stratified to match the microclimates within a hand’s breadth: pale haircap on the drip-line; dark sponge at a beam’s foot; emerald flow on the face of the wall where it permanently weeps. There are rope-gardens: lattices of living branches braided over many years into tense parabolas, each sprung from a pocket of soil brought here basket by basket. Their leaves dampen the wind and drink it at once. The built and the grown are not separate categories so much as two verbs that keep helping each other.

Because the mountain is new in its costume—the stone is raw, the ledges still edged like cut bread—erosion has not yet had its say. There are no deep gullies veining away from the rim; the scant topsoil scrims where it can, thin as letter paper. The water does not run; it meets the rock and thinks about it for a long time, then moves as a seep that outlines strata. In the bowl, every shelf, every seam, has its signature of wet. From some, drops fall from exactly the same points minute after minute, hour after hour, leaving needle-straight columns of air punctuated by glitter and percussion: plick, plock, a clock that never quite agrees with itself. From others it is weeping rather than dripping, a sheen that swells and lets go in sheets an atom thick, the way tears hang and lose their edges.

The sound of those drops is not lost in the organ’s hoot. The bowl is an amplifier with preferences: low voices are given a throne, but the high, strict notes of water find their brackets and ring like threads of ice tapped with a fingernail. If you lean against a wet wall, the thrum travels into your bones. You can feel when a gust is approaching before it pushes you—pressure rising on your back, the notes changing their color. There are places where the builders have set wind-harps you can play with your hands—stretched gut and hollow gourds tuned to the local gusts. The children know where they are. They run small cold fingers along strings that are already singing.

There is life besides them, yes, but not much, and it keeps its own counsel. Once in a long while, a bird rides the bowl like a coal thrown outward; a dark sickle turns once across the mouth of the world and is gone. There are midges over the cisterns where a patch of sun warms a cup of water; a white moth as big as your hand appears unexpectedly in a corner between two beams and beats its way away with a noise like paper. Down in a seam near your feet, a wet salamander moves its tiny hands with care, then stays, eyes closed because all days are a single long cool thing to it. Mostly what lives here are plants, and the people.

The people have rules that fit this new stone. They build out, not down. They have a law against letting weight gather over the mouth. They will not quarry the inside of the bowl because it has not finished telling them who it is. The way they test the mountain is not to strike it but to listen to its song over years. The elders keep a book of tones—they have no ink but charcoal and ochre, so their records are cords and stones, lengths and weights: this much string for the low of frost-season winds, this small blue pebble for the whistle that began last leaf-fall and did not return after snow. Children learn pitch-arithmetic the way others learn to count steps.

The technology is clever without being sharp. No blades of steel flash here; the only glints are wet and, very occasionally, copper—hammered thin and green at the edges now, laid over a slit where the wind needed a lip. Tools are wood and horn, stone polished to a gloss where it meets rope, chisels made of tough bone wedged into hardwood handles. For heavy work they use leverage and patience, and when patience is not enough, more patience. Saplings are planted with intent and coaxed with guy lines to become beams two generations down. Fiber is spun from bast and lichen-silk, then knotted into nets fifty paces wide. Nets catch fog; nets catch nothing else. The settlement’s most intricate machines are doors that are really woven curtains of wet-resistant grass whose weight closes them against the fetch and opens them to the lull, and siphons that creep out of cisterns like sleeping snakes to feed troughs in the gardens on a schedule imagined in clay.

Loudness defines behavior. Conversation happens with hands. Signals are flags, patterned cloths that pop and curl on high poles. The meanings are layered, tuned to the day's song—this twist for bringing more baskets to the third terrace because the cistern there is filling fast, that fold meaning a crack has lengthened; move the goat-hutch off that span before night. In stormweather they do not carve because the tools sing back and the wood tends to split wrong, which is how you ruin effort. In windlull they sleep in rooms that are like burrows made of woven bark, low and warm, because through the day their bodies have been a drum skin stretched over their bones.

They live in “rooms” that are more like chambers tucked into the edges of the ring: narrow and long, with sleeping planks set back from the constant drafts. Doorways are thick with planted fern to feather the air—wet muffles. The light in them is normal daylight bent and made green by leaves, or the indirect white of frost when the fogs lift on a winter noon. There is no fire in the open here. Fire is banked, a domestic thing in clay under chimneys that zigzag so their breath is broken up before it joins the organ and adds its own note. Smoke leaves in sighs; the mountain keeps its bigger music unaltered.

As for the rules of geology, this place publishes them plainly. If you place a hand flat on any of the crisp surfaces of stone, you can feel the slight spring in it when a gust hits. That cannot be true; rock is not a drum. But here, at this size, the bowl-bone of the mountain is still settling into where it wants to live. The shifts are not shocks. They are slow chords, whole days tuned a shade lower as water loads the rim and the cisterns empty, seasons bowed a key higher with winter’s dry air. The living here take readings without instruments because the instruments are all around them and already speaking: the paces between familiar drips, the beat-frequency between two harps that used to agree, the way a flag refuses to fly on a day when every other one is canted.

Half the terrain is vertical. The other half is air with a route drawn through it. That route is the bridge over the mouth, which has been rebuilt so many times that no single generation can claim it. It is terrifying and trustworthy. It sings too, but under your feet, the hum of strands under tension, a low talk you understand not with your ears but with the tendons of your ankles. In the center of the span, they have knotted a bunch of wet grass and hung a small clay dish from it. Today it is brimmed from the mist alone. A woman in a tunic of twisted reed steps out to it and tips it into a skin bottle. Her face is wet as if she has wept. She laughs, but you cannot hear the sound. The organ takes her laughter and harmonizes it into its vowels.

Beyond the ring, just a little way down from the lip, there are begins of ledges that might one day be paths. They are cairn-wide, enough to edge along if you are a goat. Nobody has sent a goat there. The goats live on the upslope, small and sure, and when they occasionally stray toward the rim, they are gently turned. There is a taboo about casting shadows into the bowl unless you must. The people believe—not from doctrine but from practice and repetition—that shadows thick with movement teach water to fall quickly, and they need it to learn to fall as seep and sheen here. If you laugh at that, you only have to spend a day with them to see how a thousand small avoidances and tendings add up to a shape in the world.

If you stay into evening, when the sun skims level with the far ridge, the loudness changes timbre without quieting. The organ warms as the air cools, notes slowly an inch lower and the hisses more like silk shredding. The plants exhale after swallowing sun all day. The drenched green darkens until the ferns are almost black, their edges limned. Droplets now catch more fire than water, each a tiny lens that keeps a shard of day. The people move less. A dog you did not see before shakes itself dry on a high deck and settles. Somewhere below, a frog makes a sound that feels impolite in all this solemnity and is answered by nothing. Night wind here is a long vow. It promises that this bowl, this new rock mouth with its fringe of crafts and gardens, will change—oh, it will, slowly—and the vow is kept not in foaming rivers and landslides, but in a millennium of drips counting themselves into a pool you cannot see. That is the tempo.

Sleep in this place is like cleft rock: a notch carved between one long tone and the next. Morning gives you back the same landscape, not quite. A bright crack like a wrinkle deepens less than a hair’s width. A net sags a finger lower under the studded weight of dew. A child you saw yesterday now carries a reed that is a joint longer. Someone has swapped one copper lip for a fresh bark edge and, imperceptibly, the organ has learned a new vowel. The mountain is still coming into voice; the settlement, with its patient tiers and its insistence that nothing crowded is likely to endure up here, is a mouth that answers.
