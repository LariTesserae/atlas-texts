First there is the sound, a continuous, body-deep roar that drowns out thought and makes a drum of your ribcage. You step into the cleft and it becomes a room where the noise lives: a rock throat high on a ridge above the world, not much larger than a chapel, the sky scraped thin and white with altitudes, the sun a hard coin, the air already short of breath. In that room, everything is water.

It hangs and rushes simultaneously, a storm held in a bowl. Sheets fold in from an opening in the roof where cloud pours through, not as rain precisely, but as a rope of atmosphere so saturated it falls in visible planes, wrung to liquid by the contour of the stone. The place is submerged without a sea: the air is mostly water, and in the moments when the flow thickens, the pocket goes truly underwater; you watch the light smear into blue glass and feel sound turn from a howl into a humming pressure. When the flow thins, breath returns as spray and mist, a gill’s reprieve. Everything cycles in heartbeats, in the twitch-time of wind, in the tremor of unseen gusts strumming the ridge.

The rock is new to the air. You can tell by the color, a deep, matte basalt that has not yet learned to shine with lichen, by the way the edges of the cracks refuse to be rounded, by the glassy rinds on some surfaces that catch sunlight and return it as stuttering rainbows through the veil of water. Columnar joints make hexagonal cupboards in the walls, the hexes misaligned just enough to create shallow cavities that the flow finds and fills and explodes from. There is little sand, little gravel. The place is too recently lifted or too constantly scoured to have made those crumbs. The stone is knife and organ at once, bevels cut for this one purpose: to turn moving water into voice.

Normal daylight slants in like a practiced hand. It knifes through cracks in the roof, through the glassed seams, through the open mouth where the atmospheric river enters. Every droplet holds a sun. Your eyelashes gain beads and prisms. Sometimes the room is all prismatic planes and moving arcs — the water makes its own architecture out of light — then the arcs are torn away, replaced by a single, milk-white wall as a new pulse arrives from the sky and crushes complexity into blankness for a beat.

The air tastes of stone and salt though the sea is unimaginably far below. The salt is a trickle-memory, a residue the water brings from a thousand kilometers of cloud-work. It leaves a quiet rime in a few sheltered notches: fine, new crystals like hoarfrost, delicate and temporary. Slight metallics ride the mist — iron, maybe, liberated from the mountain by the constant microfracturing of cavitation — and there is a sharpened smell, a tang of ozone and wet basalt you taste on the root of your tongue.

Whatever rule holds this room together is not gravity alone. Sound has jurisdiction here. The stone has been cut, whether by intent or accident, into hollows that sing; their notes thicken or thin the water. A low, fluted cavity on the east wall stabilizes into a subsonic moan every time a gust pushes from that side, and when it moans, a tulip of water blooms beneath it, holding steady as if poured into an invisible mold. Cross-currents snarl into ropes that should by every right unravel, and then a new frequency slides in and the ropes tighten into cords and you understand that shapes can be instructed to exist if the tuning is right. The place is a listening machine as much as a geologic feature. The mountain hears the wind and answers with a grammar of waves.

There are marks that someone else noticed and listened to, too. The built things are crude and deft all at once. A length of gut, silver in the mist, is strung taut across a fissure and anchored with smoothed stones wedged into opposing hexagonal cells, an instrument’s single string. In bursts of spray it thrums audibly above the crush of water, a mosquito-whine that carves out a little alcove downstream of itself by insisting on that one frequency; the flow avoids the whine, and in that avoidance the air densifies, and a hand-width volume is safer to breathe. A set of pegs has been hammered into a joint, and thin slats of water-swollen wood are pressed between them to make a primitive baffle. They’re not trying to stop the water; they’re tuning it, herding it, tilting its habits. A flat stone is chocked with smaller ones so it cannot rattle; it becomes a sustained tone instead of a clatter. A driftwood ladder, furred with algae and recent, leans in a corner where the flow occasionally slackens; its rungs are notched, and the notches are worn into tiny flumes that sing separate, bright notes when the ladder floods. A woven basket, bone-framed and gut-meshed, is lashed on the brink of a side eddy. Mostly it sits empty and taut as a drum. Sometimes, for a few minutes, it fills with small white commas — blind amphipods that have ridden winds since before you woke and have chosen, by mistake or design, to live in a thunder-cup.

Vegetation is threadbare. Steam-hair mosses cling where they can trap a little certainty in a crack. Film algae make streaming flags on a few sheltered lips, green as a held breath, lit to jewels when the sun finds them. Lichens are babies here, mere hints, dust more than paint. A few skeletal rosettes of saxifrage crouch just inland of the spray, on the dry side of the noise, tight clenched fists against the cold altitude wind. They flower between storms when the sound fails, and they seed before the next atmospheric river hooks the ridge and the room returns to drowning.

Animals are rarities, accidents of weather made local. Once, a grey gull flitted in and tried to ride the flow like ocean surf — a bright incision of wing and eye — and was thrown out of the aperture seconds later, spitting and stunned, to catch itself in blue air and continue downslope. More commonly there are little things: the blind amphipods, as pale and sturdy as grains of rice, flickering in the basket; a stonefly larva that has learned to clamp into hexagonal creases and let the torrent bring it whatever nourishment dissolves in pure noise; a salamander the color of ash with pinpoints for eyes that lurks where the sound carves a stillness and emerges to clean a film of algae with its chin. Everything trembles. Everything wears its nerves on the outside because that is the only way to make decisions here; ears and lateral lines are the same instrument. The few and the brave survive because they can feel syllables of current in their bones.

Despite the endless movement, there is emptiness. Between the sheer of water and the clean planes of rock, there is space with very few presences, and those that are here are small, tuned, provisional. No one lingers; even the mountain beneath the phenomena feels adolescent, not settled into itself yet. If you step a meter outside the mouth, the whole is gone, subsumed into the wilderness of ridge and racing cloud and the distant, sun-hammered slab of sky. The room hides in its own performance.

Temperature is not cruel but exacting. The water is mountain-cold, the kind that steals the heat from your fingers in seconds. The air, when there is air, is cool and thin; after a minute you hear your heartbeat under the roar, making its own small, private drumline, competing, not successfully, with the great instrument. Breath leaves you in smoke ribbons that vanish with a single gust. In the gloss where the room is truly underwater, you hold your breath and your bones hear instead, and there is a thought there that is not language: that you are inside a throat and the throat is singing you.

Erosion has not yet had its era. The surfaces show the last violent thing that happened to them — an uplift, a cooling, a shatter — more than they show time. You can find the freeze-spall still sharp as paper cuts on the edges. There are no bowls yet except those the sound has declared. The sound declares and deletes them ruthlessly. One hour it builds a cup that you can drink from. The next hour that cup is ground away, replaced by an angle that throws the water as a sheet. Solaris-like, sculptures of liquid self-assemble from focus and are consumed by the next idea of wind. The only reliable architecture is the pressure of sound and the ability of water to pretend it is more than one thing at once.

If there are rules beyond this, they are quiet and odd. A given note dims lightning — not from the sky, but the miniature sparks you sometimes see in cavitation when a collapse is so violent it bites the dark. When the wind drops into a certain octave, the amphipods stop moving as if hypnotized; when it lifts again, they resume. A string of gut slackening a fraction alters the breathing pocket enough to keep a fox from drowning itself while stealing the basket’s catch. The people who built these few things might not live up here, but they visit when the song allows a door in the water. They come with hand tools: stone hammers wrapped in hide for grip, bone awls, loops of sinew, wedges. They carry no metal. They carry dried grass that they stuff into a resonant slit and set with a wooden peg to quiet a shriek for an hour so their ears do not bleed. They leave quickly. Their marks are pragmatic prayers: sing a little lower; turn the torrent left not right; let this place stay, if only until morning.

Outside, the ridge knifes on, and the world is big again, and the sound diminishes into background thunder behind the curtain of spray. Inside, the conditions endure and change and endure. It is a small place and nearly empty, a hidden organ at very high altitude that the weather plays. It is submerged most of the time, almost-ocean in a cup, and inside its held flood things are constantly being born and undone by the same syllable. You could not live here, but you can be inside it while it speaks.

A glossed note for later: when the sky clears and the atmospheric river lifts, there is a half-hour when the room is simply a clean-cut chamber of new stone, slick with memory, the gut string hanging silent, the baffle slats resting like the bones of a bird. The sun burns straight in. The only remaining sound is the after-tremor you mistake for your own blood — until a wind-strand tugs the ridge again and the first sheet of water lays itself into place and the mountain begins to pronounce, and the held sea returns.
