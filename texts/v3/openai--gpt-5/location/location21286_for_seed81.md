Even before the city, the rock is wet. The high stone is hot enough to blur the air above it, yet it sweats. Thin films bead and draw together along patient, hairline grooves; droplets gather under ledges and plink with a sound you learn to measure distance by. Where the surface bows downward, lipped basins have formed from ages of mineral deposition—a pale, chalky rim around a tea-dark mirror no deeper than a hand. Everything is coated in something: silica skin, iron blush, the sheen of moisture slicking black volcanic host. If you look long, the rock itself seems to exhale.

The horizon is far and low from up here. The terrain is a weathered swell: the mouth of an ancient caldera rim and a plateau beyond, smoothed and scalloped, heaved and then settled over epochs. Edges are rounded as though someone has been rubbing this whole place with a wet thumb for centuries. Folded shelves step down in broad arcs, like amphitheaters that face a stage the clouds hide. The clouds are always there in some form—thick and dim, a lid that lowers and lifts with pressure tides. The light through them is the color of old brass. It never quite becomes day; it never quite stops being day.

Heat lives under the skin of the world. Lift a stone and steam buttonholes up. Press your palm to a shaded wall and you feel a steady exudation of warmth, not the hot sting of a fire but something deeper, like the vessel heat of a living animal. Water near the surface meets stone like a persistent negotiation: condense, dissolve, percolate, leave a ring. There is no rain to speak of, not the kind that streaks the air and drums itself into sense. Instead you get vertical veils of condensation, ghosts passing along the faces of cliffs, dew arriving sideways when barometric tides turn, whole streets that go from dry to beaded in the time it takes to cross them. Things grow not by sprouting but by accruing; a handrail thickens imperceptibly into a fat, chalked tube; steps fur with travertine; the undersides of bridges sprout translucent stalactites like teeth.

And into this, built and careful, the city. It is as skeletal as bone and as spread as a spider web. It occupies the emptiness between structures with an almost religious zeal for gap and channel. Low-slung causeways of gridded steel span mud-colored hollows and mineral flats. Lattice towers with open ribs rise high above the terraces, their exteriors armored in hammered plates made slick on purpose to coax dew. From some angles the place looks abandoned; there is too much sky between tenements, too many catwalks that lead to nothing but air and a measured drop. But walk along the rails and you hear the quiet hydraulics underneath. Sit still and you notice the movement within stillness—the blind swing of a float valve, the regular swivel of a wiper along a broad condenser face, the twitch of a filament lamp testing its own filament in the damp.

This is a city predicated on harvesting the invisible. The tall racks are for catching water from the air, black slats set in stacks, angled to create a cold edge against warm breath. You could think of them as lungs, perpetually condensing the world’s intake, wheezing it out again as it warms, pulling a trickle into gutters that sing small threads. Pipes run like root systems along buttresses, then disappear into vaults below; inside those the heat differential turns turbines idle-smooth, big flywheels wrapped in leather belts, dynamos that thrum at pitches too low for your ears but evident in the way your ribs want to agree with them. Everything drips. Even the wires are beaded.

Industry here is not loud. Mills exist, sure, but they are enclosed beneath condensation skins and muffled by water’s hands. You see riveted tanks the size of houses, their domes slick with pearl; you see little locomotives bulbous with boiler jackets creeping along rails that ring the plateau, their wheels leaving wet prints that dry to pale loops. Factory facades: thin brick that weeps salt, corrugated iron with the sheen of scale, windows that are more like gills than eyes, breathing back and forth as pressure shifts. It is not a place of roaring foundries and flaming stacks; instead it is a patient engine, a system designed to drink, to filter, to heat and unheat and unspool work from the small rope of a perpetual difference.

Part of why it is quiet is the air itself. Up this high, sound goes farther in the imagination than it does in reality. The atmosphere is not thin exactly—your lungs fill and empty—but it is soft with suspended condensate, padded with steam, and the temperature gradients bend noise. Someone calling across a block’s width becomes a thought you can almost have again twenty heartbeats later, as if the city murmurs to itself in its own future tense. The steady hiss you hear is ten different things added together and reduced to a single tone: steam leaving a fissure, brine trickling through a rack, wind combing the railings, the fine spray on the underlip of a spillway.

There is not much that is green. You see growth, yes, but none of it is leaf. In sheltered hollows, in the respirations of stairwells and beneath overhangs, fringes of web-thin pellicle lap the stone, translucent or pearl-gray, drying into paper when the barometric tide is low and returning to gelatin when it rises. Films the thickness of regret sheath the mouths of sumps; they shiver along the surface when a droplet vibrates in. There is a dust the color of tea that is not dust but feeding—colonies that brush away in filaments, that smell faintly of copper and lemon when you step on them. Under the grated walkways, sheets of something slow and purposeful ripple along, retreating from bright, loving the wet.

The fauna, when you notice it, feels improbable. Once in a week you might witness a glide: a banded shape the length of your arm unhinging itself from some hot shade and sailing, unbatting and near-dematerialized, from condenser to condenser, tongue tasting the mineral beads. It reattaches in secret and you keep walking. In a sump, very rarely, you will see a finger-width thing with a lamp-pearl at its throat roll and withdraw from the sudden shade your body is. There are the stiff-shelled sippers, little tripod things as if cut from smoke-black glass, that attach to pipes and hum for a day before falling away dull. Hear the click under your boot? A scut—white as grave wax, lensing moisture from the grating and concentrating it against a tiny mouth. All of them scarce enough that their memory feels like a dream you could have made up.

Erosion is not an enemy here; it is how the place keeps time. The edges of spillways have a fingered look, each finger representing a season of slightly different chemistry. The city adds and subtracts from itself to remain itself. Workers—few and far between, soft-footed and dressed to swick water from skin—walk the routes with long-handled scrapers, paring back where accretion would close a vein, letting grow where a gutter needs thickening to avoid chatter. Stone is not moved in blocks but in skins. The city has learned to build things that accept a slow fur; every material choice is a compromise with the habit of this sky to lay down.

A few rules everyone knows and no one writes, because how could you not learn them.

- Water goes toward heat with as much conviction as it goes toward low places. Put a cup in the shade and you will collect in its upturned lip more than you will if you hold it in the direct middle of the light. The city’s designers understood that edges are the best places to find anything.

- Corrosion is less an accident than a partnership. Copper left alone will sprout lace. Steel left plain will blacken and then glaze; glazed steel will resist. The most durable structures are not the most inert ones, but the ones that can host thin growing skins that seal them.

- Night is not cooler. Night is a mood. The clouds lower and scatter heat differently; some bays collect more dew under stars, some less. Evaporation sometimes happens downhill, fog condensing back into the earth, little falls of vapor tumbling into cracks like feathers.

- Fire is disappointing. It is possible, yes, but it resents itself, eats damp fuel with a sulky, orange slouch, and needs coaxing; it makes a lot of smoke that goes nowhere and sounds like breath being held. Sparks, though, are eager. Electricity loves this place. Lightning rarely strikes from above; more often it seeps along edges, runs in a wire where you expected rust.

- Distance is deceptive. The city is broad but not crowded; most of it is invisible when you stand in any one place, because the important things are lower than seeing or too dry to show. You must walk to know scale here. What horizon is for other cities, the next conduit is for this one.

There is weather. Not storms, but moods. A day of “weeps”—a thousand new droplets from every seam, walls going to fish scales, the ground a satin of tiny rivulets. A day of “sighs”—when the racks breathe out into the streets and every hinge, every unanswered door creaks on the exhale. Times when the cloud lid moves just enough that red light gets down the canyons and the city’s wet things burn with an inner ember, function masquerading as beauty. Times of murk, when the sound no one makes is the loudest thing of all, and the only visible motion is a hanged thread of steam curling from a fissure in the square, and you can hear, or think you hear, the weight of the plateau settling, rocks adjusting shoulder to shoulder far below.

Humans, if that is who holds the place, are rare presences within their own works. You might follow a set of prints in the shallow sheen along a deck—a splay of soft-heeled workers' shoes—up the ramp and over the grating and lose them where the sun, such as it is, has sucked the moisture back. There are rooms with tools kept finicky, rags laid just so to wick, chalk marks tallied beside a slowly thickening wall. The city is not abandoned; it is simply content to be mostly machine and weather, with people threaded thinly through it like wire. Out beyond, on the far terraces, an unmanned crane leans over a void and lifts a skin of mineral from the rim, a peel of white like unfurling bark. Somewhere deeper than deep, a piston cycles. Somewhere higher than high, a wiper arm sweeps a condenser plate, and in sweeping makes the day’s only crisp sound.

At the edge of town where the plateau falls away, the weeping is most dramatic. Water extravasates from the very cliff face, not in falls but in endless blown grains, drifting off into the low cloud. If you stand there long enough with your back to the drop, the moisture will collect in the small of your back before it does on your face, so sure is it about seeking warm shelters. Turn around and you see the dim lit plain far below only sometimes, as if the world under the world wants privacy.

You come to understand that the strangeness here is not an imposed oddity but a consequence of a few truths leaned into very hard. High heat under slow light. Air thick with promise and thin with sound. Stone that does not mind becoming other things inch by inch. A city that has made an ethic out of absence: vast space between presences, so that the presences can do what they do—condense, coil, hum, weep—without bumping elbows. It is a place of patience. It is a place that appears still because all its motions are committed to the kind of work that refuses spectacle. Every drip is an economy; every shimmer is an account kept balanced across centuries.
