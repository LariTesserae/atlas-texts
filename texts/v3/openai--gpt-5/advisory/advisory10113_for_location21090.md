Orientation
- What it is: A continuous, living interior just under a sun-scoured plain—more organ than cave—kept at a steady bright “day” by light-conducting cords and tens of thousands of tiny solar windows far above. Forms add to themselves rather than erode; cycles flatten toward maintenance. You are walking inside a plant that has decided to be architecture.
- How it feels: Warmth like an animal’s flank, humidity that comes and goes in small thresholds, silence that erases distance, light that is everywhere and never changes.
- Main constraints: Heat stress, dehydration, disorientation (no sunrise/sunset cues, shallow shadows), ethical fragility (you are inside a living body), very quiet acoustics (your noise carries), slippery/suede alternations underfoot, delicate waters and mineral crusts that don’t want you in them.
- The rule of being here: Do as little harm as possible; let the place decide your pace.

Before you go
- Acclimate to heat and humidity for 3–5 days if you can (light walking in 30–35°C, 60–80% RH conditions).
- Zero-scent protocol for 48 hours: unscented soap, no deodorant/perfume, no insect repellent, no fabric softeners. The air is information here; don’t overwrite it.
- Decontaminate gear: scrub soles, launder soft goods hot, wipe hard goods with 70% alcohol, then rinse in clean water. You’re trying not to import spores or seeds from other places.
- Mental prep: Expect time to lose its edges. Plan your own cycle with alarms (meals, hydration, rest). Decide in advance to move slowly.

Clothing and personal kit
- Clothing
  - Lightweight, smooth-surfaced layers that don’t snag. Long sleeves and pants protect you from resins and pollen without leaving skin oils behind.
  - Fabrics: silk or fine synthetic knits for next-to-skin; loose, breathable outer. Merino is tolerable but holds humidity; linen breathes but can snag; opt for tight, slick weaves.
  - Head/eye: thin cap or bandanna to manage sweat; light-tint glasses if the unshadowed brightness wears on you; carry a contoured sleep mask for rest periods.
  - Hands: thin nitrile or plant-safe barrier gloves for moments when you must touch ribs, resins, or tender tufa. Bare hands only where you are certain you’ll leave no oils.
  - Footwear: soft, non-marking soles with micro-siping (boat-shoe or split-sole dance shoe style). No lugs, no spikes. Bring a second pair of dry socks; feet will cycle from slick to suede conditions.
  - Cooling: a phase-change cooling vest (28–30°C PCM) buys calm in the hottest chambers. It won’t “ice” you, but it flattens peaks without demanding evaporation from this environment.

- Carry
  - Water: 2–3 liters per person per slow walking day, plus electrolytes. Closed hydration bladder with bite valve to keep hands off surfaces. Assume you will not drink from the cups.
  - Nutrition: dense, low-crumb, odor-minimized food (nut butters in sachets, bars that don’t shed). Everything re-sealable. Pack-in/pack-out.
  - Navigation: a retractable, bright, soft line (50–150 m) you can trail from your belt and retrieve—kept off surfaces, never tied to anything living. Tiny inert tags you can clip to your own line (not to the plant) for segmenting.
  - Sensing: thermometer/hygrometer, CO2/O2 monitor the size of a key fob, small IR thermometer for mapping warm/cool sap veins as landmarks. A watch with quiet haptic alarms.
  - Light/power: you won’t need illumination, but a low-lumen red lamp for camp tasks helps your head. Diffuse solar exists here; a compact panel rated for low-angle/diffuse light will trickle-charge if placed near a light cord for hours.
  - Seating/sleeping: thin closed-cell pad—insulates you from the organism and keeps your sweat and oils off the floor. Ultralight bivy or sheet for a clean interface.
  - Waste: double-seal waste bags, pee bottle with tight cap, spare liners. Nothing liquid or solid should touch the place.
  - First aid: heat-care kit (oral rehydration salts, anti-nausea tabs), antihistamine, hydrocortisone for resin/pollen reactions, saline vials to rinse eyes, blister kit. No topical antibiotics smearing on local surfaces.
  - Miscellaneous: soft brush to clean soles before stepping from one microhabitat to another; microfiber cloth for condensation on your gear; trekking cane with a felted tip for balance (never for probing or parting curtains).

Etiquette with a living architecture
- Treat every surface—leaf carpet, ribs, cords, tufa—as skin. If you wouldn’t press, scrape, or lean with weight on someone’s body uninvited, don’t do it here.
- Never touch the clear light cords. They are both the lighting and the circulatory system.
- Do not enter or step in the shallow pools, lick cups, or harvest dew. Those are scheduled resources for attendants and structure. Your electrolytes come from your pack.
- Avoid sitting or lying on the darkened oval “beds.” They belong to something that returns to them, precisely.
- Don’t part vine skins to “shortcut” parallel corridors. If a small creature can slip through, you still should not.
- Minimize shadowing. Your silhouette is an event here; keep a slow, predictable profile, especially near cups and fronds angled toward light cords.
- Don’t scrape resins. The local animal that does so is wise to their sequence; you are not.
- No open flame. The air may be oxygen-rich, resins may be flammable, and anyway smoke would coat this whole body with your story.

Moving through it
- Pacing: This place rewards slowness. 1–2 km in an unhurried day is ample. Heat and attention are the constraints, not distance.
- Footing: Surfaces alternate suede/slick even within a stride. Place your foot, test with weight, then roll through. The cane is for proprioception, not poking.
- Mapping: Expect long, parallel galleries separated by a single living skin. Record your choices; many “options” are equivalent and delay your return with no added insight.
- Landmarks that don’t move: junction geometry (three-way ribs vs. ovoid halls), the pitch of the hum from nearby light cords, the orientation of spiral columns, temperature signatures (a cool “river” of sap underfoot).
- Timekeeping: Set alarms for water (every 20–30 min sip), food (every 2–3 hours), rest (every 4–5 hours sit 20–30 min). The brightness will not remind you; make your own morning/noon/evening.

Heat and breath
- It is broadly, steadily warm. You may feel sudden skin dew where corridors pinch, and a quick drying just beyond. Let those microfronts pass; don’t chase them.
- Hyperventilation is common when silence and heat stack. If your CO2 monitor reads normal and you feel “air hunger,” sit, widen your posture, breathe low and slow for two minutes.
- Watch for heat illness: pounding head, nausea, chills, confusion. At first sign, stop, cool your core (PCM vest, water on wrists/neck—but keep drips on you, not on surfaces), sip electrolytes, reassess plan.

Water and food
- Best practice is to be water independent. The cups and strands are part of a schedule you can’t see; changing that clock is a harm.
- If you must emergency-drink, take only from a cup with fresh fall in an unvisited chamber; filter and UV-treat. Expect hardness and a mineral tang. Replace that volume later (and then some) by leaving earlier than you planned.
- Eat odorless, neat foods; wipe your hands within your own kit, not on leaves or tufa. Your crumbs are an introduction of novelty the system did not ask for.

Sound and light
- You are the loudest thing here. Use soft closures, whisper if you must talk, and let your footsteps be your metric of presence.
- The light is everywhere and shallow. Cameras will flatten scenes; bracket exposures and move in for detail. Keep desiccant on your lenses; humidity crawls.

Animals and their signs
- You will see few and be seen by more. Give distance to anything drinking or hovering at cups.
- Do not interpret slowness as ignorance. That white, limb-bundle that “reacts” to your shadow is practicing a posture. Step back and let it complete the gesture it started before you arrived.
- Read traces, don’t write them: browned lips of cups, soot-black spore swathes, the leaning tower of plates. Photograph; don’t fix or straighten.

Sleeping
- Rest off the travel path on your pad, on an unmarked section of leaf carpet, away from cups and ribs. Mask your eyes, set a gentle alarm. Your sweat and breath should stay on your gear, not sink into the floor.
- Don’t use the warmed ovals. They are reserved, and the owner may be larger than you.

Navigation confidence
- If lost: stop, cool, hydrate, listen. The hum will tell you where main conduits are; edges/vents are at regions of thinning ribs and a faint upward sigh. If you used a line, reel yourself home the way you came. Don’t try “new” because new here is very large and repeats itself kindly.
- Choose a firm turnaround rule tied to water (for example: head back when you’ve used 40% of what you carry).

Edges and exits
- Vents are narrow, vertical, and exhale warmer breath tasting of salt and chalk. Admire; don’t climb. The plant’s windows are higher and more delicate than they look.
- If you came from the plain above, protect your eyes on re-entry: the true desert sun is a blade after the plant’s skinlight. Wraparound shades, head cover, and a staged adaptation (2–5 minutes in the vent’s mouth) help.

Sampling and science
- Best practice is no extraction. If your mandate permits, only take:
  - Detached pollen/dust on your own gear.
  - A single fallen resin bead that is no longer sticky.
  - A small water sample (≤5 ml) from a cup only after observing that a local attendant has finished there—and only once per trip.
- Never incise a cord, rib, or tufa sheet. A handprint in tender tufa will remain until the sheath overwrites it; that is a century’s note you don’t want to leave.
- Document with notes, sketches, and audio of the hum and ticks rather than souvenirs.

Common mistakes to avoid
- Speed. Rushing writes scuffs and slides into the floor and tosses your sweat and salts everywhere.
- Leaning on ribs or cords to rest. Use your pad; use your own body for structure.
- Following “cooler” paths as if they lead out. Cool veins meander; the only gradient that persists is heat, and even that is gentle.
- Drinking from cups because they look “natural.” They are scheduled and small; you are not.
- Using scent (repellents, balms) to fight the place. You’ll just smell like the only stranger.

Emergency notes
- Heat exhaustion: stop, cool, sip electrolytes, lie with knees up, mask eyes. If confusion persists, begin return immediately while you can make safe choices.
- Air quality: if your O2 dips or CO2 rises unexpectedly, you may be in a dead-air pocket created by your own stillness in a concavity. Move ten slow paces; the plant’s micro-breathing will re-balance.
- Injury: clean with your saline; cover with your own dressings. Do not rinse wounds at the cups or strands.
- Panic or time-sickness: count drops when you hear them (they are metronomic), name five textures within reach without touching (tongue-leaf, disk-moss, resin skin, suede-underside, tufa sheet), sip water, and let your alarms hold the day for you.

On leaving
- Brush soles and wipe gear before crossing thresholds between microhabitats and again at the edge/exit.
- Once back in variable weather and angled shadow, many travelers feel vertigo or a sudden fatigue. Eat salt, drink water, sit in shade. Expect the plant’s even present tense to keep echoing in your head for a day or two.
- Decontaminate again to avoid exporting this place’s pollen to other worlds.

Essentials at a glance (pack list)
- 2–3 L water + electrolytes; odorless, neat food.
- Soft, non-marking footwear; slick, snag-resistant clothing; thin gloves; light cap; sleep mask.
- PCM cooling vest (optional but excellent); closed-cell pad; waste kit.
- Retractable soft line; quiet watch with haptics; CO2/O2 monitor; thermometer/hygrometer; small IR thermometer.
- Low-lumen headlamp; compact diffuse-optimized solar panel (optional).
- First-aid (heat focus); saline ampoules; antihistamine; cortisone.
- Microfiber cloth; soft brush; spare socks; sealable bags.

Last advice
- Let the plant set the meter. If novelty seems to stop, that’s not failure—that’s contact with a will for keeping. When you feel the desire to make something happen, choose instead to notice what already is: the seam of water correcting itself, the hum tallying light, the felt of leaf thickening under your pause. Move as if you were among the organs of a kindly giant, because you are.
