What you are going to see is smaller than your body knows how to be. Treat it as a threshold visit, not an entry. You are there to keep company with a pocket of ocean the size of a hollow lantern, to listen at its curtain and—very gently—borrow its slowness.

What this place asks of you
- Accept that you will not fit. A human cannot enter without wrecking it. You will observe from the seam or send in a proxy no larger than a finger.
- Plan to be quiet in every sense—no bubbles, no silt, no bright light, no sudden chemistry.
- Bring patience. Processes here register on the scale of hours and days; the room moves like a sleeping animal dreaming slowly.

Best season, tide, and weather
- Go on a clear, calm day with small swell; neap tides are best. The “curtain” at the seam droops rather than billows—gentle outside water keeps the inside calm.
- Aim for slack water. Your goal is to arrive when the outside is barely leaning, so the inside stays itself.
- Water temp is about 10°C; expect cold to leach through resolve and gear alike. Budget for long, still hovering.

Getting there
- Approach by boat or shore dive to a young basalt field. Expect fresh glass edges, uneven footing, and hidden cuts.
- You will not see the chamber from a distance. You find the seam by its behavior: a little bowing of water, a reluctance of silt to cross, a hush. If you carry a hand to find it, your hand is already too loud; use your eyes and instruments.

Life support and exposure
- Wear a drysuit with good undergarments; this is a low-motion, high-chill visit. If you prefer wetsuit, make it thick and accept shorter observation windows.
- Use closed-circuit rebreather if you dive. Open-circuit exhalations near the seam will lace the pocket with bubbles that linger for minutes, bruise the mats, and write your presence for days. If you can’t dive CC, watch from further back and keep your bubbles well downslope.
- Gloves protect you from glass-sharp rock but shed fibers; pick tight-weave, non-fuzzy gloves you’ve pre-rinsed thoroughly in local seawater outside the site.

Decontamination and biosecurity
- This chamber reads every touch. Do not bring it a story from another world. Before launch, scrub and rinse everything with fresh water, then with local seawater at the site. No soaps, no biocides. Air-dry, then final rinse again in local water.
- Sunscreen, lotions, insect repellent—wash them off long before the dive. Even microlayers matter.
- After the visit, quarantine and rinse your gear again to avoid exporting its microbiome elsewhere.

How to see inside without entering
- Use a tethered micro-ROV or borescope the size of a pencil, soft-bodied if possible, sheathed in smooth silicone or hydrogel so it parts films rather than abrades them.
- Thrusters should be whisper-low and vectored; if you feel the water on your face, the chamber will feel your probe like weather. Consider a passive design that you “place” at the seam and allow to settle by its own buoyancy.
- Lighting must be faint and patient. Use:
  - Far-red for orientation (many marine phototrophs don’t notice it; no one in there expects it).
  - Very dim blue/green only when you must, and pulsed with long dark intervals. Let your camera integrate over seconds. The best images here are taken by not trying very hard.
- Skip sonar. Sound condenses oddly here, and pinging will feel like bright fingerprints. If you must map, do it with slow photogrammetry and a fixed viewpoint.
- Consider a passive sampler placed just outside the seam to read eDNA and chemistry from the outflow. It will tell you who lives within without anyone noticing you asked.

Approach etiquette
- Stop finning 3–5 meters from the seam. Hover. Let your wake pass. You’ll feel when the water stops telling the chamber you are near.
- Announce yourself by doing nothing. The inside will answer with a change you won’t see for a while—the faintest bloom of confidence in a bubble, a thicker line in a mat. That is your invitation to send the probe to the threshold.
- Do not touch rock, shell, or film with hands or tank or hose. Even a scuff here rewrites a month.
- Keep brightness lower than the dimmest dusk you can stand. If something flashes—bacterial greens, sleepy copepod milk—turn the light down and wait. Let punctuation recover before you try to read again.
- If a resident animal arrives (limpet on rounds, amphipod at its door), back your probe a finger-width and pause. They were here first; their errands are the text.

Sensing and recording
- Bring a low-light camera with long integration, macro capability, and excellent stabilization. Mount it to the probe; don’t try to “fly” shots—place, then wait.
- Add a tiny hydrophone and record long stretches of “nothing.” Later you will hear the toy-silver ping, the shine of a scrape, and the way silence has grain.
- If you carry chemistry, think gentleness: external pH, O2, redox at the seam only. No injections, no tracers. If you must measure a gradient, do it once, then leave it alone for a tide or two.
- Map by thresholds instead of meters. Note: limpet track, amber niche, ferric-tea seam, elver pause point. It will help you find your way back in a room that dislikes straight lines.

Local rule drift and how to cope
- Time here “thickens.” Your instruments may log causes and effects so close they seem out of order. Don’t fight it; annotate, don’t correct.
- Expect loops: a bubble that rises then is exactly where it started; an algal blade that curls and is both curled and not. Your brain will want tidy; offer it patience instead.
- Stabilize yourself with periodic haptics (a timer that buzzes every few minutes) so you don’t slip into the room’s hours and forget your own. This is not narcosis; it’s a different coat of time.
- If piloting the probe, add deadband and damping. Movements should begin late and end early. Here, overcorrection is weather.

Personal safety near young basalt
- Fresh pillow basalts are glass-edged. Move slowly; keep your buoyancy true. Cuts here are clean and eager.
- Do not wedge yourself into crevices to get a better angle—surge can pin and peel you. Use a gentle tether from a stable stance, or clip yourself off at a respectful distance and let the probe do the threading.
- There may be sudden tiny pings as the rock finishes cooling conversations. Don’t spook; just note and carry on.
- Cold stillness drains heat and attention. Work in short watches, swap with a buddy, hydrate between drops.

If you must sample, do it like this (and only if you must)
- Take water from just outside the seam for eDNA and dissolved ions. That is the room’s breath. Anything else is a biopsy the room did not consent to.
- One high-resolution photo is worth a thousand micro-scrapes. Let vision be your sample.
- If a lost line or fragment snags inside, abandon the session and plan a very careful retrieval later. Do not “go in after it.” Better to forfeit gear than sign your presence into every film.

What to pack
- Drysuit + warm underlayers, low-shed gloves, hood.
- Closed-circuit rebreather with scrubbers fresh and silent; bailout carried but kept far from the seam.
- Neutral-buoyancy borescope or thumbnail ROV, silicone-sheathed, with minimal-thrust actuation and a smooth, rounded nose.
- Dimmable far-red light, secondary faint blue/green; external diffuser.
- Low-light macro camera with long exposure capability; passive hydrophone.
- Small slate and pencil; patience timer with haptic alert.
- Passive water sampler for eDNA and chemistry placed at the seam’s outflow (clip it, wait a tide, retrieve).
- Cutting tool for your own entanglement only; never for the chamber.
- Soft anchor or sand screw well away from the site so you don’t scull to hold position.
- Decon kit: freshwater, dedicated rinsing tubs or bags, lint-free cloths.

How to be with it
- Arrive early enough to slow your breath before you are near the seam. Let your heart rate come down; the room hears footfalls.
- Keep your curiosity broad. Don’t chase a single shot; let the chamber introduce itself in order: geometry, then mats, then tiny towns, then the residents.
- Learn the darkness by degrees. After a few minutes at lowest light, you will start to “hear” the room in shadow; let that become your primary sense.
- When you feel the urge to do more, do less. A retreat of one finger-width often yields the better view ten minutes later.

Leaving well
- Back your probe out as slowly as you entered, pausing where you paused before. Offer symmetry; it closes the conversation.
- Do not tidy anything “for it.” A drift, a shell ring, a sliver—it all belongs to the room.
- Keep the exact coordinates quiet. Share images and descriptions; protect the address.
- Rinse, quarantine, and log. Mark down what changed you. That’s part of the data.

Contingencies you’ll be glad you considered
- If visibility drops into tea-leaf fog mid-visit, do not hover hoping for a break. Pull back. The room is combing its weather; it does not want company right now.
- If bioluminescence blooms after your light touch, stop all light for a while. You just turned a page; don’t smudge the ink.
- If a starfish arm arrives, make yourself smaller. The best etiquette is to let the larger slow body have the room and your proxy the corner.

The right expectation
- You won’t get drama. You’ll get: a limpet’s comet trail sharpening by a hair, a bacterial cabbagette swelling by a film, a bubble deciding not to be brave. That is the point. You’ll come away carrying a weight you can’t name and new respect for how much story fits in a handspan.

In summary: be smaller than you are; be slower than you like; stay outside; let your tools be kinder than your hands; leave behind only a shorter list of questions than you arrived with. This chamber is a book reading itself. Visit as a quiet marginal note, then close it gently.
