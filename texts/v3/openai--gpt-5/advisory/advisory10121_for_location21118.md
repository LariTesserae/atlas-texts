What you’re walking into is a high, cool, always-underwater city whose buildings are alive and whose weather is sound. You will be held by water all the time; current, buoyancy, and noise are as constant as gravity. Preparation is less about “gear for a dive” and more about “changing your default expectations.” Think patient, quiet, slow, and plant-safe.

Baseline orientation
- Medium: Clear, cool freshwater or very lightly brackish; constant rain above means an ever-hammering roof of sound transmitted through your skull and skin as much as your ears.
- Depth and pressure: Mild. You will be within the first tens of meters—enough to feel water fully, not enough for deep-dive gas issues if you stay sensible. There is a very deep drop-off nearby; don’t let it tempt you until you understand local flow and lanes.
- Light: Honest green by day, blue glass in shade, bioluminescent pinfire at dusk. You can see far. Strong surface glare and white needles where rain meets water.
- Architecture: Everything is grown, woven, and persuaded; no metalwork, no burning, no sawn beams. Platforms, halls, and lifts breathe and flex. Property is pruning, not parcel lines.
- Sound: The roar is background law. The city rides it and talks through it. Silence is not neutral; it’s a signal.

What to bring (quiet, plant-safe, low-shed, low-gloss)
Life support and thermal
- Breathing: A silent or very low-noise closed-circuit rebreather is best. If you don’t have that, a simple, well-baffled snorkel system with a surface supply will be tolerated only in calm, supervised lanes. Avoid loud bubble storms. Bring spare scrubber, O2 analyzer, and redundant masks. Keep all metal internal and sheathed; no jangling clips.
- Buoyancy: Soft weights (sand or ceramic shot in sealed cloth) and a bladder you can trim delicately. No rigid plates or metal D-rings. Lanyards in braided plant fiber or waxed linen.
- Thermal: Smooth-skin natural rubber or high-quality, low-shed neoprene; minimal seams and Velcro. Hood for bone-conducted noise fatigue and heat. Thin, soft-soled booties with tread that won’t chew plant surfaces. Light gloves to protect both you and what you touch.

Movement and comms
- Fins: Short, soft, non-ridged, or simply go finless and frog-kick. Power and splash are rudeness here.
- Navigation: A ceramic or glass writing slate and graphite stylus; knotted line with colored, non-shedding flags. Do not set permanent markers; follow groomed paths and ropeways.
- Sign language: Learn 30–50 basic local signs before you arrive (greeting, permission to approach, apology, “guest,” “lost,” “need guide,” “I will not prune,” “I carry heat/medicine”). Body angle and finger tension carry grammar.
- Sound tools: One small, soft mallet and a drum code card given by your host. Do not bring active sonar, pingers, or beeping electronics.

Light and sensing
- Light: Dim, shielded, warm-spectrum lamp with shutters. No strobes. Use sparingly; their halls and eyes are tuned to modest light.
- Camera: If you must, mute the focus beep and use only natural light or the tiniest fill. Ask before recording.

Health and biosecurity
- Decontam: Absolute gear quarantine. No sunscreen films, perfumes, DEET, or silicon oils. Rinse with clean water; UV or peroxide vapor sterilize hard kit; freeze–thaw soft kit if possible. Inspect for spores, seeds, fibers.
- Skin and ears: Antifungal/antibacterial ear drops, barrier cream that won’t slick the water, fresh-water safe wound closures. Fin-friendly plasters. A narrow-tooth comb for algal hitchhikers.
- Water and food: The water is life, not a beverage. Bring a ceramic ultrafilter or UV straw for yourself at first. Transition to local oils, pulses, and grazer fats slowly.
- First aid: Vinegar ampoules for stings, antihistamines, an EpiPen sealed. Share with medics before use; do not dump solutions into common water.

Trade and gifts (non-disruptive)
- Best gifts: Intangible techniques (new knots, tensioned braids, canopy pleats, quiet lift balancing), stories, songs adapted to drum and body-sign, careful plant massage and grafting methods learned elsewhere, time spent helping.
- Acceptable objects: Inert ceramics (needles, awls, beads), very fine glass lenses or prisms if you can prove they won’t cut or glare, high-grade stone microblades in sheaths, plant-based cordage of unfamiliar twist.
- What not to bring: Metals in view, bright plastics, combustion tools, seeds, sonar, loud scooters, nets that chafe, “miracle” glues that don’t rot, anything that sheds.

Etiquette you can memorize on land
- Buoyancy is architecture. Don’t hang on roofs, buttresses, or fat fern fans; they are working members. If you must steady, touch stone rings or designated grip-knots.
- Property is pruning. Do not cut, nip, or redirect any living tissue without an explicit invite. If a branch crosses your space, you may shape it a finger’s width only, never to the core.
- Noise is law. Keep a presence-sound when the surface roars. Gentle drumbeats on shared tympana are “I’m fine.” Going quiet in a storm sends a welfare call.
- Time is flow. “Soon” may mean after two new rungs of a fan-fern—weeks. Set your urgency aside. They will notice if you can match their cadence.
- Lanes are for those larger than you. Leave fast passages and big aisles clear; sidle into eddies to let fluked elders and grazer herds through. Your quickness is ornamental here, not functional.
- Light is modest. Don’t chase or paint fish with beams. Save your lamp for safety checks and close work.
- Hands talk. Face heartward when apologizing; fingers relaxed for a question, taut for a warning. If you don’t know a sign, exaggerate slowness, not size.
- Ask to touch. Even market goods nest in living cradles. Point, sign ask, then lift.

Getting in without offense
- Arrive at the edge where pillars thin and the blue opens. Hold station in a small back-eddy, hood on, lamp off. Let your bubbles (if any) settle into a tidy thread. Listen three minutes. You will start to pick a foreground of tuned flutes from the weather-roar.
- Announce politely: three soft mallet taps on a designated baffle or hand-drummed “guest” cadence if shown it ahead of time. Do not whistle or clap.
- Wait to be collected. A rope cradle or a person will come. Follow neutrally; do not graze fronds with your fins or forearms.

Moving and living there
- Kick and posture: Frog-kick only; keep your knees bent, fin tips inside your silhouette. Tuck one hand, use the other to sign or trail open for balance. Avoid sculling that raises silt in gullies; silt is insult.
- Anchoring: Sleep tied. Use your own line; don’t borrow load-bearing ropes. They run rooms that breathe; you do not want to drift on a tide-change.
- Warmth and meals: Expect oils, pulses, pressed seedcakes, and dense, cool stews eaten from seedpods. Heat is eaten, not made. Accept the first cup you’re offered, however humble; it costs effort.
- Work rhythms: When tuned flutes rise a note, help tighten westward moorings. On dark, thin-sound days, locals mend and brew; offer your hands, not your schedule.
- Markets: Goods live in pockets braided from trunks. Hands behind your back until invited. Trade cleanly; barter is slower than you think but precise. They will keep count with knots, not tallies.

Hazards and how to read them
- The slow tells: A rope too old speaks—watch for fuzz and a certain hush in its twang. A starving hall goes pale and papery; don’t rely on it to bear your weight. Your host will point out “named slacks” and “named tighteners.” Memorize them.
- Fauna: Big grazers are friendly if you are not a wall. Turn sideways, make yourself narrow, let them ghost by. Librarian browsers (tentacled) will inspect your kit once; let them. Eels in buttresses are housemates; don’t offer fingers.
- Predators: Large and quiet. Locals maintain chaff-screens where small things would get drafted; don’t tear or tighten them. If a wide-fluked shadow passes and the hall leans to its voice, stop moving, make small sound, and keep to your eddy. Do not try to watch it pass by chasing the shadow; the lanes belong to it.
- Weather overhead: Lightning sheets glow; beautiful, not dangerous here. A hard, whitening of the ceiling means lashings matter—help, then rest.

Health notes your body won’t warn you about until late
- Cold creep: Even “mild” water steals heat. Shiver management is pride-neutral; add a layer or leave the work. Don’t take pride in slow hypothermia.
- Skin and ears: Rinse folds, dry the insides of your hood, use drops after long days. Waterlogged skin splits quietly on day three, not day one.
- Immersion diuresis: You’ll pee more; plan breaks away from fiber-rinsing lanes and weirs. Ask where to go; there are flows for it.
- Infections: Small cuts need serious attention. Carry, but don’t dump, iodine or chlorhexidine; apply carefully and blot the excess. Notify your host so they can adjust local flow or screens to protect juveniles.

Learning the map the locals care about
- Directions are flows and features: upflow/downflow, sunward/shadeward, ridgeward/blueward, west moorings/east moorings. There are calendars of currents the way you think of train timetables.
- Boundaries are living: hedges of trained vine and pruning rights; a legal map is a gardener’s plan, not a fence line. Ask to see the pruning map of any hall you sleep in.

What to offer back
- Be ballast: hold a rope, steady a lift, beat the small drum when the flutes tell you to. Carry loads along the fast-when-fast lanes on their schedule.
- Share patient crafts: better graft bindings, a braid that holds neutral buoyancy, a knot that slips in one direction only, a way to teach two columns to arch more kindly. They prize persuasion over force; bring techniques that persuade.
- Tell slow stories: fire exists here in narrative only; speak of it softly and as myth. They love tales that measure years in leaves, not in weeks.

A compact do/don’t to keep in your pocket
- Do: quarantine your gear; move slow; keep a gentle sound presence; ask before touching; keep to eddies; match their tempo; carry your own heat and trash out.
- Don’t: prune anything; block big lanes; use strobes or sonar; shed fibers; bring metals into view; break silence during welfare checks—or go silent during storms; kick silt; treat plants as props.

If you get lost
- Stop, reduce your profile, and listen. Find the nearest tuned-flute band; go toward the steadier pitch, not the louder one. Aim for maintained corridors (clean edges, repeat lashings) and away from “wild” pillars. Make the “guest needs guide” drum code softly on a grown tympanum; wait.

Leaving well
- Rinse your kit in a designated outflow. Comb out clinglings; return any passengers to a live cradle. Settle your trades; repay silent welfare checks with work or gifts. Leave no cuttings, no bright crumbs, no new noise.

If you stay long enough, your body will write new reflexes: frog-kick as default, hands speaking before mouth, a knack for hearing slackness in the roar. The city is made of patience. Carry some in with you, and when you go, take its grammar of growth—not its seedlings—with you.
