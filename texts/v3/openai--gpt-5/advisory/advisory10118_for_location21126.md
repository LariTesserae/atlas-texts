What you’re walking into
- A living engine room. Think tree-cathedral grown into a pump station. The floor is rootwork, the walls are trunks and aerial roots, the roof is a deep leaf canopy. Inside that, belts, flywheels, pipes, hoses, cable trays, and gauges do steady work. Humid, warm, tannic, and close.
- Sound is constant and low. The engine’s bass thrum is physical—your ribs will feel it. The rest is patient: chains ticking, condensate hissing, water pacing along grooves and gutters. You may find your own breath falls into its meter.
- Everything is rounded and softened by time and touch. Nothing sharp lasts. Nothing purely inert stays inert for long—wood takes metal into itself, metal leans on wood, and people adjust both.
- Water is everywhere and almost never “free.” Every trickle is part of a path. The air is wet enough to bead cold metal white in seconds. Heat is collected and returned. Light is green-filtered and easy on eyes.

What this means for you
- You’re entering a live workplace inside a living structure. Treat it as both engine room and sacred grove: quiet, deliberate, permission-based. Expect to slow down.
- Surfaces may move subtly under you. Clearances are tight. Overhead belts, bare conductors on porcelain, and breathing roots all share the same few meters of air. Your biggest risks are slips, snags, crush points, and stray hands where they shouldn’t be.

Before you go: acclimate and plan
- Heat/humidity: If you’re coming from dry or cool worlds, give yourself a day or two (elsewhere) to acclimate to heat and humidity. Practice hydrating without overdoing plain water; get used to salty drinks.
- Low-frequency noise: If deep hums give you headaches or nausea, pack soft earplugs. Take breaks under open sky between bays.
- Claustrophobia: Space is human-scaled but intimate. If you tend to panic in tight, busy volumes, arrange a guide who’ll keep you oriented.

What to wear
- Footwear: Closed-toe, non-conductive, non-marking, high-traction soles. Drain-friendly uppers help. Avoid steel shanks or exposed metal eyelets if possible.
- Clothing: Light, breathable, quick-dry long sleeves and trousers. Nothing loose that can drift into belts or trays. Avoid heavy cotton that turns to a sponge.
- Hair and accessories: Tie hair up. No scarves, dangling straps, long necklaces, hoop earrings, or loose lanyards. Keep watches and jewelry to non-conductive minimal.
- PPE:
  - Bump cap or lightweight dielectric helmet (clearances are low, beams are real).
  - Anti-fog safety glasses.
  - Soft earplugs (you’ll want to hear, just tame the thrum).
  - Light work gloves with grip (rubberized palm or nitrile-coated fabric).
  - Optional light knee pads if you’ll crouch near lines or root saddles.

What to carry (and what not to)
- Water and salts: A sealed bottle with electrolyte tabs. Do not refill from any drip, gutter, or flume unless a crew member explicitly offers a safe tap.
- Small towel or bandanna for sweat; a tiny bottle of no-rinse hand cleanser; antifungal foot powder for after.
- Paper and pencil, not gel pens. Damp eats ink; graphite writes anyway. A small waxed or waterproof notebook.
- Electronics: Minimal and sealed. Humidity and tannin haze love camera sensors and lenses. Use a chest harness; keep straps tight. Bring silica gel packs and a drybag. No drones.
- First aid: Waterproof plasters, antiseptic wipes, a small roll of cohesive wrap. Tetanus up to date.
- Gifts/barter (if appropriate; leave sealed until asked): blocks of pure beeswax; shellac flakes; high-quality brass feeler gauges/shims; ceramic or glass sample jars with gasketed lids; indelible carpenter’s pencils; waxed twill tape; a compact non-shedding natural-fiber wiping cloth. Avoid seeds, soil, and anything that sheds microplastics or smells of solvents.
- Do not bring: open flames or lighters past posted lines; aerosol sprays; powder lubricants; loose snacks; folding knives you might be tempted to “trim” a root with.

Arrival and entry etiquette
- Check in. This is a working bay, not a museum. Ask for the duty graftwright, rigger, or tender. Expect to sign into a ledger.
- Read the room. Rules are posted because they matter:
  - Never cut a bearer root.
  - Release collars before the wet season (translation for you: do not touch the collars).
  - No open flame beyond the yellow line (that line is not negotiable).
- Ask before touching anything alive or anything moving. That includes handwheels, gauges, hoses, and those beautiful ring collars swallowed by bark.
- Speak close and plain. People there converse like mechanics and gardeners: short, pointed sentences; one hand on the thing they mean. Don’t shout over the engine—step closer and keep it calm.
- Don’t whistle. A lot of reliefs and signals ride the air as whistles; it’s confusing to mimic them.
- Photography: Always ask. Some line routings and fixtures are proprietary by culture if not by law. Never climb for a shot.

How to move inside Bay Seventeen
- Watch your top and toes. Belts overhead, low arches, wet gratings. Keep three points of contact on stairs. Handrails may be slick with moss or oil-softened wood.
- Follow the footpaths. Slatted catwalks and iron grates are for you; cambium rims, aerial curtains, and polished grooves are not.
- Keep your mass over ribs and beams; avoid stepping on soft or swelling wood. If you see chalk lines, brass pins, or numbered marks, they’re measurements in progress—don’t disturb them.
- Cable trays are not shelves. Never set gear on porcelain-toothed trays or copper runs. Humidity turns a smear into a short.
- No leaning on ceramic-saddled pipes. They’re isolated from the tree for a reason.
- Keep clear of the pump pit edge and flywheel guards. Slow doesn’t mean safe if you’re inside the sweep.
- Mind the quick things. The small discharge fan that spins up on valve opens is fast and sharp; give it air.

Water, food, and waste
- Bring sealed snacks if you must. Eat outside the bay if you can. Crumbs invite ants, and Bay Seventeen values its rare absence of pests.
- Pack out everything—down to spent bandages and pencil shavings. Nothing leaves your pockets without intention.
- Do not drink or bathe from flumes, gutters, or runnels. The taproots and misters serve the machine; your thirst is your problem.

Health notes
- Expect to sweat without feeling cooler. Pace yourself. Sip salted water. If your head swims, step out to the canal edge, get air, and cool your neck and wrists.
- Skin: wipe off condensate and oil-mist mix; wash and dry feet thoroughly after. Treat even small scrapes; the water is warm and busy.
- Breathing: If you’re sensitive to spores, use a comfortable, moisture-tolerant mask. The “dust” in the light shafts is life worn fine.
- After-care for gear: Dry electronics and leather the same day. Swap in fresh desiccant. Open your notebook to air.

Reading the place (without getting in the way)
- Hand on bark, lightly, where someone says it’s okay: you’ll feel pressure changes travel faster through wood than air. Locals read those like weather.
- The “bird-call” you’ll hear is likely a relief valve clearing somewhere else. One short, piercing note: a breath. Longer or patterned: a message. If you’re not trained, you don’t act—your guide will.
- When the tide turns, the bass note deepens. The whole bay settles like a big animal getting comfortable. It’s normal.

If you want to be welcome
- Offer patience and attention. Ask someone what they’re measuring or scraping; listen to the answer; don’t suggest shortcuts.
- If you brought gifts, ask if they’re useful now. Hand them to people, not to bark or to rails. Never wedge anything into a living joint “to help.”
- Accept water if offered and mind where the cup drains after. Follow their waste paths.

Timing your visit
- The bay floods on a quarterly schedule to wash lower grates. Check the board; avoid those hours unless you’re properly kitted and invited.
- Morning is gentler heat; late afternoon the pump leans into tide and work thickens. Midday light is prettiest through the leaves but humidity peaks.

Red lines you don’t cross
- No flame past the yellow line. No smoking or smoldering anything inside, ever.
- Do not adjust collars, handwheels, or taps. “Release collars before the wet season” is for them, not for you.
- Don’t snip, trim, carve, or “tidy” any root or shoot. Not even a little. The entire place is a tuned structure; you don’t know which fiber is carrying what load.
- Keep your body and gear away from bare conductors and governor linkages. Humidity lowers the margin for error.

Getting out and coming back
- Step into open air between bays when you need to reset your senses. The sky over the canal helps.
- On your way out, you may be asked to step through a brush or drip station to keep spores and salts where they belong. Do it.
- Later, if you find leaf-fungus creeping onto your camera lens or boot stitching, it’s a souvenir you don’t want—clean and dry thoroughly.

Why go
- Because it shows you a city that learned to make a truce with water and time, and to build with life instead of against it. A factory that breathes. An engine room that knits itself back together, millimeter by millimeter, while people keep the tolerances gentle enough to allow it.

First five minutes, if you like a script
- Stand just inside the threshold until the hum finds you.
- Ask for the tender on watch. Offer your name and intention. “Visitor; keeping to the walkways; may I shadow?”
- Put in earplugs loosely; slip on glasses; tie back anything that dangles.
- Take one slow lap of the nearest catwalk with your guide. Touch nothing unless invited. When you want to point, point with a closed fist—it reads as “I’m not reaching.”
- When you catch yourself trying to hurry, match your steps to the pushrod’s rhythm. It will calm you and keep you safe.

If something goes wrong
- If you hear a continuous high whistle close by, freeze and look to your guide; they’ll either wave you to a wall or to a hatch.
- If someone shouts “clear,” step back from pits, belts, and trays until you’re heel-to-beam.
- If you slip a tool or drop a lens cap, do not dive after it. Say where it fell. They have ways to retrieve things without hurting the bay.

Treat Bay Seventeen like a patient heart in a living ribcage, and it will hold you. Try to make it hurry, and it will remind you, gently or not, that this place runs on rhythms older than you.
