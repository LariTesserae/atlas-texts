What you’re walking into
This city is not just wet; it is built out of wetness. Surfaces bead and weep, edges sweat, schedules are written in condensate, and the machinery of air and water never stops. Think warm, saturated air that sits on your skin; hardscape that has been rounded smooth by a million miniature storms; living masonry and microbial patinas that are part of the infrastructure; engineered weather that arrives on a timetable. The noise floor thrums in your ribs. Gravity and airflow are sometimes…persuaded. Density is total—every space is doing a job.

You’ll do best if you accept three truths at the outset:
- You will be damp most of the time.
- The ground is slipperier and more alive than it looks.
- Movement and schedule are the etiquette here.

Before you arrive
- Health prep:
  - Vaccinations: tetanus/Tdap up to date; Hep A and B; typhoid; consult a travel doc about leptospirosis prophylaxis (rodent-adjacent condensate, open sumps) and general antifungal skin care for prolonged humidity. If you’re sensitive to molds, bring a non-drowsy antihistamine.
  - Bring a small course of antifungal cream/powder (clotrimazole or similar) and a topical antibiotic for nicks.
  - Motion/vestibular tolerance: low-frequency vibration and occasional gravity skews can cause nausea or headaches; pack meclizine or scopolamine if you’re prone to motion sickness, plus a mild analgesic.
  - Respiratory: the air is clean enough to breathe, but can carry ammonia, trace hydrocarbons, and dense bioaerosols near vents/markets. Pack a compact elastomeric mask with combination cartridges (organic vapor + P-series particulate) for hotspots; you won’t need it all the time.
- Paperwork and permissions:
  - Much is regulated by rule and schedule. If you plan to tour below street level or ride maintenance windways, arrange a licensed guide and permits in advance.
  - “Empty” spaces are instruments (lungs, wind engines); don’t occupy them without local clearance.
- Money:
  - Expect mostly wet-proof digital transactions. Paper notes become pulp and metal coins corrode; use a sealed payment token or wristband. Keep a few polymer notes in a waterproof sleeve for markets that like to see “rain-safe” backup.
- Mindset:
  - Learn to read the art of damp: locals read drip speed, bead size, and banner movement like a forecast. Hire a fixer for day one to narrate what the water is saying.

What to pack (and why)
- Footwear:
  - Amphibious shoes with aggressive siped rubber (boat shoe tech), wide base, and fast drain. No leather soles. Bring two pairs to rotate and dry.
  - Thin neoprene or quick-dry socks; antifungal foot powder; blister kit. Sandals are fine only with toe protection and siped soles.
- Clothing:
  - Lightweight, breathable synthetics that keep working when wet; thin layers. Merino can go swampy here—if you bring it, keep it ultralight.
  - A hooded, short-cape shell that sheds bead-rain without trapping heat (think ventilated, hydrophobic, not a sauna-raincoat).
  - Brimmed cap or short bill with a drip edge; bandana or small shammy to keep one hand dry for grips.
  - Anti-chafe balm (thighs, underarms), zinc oxide for skin folds.
- Eyes and ears:
  - Polarized, anti-fog lenses. Contacts are a liability; if you must wear them, carry sterile saline and daily disposables. Otherwise, hydrophobic-coated glasses with a retainer strap.
  - Ear protection tuned for low-frequency rumble (musician’s plugs are better than foam; you still need to hear wayfinding hum and vendors).
- Hands and carry:
  - Thin, grippy gloves (nitrile-dipped or sailing gloves) for wet rails and ladders.
  - Pack small, secure, and close. Chest rig or high-riding backpack with drain ports, lash points, and waterproof zips. Tethers for everything: phone, keys, shades.
- Electronics:
  - IP68 devices or housings; conformal coating if you can swing it. Bring hydrophobic/anti-corrosion wipes, a handful of silica gel packs, and a roll-top dry pouch sized for your essentials.
  - Don’t count on a magnetic compass (fields and ferrous dust); download offline maps, but trust local wayfinding and sound cues.
- Micro-kit:
  - Compact squeegee or shammy, anti-fog spray, microfiber cloths.
  - Compact umbrella? Tempting, but downdrafts flip them; a hood is better.
  - Small headlamp with warm light (humidity loves to glare), plus a red mode for fog.
  - Carabiners, short lash cords, a few corrosion-proof clips.
  - Rehydration salts and electrolytes—salt aerosol and sweat make you thirsty in a way that plain water won’t fix.
  - Sealable wet bag for soaked layers; quick-dry travel towel (you’ll use it constantly).
- Materials:
  - Choose titanium, ceramic, and polymers over cheap steels; zippers and tools rot fast here.

On arrival (first hours)
- Get your ear in: stand off to the side on a main boulevard and map the sound layers—deep stack organ, pellet-rails, shutter choruses. You’ll navigate by this more than sight.
- Buy a local “tone pin” or haptic wayfinder band. The city whispers route and hazard cues at the edge of hearing; the pin translates and keeps you from stepping into a scheduled downdraft.
- Learn two rituals: lean into the midafternoon gust with everyone else, and step back under eaves at dawn for the under-eave rain (but don’t stand under harvesters—their driplines are for fauna and fines are real).

Moving through the city
- Surfaces: everything glossy is not safe—assume slipperiness. Test before you commit weight; keep three points of contact on ramps and stairs with hollowed noses.
- Edges: anything that “faces the sky beads; anything that faces the wind weeps.” That means the lee side is the drip zone. Don’t loiter under weeping edges.
- Layers:
  - Street: markets and maintenance. Watch crowd currents; you move with them, not through them.
  - Mezzanine: light freight, drones, and skyway rotorwash. Hats on, leashes tight (on gear, I mean), and keep clear lines above.
  - Under: utilities and storage, permanent damp. Go only with a guide and proper PPE; gravity skews and subsonics are stronger here.
- Schedules to respect:
  - Hourly downdraft tests at wide windways: banners go taut, vendors snap shutters. If you see the wrist-sellers collecting their shrimp tubes, follow them clear.
  - Midafternoon scour gust that cleans the upper air of spores—secure hats and loose kit.
  - Night quietule when deep-stack engines slow: the duct fauna ride the dark. It gets weird and beautiful; if you’re noise-sensitive, that’s also your best sleep window.
- Gravity and field oddities:
  - Expect 10–30 meters of “bent” gravity in service corridors. Don’t sprint into them; let a local step first and watch the condensate line—that’s the angle your inner ear will complain about. If it makes you dizzy, close eyes briefly, hand to wall, count five breaths.

Eating and drinking
- Water: condensate is abundant but not uniformly safe. Fill at marked drink stations or from vendors who run their own polishers. Your filter bottle should handle biofilms, salts, and volatiles; if not, buy water.
- Hydration: the city salts you. Use electrolytes daily.
- Food: this is a place of proteins and ferments more than greens. Eels in acetate vinegar, roof-crab fritters, hopper-grubs, vat-cultures, steamed rolls filled with fungal consortia that smell like rain on stone. Eat what’s cooked hot or acid-cured. Avoid raw mat-scrapings and anything billed as “market fresh condensate” unless it’s boiled.
- Wildlife: do not touch or feed condensate-mice, roof-crabs, swallows, skimmers, or sump-whales. Many animals carry tax chips or belong to a ledger; interacting can trigger fees or worse.

Health and comfort in constant damp
- Skin: change socks mid-day; wash and dry feet at night and powder before sleeping. Barrier cream on high-friction zones. Rinse salt off your skin when you can—salt rash sneaks up on you.
- Cuts: clean and seal immediately; the surfaces are alive with helpful consortia, but your immune system doesn’t know who’s who.
- Lungs/ears/sinuses: subsonic thrum can fatigue you; use musician’s earplugs, not total blockers. A saline nasal rinse at night helps with aerosols.
- Heat: it’s warm and sweating won’t cool you much. Move early and late, take the midafternoon gust as your reset, accept being damp.
- Sleep: book a room with a “dry hour” service—dehumidified microclimate cycles that bake linens and shoes. Ask for a stack-facing wall if you like white rumble; away from stacks if you don’t. Expect drip choreography outside; it’s oddly soothing once you surrender.

Etiquette and law of flow
- Don’t block drains, runnels, or the little scalloped lips in pavement—they’re veins, not decoration.
- Empty spaces are working organs. If you don’t know what a plaza is for, assume it’s a lung or a wind engine and skirt its center.
- Vendors sing contracts. If you hum along, that can be read as assent. Smile, nod, but keep your mouth shut unless you mean to buy; or wear a visitor tag that signals you’re not contract-literate.
- Cleaning swarms have right-of-way. Step aside; don’t film close or poke at them.
- If you drop something small and it starts moving away in a bead-runnel, let it go unless you can snag it safely; fingers in flow paths are frowned on and dangerous.

Navigation and comms
- You measure blocks by sound. Learn the signatures of pellet-rail “mercury sigh,” belt murmur, shutter choruses (weather about to strike), and the low diagnostics of maintenance swarms (don’t cross).
- The city’s wayfinding hum is just at the edge of hearing and often felt under the tongue. Your tone pin can shift it up into a comfortable band or translate into haptic pulses.
- GPS-equivalent works, but field machines and orbital-anchored structures can produce brief dead zones. When in doubt, follow drainage direction to a boulevard; boulevards are both wind and light canals.

Safety hazards to respect
- Slips and edges: keep your center low on ramps and rounded stair noses; handrails are wet—use gloves.
- Downdrafts and rotorwash: watch banners; secure hats; never step under the skyway mezzanine when shutters are snapping shut.
- Mass-lifts and field zones: invisible edges; mind signage and hum patterns. If your hair lifts or your lips feel dry and metallic, you’re near a live field—take two steps back.
- Biofilms and fungi: don’t scrape or “clean” walls; you’re not helping. If a surface glows at night, it’s beautiful; let it be.
- Theft and loss: crowds are dense; wet hands are slippery. Tether valuables; use zips that lock; avoid magnetic closures (ferrous dust gums them up).

Transport choices
- Feet are king, if you honor the flow. Practice walking like water: steady, yielding, slotting into currents.
- Pellet-trains in sealed rails for crosstown hops; you’ll feel them more than see them.
- Belts for freight—tourists stay off unless escorted.
- Skyway smallcraft are thrilling but bring ear protection; they fly in the high mezzanine, not free sky.
- Avoid rolling luggage; it hydroplanes and catches on runnels. Backpack or chest rig only.

What not to do
- Don’t taste unknown mats or drips; locals do sometimes for show, but they know which colonies are theirs.
- Don’t block a sump amphitheater’s tiers. They can flood on a schedule, and “schedule” isn’t a warning; it’s a promise.
- Don’t sit under harvesters. Their driplines are productive ecologies and monitored.
- Don’t assume a quiet corner is unowned; quiet is an instrument here. If you find a hush, move gently, listen, and keep it brief.

A simple day-plan for getting oriented
- Dawn: stand under a causeway edge (not under a harvester) and feel the under-eave rain. Watch how people tilt hats and bags; copy them. Get a hot, acid breakfast (eel or grubs with acetate pickle).
- Late morning: walk a wind-canal boulevard; practice reading drip, bead, and banner. Visit a market; listen to contracts being sung. Buy a tone pin.
- Midafternoon: find a mezzanine overlook for the scheduled gust; anchor your hat and enjoy the cleanse.
- Evening: ride a pellet-rail segment; feel the city’s deep organ at a stack plaza. Eat something cooked hard and bright with acids. Turn in before quietule or go find a guide who loves the duct fauna and will keep you safe.

If things go wrong
- Slip and cut: wash with clean water, dab antiseptic, seal. If redness spreads fast in this climate, seek a clinic; they’re everywhere.
- Overwhelmed by noise or humidity: step into a “dry node” (marked with a droplet-and-fan icon). Pay the small fee; breathe; reset.
- Lost: follow runnels “down” to a main; ask a vendor—many act as informal waypoints and will sing or hum you a route in exchange for a small purchase.
- Unscheduled weather: shutters snap, banners roar—tuck in against a pillar, back to the flow, and wait. Everyone does.

Souvenirs and gifts that make sense
- Hydrophobic nail polish, anti-fog drops, and desiccant packets are oddly appreciated as tips or small gifts.
- Bring stories or songs that play well over a low rumble; spoken rhythms that ride the city’s long vowels are remembered.

You’ll leave salted and jewelled with it, clothes beaded at the hems, ears full of organized weather. The trick is to stop fighting the damp and learn to read what it’s saying; the city isn’t trying to drown you—it’s trying to keep itself breathing, and if you move with that, it carries you.
