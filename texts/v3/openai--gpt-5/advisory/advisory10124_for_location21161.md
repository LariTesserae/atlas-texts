What it feels like
You’ll be moving through a young plain that’s been domesticated, not paved; a place where the city’s intelligence lives in thin films, microtextures, and quiet adjustments instead of loud machines. Light stays low. Sound is modest. Water behaves like a nightly performance. Space is ceremonially open, with life condensed around “eyes” of dew. The rules are not posted on signs so much as woven into air, surfaces, and timing. If you arrive expecting streets and schedules, you’ll keep missing the city by a few millimeters and a few seconds. Prepare to tune your body and your gear to boundary layers, small seams of time, and respectful quiet.

Before you go: baseline prep
- Fitness and balance: Be comfortable walking several kilometers on soft, crisp ground with very low visual contrast. Mild changes in apparent weight and stride length are normal in some zones. If you get vertigo in subtly shifting gravity or time, practice slow, deliberate gait and breath pacing before you travel.
- Eyes and lungs: The first few millimeters of air are combed and textured. Protect eyes against micro-nap irritation and high humidity. If you have dry-eye issues or reactive airways, bring protective eyewear and a gentle, unscented saline atomizer for nostrils and eyes. Asthma is usually fine here; turbulence is suppressed, but carry your inhaler anyway.
- Implants and medical devices: There are strong near-field energy handshakes under certain arches and along transit lines. If you rely on implanted electronics (pacemakers, neurostimulators, insulin pumps), consult your manufacturer about tolerance to quasi-static and low-frequency fields; bring a printed device card. Most travelers are fine, but you’ll want to avoid lingering under the more active arches without a local escort.
- Immunity and skin: Fungal composites are common. If you’re immunocompromised, talk to a clinician about antifungal prophylaxis and carry an alcohol-free, skin-safe antifungal wipe. Expect plant sugars on surfaces; they’re wonderful for dew retention and terrible for sticky hands.
- Expectations: Time and distance sometimes don’t agree with your plan. Don’t stack tight connections. Replace “at 15:00” with “during the gold band” or “after the second dimming.” Locals schedule by phase, not by minute.

What to pack (and why)
- Footwear: Soft-soled, non-marking shoes with wide, low-shear tread. No heels, no aggressive lugs, no Velcro that can catch surface nap. Aim to distribute weight without tearing crisp silt or scuffing microstructures.
- Clothing: Matte, non-shedding, non-rustling layers. Skip synthetics that shed microfibers or snap with static. Think smooth knits and woven natural/smart blends that wick without flapping. Avoid perfumes, aerosol deodorants, powdered sunscreens, and hair sprays—loose volatiles disrupt the boundary layer.
- Eyewear: Clear, wraparound, anti-fog goggles for outside; lightly amber or rose lenses help with low, warm light and soft contrast. Bring a dark visor only if you plan to visit the ocean edge during a sky opening; otherwise, ambient light is gentle.
- Hands: Thin, non-tacky gloves for touching public surfaces; plant sugars can be gluey. A small pack of neutral-pH, low-water wipes removes sugar without flooding a surface.
- Light: A dimmable, warm, non-flicker headlamp with a broad, soft beam. Flash is rude and useless; the city is tuned to low luminance. Bring a tiny pinpoint “edge finder” LED for reading crack geometry at your feet.
- Sound and comms: Use bone-conduction or near-field haptics; speakers don’t carry and are frowned upon. A simple wrist haptic communicator that can piggyback on local near-field plates is best. Disable artificial camera shutter sounds.
- Navigation: Accept a loaner wayfinding fob from hosts. It feels like a smooth stone and “tastes” the day’s accepted flows, nudging your wrist toward the correct pulse lines. Don’t rely on satellite maps; the city edits paths nightly.
- Water and salts: Dew water is extremely low in minerals. Carry electrolytes or mineral packets; sip steadily. A soft bottle with a one-way valve lets you drink without breathing into the bottle and confusing local humidity control.
- Food: Pack sealed rations. Don’t graze; many leaves are sugar-glazed for ecological reasons, and some plants are politely carnivorous. If offered local produce indoors, it’s safe—outdoor sweetness is for the system, not for you.
- Health kit: Moleskin or kinesiology tape (for unexpected long strides), antifungal skin wipes, saline vials, blister care, a compact emergency blanket (nights can feel cooler when the reflectors roll), and a small, soft brush for salt dust at the edges.
- Timekeeping: Wear a simple, low-drift analog watch for your own sanity, but trust local phase cues. Do not obsess over second-by-second precision; parts of the city deliberately borrow and lend tiny slices of time.
- Bag: A soft-sided sling with a smooth exterior. Nothing that snags threads or naps. Keep it close; wide open spaces tempt windy habits you won’t find here.

Getting there and around
- Arrival: Ships dock invisibly quiet at reed-towers. You’ll feel a soft static on your forearms as you pass beneath the first arches—that’s the welcome handshake, not a storm. Step slowly off ramps; your weight may feel a shade different.
- Streets and paths: Streets appear as episodes—soft-lit stones and breathing walkways that swell under your feet and return their energy to the net. Stick to them unless invited off. Your footprints are harvested; heavy stomping is wasteful and rude.
- Field seams: You’ll hear triple clicks when a vehicle or corridor stitches across timing margins. If you hear them at your feet, pause; a seam is passing close. Cross seams slowly, eyes up, knees loose—the world may grant you a longer stride.
- Condensers and threads: The dew filaments are almost invisible and sacred. Do not touch, drape clothing, or fly drones near them. In morning gold, watch from a respectful distance as they silver up.
- Elevation and edges: Silt banks stand unnaturally vertical; don’t trust them as seats or steps. Treat new rills and straight-sided cuts like fresh glass: beautiful, brittle, not for trampling.
- Vehicles: The motes of light are membranes, not cabins. They will envelope you if invited by your fob or a host. Inside, it feels like a hush and a pressure, not motion. If you’re prone to motion sickness, keep your gaze soft and your jaw unclenched; motion here is field, not acceleration.

Etiquette that matters
- The first five millimeters of air belong to the city: Don’t scrape, scuff, buff, or blow on surfaces. No stickers, no chalk, no gum. Set objects down gently; lift rather than drag. If your clothing swishes audibly, it’s too aggressive.
- Quiet is collaborative: Speak softly; the city carries intention better than volume. In some squares, pitch carries more than words—locals will raise tone slightly to send a message; follow their lead.
- Space is ceremonial: Structures keep distance for reasons. Don’t shortcut across bare spans unless wayfinding stones glow for you. If a pad feels indifferent or “not in the mood,” accept it and choose another route; that feeling is designed, not your imagination.
- Water is a citizen: Don’t disturb dew pockets, basins, or leaf-cups. If you drip, drip into catchments. If sugar gets on your hand, clean it with a wipe, not in a basin meant for plants.
- Photos and drones: Use high-sensitivity cameras, no flash. Do not fly drones; they shear the boundary layer. Stationary perches are provided on terraces.
- Plants and animals: Look, don’t touch. The weekly predator-plant display is mesmerizing; your hosts will tell you when it’s “not for hands.” Grazers will ignore you; ignore them back.

Health and safety in practice
- Eyes: Wear your goggles in moving air and around active condensers. Micro-nap textures can scratch tear films the way very fine sand does at a beach.
- Skin: Sugar films will find you. Remove with minimal water; don’t smear them on shared surfaces.
- Heat and hydration: Ambient warmth plus calm air means your sweat doesn’t travel far. Dress light, drink often, add minerals. You’ll feel less thirsty than you are; set a sip cadence.
- Rain events: Rare and sudden. If it starts, celebrate with everyone else, then step onto a walkway; the city will “clean up” and re-stabilize surfaces very quickly. Fresh cuts can be sharper right after.
- Time seams: If a droplet “clicks twice” while you’re watching, you’re near a lended fold. Move steadily; don’t chase curiosities across seams without a guide. Crowded schedules fail here—give yourself slack.
- Gravity lace: If you feel slightly heavier, slow down. If lighter, don’t overstride; it’s easy to tweak a calf when the world sweetens your step.

Orientation and wayfinding
- Phases instead of hours: Learn the day’s three dependable cues—dawn flex of the towers, the gold band when reflectors roll, the late-night horizon opening toward the ocean. Locals name meetings by these.
- Lines of light: At night, thin biolight pulses indicate accepted flows. Follow the pulse, not the shortest line.
- Home glyphs: People mark homes with microfeatures you feel at eyelashes or shins, not door numbers. Don’t alter or mimic these; they are identities.
- Land that edits itself: Expect a new rib or micro-terrace to appear overnight steering trickles. Your yesterday-shortcut may be today’s nursery.

What not to do
- Don’t lean on matte white tower skin. It’s alive and negotiating temperature.
- Don’t scrape salt rime into patterns. If invited to brush salt back into solution at day’s end, do it with a soft tool and a smile—that’s maintenance, not art.
- Don’t introduce pets, seeds, incense, or aerosols. Even breathable essential oils confuse the boundary layer.
- Don’t step into a condenser field to “help harvest dew.” The system knows how to drink.

If you must bring tech
- Keep ferromagnetic bulk low. Transit braids are magnetic conversations; big magnets are rude participants.
- Dampen emissions. Put phones in low-power mode; the city is a near-field postal service, not a shouting match.
- Storage: Local systems can “bank” time-dense packets. If your gear logs time precisely, expect drift notes and keep redundant, human-readable logs.

Good times to be there
- First light to the end of the gold band: Walk the perimeter of a dew field and watch the plain fill with eyes. Stay at the edge; let the lungmats breathe.
- Midday idea: Go inside a membrane house and feel it push cool through you as the building exhales. You’ll finally hear your thoughts.
- Late night horizon opening: Find a reed-tower terrace and face the black sheet where the city’s rules let go. It re-teaches you what “flat” and “wet” mean.
- Weekly predator-plant hour: Only with a host. It’s the closest thing to theater the valley performs.

Leaving well
- Debrief your clothing and soles with the soft brush. Salt and sugars go back into solution or designated bins—ask your host where. Shake nothing out into the air.
- Return your wayfinding fob; it closes your minor time accounts. If you kept to pulses and didn’t scuff, you’ll be told your “footprint dividend” covered some of your passage—smile and accept the cultural compliment.
- Plan a quiet hour afterward. The normal world will feel loud, bright, and careless with air. Give yourself time to expand back into it.

Short checklist
- Soft shoes, matte layers, wraparound clear goggles.
- Dimmable soft headlamp; tiny pinpoint edge light.
- Haptic comm; local wayfinding fob on loan.
- Electrolyte packets; sealed snacks.
- Neutral-pH wipes; saline vials; antifungal wipes.
- Small soft brush for salt; tape for feet/ankles.
- Analog watch for comfort; flexible schedule for reality.

If you treat the first five millimeters of air as sacred, follow the pulses, and let the city set the conversation, you’ll have the rare feeling of being held—not by walls, but by manners the land learned to teach itself.
