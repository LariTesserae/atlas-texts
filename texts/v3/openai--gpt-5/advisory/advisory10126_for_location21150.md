Name it whatever helps you hold it in your head: the Singing Throat, the Storm-Room, the Hex Chapel. It is a small, high, basalt cleft that cyclically becomes underwater, driven by an atmospheric river and tuned by sound. You cannot live there. You can visit if you arrive ready for cold, noise, altitude, and the fact that the room answers to pressure waves as much as to gravity. Preparation is about three things: staying oriented, staying breathable, and not changing the instrument by accident.

What to expect on arrival
- A roar you feel in your bones. Assume sustained sound pressure in the “concert-pain” band: 110–130 dB+ in air, rising to bone-conduction hums when it goes briefly underwater.
- Air that cycles between mist/spray and full submersion in pulses of seconds to tens of seconds. “Breathable” comes and goes.
- Cold everything. Water just above freezing. Windchill at high altitude. Hypothermia is faster than your sense of time.
- Rock that’s new, sharp, hex-jointed, slick when wet and glassy in places. Very little grit for traction.
- Sound-sensitive hydrodynamics. Frequencies create or destroy shapes of flow. Your own noise can move water.
- Sparse but deliberate human interventions (gut strings, slats, a basket, a ladder). They are not decoration; they’re part of the tuning.

Prerequisites before you go
- Altitude readiness: be comfortable hiking and thinking at 3,000–4,200 m (10,000–14,000 ft). Acclimatize 1–2 nights above 2,500 m first.
- Cold-water tolerance and breath control. Practice calm breath-holds of 30–45 seconds in cold shower or pool conditions without panic.
- Canyoneering/climbing movement on wet rock. Three points of contact, low-slung balance, reading water pulses.
- If bringing any breathing apparatus, get real training. A tiny bailout bottle is only as good as your practice.

Approach and timing
- Best window: the half-hour after an atmospheric river lifts and before the next gust re-threads the room. You’ll know it: the roar drops to after-tremor, light goes clean, everything gleams. That’s when to scout, rig, and decide. Then re-enter cautiously as the first sheets lay in.
- Weather tells: a sky thinned to milk, horizon-to-horizon streaming cloud, a long subsonic moan from the east wall means a bigger pulse is imminent. When the notes simplify into one white hiss, step back; when complexity (arcs, prisms) returns, you have a little margin.
- Do not go in thunderstorms (macro lightning) or high wind warnings on the ridge. The room is already making micro-sparks in cavitation; you don’t need the big kind.

Core kit (silent, water-hardy, vibration-damped)
- Body/thermal
  - Drysuit with integrated socks, latex seals, and a thick insulating underlayer (200–300 g/m2 fleece or equivalent). Neoprene hood.
  - Cut-resistant, grippy gloves over 3–5 mm neoprene liners. Basalt is a knife.
  - Canyoneering boots or approach shoes with very sticky rubber and firm toe protection. Add thin felt or rubber overlays if you have them; no jangly crampons.
- Head/face/hearing
  - Climbing helmet with a clear, shatterproof, anti-fog face visor.
  - Double hearing protection: vented surf/swim plugs seated deep (to survive immersion) plus low-profile muffs under your helmet when in air. Expect bone conduction to remain; this just buys you thinking space.
  - Soft mouthguard or bite block. It damps tooth-borne vibration and reduces jaw fatigue from the hum.
  - Low-profile goggles (anti-fog treated) for when the visor beads too much; swap as needed.
- Breathing options
  - Preferred for ordinary visitors: no open-circuit tanks inside the room (weight, snagging, and bubble noise can change the tune). Instead carry a compact bailout cylinder (e.g., 1–3 cubic feet) with a streamlined reg on a necklace, for surprise full-submersion pulses only. Train with it.
  - If not carrying air: bring a purge-valve snorkel only for the lulls at the mouth; it will flood inside. Your survival is breath discipline and retreat timing.
- Movement/rigging
  - Sit harness with a short double cow’s tail of flat webbing and auto-lock carabiners wrapped in tape to stop their ring. Bring 15–20 m of static line (9–10 mm) pre-sleeved for abrasion and pre-taped to kill whistle.
  - Passive protection that fits hex joints (tricams, hexes). Wrap metal with tape or fabric to mute. No pitons, no hammering if you can help it.
  - A compact, silent safety knife with rounded tip.
- Comms/navigation
  - Pre-agreed hand signals and a 3-signal rope-tug code. Shouting won’t work.
  - Small slate and grease pencil for notes in spray.
  - Headlamp with flood beam for white-out water moments; keep it taped so nothing rattles.
- Warmth/medical
  - Hard-to-open with cold hands? Pre-stage: chemical warmers, foil bivy, hot, sweet drink in a thermos at the ridge.
  - Hypothermia kit, hemostatic gauze, cohesive wrap. Waterproof everything twice.
- Recording (optional, be respectful)
  - If you must capture sound, bring attenuators. A hydrophone and a heavily padded field recorder will avoid saturating. Kill device speakers and vibration motors. Accept that your presence changes the song.

What not to bring
- Loose metal, jangly gear, trekking poles that click, drones, Bluetooth speakers, whistles, anything that hums. Wrap what you must carry so it doesn’t sing.
- Fire or fuel. Ozone and spray don’t love sparks.
- Detergent-coated textiles that will shed surfactants into the water. Clean, rinse, and dry your kit before travel to avoid seeding alien algae.

How to move and where to stand
- Rig a silent lifeline outside the mouth, with your belayer/watcher anchored where they can see your whole body. Keep the connection short inside so a sudden sheet can’t pin you on a wall.
- Enter on a thinning cycle. Wait for the roar to turn complex (prisms, separate notes) rather than a single white slab.
- Three points of contact. Test holds with the back of your hand—edges are razorish. Keep hips in, head low, weight over feet. Face outward; know your retreat step.
- Never stand directly beneath the east-wall cavity when it moans; that tulip of water will hold and then slap flat.
- Map your breathing pockets visually before you need them: you’ll see tiny, steadying eddies shaped by the gut string and slats. Those are where a panicked inhale won’t drown you.
- Limit your total “inside” time. Ten to twenty minutes per session, then back out to re-warm fully.

Breath discipline inside
- Breathe when the spray breaks into beads and you can feel air slide along your cheeks. Exhale slowly as the next sheet fattens; then hold. Use the mouthguard; tongue to palate softens the pulse you feel.
- If a full submersion comes early, don’t fight to inhale. Either use the bailout reg for a few calm sips or ride the pulse out. Keep one hand on rock, one on your chest strap so the reg hose or lanyard doesn’t snag.
- If you feel your timing go ragged, leave. Pride kills faster than water does here.

Hearing and head safety
- Put in earplugs before you get within the cleft; do not try to seat them in spray. Do not valsalva hard against the hum; equalize gently.
- Bone-conduction fatigue feels like a headache behind the eyes and in the teeth. That’s your turnaround signal as much as cold is.

Respect the tuning that’s there
- Do not touch the gut string unless you know what you’re doing. If you bump it and change its tone, re-tension until the small breathing alcove behind it reforms. Mark its original pitch with your recorder before you enter; match that.
- The slatted baffles and chocked stones are “pragmatic prayers.” Leave them. If a shriek is unbearable, use locals’ method: twist and wedge dried grass or raw fiber into a slit and pin with wood. Remove it when you go.
- If the basket is catching amphipods, don’t harvest, don’t feed, don’t “rescue.” Your hands are oil and soap even when they feel clean.

Reading the room by ear and eye
- White wall + pressure in the sternum = incoming sheet, back off and hold.
- Prismatic planes and separate notes = structure and short safe breaths.
- Subsonic east-wall moan deepening = the tulip is forming; its collapse will be rough.
- Ozone tang sharpens = cavitation intensifying; expect micro-spark flickers and more abrupt pulses.

Health and altitude
- Pre-hydrate and eat salt. The spray tastes of salt and iron but won’t rehydrate you. Bring a filter for calmer water outside the room.
- AMS watch: headache that doesn’t ease with fluids, nausea, clumsiness beyond cold—descend.
- Cold management: plan your session to end while you still feel a little warm. If you start to shiver hard or fumble carabiners, you’re late.

Ethics and leave-no-trace for a living instrument
- You are in a throat. Don’t try to make it sing your song. Minimize what you change; put back what you must move.
- Pack out every scrap of fiber, tape, and food. Rinse your gear beforehand so you don’t import spores or surfactants.
- If you learn something about its tuning, write it down and leave no “improvements.”

Emergency plan
- Pre-agree on: time-in, turn-around temp, and three tug signals for HELP/OUT/NOW.
- Belayer watches your posture and the light. If you go limp during a full-water cycle, they wait for the next thinning, then haul with steady pressure—no jerks that could detune something and bring a sheet down.
- First aid happens outside the mouth. Warm first, then treat. Wet adhesives don’t stick; use compressive wraps and hemostatic gauze.

If you only remember ten rules
1) Go during the lull; leave when the white wall gets simple and loud.
2) Wrap and tape anything that can vibrate. Silence your gear.
3) Double hearing protection, goggles, helmet with visor, drysuit with real insulation.
4) Pre-rig a silent lifeline outside; keep it short inside.
5) No shouting, no whistling, no metal clatter. Your noise is a tool or a weapon here.
6) Breathe in the beads; hold in the sheets. Use a micro-bailout only for surprise, not bravado.
7) Treat every edge as a knife and every ledge as temporary.
8) Don’t touch the instruments unless you’re restoring what you knocked.
9) Stop while you still feel capable; cold and hum lie to you about capacity.
10) Step back, warm up, write notes. The room will erase its own shapes; your memory keeps the map.

What it gives you if you prepare this way
- A few minutes inside a working grammar where wind tells stone which shapes of water to be, and your bones can hear the conjugations. You will not control it. You will survive it, and understand a little better why someone strung a single gut line across a hex and called that prayer enough.
