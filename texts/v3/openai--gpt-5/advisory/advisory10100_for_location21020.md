Orientation
- You are headed into a high cirque where the air is thin, the light is fierce, and everything is wet and freezing at once. Darkness here is heat; heat is erosion; stillness is a trap. Drip and film are law. People harvest water with gutters and veils; animals surf surfaces. Sound is a kind of shove.
- Prepare to be brushed, to brush constantly, to keep your voice small, and to move by ropes more than by feet.

Before you go
- Acclimatize: Spend 2–4 days at high altitude first. The bowl sits where breath thins and exertion is expensive.
- Eye discipline: Practice wearing glacier goggles or a pale, side-shielded visor for entire days. Get used to navigating with restricted peripheral vision and high glare.
- Quiet body: Practice moving while barely stirring air—small steps, knees soft, shoulders relaxed, exhale downward into your hand or sleeve.
- Cold-breath drill: Inhale through nose, exhale through cupped hand or mask so your breath doesn’t push the diamond dust into a glittering panic that announces you and ices your own gear.
- Sign basics: Learn a dozen hand signs, done low and tight: please/thanks, sorry, stop, above/behind, danger, permission to pass, where to step, price/number counting (1–10), water/gutter/veil, child/line. Speak close and into your palm.

What to wear
- All pale, matte, and layered. Avoid dark trims, logos, and elastic that drink sun.
- Base: merino or synthetic long underwear. Midlayer: light puffy. Shell: pale, non-gloss, non-crinkly. Over that, a thin, pale smock you don’t mind brushing every few minutes; nap helps catch frost you can whisk away.
- Head/face: deep hood with pale fur or knit ruff; nose guard; grease or balm on cheeks and lips (unscented, non-black). A soft, pale mask or muffler to baffle your breath. Glacier goggles with side shields; lenses pale amber/rose or mirrored, not deep black.
- Hands: two glove systems—thin liners for dexterity under pale mitts. Mitts with a sewn-on mini whisk-broom or soft brush at each wrist.
- Feet: splayed traction. Standard spikes bite the brittle sinter and can pry up sheets; instead use oversoles with many small, pale rubber nubs or felted patches. Keep them pale. Carry microspikes only for the climb in/out, not for the bowl’s terraces.
- Color discipline: if you must carry dark items (camera, phone, stove, tool handles), wrap them in pale cloth or tape. Even a dangling black strap can carve a melt-gutter.

What to carry (pale, quiet, hangs easily)
- Whisk-broom (two): soft, pale bristles. You will brush yourself, your neighbor, the rope you just touched, the tool you just set down.
- Soft hanger kit: pale cordage, a few tiny pulleys, clips—all to get everything you own off the floor and onto overhead lines.
- Light gloves-only toolkit: pale multitool, bone/ceramic awl, small scraper, a slim white tap-stick (for testing thin films and black water).
- Warmth without flame: catalytic hand warmers or chemical heat packs in pale sleeves. Open flames are for locals with hides and skill. Your stove stays packed unless invited to use it under a proper cover, and only if it burns clean and near-silent.
- Drinking system: wide-mouth insulated bottle and a second container for accepting offered water. Filters that handle heavy mineral load; a pinch of citrate/electrolyte powder to keep stones at bay. Do not rely on boiling; fuel is precious here.
- Food: small, dense, shareable, and bland-smelling. Pale-wrapped nuts, nougat, white chocolate, hard cheese, dehydrated fruit. Expect to eat local fats and gel-moss loaves; be grateful.
- Light: headlamp with a soft diffuser and a pale shroud. Red mode for indoors. No strobing, no bright beams on ice surfaces.
- Spares: extra pale tape; extra mitt liners; a pale microfiber to de-ice your goggles; a handkerchief for speaking into; sealable bags for your breath-wet things.
- Gifts (small and high-value): 
  - Pale sewing needles, strong pale thread, fine cordage, sinew substitutes.
  - A few tiny, quiet pulleys or low-friction rings for overhead lines.
  - Mesh samples of different weaves (clearly labeled) for veils.
  - Non-sooting black in sealed tins (fine lampblack or charcoal pigment) for those who know how to use it on veils.
  - A short, light, pale wood rod or two; even a single straight branch is wealth. Wrap to keep from acting like a heat-sink.
  - Lens cloths and pale microfiber squares. 
  - Do not bring coins, speakers, drones, or anything that sheds soot or flakes.

How to move in the bowl
- Clip in: whenever there’s an overhead line, use it. Pull yourself hand-over-hand more than you push with your feet; the floor is slick and sometimes hollow.
- Step light: weight spread, knees bent. Test any new step with your tap-stick. Black water is open water; ink-black “solid” is only clear ice.
- Hang, don’t lean: tools and packs hang from lines or sit on pale pads. Never leave anything tall and alone; frost will lock it to stone in an hour.
- Brush, always: yourself, neighbors, ropes, the rim you just touched. A hair of rime becomes a weight, and weight becomes shear and noise.
- Speak into your hand: words are wind; wind tips the dust and starts small avalanches of crystals off people and ledges.
- Shadow sense: your shadow is a hot knife. Don’t cast it across veils, gutters, or thin bridges of sinter. Keep your body’s darks wrapped.
- Market migration: the “safe terrace” moves. Follow locals’ lines and signs. Stalls are sleds; don’t plant your feet between sled and rope.

Etiquette and the rules made flesh
- Do not touch gutters or veils without invitation. Each drip has a keeper; a moved gutter is a stolen pasture.
- Ask with hands, thank with hands; save your voice for song or alarm.
- Accept being brushed. Brush others. It’s affection and maintenance at once.
- Offer help before asking for sights. The life here is endless small salvations; being an extra pair of hands is better than any coin.
- Sit low, never on thresholds. If you must set something down, put a pale rag beneath it and brush the spot when you lift it again.
- Never trust a new dripline: even as a visitor, don’t set your cup under a stranger without watching it through a sun-breath first.
- Dark is dangerous unless domesticated. If you unpack a dark thing for trade, keep it hooded until someone lays a pale underlay.

Hazards and how to read them
- Glare and blindness: keep goggles on. If you must remove them, kneel, face away from the bowl, and shade with both hands.
- Rime-lock: if you feel your sleeve stiffen or hear a soft crackle as you stop, you’re being grown on. Brush yourself briskly and move three steps.
- Cornice shrug: a soft far thunder, air like a held breath. Freeze, crouch, and tuck in. Let the crystals settle before moving or speaking.
- The bass drum in the stone: stop where you are. Look for fresh hairline cracks, especially across terrace lips. Reroute to rope lines; say nothing until you’re off the suspect panel.
- Animals: the snowcat is a warmth-shape under a drip; don’t approach. Froth-skunks leave oil sheens; don’t follow them into water. Bats accept moths from the palm but are not pets. Children are on leashes for a reason—consider one for yourself if you skid.
- The cave with the warm breath: go only with a local. The stone inhales; your combustion gear will misbehave; your sense of time will, too. Do not collect the singing crystals; they’re medicine and map both.

Water and food
- Drink the cup you’re handed. If you harvest your own, prefer condensation runs off veils or long-watched gutters; avoid raw weep with heavy silica unless you’re filtering.
- Eat fat early and often; it burns slow and steady here. If rancid notes surprise you, remember fuel is precious and “slow” is on purpose.
- Warmth by mouth: small sips of hot, not scalding, broth or tea under a hide. Don’t steam the ceiling.

Sleeping and staying
- Indoors is a grotto deepened by hand, lined with hide. Fires are under hides, barely breathing. Keep your headlamp on red and low.
- Hang everything. Even your boots. Brush the hanging line itself before bed.
- Your breath will frost the inside of your hood. Turn the hood. Brush it. Turn it back. This becomes a habit the second night.
- Share a small song if invited—cupped to your own palm. The wall startles easily.

Trade, gifts, and how not to be a problem
- What’s precious: time, steady drips, lines that run true, clean mesh, quiet tools, wood, and small luxuries that don’t shed soot or noise.
- Good trades: cut lengths of pale cord; a neat tin of pigment; needle sets; a single, well-made pulley; an extra whisk-broom; a pale oversole with good surface.
- Pay attention to how they price: mouth the number and sign it with both hands. Practice mirroring—your “ghost” will do the rest in the dust.

Photography and records
- Cameras are dark and love to melt saddles into snow. Keep yours jacketed pale. No flashes. Exhale down and shoot quickly. Don’t photograph people without a sign of yes. Never lean a tripod.
- A notebook will drink water from the air. Keep a pencil and write under your smock; ink freezes.

What to leave behind
- Sooty stoves, black tarps, loudspeakers, drones, anything that thrums air or throws sharp noise.
- Crampons meant for blue ice, ski poles with spike baskets, traction that chisels.
- Perfume, crumbly charcoal, powder that can blow off a veil and stain the bowl.
- Any plan that requires shouting over distance.

How to arrive and how to go
- The nick in the ridge is a razor. Grease your face, mask up, and go when the wind slacks. Enter low, let your breath fall, and pause to read the dust: it shows your wake like a conversation you haven’t had yet.
- On leaving, brush the place you occupied—the little circle where you warmed the world. Make it tidy for the next body that must move lightly and keep moving.

Mental posture to pack
- Curiosity small enough to fit in a cupped hand.
- Patience for a market that slides a few meters while you’re counting.
- Willingness to be brushed, to brush, to take three steps then retie a line, again and again.
- Delight in light behaving strangely and in the discipline of not chasing it.

If you can’t keep brushing, if you need to plant a big shadow and own a corner, if you must be heard across a square—this bowl will make you into a statue and take your heat for itself. If you can reduce yourself to an extra set of pale hands moving quietly from drip to drip, you will fit, and the place will show you its ghosts and halos, and you’ll hear the stone tune itself under a sky that hums without sound.
