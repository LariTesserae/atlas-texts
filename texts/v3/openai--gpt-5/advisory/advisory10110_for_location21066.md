Essentials at a glance
- Climate: constant warm, saturated air, never cold, rarely hot; near-100% humidity most of the time.
- Terrain: young, sharp limestone (karren, slots, sudden drop-offs), caps of basalt carrying perched bogs; many hidden weeps and springs; broad silence; sheer fog-wet cliffs at the margins.
- City: low-density clusters around water, delicate waterworks everywhere; terraces, basins, runnels, fog-catchers; wood and fiber where things must flex, stone where paths are forever.
- Biology: plants, lichens, fungi; no animals or insects at all. Expect abundant spores and pollen, zero buzzing or calls.
- Culture: pact with water and distance; quiet; don’t change the way water already moves; step where people have taught the ground to bear steps.

Before you go: biosecurity and ethics
- Absolutely no animals. Do not bring pets, feeders, bait, insect samples, honey, feathers, leather, wool, bone tools, horn, shell, meat, jerky, dairy, or any animal-derived clothing or food. Expect inspection and refusal if you do.
- No stowaways. Decontaminate all gear:
  - Launder hot, then seal in clean dry bags. Freeze anything washable at −20°C for 72 hours, or heat at 60–70°C for 1 hour to kill eggs/mites. Inspect seams and Velcro.
  - Clean soil from soles, trekking poles, tripod feet, pack frames. Scrub, then wipe with alcohol.
  - Choose brand-new socks and insoles still in packaging; open on arrival.
- Seed and microbe caution. Avoid bringing seeds, potting mix, or living cultures. If you keep a sourdough or kombucha at home, do not pack those containers.
- Medical screening. Their disease ecology is quiet. Don’t arrive coughing.
  - Be symptom-free for 10 days. Test for common respiratory viruses if asked.
  - Be fully vaccinated (tetanus, hep A/B at minimum). Carry masks for close indoor encounters per host request.
- Gifting and trade. Do not gift metals, fuels, high-tech water devices, or chemicals that alter hydrology or nutrient cycles. Offer labor, time, repair skills, songs, stories, or simple plant-neutral items (plain ceramic cup, untreated cordage you’ve cleaned). Ask first.

Acclimatization and physiology
- Altitude. The air feels dense with moisture but light on oxygen. Plan for mild altitude effects.
  - Arrive slowly if possible; take a rest day. Hydrate with electrolytes (you’ll sweat but nothing evaporates).
  - Consider acetazolamide if you’re prone to AMS. Move deliberately; overexertion in humidity overheats you fast.
- Heat and humidity management.
  - Expect to be damp constantly. Evaporative cooling is poor. Work early and late, rest at mid-day.
  - Barrier creams (zinc oxide) and antifungals (powder/cream) are not optional. Trench foot is the commonest visitor injury.

What to wear and carry
- Footwear:
  - Sticky-rubber amphibious boots or approach shoes with aggressive siping; high ankle support.
  - Avoid felt soles (they harbor spores) and metal crampons (they scar stone and wood).
  - Bring 3–4 pairs/day of thin, wicking socks; change often. Toe socks reduce maceration between toes.
  - Lightweight over-gaiters keep peat and sedge-chaff out.
- Clothing:
  - Light, quick-drying synthetics or high-density cotton that tolerates wet without sagging. Accept damp as the baseline.
  - Long sleeves and pants to keep constant drizzle off skin; soft-brimmed hat to shed veils of water from your face.
  - No leather. Mildew-resistant, unscented fabrics preferred.
- Outer layer:
  - A breathable, unlined windshirt or airy poncho. Full rain shells get clammy here.
- Hands and eyes:
  - Thin grip gloves for wet stone and rope.
  - Microfiber cloths and a gentle anti-fog for lenses.
- Pack and protection:
  - Roll-top waterproof pack liners and small dry bags; silica gel packets in every electronics pouch.
  - Trekking poles with rubber tip protectors; a 10–15 m throw line with a soft toggle for bog rescue.
- Medical and care:
  - Antifungal powder and cream (miconazole/nystatin), zinc barrier cream, hydrocolloid bandages, waterproof tape.
  - Ear drops (acetic alcohol) to prevent swimmer’s ear.
  - Antihistamine for spores/pollen; mild inhaler if you have reactive airways.
  - Blister kit; nail clippers; skin glue.
- Navigation and comms:
  - Simple analog compass (expect some basaltic deviation), altimeter watch, waterproof notepad.
  - Small, silent headlamp with warm LED; carry a spare in a dry bag. No drones—noise etiquette.
  - Whistle reserved for emergencies only.
- Electronics:
  - Keep minimal. Condensation will intrude; bring dry boxes and desiccant.
  - Solar works inconsistently under fog. A small hand crank or spare battery bank kept double-bagged is safer.
- Food and water:
  - Vegan, sealed, low-crumb rations (dense bars, dehydrated starches/legumes). No animal fats or gels.
  - Personal filter with a fine prefilter; avoid chemical treatments that could taint communal basins.
  - Electrolyte tabs without dyes or strong flavors.
- Sleeping:
  - Hammock straps and a quick-dry liner; a bug net is unnecessary but can serve as a drip baffle.
  - Light sheet; nights remain warm, but everything feels cool to the skin when wet.

Moving through the land
- Read the ground.
  - Limestone: sharp karren ridges, sudden slots; test steps. Use poles to probe ahead in fissured fields.
  - Basalt: perched bogs; sphagnum that looks solid can swallow a leg. If you break through, spread your weight, roll back, and let a partner haul you via throw line.
- Keep to the taught paths.
  - Use causeways, reed mats, and flagged walkways. Moss remembers your footprints for seasons.
  - Never step into carved runnels or across tufa lips; those are living parts of the city’s water.
- Edges are absolute.
  - The fern line near the cliff is your last friend; the pale stone beyond it is off-limits.
  - Fog along the brink destroys depth cues. Stay three body-lengths back unless roped, and never solo the margins.
- Silence etiquette.
  - Soft soles, rubber pole tips, muted voices. Turn off device sounds. The place is built to be heard working.
- Weather and daily rhythm.
  - The fog arrives like clockwork; plan crossings of open ground when visibility is best.
  - Surfaces are always wet; “it’ll dry” is a lie here. Choose routes for drainage, not dryness.

Water etiquette and safety
- The city teaches water to behave; do not confuse it.
  - Don’t wash skin, cookware, or clothing in runnels, basins, or cisterns. Ask for a washing ledge or use provided greywater pits.
  - Do not spit, flavor, or dose communal water. Draw with a cup, step aside, re-seal your filter away from the lip.
  - Keep tools and boots off tufa crusts; one careless kick undoes years of mineral growth.
- Where to drink.
  - Locals will point you to “finished” water—often from fog-catchers or sun-warmed settling tanks. If unsure, filter.
- Slime and slip.
  - Basalt pools and wooden ramps wear a living film. Plant your whole foot; avoid sudden torque. Lean into ropes that are there to lean into.

Health maintenance in continuous wet
- Foot routine, twice daily:
  - Rinse off peat acids, pat dry, air for 10–15 minutes, powder, change socks, re-cream hotspots with zinc.
- Skin:
  - Keep folds dry with cloth wicks. Treat any whitening/pruning with rest and air. Cover cuts immediately; change dressings often.
- Ears and lungs:
  - Dry ears after long drips; a couple of prophylactic ear drops each evening if you’re prone.
  - Masks can help if ferns are shedding heavy spores and your airways protest.
- Heat management:
  - Work-rest cycles; sip electrolytes. Don’t chase sweat—accept humidity and keep pace calm.

Culture and craft
- Touch lightly.
  - Platforms, ladders, and bridges were dragged, not hoisted; treat every member as precious. Step where darkened by feet, not where pale and new.
- Ask to help.
  - Work exchanges—clearing runnels, reseating wedges, mending lashings—are welcome ways to belong without importing trouble.
- Doors are reed, not walls.
  - Privacy is signaled by screens and distance more than locks. Pause, announce yourself softly, and wait.
- Food is air-fed.
  - Meals lean on wind-pollinated grains, sedges, and what will root in brown soil circles. Bring your own supplements without leaving nutrients behind.
- Smoke and flame.
  - Kilns exist but live under strict patience. Open fires are rare; your stove may be forbidden. If allowed, use an alcohol stove over a stone pad; collect condensed water, and disperse heat.

Navigation between clusters
- Lines of sight are long until they aren’t.
  - In clear, you’ll see the next terrace as a clean geometry against moving air. In fog, you’ll see your hands. Carry a buddy line if you must move in fog.
- Wayfinding logic:
  - Read the water’s story: weeps → ledges → basins → cisterns. Human spaces island around these. If lost, follow the draining edge to a terrace rather than following a rill into a slot.

Hazards recap
- Hidden slots in limestone; sharp karren that can slice boots.
- Mattress-thick sphagnum that rides your weight, then swallows it.
- Sheer fog-wet cliffs with no guardrails or birds to warn you.
- Constant wet means slow wound healing and rampant fungi.
- Quiet can be disorienting; you may misjudge distance/speed without the usual animal cues.

Leave-no-trace in a water city
- Pack out all soaps, food scraps, and human waste if asked; many clusters prefer sealed carry-out kits to keep nutrients from seeping into peatlands.
- No cairns, stakes, or permanent marks. Moss will obligingly keep your mistake for years.
- Decon on exit as you did on entry; don’t export their spores to some other fragile world.

A day there, well-run
- Wake to the first thin blue before fog arrives. Warm your joints, treat your feet, sip warm water caught overnight under a fog-catcher.
- Walk a measured quarter hour to the next cluster, keeping to causeways and reed mats. Stop at a basin, ask permission, drink where shown.
- Offer an hour helping re-plane a tufa lip or clearing sedge from a drain. Lunch in quiet, away from the runnels.
- Cross open ground while the sky is legible; turn back at the fern line near any brink.
- As fog thickens, sit still under a woven catcher and listen to the place knitting itself together: droplet rings, soft pours, no creak. Let your voice match the air if you must speak.

If you remember only five rules
1) Bring nothing that flies, creeps, crawls, or was cut from anything that once did.
2) Keep to what is already taught: paths, screens, lips, drains.
3) Take nothing’s dryness for granted; design your day around damp.
4) The pale stone beyond the fern line is never for you.
5) Don’t change the way water moves—only borrow it, briefly, with thanks.
