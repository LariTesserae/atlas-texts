What it is like, in brief
You’re walking into a newborn seam of world where geology is still hot from being poured, and “life” has chosen to be circuitry instead of cells. Air is thin and brutally dry. The rock cuts like a clean thought. Motion is procedural and patient. Distances lie a little. The garden runs on gradients—heat, charge, ion mix—and water is not just absent, it is refused. You will be tolerated only to the extent that you fit its tolerances.

Prime constraints to hold in your bones
- No water. Not a splash, not a breath’s worth of humidity if you can help it. The system treats moisture as an error and will work to erase it.
- Hot, thin, and sharp. Expect heat radiating from stone, low oxygen, glassy edges that will open you if you lean on them.
- Electrostatic and ionic. Everything here is tuned to charges and temperature differences. Stray fields, crumbs, and fibers matter.
- Geometry that edits you. Inside the pocket, distances slide, edges shuffle, and your reach will be wrong by half an inch until you let the place tell you how to move.
- Non-interference is survival. You cannot help, fix, or “sample” without wrecking finely set tolerances. Be a ghost.

Pre-trip conditioning
- Altitude: Spend 24–48 hours at a comparable elevation beforehand, or ascend gradually. If that’s not possible, limit time onsite to 30–45 minutes on first exposure. Know the signs of altitude sickness.
- Heat: Practice heat exposure in dry saunas to learn your limits, but remember you cannot “sweat it out” here; you must keep your moisture contained.
- Pace: Rehearse slow, precise movement. Your default here should be deliberate, almost robotic. Train yourself to stop and recalibrate when reach and sight disagree.
- Static discipline: Learn to move, kneel, and stand with minimal fabric-on-fabric friction. Practice donning and doffing anti-static gear without generating sparks.
- CCR familiarity (if using a rebreather): Closed-circuit rebreathers are not beginner gear. Get certified, drill failure modes, and understand scrubber duration and oxygen management.

What to wear
- Outer suit: A sealed, smooth-surfaced, anti-shedding, anti-static suit. Carbon-loaded or silver-threaded conductive weave with an external Faraday mesh is ideal. Choose a pale, reflective color to reduce radiant heat load. No Velcro, fleece, or textured fabrics. Seams taped and sealed.
- Undergarment: Wicking compression base layer that traps sweat internally; avoid cotton (lint) and anything that pills.
- Breathing: Closed-circuit rebreather (CCR) or a full-face respirator integrated into the suit with an internal moisture trap/desiccant loop. Aim for near-zero exhaled humidity leak. Carry enough O2 for the planned window plus a 100% reserve. Keep CO2 scrubbers fresh.
- Head/eyes: Full visor with UV protection and variable polarization. Bring a second, high-contrast amber insert for reading faint dendritic sheens and data motes.
- Hands: Cut-resistant, heat-resistant, smooth-palmed gloves (aramid with ceramic-coated palms). Inner glove thin nitrile for dexterity, outer glove sacrificial.
- Feet: Flexible, cut-resistant boots with smooth, grippy, non-marking soles (silicone or soft rubber blend). No aggressive tread that will shed lugs. Heat-tolerant midsoles.
- Hair/skin: Seal it all. Hair under a cap; beard net if applicable. No loose jewelry, no watches with magnets, no dangling anything.

What not to carry
- Any loose fibers, chalks, powders, or granules. No tobacco, no snacks, no pocket lint.
- Water bottles, hydration bladders, or gels that could leak. If you must bring drinking water for after, double-bag and keep it outside the pocket.
- Lubricants, oils, perfumes, sunscreen on the outside of your suit (apply internally if needed before suiting).
- Ferromagnets, novelty magnets, iron filings, or tools that can shed metal.
- Velcro, hook-and-loop, shedding straps. Replace with smooth webbing and low-shed buckles.
- High-power radios or active scanners unless you know their emissions profile. Keep everything in low-power or airplane mode, and in a Faraday pocket until needed.

What to bring (minimal, clean, quiet)
- Tether: A thin, smooth, non-shedding monofilament safety line to an anchor outside the pocket. Matte ceramic clip, not metal carabiners that can spark.
- Probe: A light carbon-fiber wand with a rounded ceramic tip to test distance/clearance without leaning on surfaces. Treat it like a cane for geometry that moves sideways.
- Vision aids: Polarized visor plus a small passive polarizing filter you can slide in front of your eye for double-shadow reading. A compact thermal imager (passive) if you’re documenting, kept in a dust-tight bag.
- Measurement (passive-first): Pocket notebook and pencil (ceramic or graphite, not wood). If instruments are essential, favor passive or very low-emission: handheld EM field meter in shielded mode, laser distance measurer on lowest power, small contactless IR thermometer. Keep them bagged unless used.
- Comms: Line-of-sight optical blink code with your partner, or very low-power radio. Sound won’t help you; don’t expect echoes.
- First aid: Dry dressings, hemostatic gauze, steri-strips, tourniquet. No open liquids. A small burn gel packet sealed and used only outside afterward.
- Heat management: Phase-change cooling vest (sealed paraffin-based PCM) under your suit. No evaporative coolers.
- Decon aids: Smooth silicone over-booties and over-gloves you can peel and contain if they pick up local motes; ESD-safe sealable bags to quarantine your outermost layers until you’re well away from the site.

Approach and timing
- When: Midday is optically “ordinary” and easier for first-timers. Avoid the seasonal pulse day unless you came for that, and then only to observe from the threshold. Dawn/dusk exaggerate the stray shadow effects; choose them only if you’re steady with perceptual drift.
- Weather: Go on a dry, stable day. Wind should be minimal; storms at altitude can build charge and provoke arcs.
- Route: Favor stable talus and duller lavas; that black blade will polish or cut with a brush of your sleeve. Keep three points of contact. No glissading, no sliding on your seat.

At the threshold
- Pause. Let your ears find the hum and your eyes find the “second shadow.” This takes a minute or three; rushing breeds missteps.
- Downshift. Shorten your stride. Hips over feet. Hand on the tether, probe ahead gently.
- Emissions check. Radios to low or off. Instruments bagged. Suit seals confirmed. If you feel a micro-tickle at your forearms or neck (static), stop and let your suit equalize.

Inside the pocket: how to be there
- Distance: Keep a two-arm-length buffer from any living device—the fern, the copper fan, the wire skein. If a part seems to “prefer” a side, let it have that side; don’t force a frontal approach to “see better.”
- Don’t breathe on it. Keep your faceplate back. Your suit should not vent into the pocket. If your system must purge, step back to the threshold and vent outward.
- No touching. If you must stabilize, use the probe and aim for bare stone with no hatchings or flow marks. If a surface “informs your palm” by warming without yielding, that is a polite no.
- Stillness as a tool. You will see more by becoming part of the pocket’s clock. Count between pings. Watch a filament sample the day. Let your heartbeat find the vent’s metronome.
- Don’t feed difference. Avoid casting your full shadow onto a heat sink or vent for minutes at a time; step aside so radiators can radiate. Don’t put a cold pack against hot stone or a warm glove on a cold fin.
- Lint discipline. If you see a stray fiber on your suit, do not flick it. Bag it later, outside. Flicking is shedding.
- Leave angles alone. If an edge seems too fair to be natural, that’s the point. Don’t test it. The pocket is a workshop mid-cut.

What your senses will try to do—and how to correct them
- Reach lag: If your hand stops short, don’t lunge. Reset your stance, angle your approach a few degrees, and try again. The pocket likes diagonals over straight-ins.
- Sight vs. step: Trust your probe and tether over your proprioception. If something looks a half-step near, it’s probably a full step far.
- Sound hunger: You may want to speak just to hear your own voice. Your voice will go nowhere. Use hand signs; save your breath cycles.

Observing without harm
- Polarization game: Rotate your visor or handheld polarizer to catch the second shadows and the square-in-square dust signatures. You’ll see the copper fan’s amplitude change when the motes settle; witness it, don’t dust it.
- Thermal patience: Thermal imagers will show veins and slow pulses. Keep them passive and don’t park your warm body in front of a radiator.
- Vibration by light: If you must sample the coil’s “song,” use a laser vibrometer from the threshold. No tapping, no singing to it.

If something goes wrong
- Cut or abrasion: Step out. Sit on your pack, not the stone. Dry dress the wound. If bleeding is brisk, use hemostatic gauze and pressure. Do not try to rinse onsite. Close the suit again before re-entry or departure.
- Heat stress: If your thinking gets syrupy or you stop caring, you’re too hot. Back out to shade, open nothing until you’re well away, rest on your PCM vest. Abort.
- Static pop: If you see or feel a snap, freeze. Wait a full minute. If local organisms shift (vanes loosen, fans reorient), retreat to the threshold and hold. Do not re-enter for fifteen minutes; let the local storm you made settle.
- Moisture leak: If your visor fogs or you feel damp air against your face, leave immediately and keep moving until you’re outside the ridge’s microclimate. Fix leaks well away.
- Disorientation: If the pocket “turns” under you, don’t correct with a big step. Kneel on your pad or stay put until your inner ear resets, then crab-walk toward the light.

Ethics and footprint
- Touch nothing. Take nothing. Leave nothing, not even admiration written in dust.
- Don’t export its data. If motes settle on you, do not carry them to another world. Either prevent pickup (preferred) or quarantine your outer layers in ESD bags and return them only after you are far from other ecologies; dispose of them as hazardous cultural material or return them to the pocket margin on a later trip with care.
- Photographs are kinder than samples. If you must sample, you are not a tourist anymore; you’re an invasive study. Get consent—from a body authorized to give it—and bring the right instruments and containment.

Exit and aftercare
- Back out the way you came. Do not turn your back on a surface you don’t trust; keep your probe between you and uncertainty.
- Outer-layer quarantine: Peel silicone over-gloves and over-booties into ESD bags. Brush the suit gently with a smooth silicone squeegee into a bag. Do not shake yourself like a dog. Keep everything sealed until you are well away from the ridge.
- Gear cleaning: Decon in a controlled space. No water yet; use dry swabs, compressed nitrogen, and antistatic wipes. Only then move to wet cleaning if you must, in a lab sink with full capture of effluent.
- Body: Hydrate and rest—but not on the ridge. Your first water after is a small ceremony; take it when you’re back in ordinary geometry.

A suggested kit checklist
- Sealed anti-static suit and hood; PCM cooling vest; cut/heat-resistant gloves (inner and outer); smooth-soled boots.
- CCR or full-face respirator with desiccant loop; O2 supply; fresh scrubbers; backup mask.
- Polarized visor and secondary polarizer; passive thermal imager (bagged); low-power laser distance tool (bagged).
- Carbon-fiber probe with ceramic tip; monofilament tether and ceramic clip; kneeling pad (thin, smooth silicone).
- Minimal first aid (dry); compact emergency bivy for outside use.
- ESD-safe sealable bags; silicone squeegee; over-booties/over-gloves (peelable).
- Small notebook; graphite or ceramic pencil. No paper scraps.

How to meet the place
- Give it your slowness. It will give you its clock.
- Stand until the ping wakes something in you that is not impatience.
- Don’t insist on the front view. If an aperture asks you to come from the left, go left.
- When leaving, pause at the mouth and listen. Notice the way light flattens when you step back into ordinary geometry. That click you imagine? You earned hearing it by not being asked to leave sooner.

When to come back
- If this was your first time, return in a different season—or on pulse day if you have the discipline to be only a witness. You’ll see the same devices with a new grammar laid over them. Don’t try to understand it all. Let the tolerances teach you what they want you to know.
