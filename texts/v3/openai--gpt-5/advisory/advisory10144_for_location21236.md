What to expect
- Think of a warm, breathable ocean made of darkness and water. The air is hot-wet and heavy on your skin; surfaces are slick, pocked, and always beading. Light doesn’t travel far, sound does. Distances are huge, edges are lies, and everything that looks still is quietly moving.
- You will navigate by feel, by the push of air and rain on your face, by the quality of sound on rock and leaf. Vision is for the near-at-hand—your own shoes, the next handhold, the rope you’re clipped to.
- Nothing is dry. Anything warm or dry becomes irresistible: it draws moss-film, insects, curious vines, and hunters that read pressure and heat the way we read writing.
- Machines and organisms have treaties you cannot hear yet. Factories talk in bass; birds ride fog like a river; even flowers keep the beat.

Prepare your body and your expectations
- Train for wet rock and rope: canyoning, caving, and via ferrata skills. Practice moving silently and slowly with three points of contact. Learn to self-belay, ascend/descend fixed lines, and recover from a slip without flailing or splashing.
- Condition feet and hands for immersion: daily wet–dry cycling, toe strength, balance on slick surfaces. Practice foot-care protocols to prevent immersion foot.
- Practice night adaptation: spend hours in low light without headlamps; learn to let your hearing lead and to stop moving when you lose the map in your inner ear.
- Breathing discipline: slow, quiet, through your nose. Fast, warm exhale can summon curious life; CO2 draws vines. Meditation and freediving-style breath control help.
- Accept perpetual damp. You will not “dry out” until you’re in a sealed shelter. Relax into it; fighting the wet makes you tired and careless.

Rules you must actually live by
- Don’t make dry spots outside your shelter. A towelled rock, a hot exhaust, a dehumidifier vent—these become magnets.
- Don’t shine distance light. Keep light hooded, local, and low. A beam across space is a dinner bell.
- Don’t make public noise. Your footfall is a broadcast; your dropped carabiner is a siren. Speak softly; prefer touch-signals.
- Expect your straight edges to soften. Anything sharp, square, or taut will slime, sag, or grow ropes of beads.

Core kit (everything corrosion-proofed and double-bagged)
- Movement and fall protection
  - Harness with two lanyards (via ferrata style, energy-absorbing), plus one short daisy for close clipping.
  - Static lifeline (pre-rigged with knots you can feel to count distance), two auto-lock carabiners (marine-grade).
  - Canyoning boots with sticky, siped rubber; ankle support. Neoprene socks or wool liners for warmth and blister control.
  - Gloves with wet-grip palms (nitrile-dipped or sharkskin-texture); thin liner gloves for dexterity.
  - Trekking poles with wide, soft rubber tips (no carbide—too loud, too damaging).
  - Compact ascenders/descenders designed for muddy, wet rope; practice wet use.

- Clothing
  - Amphibious softshell layers: hydrophilic outer (so it beads evenly), thin insulating mid (that still insulates when wet), wicking base. Expect saturated conditions; avoid cotton.
  - Hood with stiff brim to control drip-line and keep light inside the hood.
  - Lightweight vapor-barrier socks for camp only (inside sealed shelter) to dry feet.
  - Buff/face veil to diffuse breath when you must go lamp-up-close to life-rich surfaces.
  - Permethrin-treated outerwear; insect head net for still periods.

- Lighting and sensing
  - Two hooded, dimmable lamps (0.2–5 lumens working mode) with tight beam and blackout skirt. Bring amber and deep-red filters and test which spectrum is ignored locally (ask guides).
  - Passive night-vision monocular (image intensifier). Use sparingly; it helps read biolum “close-talk.”
  - No strobe, no wide floods. No UV “scorpion” lights—many organisms will notice.
  - Gear markers: tritium vials or very low-glow phosphor tabs for finding zips and carabiners by touch-glance at your chest.

- Navigation and comms
  - Tactile map and route strip with raised relief and notch codes for hazards.
  - Wrist vibro-compass (muted), inertial step-counter logger (passive; keep radio off unless needed).
  - Bone-conduction comms with throat mic, vibration-only alerts. Radios waterproofed, antennas short; keep transmit power low and use prearranged time windows.
  - Rope talk: learn the local tug codes; carry a small haptic clicker on your lanyard.

- Breathing and thermal signature
  - Optional but valuable: a small, passive rebreather/CO2 scrubber mask or a “breath condenser” that cools and re-saturates your exhale before it vents. If that’s too technical, use a baffled scarf and exhale down-chest under your jacket to diffuse breath and warmth.
  - Avoid fans or powered respirators; motor whine is public.

- Shelter and camp
  - Sealed micro-shelter (single-wall bivy or tent) with condensation management that keeps all exhaust air warm and saturated before release. Blackout skin; no light leak.
  - Hammock or cot to keep you off slime and seep. Drip lines on suspension.
  - Inside-only “dry zone” kit: desiccant canisters, a tiny, silent chemical dehumidifier, microfiber towel—use only within sealed shelter.
  - Flameless ration heaters for meals inside the shelter; vent heat through a condenser sleeve if possible. No open flame outside.

- Water and food
  - Don’t drink raw blackwater. Treat with hollow-fiber filter + activated carbon + chlorine dioxide. If you boil, do it sealed; don’t pipe steam into the world.
  - Electrolytes and high-calorie cold rations. Cooking odors travel; keep meals neutral.
  - Probiotic course for gut resilience; vitamin D supplements for long stays.

- Medical
  - Antifungals (topical azoles), barrier creams (zinc oxide), broad-spectrum antibiotics as prescribed (consult travel clinic about leptospirosis prophylaxis), tetanus booster.
  - Waterproof dressings that tolerate maceration; adhesive fails when soaked—bring cohesive wraps and hydrocolloids.
  - Eye care: sterile saline, anti-fog drops.
  - Insect bite kit and antihistamines.
  - Ear protection for the rare “singing cliff” events (vibration-damping plugs).

- Tools and materials
  - Stainless/titanium multi-tool; ceramic blade (no rust).
  - Non-sparking, soft mallet for testing surfaces.
  - Biocide-safe gear wash for end-of-day decontamination (use inside shelter; don’t dump).
  - Dielectric grease for all connectors; conformal-coated electronics; IP68 everything.
  - Spare lanyards and short “bead ropes” (thin lines that encourage water beading and mark your path without adding noise).

Travel tactics
- Move like an amphibian. Slow, close to the surface, weight over feet. You’re trying not to excite pressure waves. Step where the water already moves; avoid sudden swallows that sound like an animal slipping.
- Clip often. If there’s a handline, you’re on it. Double-clip across every change in level. This isn’t paranoia; it’s politeness to your future self.
- Keep your light within your hood. Work by shadow and fingertip. To check a wider space, crouch, shield, and lift your light only until it kisses the nearest surface.
- Don’t brush your hands along every wall. Many skins here are alive and reactive. Touch with intent, on surfaces already glazed by traffic.
- Treat black lakes as if they have no shore. Probe with pole, not boot. If you break the surface membrane you make a sound that carries.
- When a wall booms, still yourself. That’s a big underground river turning. Wait: sometimes overhangs sweat loose in those moments.
- If you feel a rush of cool mist from a sinkhole, that bowl is “raining up.” Hood your lamps and take the long way; up-storms are social events for small things with teeth.

Etiquette with life and infrastructure
- Ask for the local “quiet band.” Operators tune their machines below certain choruses; they’ll tell you which frequencies to avoid with any device that hums (drones, fans, even chargers). When in doubt, keep all motors off.
- Stay off sinter shelves and fragile “frozen curtains.” They regrow, but in centuries.
- Pitcher colonies that glow are traps—for insects, sometimes for your curiosity. Don’t lean in; don’t share your breath.
- If you meet cloud-swimmers (blind, soft, flocking), freeze and lower your profile. Let the fog close again.
- Factories have foghorns and bass beacons marking safe routes; learn their call-and-response. If your teeth buzz, you’re on a spoke—good for navigation, but keep your own gear quiet.
- Offer no gleam. Shiny, hard-surfaced tools should be sleeved. Windows, screens, watches—cover them.

Campcraft in permanent damp
- Rig under overhangs that already drip; avoid “quiet” rock where your shelter would create a new dry patch.
- Hang sacrificial bead-lines to carry water away from zips and entry points. Train every drip a path you choose.
- Inside your sealed shelter: dry feet, change into vapor-barrier socks, eat, do medical. Outside: accept wet.
- Never vent heat or light directly to the open. If you must dry a small item outdoors, wet it first, then wring within a bag, then return it inside—the goals are “no steam, no scent, no heat patch.”

Health and hygiene
- Feet: rinse, dry inside shelter, powder, rotate socks. If skin blanches or creases, you’re overdue to rest them.
- Skin: rinse slime off cuts; dress immediately. The water is alive.
- Lungs: if you taste metal too strongly or your chest feels heavy, slow down; that’s you outpacing your heat management in saturated air. Sip air, cool your breath under your jacket.
- Sleep: earplugs that damp but don’t occlude bone-conduction; a mild sleep mask for the small close-glows of your own gear.

Navigation without horizons
- Build a “sound map”: learn the metronomes of leaf-rows, the chord of a frog bowl, the hiss-signature of a lace-fall you can follow. They’re stable most days.
- Count steps between known bass beacons. Knot codes on your lifeline help when batteries die.
- Avoid traveling during the big fog tide when the basalt cap rolls its own weather down; edges blur, and predators reposition along the sound-corridors.

Working with locals (if present)
- Tie into their lines if invited. Don’t re-rig unless asked.
- Keep your lamp inside your hood in company; show your hands, not your beam.
- Pay attention to how they pause and listen at thresholds. Imitate that.
- If a caution gong is strung over a void, don’t silence it; step wide and quiet. That gong isn’t for you alone.

Emergency basics
- Slip into a sink: don’t thrash. Inflate a compact PFD if you carry one; back-kick to a weeping wall and cling. Signal with two slow tugs on your line; three tugs is medical distress.
- Predator interest (pressure-wave hunter): become small and steady. Press your chest to the rock, reduce breathing amplitude, and let background water-noise cover you. Don’t throw stones—sound is public and scatters panic.
- Injury: get into your shelter bubble fast. Sepsis rises fast here. Dose antibiotics only as advised by your medic plan.
- Lost: stop. Light off. Listen. Find the nearest persistent metronome (leaf drip, lace-fall) and follow the edge of its sound until you meet structure or a handline.

What not to bring or do
- No drones, no whistles, no bug zappers, no campfires. No wide-angle floodlights. No alcohol stoves (scent and heat).
- Don’t blast music or use phones on speaker. Disable device chimes, alarms, haptics that click loudly.
- Don’t carve arrows or stack cairns; moss will edit them and you’ll mislead the next traveler.
- Don’t wash gear in open water with biocides. Keep your chemistry inside your shelter bag.

Pre-departure checklist
- Vaccines up to date; consult a travel clinic about leptospirosis risk and prophylaxis. Pack antifungals.
- Test all electronics for fogging and seal integrity in a steamy bathroom for an hour. If they fog, they fail there.
- Break in boots and gloves on wet stone. Practice clipping blind.
- Tune your lights to the lowest useful lumen and memorize where the dimmer is by feel.
- Agree on rope codes and radio windows with your party. Practice hand-on-line communication with eyes closed.

Why go
- To learn a different grammar of space. Here depth is tasted, not seen; time is kept by drip and chorus; your body becomes an instrument the place plays. If you let it, the plateau will teach you to be present within arm’s reach—and to hear the continent breathe around you.
