What it is you’re visiting
- A living, tenuous city no bigger than your palm, in a pencil-thin crack between a peat root-mass and a flat stone on the rim of a marsh. It runs on tension, humidity, and patience. Everything that holds it together can be ruined by a single careless breath, a fingertip, a warm light, a vibration.

First principle
- Do not try to enter. You will not fit. Your breath won’t fit. Your scale, temperature, and microbes are catastrophic here. You are a weather event to them. Your only ethical mode is distant, cold, slow, and sealed.

When to go
- Choose a stable, cold spell above freezing, with no rain forecast for 24 hours before and after. Avoid hard freeze (the pocket vitrifies and sound leaves; don’t disturb that), and avoid thaws or storms (when the weeping becomes running—market, maintenance, and flood response; anything you do will be amplified).
- Early morning in a string of calm, overcast days is best. Wind makes reeds clack and moves leaves; even a parted leaf shifts light inside like thunder.

How to approach the marsh
- Pre-wash body and gear with unscented soap; skip deodorant, bug spray, sunscreen, and any lotion that can drip. Wear clean, dedicated clothing that has never been near pesticides or strong scents.
- Wear marsh shoes or wide bog boards to distribute weight and avoid shifting the peat. Test each step; pause until the ground settles before the next. You’re managing pressure waves through soil and root.
- Kneel only on a clean, rigid ground board. Every new contact point is a tremor inside their world.

Personal containment
- Wear a clean suit or smooth, lint-free clothing. Hair fully covered. Double nitrile gloves; wet the outer glove lightly with filtered, sterilized marsh water so it doesn’t wick or shed skin oils. No powder.
- Use a respirator or mask that traps exhalation (no valve). Better: work under a small, clear, cold “still-air” hood so your breath never brushes the slit. Chill the hood beforehand to ambient so it doesn’t fog or warm the pocket. Let it equilibrate before you uncover the slit.

Finding the slit without announcing yourself
- You will not see it at first. Work by touch and shadow on the stone’s edge; never probe the gap.
- Do not shine a bright light; inside, “too much light” is a weather calamity. If you must illuminate, keep intensity dimmer than starlight, feed it through a fiber optic with the lamp far away and heat-sunk, and aim past droplets, not through them. Water here is both window and door; a beam can shatter a meniscus with heat alone.

Your field kit (non-invasive, cold, quiet)
- Weight distribution: bog boards, non-squeak kneepads, a quiet mat.
- A small clear shield (still-air hood), edges padded to rest on moss/stone without cutting. Vent it laterally, not downward.
- A hair-thin, passively cooled borescope or fiberscope with a long working distance. Hydrophobic tip so it won’t wick a droplet onto it; rounded, polished; disinfected, then rinsed in sterilized site water; cooled to ambient. Never let the tip touch a droplet, hypha, moss hair, or root skin.
- Cold light via fiber optic; spectrum shifted to dim red or very cold, faint blue. Total illumination within the pocket under 0.1 lux. Pulse short, observe longer in darkness. Watch for condensation or twitching threads: that’s your “too much.”
- Tripod or arm that clamps to your board, not to the stone or root. No suction cups, no adhesives near the slit.
- Thermometer/hygrometer for outside the crack; never insert them.
- A notebook and soft pencil. Your phone on airplane mode and haptics off; its weight and heat stay on your board, not on the rock.

Decontamination and biosecurity
- Sterilize gear with UV or heat, not with solvents that outgas. After sterilization, rinse contact surfaces with filtered site-water so they don’t bring alien chemistry in.
- Do not bring living “gifts” (larvae, cultures, plant bits) from anywhere. Do not take samples out. The smallest spore from you could found an invasion; one of theirs could, too, elsewhere.
- Dedicate this kit to this pocket only. Never reuse it at another microland.

How to be present
- Be colder than your instincts. Keep your face, breath, and hands away. Let the hood do the nearest work.
- Move on a slow count. Between each adjustment, wait until droplets stop trembling and hyphae fall quiet. Your patience is part of your PPE.
- Speak not at all. Sound here is bone and string. Even a whisper is a shove.
- Don’t drum the ground, the board, or the stone, not even idly. They have a rule: never three taps on the main root—centipedes have learned that pattern means food. Your heartbeat through the board can be language to them; keep it steady.

What not to do (this is their law, adopt it as yours)
- Don’t break a bead’s skin. Every droplet is architecture and door. Touching one is vandalism and a flood.
- Don’t cut or lift hyphae, moss hairs, liverwort lobes, or root skin. They are beams, roads, lungs.
- Don’t widen voids. Your fingers and tools take too much. If you dislodge anything, you probably cannot repair it without causing more harm.
- Don’t introduce warmth. No hand warmers, no warm breath, no hot lenses, no electronics that get cozy near the slit.
- Don’t send vibration messages through the root. Even “hello” at your mass is an earthquake.

Seeing without touching
- Use existing droplets as lenses: angle dim light so a bead throws an image back to you. This is the least destructive “window.” Keep the beam cold and brief.
- Train your ear to hear through your board: ping of shell cisterns, the padded whispers of mites, the root’s small moans. You are eavesdropping on their city ordinances in sound.

On contact and “gifting”
- Assume residents exist and already have rules. Assume you are not invited.
- If you must signal existence, do it once, faintly, and not on the main root. A single, low, long, pressure-less hum through a long grass culm pressed to the rock, not the root, might read to them as “far thunder” rather than “predator.” Better yet, do not.
- Do not gift light, however tempting. Here, light is currency and covenant. Your LED, however cool, is technology from nowhere; it will warp their treaties.
- If you cannot resist leaving something, leave absence: shade. Briefly block an outside gleam to normalize their night if you accidentally brightened it. Then leave.

If something goes wrong
- If your tool breaks a droplet, do not try to “fix” it by bridging or blowing. Back away. Your air and touch are worse than the loss of one bead.
- If you dislodge a shard of stone or peat, do not jam it back. Weight and alignment are everything; you’ll shear more hyphae resetting it. Note it, record it, and stop.
- If a salamander snout dips, freeze as they do. You cause wakes they cannot control.
- If heavy rain begins or the weeping turns to running, pack up instantly. Their “market” and flood control depend on open sluices you might block with your presence.

Clothing and comfort for you
- Dress for damp cold and long stillness: layered, silent fabrics. Waterproof but non-crinkly outer layer. Quiet hat. You’ll be kneeling a long time.
- Bring a thermos, but open it far away. Steam is weather.
- Mosquito net for your head while you’re away from the slit; remove it before you approach. Repellent stays at home.

Fieldcraft and mindset
- Measure time in drips, not minutes. Let the tempo of the cistern teach you pacing.
- Let go of the urge to catalog or improve. You are not a city inspector, you are a distant moon.
- Record with drawings as much as photographs. Camera light is taxes they don’t owe you.
- Leave the site unmarked. No flags, no stones, no cairns. Annotate your own maps instead.

Afterward
- Decontaminate gear. Dry, then store sealed with a note: used in Root-Rock Pocket; do not cross-deploy.
- Log weather, ground feel, sounds, any distant brightening you caused. Your record helps you do less next time.

If you’re determined to attempt communication later (only after repeated, impact-free observations)
- Build a sealed, inert “message bead” from glass or sapphire, no larger than a springtail, perforated in a simple, redundant pattern that mirrors their leaf-skin script (holes, not ink), and pre-wet it. Cool it to ambient. Deliver it by placing it gently on bare rock ledge outside the main tension lines so it can be discovered without breaking a meniscus to reach it. Even this may be too much. If it is taken into use or pushed away without harm after a season, consider one follow-up. If it vanishes into a web, leave it be; you have been understood as “harmless enough to salvage.”

What to look for, humbly
- The law in sound: shell pings, web twangs, the root’s frost-groans.
- The politics of water: who owns a meniscus, how a droplet’s decision makes a street.
- The glowing grammar: fungal blue threaded where work needs saying.
- The hush when a salamander drinks: a civic reflex you should emulate.

A last reminder
- Darkness here is not an absence; it is governance. Tension is not fragility; it is law. You can’t be part of it; you can only avoid being weather. Prepare as if your presence is a storm and aim to be mistaken for a passing cloud that never broke.
