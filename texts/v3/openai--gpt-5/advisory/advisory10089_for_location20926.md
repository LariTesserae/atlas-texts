What this place will ask of you, before anything else
- To become deliberate. Here, every action has a weight and a number; nothing should be casual, nothing careless. You will be happiest if you move like you are already part of the infrastructure: quietly, exactly, and in balance.
- To accept sameness as a kind of luxury. Days rhyme; novelty is fine, but rhythm is the point. You will feel calmer if you learn the cadence rather than trying to introduce your own.
- To let the landscape set your scale. This city measures things in handspans, steps, and drops. Work small, think small, and repeat well.

What it feels like on arrival
- Altitude without drama. The air is cool and damp; you’ll likely notice your breath shorten and your voice sink. Nothing howls or gusts, but your lungs will know you’re high.
- The world is softened and sharpened at once: stone edges are exact; sound is hushed; colors are saturated; your footsteps, if they’re loud at first, will quickly feel rude.
- You’ll notice water before you see it: the ticking of drips, the glint on a net, the small satisfaction of a jar filled exactly to its line.

Acclimatization and approach
- Ascend gradually if you can. Treat it like a 2,500–4,000 m environment: sleep low, climb high, add a rest day after every 1,000 m of gain. If arriving by portal or other sudden means, plan a full day of minimal exertion on arrival.
- Watch for altitude illness: headache, loss of appetite, nausea, dizziness, breathlessness at rest. If symptoms worsen with rest, descend or seek a lower terrace; they will help you find a gentler shelf.
- Hydrate steadily but modestly. Overdrinking is as frowned upon as underdrinking. Sip; don’t chug.

Clothing and personal gear
- Fabrics: favor quiet, breathable layers. Wool and well-worn cotton-linen blends work best here—warm when damp, nonsqueaky, nonsynthetic shine. Leave crackly rain gear; mist is more common than rain.
- Warmth: midweight base layer, light sweater/fleece, windproof shell, hat, thin gloves. Nights are cool; humidity makes chill persistent.
- Footwear: soft-soled, grippy, quiet shoes or sandals with snug straps. Avoid lugged boots that chew edges or clack on stone. Bring a spare pair of dry socks; fungus loves steady damp.
- Sleep: a lightweight quilt or bag comfortably rated to 5–10°C, plus an insulating pad—stone pulls heat. A thin ground cloth keeps your bedding clean without blocking floor seams.
- Pack: nothing that dangles or jingles. Wrap buckles in cloth tape; silence zippers with tiny fabric pulls. If you use trekking poles, cap the tips with leather or rubber.
- Light: a headlamp with a warm, low setting and a red option. No strobes; cover the lamp with your palm around others.
- Water vessel: one small cup or flask with a narrow mouth and a clear, simple fill line marked. Don’t bring bladders that encourage thoughtless overfilling.
- Tools: a pocket knife you can open silently; a sewing kit; a small sharpening stone; beeswax; a few fine needles and awls. If you carry metal, pad it. Loud clinks are the local equivalent of shouting.
- Health: altitude meds if you use them, analgesic, anti‑nausea tabs, blister kit, antifungal cream, basic dressings. All unscented. Sunscreen for the rare bright hours.
- Electronics: plan to be mostly offline. Moisture is persistent; power is scarce; glare is impolite. If you must bring a device, use airplane mode, silence all sounds, dim the screen, and keep it pocketed.

Etiquette that matters
- Water is counted. Fill only to the marked line on communal jars. If a vessel has no line, stop when the wet darkening kisses the lip. If you take, you wait for the next three drips; if there’s a queue, place a small stone token at the base and step back.
- Do not meddle with the city’s nervous system. Don’t tug the fine fog nets or their bell-ropes. Don’t redirect a drip, even to be “helpful.” Don’t touch the sumps (the shallow listening pools); these are timepieces and civic altars.
- Walk on polished paths, not moss. When given a choice, put your foot where countless feet already have. If a stair has a rhythm, match it.
- Quiet is courtesy. Speak softly, laugh close, close things with a hand on them. Break any loud task into smaller, quieter parts.
- Present things in balance. Offer gifts in pairs or small even bundles. If you buy plums, don’t ask for one more “for free.” When you return something, return its number exactly; if you improved it, excuse the difference by carrying an old piece away.
- Trees are trimmed, not taken. If you are part of pruning, seal the cut faces with sap or ash mixture as they do. Do not peel bark ribbons.
- Animals: goats have right of way on stairs; condors are watched, not pointed at; ravens are not fed. Salamanders slide; you don’t.
- Breath-wells (spring shelters): remove sandals; approach slowly; drink only if invited; taste, don’t bathe.

The ledger of small fairnesses
- Expect to be given a cord, a tally stick, or a small notebook as your “ledger.” You will be asked to mark ins and outs: water taken, meals shared, help given, steps worn.
- Currency is attention and time. You will pay for your stay by matching numbers: an hour of net-mending for a bowl of fruit, a morning of wood-pegging for a corner by a hearth.
- If you break something, keep every piece; you’ll help sand and inlay the shards. Nothing should disappear; nothing should become too many.

What to bring as gifts
- Light, exact things that leave no debt:
  - A pair of very fine, quiet bells tuned to be heard only at arm’s length.
  - A little spool of strong undyed thread, two bone or brass needles, beeswax.
  - A small, well-made wooden ruler and a plumb line with a smooth river pebble (offer the pebble as part of your departure, not a permanent fixture).
  - Stories or games with steady measures: clapping patterns in fours and eights, shadow-chasing games for mid‑morning. Teach, then take the story away with you; leave only the pattern, which they already know how to hold.
  - Your hands, patient and unhurried.
- Avoid: plastics, bright synthetics, big machines, “efficiency” devices that increase take (bigger fog-catchers, pumps), flashlights with siren modes, single gifts that upset evenness.

Food and drink
- Expect: cold-sweet fruit grown in dew rings, herbs, ferns, sedges, goat’s milk and cheeses, broths, tubers coaxed from pans of soil, flatbreads when grain is traded in.
- Portions are modest and exact. If a plate holds eight plums, it holds eight; don’t pick one more. Accept what’s offered; finish it.
- If you bring food, decant it into cloth or quiet jars before arrival. No crinkly wrappers. Share as pairs: two handfuls of nuts, two slices of something sweet.
- Spice is plant resin and herb ash: resinous, fragrant, gentle. Avoid bringing overpowering spices or perfumes that overwrite the air’s own scent.

Work that finds you welcome
- Counting and calibration: helping tune a bell, shave a pot’s lip to true, check slopes of a channel.
- Care of things: combing moss from a gutter, drying nets, twisting fiber, oiling wooden pegs, burnishing ceramics (offer a forearm only if invited; it’s intimate work).
- Watchkeeping: listening at sumps to learn the day’s drip pattern; recording in ledgers; adjusting the afternoon’s trickle at a sluice.
- Teaching a knot, a stitch, a splice; mending worn straps; quiet craft that improves fit rather than increases force.

Safety and don’ts
- Edges are real. Arêtes can refuse a foot. If a ridge makes your stomach drop, don’t argue with it. Ask for a gentler path.
- Stone is young and crisp. Don’t pry at flakes; don’t test the “fresh” faces; they are meant to stay this way by agreement if not by nature.
- Moss is beautiful and treacherous. Step around the cushions; use smoothed treads; no running on green.
- Weather is steady but unforgiving. If you get damp and still, you will get cold. Change layers before you feel chilled.
- Insects hum but rarely bite; still, use unscented repellent sparingly if you must. Bright lights at night summon what the daytime forgets.
- Don’t optimize. Don’t suggest doubling the fog-frames or rerouting a gully “for efficiency.” The city’s arithmetic is deliberate; changing a number changes a lot.

Finding your way
- The city is defined by its clothesline and its causeways, not walls. If lost, drift toward the long colonnade of poles and hangings; that’s the spine of daily life.
- Terraces are discreet neighborhoods of function, not class: hearth-courts, gathering nets, orchard rings, plazas, craft shelves, cool storehouses burrowed into rock. Follow what you need by its sound: trickle for water, faint clink for craft, soft bleating for animal stairs.
- Bell language is gentle: a brisk sprinkle of notes means fog is plentiful; a sparse, slow count means rationing. If you hear nothing, go see—arm’s length is the intended range.

Sleeping and daily rhythm
- You’ll likely be offered a corner near a dish-shaped hearth, under a woven mat roof weighted with pebbles. These are rooms like open palms: partially sheltered, open to air. Keep your bedroll tidy; don’t spread into the pathway.
- Mornings are for tending: soothed fires, opened channels, checked nets. Mid‑morning is for games and exacting work; light is true, shadows clear.
- Afternoons are a gentle hum. The chimes along the clothesline show what’s drying and what’s being made that day.
- Evenings are close and quiet; coals glow instead of flames. Stories reside in the whispering untie of red-barked leaves and the slow count of the drip.

Animals you’ll share space with
- Goats: stairwise citizens, softly insistent. Step aside; let the hundred hooves pass like rain that chose to delay.
- Ravens: wet-iron and curious. They watch themselves in your metal. Secure shiny things; better yet, pad them so nothing invites mischief.
- Salamanders: glossy, dignified, propped like commas on warm stone ramps. Admire; don’t touch.
- Hummingbirds: they write in the margins of the cloud. If you wear hot colors, expect attention you cannot satisfy—don’t.
- Condors: timekeepers with wings. People will glance, not point; you should too. If you feel the urge to cheer when one loops its loop, let the smile stay inside.

Waste and leaving no remainder
- Pack out everything you brought that the mountain cannot turn into moss or shard. If you break pottery (yours or theirs), sand and inlay the remains on a floor; leave a visible memory rather than a hidden absence.
- Human waste: there are designated latrines; they are part of the water arithmetic. Use only those; add the correct dried plant matter afterward; note your use in your ledger if that’s the custom where you stay.
- On departure, settle your ledger. If you ended with more in your favor, spend the credit on tasks no one asked you to do but everyone will notice: oiling a peg line, mending a tear, smoothing a nick from a stair. If you ended with a small debt, it is better to carry it out with you than to try to erase it by piling on gifts.

If you bring a camera or notebook
- Ask before you capture the sumps, breath-wells, and work patterns. Some patterns are considered common knowledge; others are guarded, not out of secrecy but to protect their number from becoming too many elsewhere.
- Switch off sounds. No shutter clicks, no beeps. Condense your notes into small, tidy pages; don’t fan out sheaves on public stone.
- Fog eats batteries. Keep devices bagged with a small desiccant, but don’t leave desiccant behind; it alters this place in ways you can’t see.

If you plan to stay longer
- Expect the mountain to teach you your step. You’ll find yourself reproducing the same riser height without measuring; you’ll begin to reach for cups that fit a particular drop’s curve. Let it happen.
- Learn one local count thoroughly—the bell count for fog, the stair’s cadence, or the market’s plum numbers—and keep it flawlessly. You will be trusted with more when you’ve shown you can keep a number.
- Acquire quiet habits: wrapping metal in fiber, warming stones before setting them as plugs, turning jars so their lips rest in shade, trimming resin with a soft hand.

Leaving well
- Plan your goodbye a day early. The city prefers goodbyes that don’t rush the arithmetic: finish the task you started, balance the bowl you borrowed, return the step you wore by tending another.
- Say thanks in the local grammar: an even bundle of herbs for your host, a fingertip touch to the stone lip where you filled your cup, a last slow listen at the sumps.
- As you go, keep your eyes soft. If the condor writes its loop as you leave, take it as a blessing only if you already counted on it not to be for you.

A final note on mindset
- You will be tempted to square this place with places you know—high deserts, cloud forests, ridge cities. Better to let it teach you its own arithmetic. Here, erosion and growth tally to zero on purpose, and people are proud to be part of the sum. If you can match their modesty and consistency, you will be welcomed; if you can learn to enjoy a drop that falls precisely when it should, you will understand why they keep counting.
