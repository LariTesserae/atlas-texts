Orientation: what you are going into
- A very deep, very old, very quiet oceanic basin that has been shaped by hands and then by centuries of water. It is cold, dark, and crushingly pressurized. The whole place is built—terraces, channels, vanes, cisterns—now thickly mantled with slow-growing mats and calcifying plantwork that live on chemical gradients rather than light. There are no animals here. Time moves in centuries. Your noise, wake, heat, and breath are the most disruptive things present.

Baseline constraints you must accept
- Pressure: abyssal/hedal. You will not “dive” this in any human sense. You go inside a pressure-stable craft or suit and treat the ocean as stone.
- Cold: near-freezing, constant, conductive. Your own heat is a pollutant here; manage it.
- Dark: almost total. Vision is a courtesy, not a guarantee.
- Slowness: currents, collapses, changes—everything is patient. You must match that patience.
- Fragility without brittleness: mats will hold until they don’t; they slide more than snap; they remember disturbances for a very long time.
- Chemistry: low oxygen microzones, likely H2S, Fe2+/Fe3+ cycling, CO2 and methane seeps. Your sensors keep you safe; your nose won’t help you here.

Civic/ethical stance
- Treat this as a sanctuary and a historical site. It was made, and then remade by life. Your obligation is noninterference:
  - Take nothing; leave not even a wake.
  - No anchors into fabric. No pruning. No “just a sample.”
  - Do not introduce animals, spores, oils, or light and heat in doses that force adaptation.
  - If you document, do so with reversible beacons and gentle instruments.
  - If you must touch, touch stone already bare, never the living seam.

Training to do before you go
- Neutral-buoyancy body control and micro-thrust piloting in silt-out simulators; practice making and holding stillness.
- Sensory deprivation acclimatization: low-light, low-sound sessions to discover how your mind behaves in near-zero stimulus.
- Cold discipline: operate hands/tools in a cold room with reduced dexterity; practice not rushing.
- Line work: guideline deployment and retrieval without tangling; one-handed problem solving.
- Slow navigation: moving by acoustic landmarks and touch rather than sight.
- Emergency drills: entanglement, thruster failure, silt-out stop protocol, gas-pocket encounter, and lost-in-maze return on breadcrumb beacons.

Platform choice: how to be there
- Best: a one- or two-person atmospheric micro-sub with ultraquiet, low-cavitation thrusters and good station-keeping. Stay inside. Treat EVA as optional, rare, and short.
- If EVA is essential: an abyssal-rated atmospheric diving suit (ADS), neutrally buoyant, with:
  - Heat-balanced underlayer and limb-specific heating (hands, feet) to avoid dumping unnecessary warmth.
  - Soft-edged, non-snag geometry; joints shielded against grit; anti-scale coatings.
  - Force-feedback gloves with haptic pads for reading texture; no sharp tools on the exterior.
  - Micro-thrusters that can pulse below 0.1 m/s, vectored to shed wash up and away from surfaces.
  - Integrated guideline reel with low-tension payout and tactile markers every meter.

Life support and power
- Keep yourself at 1 atm. This avoids biological decompression and simplifies emergencies.
- Redundant O2 supply and CO2 scrubbing with independent monitoring.
- Battery packs kept within thermal envelopes; cold saps output—oversize by at least 50%.
- Strictly closed-loop waste; no venting. Condensate captured and stored.
- Thermal management that keeps your skin warm while limiting hull heat plume. Think of your craft as a thermos, not a radiator.

Sensing and lighting
- Passive first: wideband hydrophone array. Learn the hush, the hum of domes, the change in sibilance at vanes.
- Imaging sonar (chirped, low-intensity), short-range in front and to the sides; keep gains low to avoid stirring silt with your own acoustic field.
- Doppler current profiler in micro range to read shears across terraces.
- Chem suite: O2, H2S, CH4, CO2, pH, Eh (redox), Fe2+/Fe3+, temp, salinity. Alarm on H2S spikes and negative redox pockets in confined spaces.
- Cameras: high-ISO, no strobes. Use dim, narrowband blue‑green for distance; very low-power amber for close work. Keep beams tight and brief. Let bioluminescence be your companion when it offers.
- Tactile: a soft contact wand on a retractable arm to “read” surfaces without scraping—feel grooves, reed impressions, wedge marks.

Navigation and comms
- Dead reckoning plus:
  - A pocket constellation of pea-sized acoustic breadcrumbs you can place and later retrieve. Each whispers a unique ID at long intervals; biodegradable housings; retrieval strongly preferred.
  - Inertial nav with periodic fixes on your breadcrumbs.
- Lines: in close work, always run a guideline. Never trust memory in a repeating maze.
- Comms: low-power acoustics with short bursts; minimize chatter. Agree on silence windows to let the place speak.

Approach, anchoring, and rest
- Approach with the current, not against. Pulse, don’t push. Watch your wake against a beam; if you lift silt, you’re moving too much water.
- Do not anchor into anything. If you must pause:
  - Use soft suction pads on already bare stone, or brace gently in preexisting dowel holes without expansion.
  - Keep at least a meter off any living seam or cushion.
- A “camp” is a driftless hover in a cul‑de‑sac of flow; never a set-down on vegetation.

Movement doctrine
- Nothing goes fast. Your model animal is a satellite, not a fish.
- Hold neutral, trim, and quiet. Micro‑thrust, then wait. Let the water rewrite after your passage before moving again.
- Keep your vertical profile high in channels to avoid scouring beds; if a ceiling lowers, back out—don’t squeeze.
- Hands to stone only; stone that is already nude. If you brush a mat, stop. Watch it. Apologize by stillness.
- Avoid culverts and low-lintel passages unless you have a line, a size check, and a second way out. Gas pockets love roofs.
- If you cloud the water: stop entirely. Lights down. Wait for the texture of the hush to return. Then backtrack on your line.

What to look for, and how
- Read the method in the material: wedge-marks, reed-pattern ghosts in plaster, rope-groove polish. Use your haptic pad; close your eyes and let texture tell you the sentence.
- Listen for architecture: the subtle hum of domes, the changed sibilance near vanes, the rare fizz of a gas veil unzipping upward. Log times; the place keeps a schedule you can learn.
- Watch edges where regimes meet: the lip of a cistern that glows faintly, a seam that breathes more in warmer layers. If chemistry is doing something gentle and regular, you’re at a hearth of this world.
- Learn warmth: a few centimeters of gentler water under an overhang can reset stiff hands. Do not hover there long; you are a heat source too.

Season and “weather”
- Expect long, slow layer shifts: colder brine slipping in makes edges sharper and flow clearer; older, slightly warmer water blurs and fattens the breathing mats. Plan two passes hours apart to feel the difference through your sensors and skin.

Decontamination and biosecurity
- Before: clean-room prep. External surfaces sterilized (UV, peroxide rinse, then freshwater, then dry). Filters at 0.2 μm for any venting atmosphere inside the craft when doors open. No fabrics that shed; no Velcro outside.
- After: complete external rinse in capture bay; catalog any hitchhiking growth; nothing goes to a natural sea elsewhere. Suits and sub undergo low-temp, long-duration sterilization that spares the craft and does not aerosolize.

Sampling and souveniring
- Don’t. If you must for science, restrict to fallen fragments not in contact with living mats, with micro-core tools that shave milligrams. Seal immediately. Log position. Request of the place, and repay by data you share back to its stewards.

Emergencies: simple rules you will remember
- Silt-out: stop. Lights low. Orient by line or by gravity (bubble level in suit). Wait until your sonar regains edges. Backtrack, don’t press forward on guesswork.
- Entanglement: stop movement everywhere; kill thrusters. Assess with cameras. Retract limbs to minimum profile. Use the soft cutter only on your own line if necessary; never cut the place.
- Gas upwelling: sensors alarm or you see silver streamers. Back up and up-current slowly. Do not inhale outside air (you won’t, you’re sealed). Avoid domed spaces; they trap.
- Mechanical failure: you carry redundancy and a retrieval tether to the sub or to a hover buoy. Call it. Accept that you’re done for the day.
- Lost: drop a breadcrumb, hold station, listen. Then methodically ladder back along your breadcrumbs. Do not guess; the maze will defeat confidence.

Itinerary template for a first visit (conservative)
- Descent: 2–4 hours in micro-sub. Minimal talk. Start listening early; let your nervous system reset to hush.
- Perimeter drift: 90 minutes skirting a wall-and-channel complex; no contact; map acoustically; place two breadcrumbs at the “door” you’ll use later.
- Stillness practice: 20 minutes hovering over a bare tessellated patch; practice zero-wake holds; observe mineral snow.
- Close reading: 45 minutes at a brick face with reed-memory plaster; use haptics; document marks; one brief light sweep.
- Vane pass: 30 minutes crossing the lee of a large stone vane; listen to how it edits current; log shear with your Doppler.
- Pause at a cistern lip: lights off; wait for faint glow if present; record chem gradients without dipping probes into living seams.
- Return on breadcrumbs; retrieve them. Ascent as silent as descent.

What to bring that isn’t equipment
- A bias toward apology. Slowness as a bodily skill. A promise to keep the vow you touch.
- A notebook for after, when words return. You will want to name patterns you felt more than saw.
- One silent ritual for beginning and ending: a way to say hello and to let go.

How you will know you did it right
- Your wake never curled a cushion. You left no new brightness or warmth that wasn’t there. Your recordings let someone else learn the sounds without you being present. You retrieved your breadcrumbs. You can point, on a map only you can see, to the place where the hush changed a little, and you can say why.

Final reminders
- You do not make speed down there; you make shape with your presence. Make as little of it as you can.
- If you feel urgency, it is yours, not the place’s. Let it pass before you move.
- This is the inside of a long-kept vow. Match it with one of your own: touch lightly, listen long, and leave the world able to forget you were there.
