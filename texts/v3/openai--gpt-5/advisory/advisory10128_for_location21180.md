What to expect
- High, cold, wet dark. The air is thin and constantly near freezing, saturated with microdroplets and rime. Hypothermia sneaks up through condensation, not rainfall. Your breath will ice your own mask if you don’t manage it.
- Light is mostly useless and culturally rude; sound and touch are the working senses. The built world is acoustically tuned; echoes, hums, and the pitch of thresholds do what signage and streetlamps would in other places.
- Water is everything: obsession, infrastructure, etiquette, currency. Never interrupt a drip, weep, or fog path, even by accident.
- The city is carved into a new, sharp cliff-edge. Exteriors are nonreflective and thermally discreet; inside are long galleries, ribs, shafts, and narrow civic skin where rock, harvesters, fauna, and people meet.
- Life is abundant and slow. Small arthropods, leeches, snails, mats; giant millipeds and bivalve keepers. They navigate by pressure and sound, not sight. Stillness invites colonization.

Before you go: training and acclimatization
- Altitude prep: If you’re sea-level based, acclimatize for 2–3 days somewhere high and damp. Practice light exertion in cold, saturated air. If you’re prone to AMS, bring meds and climb slowly on approach.
- Cold-wet discipline: Drill dressing/undressing in tight quarters without wetting base layers. Practice staying warm by motion, not heat dumps.
- Sound etiquette: Learn three visitor chords and five threshold notes from a local liaison or guide service:
  - Two-stamp perimeter courtesy (alerts ledge fauna to part).
  - Low hum for cleaning colonies to stay put.
  - Warding buzz for intake guardians.
  - Your lodging’s “vowel” note.
  - The silence-pass technique (brief, shaped hush) for sound-sensitive halls.
- Micro-movement habit: Train yourself to sway slightly at rest and hum or breathe audibly through the mask. Stillness equals “available substrate” to many organisms here.
- Low-vision confidence: Practice navigating by handrail, tactile markers, and passive listening. If you need a light, learn to shroud and occlude it to keep beams off shared spaces.

What to pack
- Face and breathing
  - Closed-circuit or semi-closed rebreather mask with heat/moisture exchanger and anti-biofilm internals. Hydrophilic trap for exhale; drip-back connection if your lodging has a port.
  - Nose/ear passband set: adjustable active earplugs that pass local civic bands and block dangerous infrasound; pair with a bone-conduction guide to play visitor chords without masking the city.
- Clothing
  - Layered, nonreflective, acoustically damp kit: hydrophilic base (pulls condensate off skin), hydrophobic mid, and microtextured dark outer that sheds film and doesn’t squeak.
  - Gloves with high-grip tactile pads; one fingertip bare or conductive for reading tactile signage if permitted.
  - Hood and neck gaiter that don’t rustle; no dangling straps that can wick water into places you don’t want it.
- Feet
  - Soft-soled boots with fine ridging and self-vibrating insole (low-amplitude deterrent) tuned to the approved bands. High, sealed gaiters. No clicky heels. Quick-drain welt that doesn’t leave drops behind.
- Heat management
  - Personal heat is private. Bring a body-worn phase-change vest for brief comfort, plus a rock-coupled “phonon sink” or local heat-bleed clamp you press to bedrock to offload heat silently. Do not radiate heat into open air.
- Light and navigation
  - If you must bring light, make it a dim, warm, fully hooded task lamp for inside your clothing or a covered palm glow for reading your own hands. Outside, rely on a haptic map band and passive listening.
  - Tuning forks or a compact transducer that produces the sanctioned chords at low amplitude. Never improvise tones.
- Hygiene and bio-interface
  - Saline squeeze bottle and soft looped tool for leech removal (slide, don’t yank). Sterile gauze; no caustic chemicals that might wash into sumps.
  - Anti-biofilm wipes safe for local systems (buy locally). Ultrasonic travel brush for decolonizing boots and seams on reentry to lodging.
- Shelter and sleep
  - Sleep sling or net rated for damp cold, with micro-vibration mode to discourage settling fauna. Vapor barrier liner for your bag; nothing down-filled.
- Food and water
  - High-calorie sealed rations that don’t crumble. Water rights are purchased; do not harvest fog on your own. Carry a small sterile bottle only for mask-system maintenance.
- Power and tools
  - Conformal, sealed batteries; no loud fans. Soft tools. Non-sparking. Nothing that emits strong EM or heat plumes. Mechanical backup for anything critical.
- Paperwork and trade
  - Water-credit token or local account. Your visitor chord license/tag. Quarantine kit for exit (bag and tags for hitchhikers to be returned, not killed).

On arrival: first steps
- Report to the civic skin gatehouse. Exchange bright gear for local-issue black outers, have your ear passbands set, and get your route’s chord map.
- Learn your lodging vowel by feel in your sternum. Hum it once inside, never outside.
- Pay an initial water allotment. They’ll connect your mask’s trap to the return if appropriate.
- Hire a guide for your first day. The place is legible by sound once you know what you’re hearing.

How to move and behave
- Always be a little in motion. Sway, shoulder-roll, finger-tap against your sleeve. Keep a low, steady breath-hum in public spaces.
- Threshold etiquette:
  - Stamp twice before stepping onto a ledge or into the civic skin.
  - At marked lay-lines (you’ll feel a faint tremble underfoot and a buzz in the teeth), step over cleanly; don’t pause on a boundary.
  - Doorways have pitch. Match or hush as indicated by the tactile plaque.
- Don’t bring flame. Ever. Heat is moved into stone, not burned. If you’re cold, move, or clamp your heat sink to bedrock in a private nook.
- Keep your silhouette narrow. Packs and shoulders can divert weeps—choose routes that don’t place you under active drip-work. If you do interrupt a film, back out along your inbound track; don’t try to fix it by hand.
- Light discipline. Keep your task lamp hidden in clothing or a hood. Never sweep beams across the civic skin or fauna. Light doesn’t help you and offends locals.
- Rails and spines are for hands, not perching. When big millipeds pass, brace and let the rail quiver; don’t add your weight to living infrastructure.
- Food discipline. No crumbs, no open aromas. Eat in designated hum-rooms where air fields keep droplets poised. Seal waste tight and hand it to your host; it’s a resource here.

Reading the environment
- Sound maps. Corridors that brighten your whisper are arterial; ones that fold sound flat are for quiet passage.
- Infrasonic throb means a deep shaft; stay to the inside curve and don’t vocalize—your note can stack with the shaft’s and make people ill.
- Water talks. A fast, even gutter-song is healthy; erratic, high plinks near a smooth plane suggest a new path forming—give it space.
- Black-on-black isn’t blank: tactile stripes, grain, and micro-whiskers under your palm tell you what an exterior does. If it feels “furred,” don’t lean; it’s harvesting.

Dealing with fauna
- Small grazers: they’ll comb your boots like you’re another rock. Keep moving; a soft sleeve-hum and sole vibration usually discourage settling. If they layer, step into a cleaning corridor or brush them off over a collector screen. Don’t flick them into open walkways.
- Transparent leeches: you’ll feel a tiny, cool weight. Slide a saline-wet fingertip or blunt tool under the anterior end and stroke them into a vial. Your host will return them to an intake. Tearing leaves mouthparts; don’t.
- Snails with glass hoods: avoid stepping on them; they ring faintly when they bump. Use the two-stamp and wait; they part.
- Keepers (the big bivalves): give the cliff lip a meter. If a keeper begins to close, hold position; don’t try to squeeze past. Their cycles are minutes to hours—plan your pace accordingly.
- Intake guardians: if a low, steady buzz meets you, you’re near protected flow. Hum the warding note; if you don’t know it, turn back and ask a local. Do not test frequencies.

Health and safety
- Hypothermia: the enemy is wet-cold, not just cold. Change any soaked base layer immediately in a dry room. Warm by motion first, heat clamp second.
- Ears: long infrasound exposure can nauseate you. Use your passband plugs in shaft districts. If your bones feel “too big” or “too small,” you’re in the wrong band—step away and retune.
- Skin: treat micro-abrasions promptly; this is a microbe-forward ecosystem. Rinse with clean water, dry, and seal.
- Foot care: inspect feet at every stop; prune macerated skin, powder sparingly, and air-dry in hum-rooms that pull moisture out of fabric.
- Altitude: hydrate according to your water allotment, not your sea-level habit. Overdrinking wastes currency; underdrinking worsens AMS. Pace yourself.
- Panic moments: auroral events can set every carapace buzzing at once. It feels like the world is shouting into your bones. Step into a marked refuge or wedge a palm to rock, bow your head, and match the visitor hum. It passes.

What not to do
- Don’t dam a weep with your body, pack, or gear.
- Don’t improvise notes, whistles, or sonar. The city runs on sound.
- Don’t radiate heat into open air. Share heat with stone only, privately.
- Don’t try to see. Touch maps; sound thinks. Visible light won’t tell you what matters here.
- Don’t bring oils, repellents, or cleaners not certified locally. They end up in sumps and farms.
- Don’t harvest fog, scrape mats, or pocket fauna. Everything has a job already.

How to sleep
- Use a sling or net. Clip in at three points; check for drip-patterns above you. Engage low micro-vibration and the room’s approved hush field. Hum your lodging vowel once, then go quiet. Mask on or exhale into your bag’s trap; don’t fog the room.

Paying and trading
- You pay for pressure-managed water and hum-rights as part of lodging. If you need extra heat-sink time or a private hum-room, arrange it with your host.
- Sound is “territory.” Your visitor chord tag tells the city who’s singing; keep it on. It’s not language so much as identity and permission.

Photography and souvenirs
- Photos will mostly be black and condensation. If you capture sound, ask before releasing it elsewhere; long-range chords are civic signatures, not ambience. Don’t collect anything physical.

Leaving well
- Decon your gear in the gatehouse: ultrasonic brush, saline rinse, and a minute in the visitor hum-field to encourage hitchhikers to step off. Return any collected fauna vials. Settle your water account.
- Dry your kit fully before you step into a bright, warm world; latent biofilm is a gift you don’t want to spread across universes.

If you get lost
- Stop adding sound; use the silence-pass technique and put your palm to any exterior plane. Tactile signage will guide you to the nearest attended threshold. If the surface “sings” under your teeth with a fine, structured beat, you’re near a reservoir—follow the downtick to a service room and wait. Stamp twice every few minutes so the corridor knows you’re there.

- Sleep sling/net with micro-vibration; vapor barrier

- Sealed power; soft tools; no open flame; no loud fans
- Guide booked for day one; chord map memorized; hums practiced

Mindset
- Patience over spectacle. You won’t “see” the city; you’ll learn it by sternum and soles.
- Politeness is physics-aware: move so water can keep choosing its paths, and sing only what the cliff already knows how to carry.
- If you wait long enough, nothing dramatic happens. That’s the point. You’re visiting a place that learned to be a landscape, and your job is to be a visitor who learns to be almost-stone.
