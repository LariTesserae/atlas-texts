What you’re walking into is a living acoustic machine that just happens to be a cave. Assume vision is a courtesy at best and plan to survive by touch, balance, breath, and sound.

Big picture
- This is an immature collapse-karst underworld: sharp, fresh rock; unstable floors; intermittent, brackish water; pressure-driven “weather”; enormous bat biomass; dense fungi; insects and crustaceans by the thousands; predators you’ll rarely see.
- The world runs on sound. Anything you do acoustically is as visible here as a flare would be in a night meadow. Your footsteps, your breathing, the pitch of your gear zippers—these all mean something to the locals.
- It floods sideways and upward. River bores can arrive out of apparent stillness. Tides lift and shuffle the air twice daily. Ceilings sometimes decide to keep falling.

How long to go and when
- Keep it short and mobile. First forays: 6–10 hours door to door, no bivouac. Don’t plan to build or leave anything standing.
- Time your entry so you’re on higher ground before both daily tidal pulses. Expect a cool gust before the lift and a saline tang after. Keep a cushion: be in safe zones at least 45 minutes before predicted lift; remain 45 minutes after the fall.
- Avoid going right after major surface storms or earthquakes. Aftershocks cascade here as delayed ceiling failures days to weeks later.

Skills to practice before you go
- Silent movement and foot placement on loose, sharp, wet surfaces. Train on talus, scree, and algae-slick rock in the dark.
- Low-light proprioception: moving with your headlamp off for short periods using a pole and your breath to map space.
- Tongue-click echolocation basics and “clap-lag” chamber sizing. Learn how delay and timbre change with volume and distance.
- Handline travel in a tight file, speaking little, using touch-cues and short patterned taps to communicate.
- Calm apnea breathing. You will need to pause and listen often without panting into your mask.
- Throwing pebbles purposefully to sound depth and trigger surface films without stepping in.

Personal baseline
- If you have asthma, chronic lung disease, heart rhythm issues, or are immunocompromised, do not go. The spore load and CO2 swings can be unforgiving.
- Update your tetanus shot. Trim nails short. Cover and seal any skin breaks 24 hours prior.

Core gear (keep it simple, rugged, quiet)
- Head and eyes
  - Two waterproof headlamps (IP68) with low-lumen, warm/amber modes and a soft diffuser. Keep beams down and close-in; don’t sweep ceilings.
  - One tiny finger lamp or tritium bead for kit checks without waking the world.
  - Spare batteries in vacuum-sealed bags. Salt air and condensation eat contacts.
- Ears and comms
  - Musician-grade earplugs (12–20 dB flat attenuation), lanyarded, so you can reduce sheer volume without losing frequency detail.
  - Bone-conduction mic/earpiece with passthrough and push-to-talk to a partner within 10–20 meters. Keep radio output minimal; voice quietly when you must.
  - Small hand drumstick or rubber-tipped baton for controlled, low-volume taps on rock. No metal-on-crystal.
- Breathing and bio
  - P100 or P3 half-mask respirator with exhale valve and low breathing resistance. Bring a spare filter in a drybag.
  - Compact 4-gas meter (O2/CO2/H2S/CO) with data log. Wear it high on your torso; watch for dips in O2 or surges in CO2 near lows and during bores.
- Touch and movement
  - Cut-resistant, quick-drain clothing: lightweight long sleeves and pants, abrasion-rated. No Velcro if you can help it (it’s loud).
  - Soft, close-fitting gloves with grippy palms and cut protection; a thin nitrile liner under them if you’ll touch bio-slick.
  - Knee and elbow pads with quiet shells (rubberized or fabric, not hard plastic).
  - Footwear: sticky rubber approach shoes or canyoning boots with siped soles; midweight soles to blunt chert knives; neoprene socks for warmth and leech barrier; snug gravel gaiters.
  - A collapsible probing pole (1.2–1.6 m), dull tip, for films and depths. Wrap it with a soft tape in sections you can flick for controlled clicks.
- Protection from the world and yourself
  - Climbing/caving helmet with chin strap; low profile; foam that won’t waterlog.
  - Slim CO2 inflatable belt PFD if you will be within reach of bore channels. Keep it leash-tethered so it doesn’t auto-inflate from spray.
  - Minimal vertical kit only if absolutely required: static rope with robust edge protection, wide slings for natural horns, and multiple-point anchors. The rock is young and untrustworthy—spread the load, pad every edge.
- Navigation and sensing
  - Wrist altimeter/barometer; log pressure micro-changes. A sudden, smooth pressure dip or rise can herald tide-linked drafts; a sharp gust can precede a bore.
  - Simple IMU-based dead-reckoning logger; a pencil and waterproof notebook for time-stamped echo notes (“3-sec fat clap return; left wall sings at G; sand crunch underfoot”).
  - Pebble pouch with sorted sizes. You’ll throw these more than you think.
  - Small mirror and signal whistle. Only for last-resort rescue; whistle will carry a long way here.
- Water, food, heat
  - Bring your own water. Nothing here is reliably potable—salts, spores, biofilms. Carry 2–3 L per person for a day run in flexible bladders. Electrolytes, not just plain water.
  - Dense, quiet food: soft bars, gels, nut pastes. No crinkly wrappers.
  - Light emergency warmth: thin reflective bivy and a dry synthetic midlayer in a roll-top drybag. The air is warm, but prolonged stillness when wet can chill you.
- Medical and repair
  - Small kit: pressure bandage, hemostatic gauze, steri-strips, tweezers, leech salt/vinegar ampule, antihistamine, inhaler if prescribed, broad-spectrum antibiotic ointment, blister kit.
  - Tape, zip ties, small epoxy putty, cord. Moisture eats plans; redundancy keeps you moving.
- Bags
  - One close-fitting, drainable pack with external lashing and internal drybags. Keep the center of mass glued to your back.

How to move through this place
- Stay narrow and low. The ceiling’s story changes every hour. Helmets are not optional.
- Read the floor with your shins and pole before your feet do anything heroic. Tap surfaces ahead lightly; listen for film, hollows, or the rubbery whisper of mats.
- Avoid bowls and fresh-bright rubble where possible. Those islands of chalky brightness are newborn and slide under body weight.
- Treat any channel with smoothed, scalloped rock as a floodway. If you can’t cross that zone to high ground in three running seconds, don’t enter it within two hours of tide.
- Move on texture that feels “held”: old crust, consolidated sand, ribbed algae that doesn’t shear at a touch.
- Pause often. Go quiet. Count your breath to 20 and listen: are the isopods still scouring? Do the crickets maintain antiphony? Has the low bat chord thickened? Absences telegraph predators and bores.
- Never stand under overhanging “teeth” when you hear fine chimes or long crystalline notes rising. Crystal whiskers that sing can also shed. Back away without speaking.

Sound discipline and navigation
- Keep speech to whispers. Your words are flares here.
- Agree on a tiny set of tap codes with your team (example: one dull tap = halt; two = come; three = retreat; slow roll = danger overhead; rapid doublet = water rising). Keep them quiet and consistent in rhythm but varied in substrate so they don’t mimic local calls.
- Map by ear. Clap small. Track delay and color: thin, fast returns = tight chute; fat, late booms = big room and possible multiple exits; flutter echoes = ribs and pillars. Make notes you can later triangulate with time and pace count.
- Use pebbles to sound pools. Toss a single, then a chain, listening for the depth gradient and whether the surface film “answers” (a tight, tacky slurp). If the film speaks back, don’t step—leeches here coordinate.
- Don’t imitate local music. Be careful with steady pitches against crystal seams. Some fungi and vines respond to specific notes; you don’t know which ones and you can’t take it back.

Reading the weather
- Tide: feel for a cool, dryer push at shin height and a smoothing of the bat chord. Within 10–20 minutes, expect higher humidity and saline on the lips. Be above all active channels during this window.
- Bore: an off-beat wallop or a sudden silence of millipede scrape can come a minute or less before water. If in doubt, scramble up any available rib—height wins.
- Rain inside: sudden fat drops out of stone during warm fronts. Shelves become skating rinks. Find friction, widen your stance, and move deliberately or wait it out.
- Quake-storms: after a distant fall, air shivers and the dust wind tastes metallic. Do not investigate the new “room.” The system will be unstable for hours to days.

Wildlife etiquette and self-preservation
- Bats have air right-of-way. Duck, make yourself small; let the dark wind pass.
- Avoid standing under dense bat flows. Guano is slippery and the spore load is worst there. Mask up well when you must cross.
- Crabs drum in numbers when pressure shifts. That thunder is a marker: get off marginal ledges; bores often follow.
- Eels like damp ledges and ropes that look like dinner. Keep ropes dry and off the floor; don’t dangle loose loops.
- Blind birds nest in drafts. If you feel sustained cool lift and a soft, fibrous smell, skirt wide; they will defend by sound and mess with your navigation.
- If the small things vanish at once, you should too. Retreat to last known safe high ground and wait three minutes. Let the world repopulate before proceeding.

Food and water
- Do not drink from pools without serious treatment. Even then, salts and organics make it barely worth it. Bring your own and ration by activity, not time.
- Eat on rock you’ve checked is old, dry-ish, and out of flow paths. Leave nothing that can be consumed; in this place, it will be, by the wrong things.

Camp and rest
- Don’t build. Rest in brief spells on high ribs or old slabs you’ve tested. Backpacks become your chairs; helmets your headrest.
- If forced to stay, hang a minimalist hammock from three separate points, all padded, with a knife within reach for an instant dump if an anchor goes. Keep your pack leashed to you.

Hazards you’ll meet and how to answer them
- Knife-rock and fresh chert: keep moving with intent; don’t slide; check gloves often; tape cuts immediately.
- Biofilms: felt soles are great but carry spores; use siped rubber and attention. If you shear a mat, stop before you make a chute.
- Leeches: if they coordinate on your boot, use salt or vinegar, then rinse; don’t rip. Check socks at each halt.
- CO2 pockets: if your meter chirps low O2 or high CO2, drop your breathing rate, backtrack a body-length high, then laterally. Don’t run.
- Sudden darkness from lamp failure: stop moving; breathe; count to 60; switch to backup; do not lash out or wave your light around—things notice sweeps.
- Rope snag cascades: never drag coils; pack line tight; manage tails; keep one cutting tool per person accessible with either hand.

Ethics and impact
- This system is young. Everything is still deciding what it will be. Touch lightly.
- Don’t steal or rearrange objects that obviously “belong” to someone else’s method—pebble chains over pools, rib bones used as pendulums, curated shell piles. That’s not decoration—those are tools.
- Mark your passage in ways that will fade: time-stamped notes, recorded echo signatures, footprints in sand that the next bore will erase. If you must leave a message for safety, build a small, distinct pebble cairn with a sound signature that only your team knows, and dismantle it on return.

Abort criteria
- Your gas meter trends the wrong way for more than five minutes despite moving to higher ground.
- Tide cue arrives earlier than predicted and you’re not yet off a flood path.
- You hear crystalline singing intensifying overhead; dust wind picks up.
- A team member loses their calm in the dark. Panic spreads faster than water here.
- You realize you’ve begun to depend on your lamp. This world will teach you by taking it away.

Psychological prep
- Expect to feel watched by sound. You are. It’s not hostile; it’s simply the rule set.
- You’ll get ghost-edges in your periphery and the sense of huge things moving. Trust your instruments and your pauses, not your imagination.
- Plan deliberate “listening halts.” They’ll become your map. Measure time by those, not minutes.

What success looks like
- You go in small, quiet, and light. You come out with clean notes, no trace, and a head full of the system’s grammar: which textures mean “old,” which pitches mean “big,” how the tide breathes in stone.
- On later visits, you’ll move more by ear than by lamp, and you’ll know you belong there when the crabs stop drumming as you pass because you’ve learned to keep your sound below the threshold that matters.

If you remember nothing else
- Up and out beats through and across.
- Listen before you step; tap before you trust.
- Your loudest tool is your silence.
