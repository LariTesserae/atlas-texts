What you’re walking into
- Think of a city that is more cliff than city. Height is the ground. Air is thin, dry, and chemically odd; sound dies young; wind and gravity have moods. Edges are not just geometry but behavior. Distance is honest yet elastic. Light accumulates, heat forgets, names have mass. Living things eat gradients.

Prepare your body and head first
- Altitude and dryness: Train for hypoxia. If you can, spend a week at 3,000–4,000 m equivalent or use a hypoxia tent. Learn your symptoms of AMS/HAPE/HACE. Ask your doctor about acetazolamide for ascent and carry dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergencies.
- Balance and commitment: Practice decisive movement. Slackline, via ferrata, scrambling on exposed terrain, and “commit-step” drills (look once, set your line, then step without pausing). Learn to keep three points of contact without dithering.
- Breath discipline: Box breathing and slow nasal breathing help in thin, halogen-tasting air. Learn to calm the flinch reflex—hesitation is punished here.
- Skin and eyes: Precondition to extreme aridity. Get used to moisture-chamber goggles and nasal humidification so they don’t distract you.
- Headspace: Accept that directions are opinions. Resolve to treat edges like agreements, not obstacles. Practice noticing micro-cues (a whisper of cloth, a faint tug on a line) and reacting calmly.

What to wear and carry
- Respiratory and eye protection:
  - Closed-circuit rebreather or oxygen addition system sized for low ambient pressure. If you can’t source that, bring small O2 bottles with a demand valve and use sparingly during exertion and sleep.
  - Full-face mask or half-mask respirator with acid gas cartridges plus P100 particulate prefilters. The halogen “kiss” and ammonium frost are irritating; the filter will not add oxygen but will protect tissues.
  - Moisture-chamber goggles; bring spare lenses. Include a soft nose humidifier or saline gel for nostrils.
- Clothing:
  - Layered, antistatic, breathable fabrics. Avoid synthetics that hold big static charges unless they’re engineered ESD-safe.
  - A smooth, hooded shell that sheds brittle “glass rain.” Add forearm and knee protection for controlled slides.
  - Thin, grippy gloves plus a cut-resistant outer glove for glass and bone. Keep a clean pair for interacting with locals and mineral lace.
- Footwear:
  - Edged soles with mixed grip zones: gecko-like rubber at the toe/ball, micro-spikes recessible at the heel. Avoid hard mountaineering lugs that skate on glass bands and salt.
  - Low-profile ankle support that permits proprioception. Carry soft-soled slippers for delicate galleries.
- Harnessing and tethers:
  - Lightweight climbing harness; twin lanyards with devices you can lock quickly to handholds and named ribs. Keep them slack while committing to a step; use only as a backstop, not a crutch (indecision lightens you).
  - 30–40 m of supple, antistatic line; a handful of wide slings for big bones and buttresses. Avoid metal cams/bolts; they bite poorly in glass, salt, and bone-ceramic and can start bad fractures.
- Anchoring and “naming” kit:
  - A slender scribe of bone or ceramic for discreet inscription.
  - Biodegradable, mineral-compatible marking wax to trace letters where locals indicate. Do not overname; naming changes weight.
  - A few small bone or salt-porcelain tags pre-etched with your own travel-name and date. You’ll hang or wedge these only with permission to stabilize a bivy or span.
- Sensing and navigation:
  - A ribbon kite or streamer reel to read sideways drift and static “steps” the skeins ride.
  - A pocket “edge finder”: a light, flexible wand with a tuft of fine fibers; sweep it along surfaces to feel where edges gather or diverge.
  - Analog topological sketchbook and pencil; GPS/compasses are psychological comfort only. Radio and magnetics can be bent by strata fields; don’t rely on them.
  - A small laser pointer for silent line-of-sight signaling; keep beams brief so you don’t pool too much light.
- Light:
  - Warm, low-lumen LED headlamp with tight beam and diffuser. Keep it dim; strong light can “fall” and pool below, changing places. Red or amber modes are gentler.
  - Chemical light sticks for emergencies; they don’t spark in charged dust.
- Communications:
  - Line-of-sight signal flags, hand signs, and a thin “talk line” (tactile tug codes). Throat mics/radios may work short range but expect dropouts and static storms.
- Water and food:
  - Bring all your water or a closed-loop reclaimer. There is no local liquid water; fogs condense into salts, not safe drinking.
  - High-calorie, low-bulk foods that are palatable dry; hard candies for salivation; salt tablets are unnecessary here—there’s salt in everything already.
- Shelter and sleep:
  - A low-profile bivy that anchors at four points and can tolerate sideways drift. Think hammock-tent hybrid that works in narrow slots.
  - Conductive groundsheet to bleed some static; avoid pegging into fragile glass bands.
- Medical:
  - Hypoxia pulse oximeter, blister kit, irrigating eyewash ampoules, neutral pH skin rinse for halogen/ammonium exposures, hemostatic gauze for cuts on glass.
  - ESD-safe pack for electronics; spare batteries packed as if for aviation.
- Courtesies and trade:
  - Water to share is valued. So are durable fibers, fine filaments, and repair needles. Heavy words (promises kept) are also value here.

How to move there
- Read the day’s gravity and wind before you step:
  - Morning: updraft from the abyss; good for travel. Midday: thin air, surfaces tense. Late afternoon: a “slump” deepens weight; stairways may change allegiance. Midnight: sideways drift; curtains sway, bridges lean—don’t begin crossings at night unless taught.
  - Listen to wind-harps and bone charms; note their voicing. Locals treat them as weather organs.
- Ledge etiquette:
  - Before committing, stand still until the place “learns” your weight. You’ll feel a barely-perceptible settling. That is permission.
  - When passing someone, meet eyes, count to three together, and step at the same time. You both get heavier; use that gift.
- Crossings and true falls:
  - Across a true fall, narrow ledges exist that are more agreement than stone. Choose, breathe once, step without pause. Doubt lightens you. Don’t glance down mid-step.
  - Keep tethers slack when you commit, taut only afterward. Practice this.
- Handholds and stairs:
  - Handhold patterns teach your fingers; don’t fight them. If a stair argues about “up,” accept and choose a different path; forcing is how you earn a long moment you can’t pay.
- Surface sense:
  - Black glass hums, shifts with temperature, and “clicks” at morning—treat as alive. Salt-porcelain flows in the long term; anchor wide, not deep. Bone-ceramic is strong along ribs, weak across thin webs. Porous foams crush under point loads; distribute weight.
- Don’t touch the lace unless invited:
  - Mineral lace twins your fingerprints and is livelihood and shelter here. It is not decoration. If you must pass, do so by its shadow edge, not its luminous face.
- Noise and light:
  - Speak close and quietly. Clapping or shouting is wasted breath. Move your lamp like a brush, not a hammer. Be aware that strong light can accumulate in fissures; leave places as dark as you found them unless you intend to tend that light.

Hazards and what to do about them
- Thin air and halogens:
  - If you taste chlorine/bromine more sharply or your chest feels papery, slow down and add oxygen. If cough or eye sting rises when the ammonium rime “smokes” underfoot, mask up and step to a shaded lee.
- Inert gas fogs:
  - You’ll feel them in your joints first. Pressure rises slightly, oxygen fraction drops. Mask on, reduce exertion, descend a few ledges if safe—the pressure bump can help—then wait it out. Don’t chase the glow; those are field-strong seams.
- “Glass rain”:
  - Afternoon flurries of needle-fine, field-frozen shards. Hood up, goggles tight, palms in. They hiss on warm skin and don’t melt. Hunker behind a rib; wait 10–20 minutes. Brush off with a soft cloth; don’t wipe.
- Lightning along strata:
  - Avoid seams that glimmer or hum. Unclip anything metallic. Don’t camp strapped across a seam-line; bivy across ribs instead.
- Sideways gravity:
  - If you feel curtains lift or grit marching, drop your center of mass. Sit with your back to a named rib, clip in short, and ride it out. Don’t try to “correct” your walk against the drift.
- Animals:
  - Skeins and gliders feed on charged dust and sometimes the lace; they’re curious about static on your gear. Antistatic spray your pack. The stilt-limbed ones prefer seams; give them space, go still, and let them fold away. Don’t swat edge-gnats on named thresholds; they mark useful math there.
- Erosion and refusal:
  - Some newer structures “refuse” to exist during strong sideways drift. If a place feels too smooth, too eager, or oddly quiet—leave it be that day.

Navigation that works here
- Make topological, not metric, maps: sequences of edges, names, and behaviors. “Rib with wind-harp (low three-note voice) → scalloped court that looks like a hand → left finger to bone span named ‘Bread-Ladder.’”
- Aim is a contract: pick a visible balcony or tower and keep it in mind. The path behind you will not be the same; loop routes are made, not retraced.
- Mark softly: a whisper of wax, a hung token, or a borrowed name said close. Ask locals before carving. Never put your mark on the living lace.
- Forecast with your senses: streamer angle, harp timbre, how grit clicks underfoot, how your pack tugs. Collect these like weather.

Making camp
- Choose “patient” surfaces: old curves with wear-polish, not crisp young cuts. Places that feel like they remember people.
- Name what keeps you: speak or sing your travel-name into your shelter lines and record it on a discreet tag. Things with names are heavier. Keep it humble; weight is a responsibility.
- Teach the ledge: arrive early, stand still, share weight by stamping gently in a slow rhythm. Cook (if you must) with cold-fuel stoves that don’t spark; don’t try to burn anything—combustion is fickle in thin, odd air.
- Light discipline: use only as much as you can carry morally. Extinguish slow; watch for pools of light forming in cracks; don’t leave them behind unless you trust the next traveler to tend them.

Etiquette with people, if you meet them
- Assume the locals are better at being part of falling than you will be for years. Let them set pace and path.
- Ask permission to name. Offer water first, then fine filaments, needles, or repair skill. Stories that carry weight (kept promises, true names, clear maps) are more valuable than trinkets.
- Don’t “improve” their infrastructure. It’s already perfect for this conversation of matter and temper. Your clever hinge is an insult here.
- Observe quiet. Let wind-harps speak. Don’t move bone charms; they are memory and forecast, not fetishes.
- Wear your clean gloves when handling communal objects. Don’t leave your fingerprints in the lace.

What not to do
- Don’t rely on shouting, whistles, or drones—the air won’t carry them far, and drones lose purchase in drift and thinness.
- Don’t pound pitons or drill anchors in glass, salt, or bone-ceramic. Use wide, soft slings around existing ribs or named points.
- Don’t chase mirages of “easier” lines when you’ve already begun a crossing. Finish what you start.
- Don’t bathe in “light pools” or disturb brittle frost records; they are how the city remembers.
- Don’t lick the fused lightning trails for metals. Beautiful, toxic, and culturally rude.
- Don’t touch living lace unless you’re prepared to be responsible for what your touch starts.

Exit and aftercare
- Acclimate back down: hydrate deliberately; your mucosa will be salt-burned and dry. Rinse eyes and sinuses gently. Treat micro-cuts from glass promptly.
- Discharge your gear: lay it on a named rib or conductively to let stray charge and dust go home. Check for hitchhiking skeins in folds.
- Be mindful of what followed you: pooled light in your pack, a shadow that isn’t yours. Return what belongs there if you can—enclose bright things, let dimness be dim again.
- Debrief your maps as stories of edges and behaviors, not just lines. They will help the next traveler more than coordinates.

A sample day plan for a newcomer
- Dawn: Eat, oxygen-check, streamer-check. Walk easy routes while the updraft is kind. Study handhold grammar. Practice commit-steps across shallow “falls” under supervision.
- Midday: Retreat to patient galleries; rest; learn wind-harp voices; trade and ask about naming.
- Late afternoon: Short moves only. Expect the slump; use it to sleep heavy. If glass flurries hiss, shelter and watch how they drift—they’ll tell you about tomorrow’s seams.
- Night: Stay put. Listen to lightning walk the strata. Note the pattern of heat’s forgetting on lintels; tomorrow’s weather hides there.

Bring humility most of all
- This place isn’t broken; it’s fluent in a grammar you don’t yet speak. Let edges teach you. Name sparingly. Step like someone who has practiced being a part of falling. Keep what you carry light except for your word.
