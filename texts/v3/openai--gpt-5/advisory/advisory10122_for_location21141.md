What this place is like, in the body

- Air that looks clear and limitless but gives you very little. You will breathe often and feel strangely clean and underfilled. Your voice will not carry; even a shout dies against your teeth. You will taste metal and stone. The sun etches crisp shadows that do not warm you; a light breeze lifts heat off your skin as if it is stealing something.
- Dryness as a law. Your lips, sinuses, eyes, and hands will try to turn to paper. You’ll crave water not as a sensation in the throat but as a memory your body keeps replaying.
- Ground that is older than your habits. Surfaces vary by a fraction in gloss and density and those fractions matter. Some patches lengthen your stride as if the world is fractionally generous; others draw your foot down quicker than your expectation. You need to learn to feel with your soles again.
- Quiet. Not peace, exactly—charge murmurs in your bones now and then, and microneedles tick invisibly against your shell—but a rule that the world does not come to meet your ears. You bring your own sound with you.
- Wind structured into lanes and stacked rivers that do not argue with themselves so much as they slide and fold. Watch long enough and you begin to see them by what they do to light. Their rules become a way to plan your day.

Primary hazards to respect

- Hypoxia and low pressure. It can feel like 4,500–6,500 m (15,000–21,000 ft) equivalent, sometimes more in patches. Your saturation may drift suddenly as you cross a line the sky has drawn for itself. Ears will pop in sheltered bowls.
- Cold in thin sunlight. Convective cooling is relentless; radiative losses spike at night. Hypothermia can creep even under a hard sun.
- UV and radiation. Ozone is someone else’s problem here. Expect savage UV, and at night, aurora implies charged-particle activity. You cannot feel ionizing radiation; bring a dosimeter and behave as if the sky has teeth.
- Zero water on offer. The “frost” is not water; nothing here will help you hydrate. There is no ground moisture to wick or filter.
- Electrostatic and field effects. Silent blue rakes of discharge along ridges. Invisible threads between masts that make dust remember a color. Microshocks that don’t hurt until they kill a circuit. Gravity fields tuned in old patterns you’ll feel in your legs.
- Blown particulates. Not sand, but micro-needles of nitrate, carbonate, salt, silica, and charged ice. These will skate into eyes, abrade lenses and skin, and infiltrate gear.
- Old technologies still half-awake. Masts, threads, bowls, geodesic webs, field sinks at the rim. They are not for you. Some still operate; some are inert but sharp; some are invisible until you trespass into their geometry.
- Fragile life that cuts. Dark, varnish-colored films that are both razor and living skin. Touching them is a kill. They also bite back with edges and reactive dust.
- Edges and updrafts. The rim is an abyss with a river of wind falling off it. You will not hear it. You’ll assume your weight is yours until it isn’t.

Pre-trip conditioning

- Cardiopulmonary prep: Train for high altitude. If you can, spend a week at 3,000–3,500 m before arrival; know your symptoms of AMS, HACE, HAPE. Practice walking slowly, nose breathing, and pulse-ox monitoring until it’s second nature.
- Dryness habituation: Practice with a moisture-recovery mask in arid conditions. Get used to drinking on schedule, not thirst.
- Cold management: Drill your layering system in windy, dry cold. Learn to add and shed layers without exposing skin or opening your shell to the wind longer than a breath.

Kit that works here

Life support and protection
- Breathing: A compact rebreather with humidification, or a pressure-demand oxygen system with heated humidifier. Carry enough O2 to keep SpO2 above 90% while moving (expect 1–2 L/min equivalent supplemental for most). Include a foldable emergency pressure bag (Gamow-type) for AMS treatment.
- Face and eyes: Full-seal goggles with side shields, anti-fog and anti-scratch. A flip-down clear visor for micro-needles and charged grit. Spare lenses.
- Skin and clothing: Smooth, abrasion-proof, anti-static outer shell with full coverage; UV 50+ fabric. A low-profile helmet that presents nothing for the wind to chew. Conductive underlayers and wrist/ankle ground straps to bleed charge slowly. Insulation that traps without bulk: high-loft midlayer, windproof outer, vapor-barrier in extremis. Occlusive balms for lips and hands; saline gel for nostrils.
- Hands: ESD-safe gloves (thin inner) plus abrasion-resistant outer shells. Keep fingertip dexterity without exposing skin.
- Feet: Boots with resilient midsole, sticky, low-profile tread that grips polished stone without biting films. Conductive soles to minimize static buildup. Gaiters that seal tight.
- Hearing: It’s quiet, but keep ear protection for sudden discharge snaps near you—startle is a hazard.

Shelter and sleep
- Bivy or tent: Low-slung, teardrop cross-section, abrasion armored, guyless or with gecko-pad anchors/suction feet that do not scar varnish. Slight positive internal pressure with intake filter for particulates and a humidity control that lets you wake with a throat.
- Ground interface: Aerogel or closed-cell pad with a radiation-reflective layer; groundsheet that won’t abrade films and can be cleaned of reactive frost.
- Heat: Catalytic or electric heaters that don’t need ambient oxygen. Chemical hand/foot warmers for emergencies.

Water and food
- Water budget: 4–6 L/day/person baseline in this dryness; more if moving fast. You can cut resupply by 25–40% with a closed-loop moisture-recovery mask, a urine recycler, and condensers in your shelter, but you’ll still need to carry bulk. Cache if you can.
- Storage: Insulated, opaque, antistatic bladders. Don’t rely on rigid bottles; the pressure swing at night can crush or vent them.
- Food: Energy-dense, low-crumb, low-outgassing, high-fat with electrolytes. Your palate will dull; salt and sour help. No powder foods that the air can steal and rub into everything.

Navigation and sensing
- Maps: Build your own as you go. The place redefines “distance.” Use inertial nav plus star fixes; barometric altimeters lie near bowls and sinks. Magnetic compass is entertainment, not truth.
- Field readers: Handheld E-field detector, UV/IR camera, polarized goggles. Laser rangefinder can help judge the lie of the ground in the extreme clarity. Small LIDAR to paint near-invisible threads and webs.
- Marking: Don’t build cairns. Use ephemeral, low-profile light pips or ground-level RFID pucks you recover on the way back.
- Comms: Line-of-sight radio with low power and robust surge protection. Bone-conduction headsets. Pair with optical signaling if you must; keep beams low and short.

Power
- Solar is good here. Flexible, abrasion-resistant panels, edges protected from micro-needle undercutting. Carry a dust whisk and antistatic brush. Surge protection everywhere; keep spare fuses and a Faraday pouch.

Tools and anchors
- Movement aids: A short staff no taller than your sternum, nonconductive, with a sacrificial abrasion tip. Trekking poles kept low; never wave anything higher than shoulder height.
- Anchoring: Vacuum/gecko pads and sand snake equivalents filled with water weight. Micro-cams for rare fissures. Never bolt. Don’t tie into unknown devices.
- Detection: Dry pigment puffer or chalk ball to visualize laminar flows and reveal invisible threads ahead of you—use sparingly and away from living films. A UV torch makes some threads fluoresce faintly when dust crosses them.

First aid and health maintenance
- Altitude meds: Acetazolamide for prevention, dexamethasone rescue. Nifedipine if you know your HAPE risk.
- Ocular: Sterile eyewash, sclerals or wraparound contacts if you tolerate them, antibiotic drops for corneal scratches.
- Wound care: Non-adherent dressings, cohesive wraps (adhesives fail in dust and dryness), cyanoacrylate for skin if needed. Antiseptic that works without water.
- Skin and mucosa: Heavy occlusives, nasal gel, throat lozenges that don’t crumble to dust.
- ESD/EMC: Spare electronics sealed and shielded. Keep one of each critical device in a Faraday bag.

How to move and behave

- Keep your silhouette low. The plateau teaches the rule: anything tall is a consumable. Pack tight; no flappy straps; antennae stowed. Your tent’s profile should be a shrug, not a flag.
- Walk on the old and glossy. Seek pavements and varnish that look fully negotiated with the wind. Avoid the brittle, craquelure textures of living films and lifted edges. If you must cross a film, don’t—find another way; they are alive, and they cut.
- Read the sky the way locals eat. Knife-thin lenses point to looser gravity and steady air; standing waves sit like rulers you can step along for easier breathing. Luminescent braids announce charged conductors—arc corridors—give them space.
- Respect threads and masts. If you see dust fluoresce between two nothings, that is a line you don’t cross. Give lonely masts a wide berth; don’t touch. Blue crawls along ridges are pretty the way a predator is pretty.
- Use field sinks at the rim as rest stops, not furniture. You’ll feel the air densify there; breathe, eat, and leave no mark. Don’t park electronics on them; they may “sip” your device in ways you’ll only understand when it dies.
- Time travel by effects. Choose to “follow coolness,” “walk in a soft gravity,” or “chase a lenticular’s shadow” as your unit of distance. The plateau cares little for kilometers.
- Tether near the abyss. Low, wide stance. Anchor to yourself and the ground, not to mysterious pallets. Updrafts are invisible and opinionated. Assume the edge is where your body forgets itself.
- Don’t make sound your plan. Use radios and gestures. Practice hand signals; rehearse the feel of your teammate’s tug on a flat tape tether. Whistles are theater here.
- Dawn and dusk are chemical. Wear filters; let the frost fall and sublimate before you break camp. The dust right after sunrise feeds films and can be reactive; don’t inhale it raw.
- Night is for astronomy and restraint. The stars will overwhelm you; aurora will drape across ridges that learned lightning. The devices talk to themselves in tiny points of light; do not answer back with your own beams.
- Sleep where gravity is kind. Microdust halos over “vents” are places your body feels slightly less itself; good for rest. If your dreams are of flying, that’s your inner ear noticing the calculus.

Animals and other occupants

- Gliders that eat potential difference are not furniture or toys. Don’t throw dust at them, don’t chase their coronas with cameras. They need the wave the way you need your mask.
- Pale vane-creatures scrape film; give them the space we give a worker. If you see glyphs in varnish, that patch is a kitchen—don’t step there. Watch. Learn.
- Calcite “hand” harvesters at field sinks move in hours, not seconds. If you wake to find one within its own shadow near your camp, you pitched too close to a pantry. Move camp, silently.
- Metal “ash” above bowls are caretakers. If they notice your heat, you are too near. Back away; tiles are not just tiles.

Interaction with old devices

- You are not an operator here. Do not activate, disassemble, “repair,” or “clean” anything you did not bring. The bowl that drinks light, the webs that direct dust into ruler-straight dunes, the masts that hum in your bones—each is part of a sleeping argument older than you. Your curiosity can break a thing that keeps the plateau as itself.
- If you must pass through a web, find its geometry and step in the gaps it already acknowledges. Use LIDAR and dust puffs to see its bones. If you hear nothing from it, that’s not reassurance—silence is the native tongue.
- If you find the door—the stone that does not ring—treat it like an emergency cache in an old religion. 
  - Test atmosphere through a micro-needle valve before opening.
  - Stage a decon: brush and ionize dust off, bag your outer shell, step in wearing clean inner layer.
  - Inside, do not harvest water unless you must; if you do, pay back by leaving power (charge a bank the lattice can drink) or by closing and resealing properly. Don’t release outside dust inside; don’t bring inside gels or life to the outer world. Leave no camera running networks inside; it is rude to listen to what is not yours.
  - Keep time short. The chamber is an eddy in a vast flow; treat it like shelter from lightning—use and leave.

Leave-no-trace and ethics

- Every living film, every ridge polish, every hairline of dust is the culmination of arguments between wind, charge, and time. Your boot is a shout in a quiet room. Step softly and on places that have stopped arguing.
- Pack out everything. Human moisture and salts are potent fertilizers here. Do not gift the plateau your biology.
- Decon before and after. Don’t bring seeds, spores, films, or oils in or out. This place runs on siloxanes and fluorocarbons and charges; your world’s grease is a contaminant.
- Don’t stake, scratch, bolt, or engrave. The rock is a membrane stretched over time. Your name will be polished away, but it will damage a page you did not write.

Failure modes and what to do

- Hypoxia: Headache, nausea, poor judgment. Stop, rest in denser air (seek a field sink), add oxygen. If symptoms worsen, get in the pressure bag and descend toward the rim only if you have a safe route away from updrafts and your tether plan is solid.
- Freeze despite sun: Shivering, clumsiness. Add layers, shelter from wind. Warm core first. Eat.
- ESD strike to you or gear: If your hair lifts, crouch low on insulation, keep nothing raised. After a shock, check electronics; switch to spares from the Faraday pouch. Don’t touch masts or threads to “discharge yourself.”
- Eye abrasion: Irrigate copiously; shield; avoid rubbing. See through one eye rather than both until grit is gone.
- Invisible web encountered: Backtrack in your own footprints. Don’t step over a strand you can’t fully see both ends of; threads can be multiple meters tall in function even if they look like ground lines.
- Fall or slip on “heavy” patches: Slow down. Use the staff like a whisker, not a lever.

Daily rhythms and small rituals that make it easier

- Start slow. The plateau rewards patience; rushing is punished with mistakes you only notice when the quiet has resumed.
- Drink on a watch schedule, not on thirst. Every hour, a measured swallow. Use salted drinks; pee into the recycler without complaint.
- Check your SpO2 at rest, then after ten minutes of walking. Write the numbers down. The air lies to your sense of effort here.
- Clean solar, whisk your seals, check for micro-needle accumulation along edges and in zippers. Make abrasion your daily enemy.
- Before sleep, plan your next day by sky: where the standing wave sits, what the lenticulars point to, how the thin auroral veils drape at the ridges. The plateau leaves notes if you can read them.

What you will carry home in your body

- The memory of being lighter and heavier by fractions, and that this mattered. The way your breath went farther than your voice. How a blue discharge could be so silent your bones did the listening.
- The discipline of keeping everything low and smooth because that’s what survives. The habit of feeling the ground’s gloss before you trust it.
- A sense of time that has married “finished” to “still working.” The plateau is an end state in motion; it will neither hurry nor remember you unless you force it to.

A minimal packing list distilled

- Rebreather or O2 with humidifier; pulse oximeter; AMS meds; emergency pressure bag.
- Full-seal goggles + visor; low-profile helmet; anti-static, abrasion-proof, UV-proof layers; occlusives and nasal gel.
- Low-slung tent/bivy with filtration; gecko/suction anchors; insulated pads; catalytic/electric heat.
- 5 L/day water in antistatic bladders; food that doesn’t shed.
- Navigation: inertial unit, star map, LIDAR, E-field meter; radio with surge protection; optical pips.
- Power: flexible solar, whisk/brush, surge protectors, Faraday bag, spare fuses.
- Movement: short nonconductive staff; flat tape tether; gecko anchors; micro-cams; chalk/dust puffer; UV torch.
- First aid: eyewash, dressings, cyanoacrylate, antiseptic, antibiotics eye drops, blister care.
- Repair: patch kits for shells and bladders, zipper lube that isn’t oil, spare gaskets and O-rings rated for low humidity.
- Waste: urine recycler, fecal bags with desiccant, airtight trash pouches.

Finally: let the place tutor you. If your hair rises, if you taste penny-metal, if your step suddenly lengthens, if the dust ahead glows where nothing stands—pay attention and adjust. Keep nothing above your shoulders. Don’t expect echoes. Breathe on purpose. And when you feel the plateau’s buried lattice turn in its sleep and the sky flex in answer, be still a moment and listen with your bones. Then go on, low and smooth.
