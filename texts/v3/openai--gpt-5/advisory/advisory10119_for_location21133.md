First, understand the premise your body will not: this place is warm without light, loud with events but quiet to your ears, brand-new and still deciding on its own shape. You will be a soft bag of water among knives, in a dry metallic breath under a star that prefers to be felt. Preparation is not optional. Think “planetary EVA,” not “hike.”

What it feels like
- The light is ember-red and insufficient. Your eyes will insist it is dusk; your skin will insist it is noon. You will often trust the wrong one.
- Heat arrives by radiation from a low, invisible sun and from black rocks that drink it. Pale things feel cool. Shadow is not necessarily relief.
- The air is thin, metallic, and dry past the idea of thirst. Sound is late to your party. Your feet will know an avalanche is coming before your ears know there was one.
- The ground is edges. Obsidian and young basalt will open you and your equipment with no ceremony. Talus is crisp and mobile, not yet taught to stay.
- The place moves. Slow quakes, cliff-shedding, and dry rockfalls happen as if at the speed of thought: you see a change and only later hear the thud.

Go/no-go baseline
- If you are not prepared to wear a pressure-rated suit with its own air and cooling for hours, do not go.
- If you cannot resist the urge to shelter under overhangs, do not go.
- If solitude and delayed sound scramble your judgment, do not go.
- If you plan to bring open flame, unsealed liquids, or curiosity without caution, do not go.

Core hazards (the short truth)
- Atmosphere: too thin for bare lungs; composition hostile (acidic and metallic vapors). Oxygen content unreliable. Dry enough to mummify your sinuses. Treat as unbreathable and corrosive.
- Thermal: high IR flux; black rocks may be hot enough to burn through clothing. Thin air means poor convective cooling; you can overheat inside a suit while feeling “not hot” on your face.
- Mechanical: obsidian edges, glass splinters, unstable talus, cliff peel-offs, hidden voids in vesicular rock.
- Electrical: lateral discharges along rock faces and seams; corona glows; fulgurites are brittle glass lattices that cut and collapse; strong static fields can arc to or through you.
- Seismic: low, constant micro-tremors and occasional larger sighs. Rockfall risk is everywhere near walls and below crowns of lightning glass.
- Chemical: SO2, HCl, HF and metal vapors near vents; condensate “whiskers” that corrode and short electronics; sinter rooms full of hot, corrosive gas.
- Radiation: thin air means more cosmic radiation. The star is IR-weighted, but discharges can spit UV and a little X-ray. Budget exposure hours, especially if you are doing multiple days.
- Biological/ethical: there are slow, charge-following mineral films along fulgurites and heat seams that behave like a memory. Treat as life. Do not step on, arc across, or harvest them.

What to wear
- Suit: a soft planetary EVA suit or pressure-rated drysuit equivalent, 0.6–1.0 atm internal, with:
  - Full-face, acid-resistant visor with UV/blue flare attenuation. Anti-fog is critical.
  - Multilayer outer shell: pale, IR-reflective, and chemically resistant (fluoropolymer or ceramic-coated fabric). Reinforce knees/elbows/hips.
  - Integrated Faraday layer and bonded grounds so your whole body remains at one potential. A trailing conductive brush or braided tail to bleed charge into the ground as you walk.
  - Abrasion and cut-resistant overgloves (ceramic fiber/Kevlar blend) and a second sacrificial layer you can swap midday. Same for boot covers.
  - Liquid-cooling garment; active heat dump to adjustable radiator panels. Thin air means your suit must radiate; convection won’t save you.
  - Positive-pressure air system. Rebreather with scrubbers plus backup compressed O2. Do not rely on external air. Add acid/halogen inline filters even though you’re closed-loop; leaks happen.
- Boots: stiff-soled with puncture plates and replaceable carbide microspikes/microspines. Gaiters that laugh at glass dust.
- Helmet: integrated E-field meter and “blue-crown” detector that vibrates before you see a corona. Bone-conduction audio so you can keep hearing your own geophone feet.

What to bring (and why)
- Power: visible-light solar is poor. Bring:
  - Primary: dedicated battery packs sized for all-active cooling. Or a compact fuel cell.
  - Secondary: IR-tuned thermophotovoltaic or thermoelectric panel you can lay on hot black rock while radiators face the cold sky. Slow, but works here.
- Navigation and sensing:
  - LIDAR mapping puck or shoulder unit; the map will change—update constantly.
  - Two scouting drones: one for pathfinding in canyons, one to loiter as a radio repeater above ridge lines.
  - Inertial nav and a breadcrumb string of micro-beacons for your return line; cliffs will kill GPS analogs and line-of-sight comms.
  - Handheld thermal camera; use it like you would use your eyes in fog.
  - Gas sensors (SO2, H2S, HCl/HF), barometer, hygrometer, and surface temp probe on a wand you can place without bending over hot glass.
  - Pocket seismometer or in-boot haptics tuned to long-period tremor; you need advance notice of wall-peel events.
  - E-field meter with alarms at thresholds where lateral arcs become likely.
- Mobility and protection:
  - Trekking poles with ceramic tips; they earn their keep in talus. One short, one long.
  - Compact diamond corer and expansion bolts for emergency anchors in columnar basalt; do not hammer pitons into glassy faces.
  - Two 10–15 m lines with heat-resistant sheathing and a small powered winch. Knife for emergency cutaway.
  - Ultralight deployable “step” planks (gecko or microspine feet) to cross knife-backs and gaps without loading edges.
  - Collapsible, pale ground mat to sit or kneel on; black rock will cook you through your suit if you rest wrong.
- Shelter and survival:
  - Pressure bivy/tent with a radiant shade fly, ceramic standoffs, and internal frame. Anchor away from walls on proven bedrock.
  - Thermal isolation pads; do not let the tent floor touch black rock directly.
  - Radiation/time budget: dosimeter; set a hard line for accumulated dose and suit heat load.
  - Water loop: suit recycler plus reserve. All cooking and drinks inside pressure shelter; low external pressure ruins boiling and hydration cues.
- Medical:
  - Laceration kit for glass cuts; butterfly closures, steri-strips, tissue glue.
  - Burns kit; hydrogel, non-stick dressings.
  - Chemical exposure rinse (calcium gluconate gel if HF is even a rumor), eyesafe saline pods.
  - Heat stress protocol and meds for motion sickness; the ground’s breathing can make you queasy.
- Comms and data:
  - Short-hop radios with mesh capability; launch a repeater drone early.
  - Red/IR-safe task lights; visible light won’t reach far here.
  - Multispectral camera; you’ll want to see the “vegetation” in bands your eyes ignore.
- Housekeeping:
  - Antistatic wipes, dust covers for every hinge and port. Expect metallic whiskers to grow and short things near vents.
  - Non-sparking, mostly nonmetallic hand tools.

How to move through it
- Choose your zone with malice aforethought:
  - Avoid directly beneath sheer faces and below fulgurite crowns. Rockfall here is a lifestyle, not an event.
  - Ridge knives are alluring but exposed to lateral arcs. If you must take a crest, keep to low spots, avoid proud points, and watch your E-field meter.
  - Mid-slope buttresses and wide shoulders are your friends; cross talus quickly and lightly.
- Pattern of travel:
  - Leapfrog your drones: one ahead mapping, one high relaying.
  - Place micro-beacons whenever the terrain funnels you; today’s canyon can be tomorrow’s cliff.
  - Rest often not for breath, but to listen. The long purr means “a wall is reconsidering itself.” Look up. Pick a new line if needed.
- Electrical etiquette:
  - If your visor blooms a faint blue at edges or your meter hum climbs, crouch low on your mat, stay away from seams and fulgurites, keep everything bonded. Do not touch the rock with bare metal. Wait it out.
  - Never camp, sit, or step inside a crown of fulgurites; they are brittle glass knives waiting to become many smaller knives.
  - Keep antennas retracted near ridges. Your own conductive silhouette invites arcs.
- Vent protocol:
  - Treat tufa rings and sinter “rooms” as booby-trapped. Their floors can go; their air can melt lungs and optics. No entry. Observe with a probe if you must.
  - If you see metallic whiskers, you are in a plating zone. Cover ports, shorten your dwell, and expect electronics to behave badly afterward.
- Seismic protocol:
  - When a tremor rises through your boots, you have seconds. Step out from below faces. Face the slope so you can see the peel line. Crouch balanced, ready to move sideways, not downhill.
  - After a big thud, pause 5–10 minutes. Aftershocks are shy but punctual.
- Heat protocol:
  - Check before you touch—everything. Fall in love with your thermal camera.
  - Sit only on the pale mat. Pale rock is cooler but still check it. Black rock is a stove.
  - Use radiant shade while you work; a small fly above you can drop your heat load dramatically.
- Navigation mindset:
  - Distances lie. Sound lies more. Believe your map, your clock, and your feet.
  - Accept that the route you used in will not be the route you use out. Leave yourself two exits.

Water, food, and time
- You will not find water. Full stop. Your body will leak it into a vacuum-dry world. Bring more than you think and let your recycler work.
- Eat inside pressure. Even “snacking through the helmet port” invites contamination.
- Set alarms for hydration and for heat; you won’t feel thirsty or hot in time to save yourself.
- Time feels wrong here; the sky does not change enough to guide you. Keep a schedule, and stick to it.

Respecting the resident patterns
- Those slate-gray films that fret the edges of glass and fulgurite—treat them as living archives of charge. Photograph, map, and measure from a distance.
- Step around seams and margins. If you must cross, bridge, don’t tread.
- Don’t key your radio at full power next to them; EM fields are their weather.
- Take no crowns, take no films. Leave the grammar of this place unedited.

Where to sleep
- A freestanding, pressure-tight bivy on a pale, stable shoulder at least twice the height of the nearest wall away from it. No crowns within sight. No vent plumes upwind. Anchor to drilled basalt, not to glass or blocks that move when you kick them.
- Fly a radiant shade. Put your water and gear between you and the open sky for radiation massing.
- Geophones out in a triangle around camp; let them buzz you awake before rock does.

When the horizon blinks
- That blink is a fresh collapse somewhere you’re not. Dust will arrive later. If the air tastes more of coins and you feel warmth rise, hunker. Radiant shade up, face away from the event, mask filters ready. Wait. Maps are about to be wrong again.

If something goes wrong
- Suit puncture by glass: sit, mat down, pressure patch now, then second layer. Don’t walk it off; every move cuts more.
- Heat spiral: drop activity, deploy radiators and shade, recirc coolant to max, lie with your back to the sky or a pale slab. If you can’t get your core down fast, abort day.
- E-field spike while you’re exposed: go low, bond, stow metal, wait for the whispering blue to die.
- Chemical burn: rinse longer than feels sane. Recoat optics. If you suspect HF, treat as serious regardless of pain.
- Lost path in new terrain: send the drone, plant a beacon, accept that your “there-and-back” just became a “there-and-around.”

Powering your time here
- A radioisotope battery is perfect and impolite. A fuel cell is polite and heavy. Thermoelectric plates against a hot black slab with your radiator to the sky will sip you back to margin while you watch mountains be born. Plan your power like you plan your breath.

Psychology and pace
- Expect reverence. Expect unease when the world moves and does not sound like it. Expect to begin trusting your feet more than your ears.
- Build quiet into your schedule. This place rewards stillness; it tells you things only when you stop trying to get somewhere.

What to practice before you arrive
- Moving on broken glass without cutting yourself or your suit. Step, test, transfer, commit.
- Drilling and setting anchors in hard volcanic rock. No hammering.
- Reading a thermal camera as if it were your second pair of eyes.
- Suit heat management drills. You’ll rely on them more than you want to.
- Field repairs to suit layers and hoses with tired hands.

A simple day-plan template
- 00:00–00:30: Suit-up, diagnostics, power and coolant check. Launch repeater drone.
- 00:30–02:00: Traverse to objective on shoulders, avoiding faces. Drone leapfrogs. Beacons placed at funnels.
- 02:00–03:00: Observe fulgurite crown or vent from standoff. Multispectral and thermal mapping. No samples.
- 03:00–03:30: Shade break, core cool-down, hydration alarm.
- 03:30–05:00: Return along alternate line, update map as you go. Retrieve beacons where safe.
- 05:00–06:00: Camp tasks under shade, sensors set, log entries, decon dust/whiskers from gear.
- Overnight: Geophones and E-field alarms armed. Wake for anything with a purr.

What not to do, ever
- Shelter under an overhang, however perfect it looks.
- Step into a sinter ring or a vent’s “room.”
- Touch a fulgurite crown with anything but your eyes.
- Set metal directly on a ridge lip in growing corona light.
- Assume stillness here means safety. Stillness is just the space between events.

Why you came
- To watch a world practice becoming one. To feel maps go obsolete under your feet. To learn the sound of stone thinking. To meet a life that writes itself in gradients and remembers without cells. To be the only soft motion in a place full of hard decisions.

If you prepare like this, you can be present without becoming an event yourself. That’s the discipline here: carry your own day, keep your edges smooth in a country of edges, and leave with your suit full of heat and your head full of the world’s first drafts.
