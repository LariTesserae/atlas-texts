Begin with weight. Train for it. The water under an ice roof will not let you pretend you are light. It is dense with salt, dense with silt, dense with its own old cold. Prepare your body and your expectations to be carried, pressed, slowed, and sometimes fixed in place by a medium that always has the advantage.

What you are visiting
- An under-ice marine plain: a broad, low cavity roofed by ice, floored by glacially planed rock and mud, threaded with sills, gutters, and an older channel that deepens to a trench.
- Light is a rumor. You will navigate by instruments, touch, and a private constellation of small lights rather than by sight.
- The ceiling is close, scalloped, hung with brine fingers (“brinicles”), and dynamically alive. There is no direct ascent to sky anywhere you will go.
- The water column is stratified: layers of different temperature and salinity flow past each other in sheets and bands. Currents can run opposite directions at chest and knee height. Silt is always present.
- Life is sparse, anchored, and tough. Everything that lasts grows low and adheres. You are the only thing that rises.

The safest way to visit
- First choice: a one-person electric micro-sub or low-noise glider with redundant thrusters, life support for 24 hours, heated cabin, shrouded propulsors, and a fine-control hover mode. It should carry sonar (multibeam + imaging), Doppler velocity log (DVL), inertial navigation, a compact conductivity–temperature–depth (CTD) probe, and an acoustic modem. Build in a “soft touch” flight envelope—no surging, no prop wash that scours sediments or mats. Think “gliding in a cathedral,” not “flying a drone.”
- Second choice: an articulated hardsuit with integrated heating and thruster pack, designed to shed ice nucleation and to keep your hands free. It gives you tactile contact with the place while preserving overhead safety; plan for shorter excursions.
- Only if you already have excellent training: closed-circuit rebreather (CCR) cave-diving with a scooter (DPV). This is advanced overhead-environment work with brine, anchor ice, and silt—three independent ways to get stuck. If you are not already trained for ice/cave CCR, choose the sub.

Access and base
- Reach is through the ice. Establish a warm, clean staging habitat at the surface with a drilled borehole or natural tide crack stabilized and sleeved. Rig a rigid access tube that resists freeze-in.
- Hang an under-ice “porch”: a small, heated bell or shelter just below the ceiling, tethered to the access tube. Stock it with emergency breathable gas, heat, dry supplies, a spare light set, and a hot fluid line for de-icing.
- Set an acoustic transponder ring under the ice within a few kilometers of the entry to build your own positioning constellation. In a place without sky, you must make your own stars.

Personal preparation
- Cold: Acclimate to extreme cold and long-duration stillness-in-cold. Practice with active-heated garments; learn the feel of hypothermia before it surprises you.
- Darkness: Spend time in controlled total darkness, moving by touch and instrument. Learn how your mind invents shapes and how you calm it.
- Breathing: If diving, train CO2 management on a rebreather in current. Working hard in cold, dense water while controlling buoyancy and silt is a skill, not a promise you can make yourself on the day.
- Patience: Everything here is slow. Cultivate the pace that keeps you safe, and the attention that keeps you interested.

Environmental hazards (know them by name and sign)
- Brinicles / brine falls: Super-saline, supercooled plumes hanging from the ceiling. The water around them can freeze onto anything it touches. Their fingerprints: a feathery curtain from above; a knife-cold “sting” through gloves; local salinity spikes on your CTD. Rule: never pass directly beneath; give them a wide berth and a respectful up-current side.
- Anchor ice: Supercooled microcrystals that nucleate on protrusions—rocks, lines, the seams of your suit, props. It adds drag and buoyancy and can fix you in place. Avoid supercooled zones (watch CTD and ice “hiss” in your audio), keep moving gently, use anti-nucleation coatings, and carry a soft brush and a low-power de-icing jet for spot removal.
- Silt-out: The bottom is talc-fine. Any strong thrust, fin scull, or touchdown raises a private weather system that blinds you and settles slowly. Fly high over the floor; use frog kicks and gentle trim if you are in water; keep DPV/sub thrust laminar and diffuse.
- Stratified currents: Internal waves and density sheets look like nothing but move like conveyor belts. You’ll feel a clean “step” in temperature or salinity and a change in the force on your body. Ride them when aligned with your route; do not fight crossbars—slide diagonally, like fording a river.
- Sills and throats: Where the plain narrows, the sea clenches. Those jets can be near-silent until you enter the stream. Time crossings at slack tide; scout with a probe drone first.
- Ice roof dynamics: Infrasonic groans precede shifts. You may feel a low hum in your bones and see micro-snow in your lights as crystals fall. Back away from contact with the ceiling; secure lines so nothing can be pinched.
- Chemistry pockets: Near microbial mats and seeps, expect low oxygen and small H2S/Mn/Fe anomalies. Respect your O2 setpoint; do not sit in a stagnant eddy and breathe it down.

What to wear/bring (diver or hardsuit)
- Thermal: Active-heated base layers with redundant power, a high-loft mid-layer, and a trilaminate dry shell or hardsuit insulation rated below −5°C water. Warm the suit before entry; warm, don’t chase warmth once cold.
- Hands/face: Full-face mask or helmet for thermal protection and comms. Heated, dryglove system with sacrificial overmitts you can shed if iced. Noseclip for equalization under mask.
- CCR: Well-understood rebreather with anti-freeze regs, heated scrubber canister, external gas block for bailout switching you can operate in thick gloves. Oxygen sensors with thermal jackets. Carry two independent bailouts and a travel stage; plan gas by rule of sixths, not thirds (overhead + current).
- Mobility: DPV with soft-start, wide, shrouded prop and silt guards. Mount at chest to keep thrust above floor. If in a sub, set max thrust ceiling and obstacle standoff in software.
- Lights: Minimum three independent lights on your person, plus sub/hardsuit arrays. One should be a very soft flood for close work that won’t backscatter silt into blindness. Bring low-lumen red for notes to yourself.
- Navigation: Primary guideline from the porch with distance markers every 10 m and tactile arrows home; two spools for jumps; personal tags. In a sub, deploy acoustic “breadcrumbs” (palm-sized pingers) you can recover on exit. CTD on a wand. Wrist compass is nearly useless this far poleward—trust DVL/INS and line.
- Cutting and clearing: Two line cutters and a blunt-tip tool for scraping ice, not stabbing rock. A small, warm-water spot de-icer (food-grade glycol loop) for anchor ice on valves.
- Sensing: Compact multibeam for roof mapping; a forward-looking imager for sills; passive hydrophones to hear the place (and approaching jets) before your body feels them.
- Protection: Hydrophobic/icephobic coatings on all protrusions and line; elastic line floats to reduce floor contact; non-shedding gear—no Velcro fuzz, no dangling webbing to collect crystals.

Procedures that keep you alive
- Lines are law. In silt and darkness and moving layers, your guideline is not a suggestion. Keep it off the floor; keep a hand or eye on it whenever visibility drops below your reach.
- Tides are calendar. Build a tide table from a week of under-ice observation; plan all sill work at slack. When in doubt, wait. Waiting here is a skill.
- Never under a brine fall. Cross the flow field far down-current if you must, where its thread is diluted. Do not expose valves, zips, or thrusters to direct brine.
- Silt discipline. No fin pivots. No anchor points on the bottom. If you must rest, hook gently to a rock shoulder already scoured by currents, not a clean dome or mat.
- Gas conservatism. Overhead + cold + current: turn early. If anything feels “a little off,” it is already off. Go home. There will be another tide.
- Team spacing. If diving, stagger so each person has clear water, and never more than a single silt cloud between you and the next light. Communicate by agreed light codes and touch on the line; voice comms are gravy, not a lifeline.
- Current reading. Learn to taste salinity on your lips and feel micro-shears on your suit. Your CTD confirms what your skin first tells you.
- Set dwell points. Place two or three fixed “listening posts” on your route—spots you stop, go dark, and let the place declare itself before you proceed. You’ll hear jets you cannot see.

Contingencies (rehearse until boring)
- Lost line: Stop. Silt settles on its own time. Do a slow 360 scan in low light to catch the glint of line; if none, run your reel in a tidy, shrinking arc. Do not chase currents; make the world smaller until it offers you something.
- Light failure: Lights die in threes only if you were lazy. If two go, you turn.
- Anchor ice on you: Move into gentle turbulence (midwater in a corridor), keep limbs in motion, brush and warm critical points (valves, DSV, seals). If on a sub prop, shut down, wait for melt from motor warmth, clear before restart.
- Brine on suit seam: You’ll feel a local numb-burn. Back away. Warm that area with your body or a spot de-icer. Inspect for new rigidity before continuing.
- Trapped by jet: Streamline. Dump any dangled gear. Crab diagonally to the lee of a rock hump or roof scallop and “eddy out.” Wait for slack if needed. The place runs on calendars.
- CO2 spike (CCR): Bail out without theatre. In cold + work + current, treat early symptoms as late-stage.

Biosecurity and etiquette
- Decontaminate everything before entry: heat, peroxide vapor, UV. You are the most dangerous invasive this place could meet.
- No sampling unless you are permitted and prepared to do it aseptically. Even your wake is a study; do not turn it into a scar.
- Stay off microbial lawns and sponges. If a surface looks improbably clean or faintly cream-colored, it is alive. Use the scoured, dark rock shoulders for contact if you must.
- No bubbles if you can help it. Rebreathers and subs leave less chemistry behind.

Windows and seasons
- Slack tides and neaps are your friends. Map them locally; do not import a timetable from a coast you cannot see.
- Brine fall intensity pulses with winter consolidation above; they are weaker in the “green weeks” when fissures open to sun. Anchor ice is worst in the deep winters and after sudden weather-driven coolings topside.
- Oxygen tongues near the grounding line bloom with meltwater in late summer. That is when the glass sponges stand tallest and are most tempting—steer wide.

What it will feel like (so it does not surprise you)
- The medium presses, not painfully but incessantly. Your body becomes an instrument: you feel layers slide over you, taste shifts in salinity, sense the roof as a presence even when your eyes cannot.
- Sound arrives through bone: a bowel-hum from the ice, a hiss like wind that is not wind from brine rain, the woolly rustle of silt moving. You will have the urge to turn everything off and just listen. Give yourself a minute for that; then turn the lights back on and continue.
- Distance loses drama. You will travel for long minutes with only edges for reference. Do not interpret that as boredom; it is the place’s pace inviting you to match it.

If you insist on swimming it (minimum standard)
- Certifications: Full cave + CCR + under-ice, with dozens of real overhead dives in cold water, not pool cards. DPV training in silt.
- Gear: Heated drysuit, full-face mask, CCR with heated scrubber, two bailouts, DPV, three lights, two spools + primary reel, CTD, acoustic pinger/receiver, line markers, line cutters, spot de-icer, soft brush, small inflatable “rest pillow” you can wedge into a roof scallop in still water if you must stop.
- Plan: Rule of sixths for gas; hard turn at the first of (time/gas/temperature/any gear weirdness). Maximum penetration set by the most conservative member. Zero solo.

Things to practice before you go
- Riding a density interface: in a quarry or fjord, find a thermocline and travel along it without yo-yoing across.
- No-silt hovering: hold a stable stop over powder sand for 10 minutes without a puff.
- Lost-in-zero-visibility drills: lights out, silt cloud, find and follow line, gas switch by touch, sort a line tangle blind.
- Working while cold: change a battery, tie a loop, and operate your bailout valves wearing the exact gloves you’ll use there.

What to leave behind
- Expectations of spectacle. The theatre here already happened. Prepare to be moved by simple forms well made: low, rounded, settled.
- Hurry. Nothing built endures above the flow line. Your respect will.

What to bring back
- Notes on the timing of sills, the taste of layers, the way a brine fall hums in your bones at two arm-lengths and not at three. They will serve you better than photographs.
- A practiced humility about overheads and cold. It carries into other worlds.

One last instruction
- Let the medium teach you. When in doubt, stop, go dim, listen to the hiss and hum and the nearly inaudible crackle of ice making and unmaking itself. This place rewards travelers who are instruments first and intruders second. If you can match its choreography—move in pulses, hug what needs hugging, leave as little wake as you can—you will not only be safe; you will be welcomed, in the quiet way old places welcome anything that remembers the rules.
