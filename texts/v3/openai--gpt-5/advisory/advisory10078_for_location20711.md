What you’re really visiting
- A planet-scale, enclosed, engineered ocean. Think cathedral-sized intake canyons, continent-length flow loops, and a luminous ceiling that simulates daylight. It’s cold (around 10°C), very loud, very clean, and very organized. Currents, light, and even “tides” run on schedule. Wildlife is present but choreographed; machines are everywhere and they have right-of-way.
- Depths are serious. The places worth seeing are hundreds of meters down, sometimes more. You don’t “swim” here in the recreational sense. You arrive in a vehicle or a pressure suit, you listen to the rules the sea itself speaks, and you move with the flow or you wait in its lee.

Before you go: permissions, orientation, and timing
- Get authorization. This basin is infrastructure, not a park. You’ll need a visitor clearance, a liability bond, and a “voice” (acoustic ID emitter) registered to you. Nothing is allowed to drift unlabeled here, including you.
- Book an orientation block. You’ll be taught:
  - The chime language (descending tones mean a flow change in ~60 seconds; there are other codes for maintenance swarms, filter dumps, and whale priority).
  - Safe lanes and lee pockets on current maps for your day and depth.
  - What not to approach (intake mouths, valve galleries, chemical drippers, trains).
  - Night-mode etiquette (light limits; how to go dim without disappearing from the network).
- Plan your visit around a tide-engine cycle. Reversals happen roughly every nine hours; the best “show” is often bracketing a reversal so you feel the basin breathe both ways. Set alerts for internal waves and acoustic storms.

How to be there (choose your body)
- Easiest and safest: ride a pill sub. A small, two- to four-seat, pressure-rated capsule with excellent visibility, active acoustic cancellation, and a registered voice. You’ll feel the hum through the hull but you won’t have to manage your own physiology or currents. This is the default for ordinary travelers.
- If you must get wet: only in designated service volumes, only with a guide, only if you already have cold-water and current training. Depths deeper than 30 meters here are not for recreational diving; the operational zones you came to see require either an atmospheric diving suit (ADS) or a sub. The basin’s microbubbles, flows, and noise make it a poor place to learn anything for the first time.

What to wear and carry
- Thermal: base layers under a soft harness in a sub; if you’re in an ADS, wear moisture-wicking thermals and heated undergarments. If you’re in a wetside service bay, think drysuit with a thick underlayer; the water is 8–10°C and the cold soaks fast.
- Hearing and head: in a sub, you’ll have active damping; bring a jaw relaxer or mouthguard if you’re sensitive—the infrasonic hum can make your molars feel busy. In an ADS, use an in-helmet bone-conduction set that can pass the basin’s safety tones while filtering everything else.
- Eyes and lights: bring an amber-filtered task light and low-lumen floods. No strobes. In night mode, keep your beam warmed and narrow; let your registered voice announce you instead of blazing.
- Hands and contact: cut-resistant gloves for fresh edges; the fabrication here is young and sharp where not rounded on purpose. Bring a non-magnetic clamp or vac pad for resting against composite; magnets only help on the stainless ribs and can annoy cathodic protection.
- Navigation and sense: a compact flow meter, a wrist acoustic map repeater, and a short-range imaging sonar. The uniformity of the space can trick your sense of scale; your map and the ceiling grid sharpness are your friends.
- Your “voice”: a harmonic tag/tonal beacon registered to you, set to “passive-human” and slaved to your guide or sub. The machines will know you’re there; so will the seals.
- Don’t bring: loose textiles, glittery finishes, flaking adhesives, unregistered drones, fishing gear, or snacks meant for animals. Anything that sheds, slimes, or looks like biofilm invites a cleaning swarm.

What the environment will do to you (and how to cope)
- Sound. It’s nonstop: pump basslines, valve thunder, cavitation fizz, tones. Most people acclimate in an hour; until then, you may feel a pressurey unease in your ribs and jaw. Breathe slow, unclench, and let the schedule become predictable. If you’re prone to motion or sound sickness, pre-medicate.
- Cold. Ten degrees reads crisp but feels hungry with time. In a sub, you’ll be fine. In any wetside work, plan duty cycles and warmups. Numb fingers and false confidence appear before core cold does—watch for them.
- Currents and internal “weather.” The basin changes direction with warning. Laminar corridors behave like highways; eddies like lay-bys. Your guide will park you in lee pockets to rest and watch predators transit the flow. Treat tall comb-tooth walls as weather, not walls.
- Visibility and scale. Microbubbles frost the water; distances lie. At fifteen meters the ceiling lattice looks sharp; at fifty it’s gauze; beyond that you’re painting in tone. Accept that you are small and the space is slow.

Etiquette with life and with the machine
- Whales have right-of-way. When a long, narrow-band song enters the safety band, everything slows around it. Do not chase, flank, or cross their bows. Let the ship pass.
- Seals are hooligans. They’ll pop out of the blue, inspect your weld seams, and disappear coughing. Keep hands in; don’t bop noses; secure dangling kit.
- Fish treat predictable jets as restaurants. Do not block standing streams where mackerel queue or lurkers wait; the sea’s metabolism depends on these habits.
- Octopuses are magpies. If you set something down near a den decorated with snap-rings and clips, assume you’ve gifted it away.
- Night mode is for the smallest eyes. Dim early. If you must work bright, get an escort so your voice can warn blind corners and wake the right networks.
- Leave no chemistry. Your skin oils, your suit’s micro-shed, your dropped crumb—it all registers. You’ll do decon on entry and exit; treat it as holy.

Places to avoid and how to survive mistakes
- Intake mouths and valve galleries: never approach a squared blackness that hums without explicit clearance. “Safe” differentials can still make you theirs. If you find yourself being drawn, turn broadside, kick to the ribbed walls, stick with a clamp, and call it in. Your registered voice lets the system carve a temporary eddy.
- Chemical drippers and filter dumps: marked in maps and tones. If the water starts glittering or snowing with fine white, hold position in a lee; don’t swim face-first into a purge.
- Maintenance swarms and crawlers: pea-sized cleaners look like krill but kiss with acid; big beetle-crawlers drag umbilicals like quiet snakes. They have lanes. Yield. If you’re approached, stay still and let the swarm reclassify you as “hard smooth object with a voice.”
- Acoustic storms: sometimes every rib sings. If your bones feel like a freight train is passing, you are not dying; you are in infrastructure weather. Sit in a lee, dim down, wait five minutes.

Moving well
- Learn the cadence: chime… pause… first tug… build. It’s the breath of the place. You’ll do better if you move at the beginnings and ends of each inhale.
- Ride where it’s straight; rest where it curls. Your guide will thread the flow-shapers; memorize how ridges make lee shadows so you can ask to linger where the world passes by.
- Use features like addresses. “Eddysix” and “The Cusp” are crossroads in the blue; famous to both predators and operators. Expect thirty quiet minutes, then ten when your whole world is whale or sled, then quiet again.

Health and safety specifics
- Pressure: in a sub or ADS you’re at one atmosphere; decompression isn’t your problem. In any wetside work over 10 m, follow local tables; the basin’s microbubbled water is for its lungs, not yours.
- Hearing: let the system feed you the safety tones; don’t mute them. Filter music and chatter. Your jaw soreness will ease when your brain starts predicting the pump cycle.
- Burns and bites: fresh paint, crisp edges; don’t slide fast along anything that looks newly beveled. Animals here are not cuddly but they’re professional—bites are rare unless you poke or present a shiny wiggly thing.
- Psyche: the emptiness plus loudness is strange. If you feel unmoored, count the pump cycle, read the light grid sharpness, sip something warm, and let your guide narrate what the sea is currently doing. Naming the weather helps.

Photography and collecting
- Shoot close and sideways to the bubbles. Blue-green wavelengths behave best. No lasers, few flashes. The best images catch habits—mackerel in a jet, a wrasse garden on a peel-out panel, a whale towing a living fog.
- Take nothing. Not a snap-ring from an octopus den, not a pellet of filter media. The whole basin is a circuit and you’re borrowing time inside it.

A suggested first visit
- Two-cycle ride: arrive an hour before a scheduled reversal; watch a predator highway come alive; park in the lee of a comb-tooth wall; feel the chime and the basin’s inhale; drift a corridor toward an intake forest; hold well back. After the reversal, cross to a dim “canyon floor” where the ceiling light is dusk-soft and wait for a maintenance sled to pass and briefly become your whole sky. If you’re lucky, a whale will sing the engines sleepy around you and you’ll feel the machinery make space for a body.

Departing and after
- You’ll decon twice: machinery first (salt, bubbles, microfilm), then biology (DNA, spores). Expect your voice to be silenced at the lock; you’ll feel oddly naked without it.
- Don’t expect souvenirs. Expect an afterimage: the long hum braided with breath, the memory of walls you learned to call weather, and the sense that this new sea is young in the way a city is young—alive by habit and design.

Packing checklist, short and honest
- Required: authorization, registered acoustic tag (“voice”), orientation slot booked to your cycle time.
- Strongly advised: thermal base layers; jaw relaxer; nausea meds; amber task light; non-magnetic clamp; wrist map/flow meter; cut-resistant gloves; soft kit bag that doesn’t shed; a willingness to be very small in a very well-run ocean.

If you take one rule with you, make it this: here, the rules are not signs—they are physics. Learn the chimes, keep your voice on, yield to the whales, and let the sea’s schedule carry you. The place will open.
