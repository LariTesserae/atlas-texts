What you’re walking into
- This is a young undersea mountain range. Everything is close, green, and hushed. Light filters down tired and pewter; visibility changes in sheets. Think canyon country made of new glass, strung with gardens and walkways of rope, lived in by people who have turned buoyancy and salinity into tools.
- There is no dry. Heat is a place you go to (warm vents), not something you make. Movement is by net, line, and the slow slide of weighted bladders. Sound doesn’t travel far. The city is woven into the ridge, not built on it.
- The most important physical fact: layers of fresh water are continuously falling through salt like invisible rivers and curtains (haloclines). Crossing them changes buoyancy, taste, and even how your eyes and brain read distance. It’s beautiful and destabilizing.

Before you go: the short version
- Do not plan an overnight unless you have a proven, pressure-rated air habitat and local permission to anchor it. A first visit should be a long dive, not a stay.
- Train for a closed-circuit rebreather dive with overhead/entanglement protocols. If you’ve never swum through a halocline (e.g., in a cenote), go do that first.
- Expect to move slowly, to keep your hands on lines, and to talk mostly with fingers, taps, and rope codes. Expect to be watched by the net-keepers; it’s not hostile—this place survives by attention.
- Strip your kit of anything shiny, dangly, or noisy. Propellers, DPVs, and scooters are not only rude here; they’re unsafe in this web of lives and lines.

Depth, time, approach
- Typical working depth feels like 15–35 m (50–115 ft), but the ridge has sudden drops. Plan gas and decompression for 40 m/130 ft, with margins.
- Storms above can be violent. Arrange a submersible or downline drop to a fixed rendezvous on the ridge with the city’s permission. Surfacing free in weather here is not a backup plan.
- Build long dwell time into your schedule. Everything—greetings, crossings, even finding your rhythm on a catenary road—takes longer than it would in open water.

Essential skills to have beforehand
- Rebreather proficiency: setpoint management, bailout drills, scrubber discipline, and bubbleless swimming. Bubbles disturb the fields and the veils and mark you as careless.
- Line and entanglement work: clipping in/out smoothly, staying neutral while tending a reel, cutting yourself free without panic if you must. Practice with gloves.
- Halocline sense: how light warps, how your buoyancy jumps a little, how to keep a line or landmark at the edge of your vision to anchor your inner ear.
- Quiet movement: frog kick and modified flutter that don’t throw wakes into fresh-fall gardens. Hands-on nets, not midwater sculling.

What to bring and why
Life-support and exposure
- Closed-circuit rebreather with at least one independent open-circuit bailout. Keep your loop quiet and tidy; route hoses where they won’t snag in ropework.
- Full-face mask or well-sealed half-mask plus a low-profile hood. Haloclines can carry microbes and off-gassing from vents; protect eyes and mucosa.
- Cut-resistant, snag-aware exposure suit (3–5 mm for warm ambient, with optional thin heated vest for long spells away from vents). This rock is new and knife-sharp.
- Hard-soled, flexible booties with tread that grips rope and net; avoid long fins. If you insist on fins, choose short, stiff fins and practice kicking without fouling lines.

Navigation and safety
- Primary and backup lights in low-lumen, warm color temperature. Bright white scatter blinds you in veils; strobes are taboo. Think glow more than beam.
- Two independent cutting tools: a line cutter/EEZYCUT-style ceramic and blunt-tip shears. Knife points and sawing are for real emergencies; cutting a city’s line is like breaking a bridge—never casual.
- Spool/reel with tactile markers and a locking clip for attaching to catenary roads when you must leave the main path.
- Computer with reliable depth/temperature logging and conservative deco settings. Haloclines and thermals will mess with your head; your computer doesn’t care.
- Compact chemical/thermal burn kit (for vent mistakes), waterproof wound closure strips, sterile tweezers, broad-spectrum antiseptic that plays well with saltwater, and a post-dive antibiotic plan cleared with your medic for volcanic-glass lacerations and Vibrio risk.
- Corrosion-resistant kit only. Vents and bacterial films are not kind to metals.

Comms and courtesy
- Wrist slate and graphite, plus a rope-talk crib card on a retractable tether. If you can learn 20–30 local touch signs ahead of time, you’ll make friends.
- Haptic signal clip (a little vibrating puck) you can pin to a shared line for agreed tap codes; ask before deploying.
- Low-shed, matte kit wraps. Microplastics are a moral failing here—decon your gear thoroughly between worlds.

Food and water
- You can’t snack socially underwater the way they do. Bring neutral-buoyancy gel packs and an in-mask drinking valve with inline microfilter if you’ll be offered fall-water. Don’t drink raw fresh-fall without filtering; your gut isn’t from here.
- Hydration is tricky: immersion diuresis will dehydrate you. Prehydrate, bring electrolyte gels, and limit visit length.

How to move through the city
- Always be attached to something when you can. Clip to main lines; the catenary curve is the local map. Let the rope teach you how the weight moves.
- Cross haloclines slowly and head-on. If you angle through, your shoulders will be in one density and your hips in another; that’s how people tumble into veils or step into nothing.
- Where you see gardens—big, loose baskets inside fresher water—your wake is a weather event. Skirt the down-current edge, keep kicks tiny, and pass with a hand on the supporting line so your body doesn’t bobble the plants.
- Warm columns are for elders first. You’ll feel them: a soft rise of heat, a shimmer in the water. If you’re invited in, slide to the margin and mind your buoyancy; don’t expand your suit gas there and then rocket when you leave.

Etiquette that matters
- Quiet is not just preference; it’s safety. Loud gear is read as incompetence. So is hurry.
- Don’t cut lines. If entangled, signal. A keeper will free you or guide your own cuts. Only in an immediate life threat do you sever city rope, and you report it after.
- Don’t handle counterweight baskets. They’re the circulatory system. A slow-moving freshwater globe is a vehicle, a tool, or a stored meal; not a toy.
- Yield warmth. If someone with stiff hands approaches a heat-wrapped space, you make room.
- Ask before touching chalk-bearded weirs and milky films on fronds. Those “seams of living chalk” are time made visible—someone planted that season’s wall.
- Waste is placed, not released. They have latrine nets down-current of gardens and near vents for composting. Peeing freely into a plaza is as rude here as anywhere.
- Bring rope you tied yourself, even if humble. Time and knots are legible gifts. Your coil will be read like handwriting.

Hazards you can plan around
- New rock is glass. Cuts are easy, infection easier. Wear gloves and move slowly. If you’re bleeding, control it and stay out of gardens; blood chemistry can sicken their chalkers.
- Halocline vertigo. If the world “re-bends,” stop, touch a line, take three slow breaths. Trust touch over vision until your brain recalibrates.
- Downwellings after surface lightning. The falls glitter. Locals will steer you leeward of fresh veils then; follow.
- Entanglement. Keep kit tight. Anything that dangles will find a net. Practice a blind, breath-counted disentangle drill before you ever arrive.
- Thermal exposure. Vents can flash from pleasant to painful with a shrug of geology. Test with the back of a gloved hand before committing your body.
- The drop. The ridge ends. Do not step off the last cut into blue because something green flickered below. The steps shift seasonally; ask a keeper where the “last sure foot” is this week.
- Big jelly. If the stained-glass bell drifts up through a warm draft, hold position and let it pass. Ten meters is polite; five is foolish.

How to be useful
- Offer hands for slow work: knot dressing, edge-wrapping, pith shredding, chalk seeding. They trade in patience.
- Share maps only if made quietly. No sonar blasts; use passive photogrammetry with soft light and give them the data. They’ll add it to the rope memory.
- Bring skills in line maintenance, fiber proofing, or gentle corrosion control. A small vial of inert, aquatic-safe resin and the knowledge to apply it where rope chafes against new stone is worth more than a chest of coins.
- If you must bring objects: non-shedding ceramic needles, bone-smooth burnishers, and clean, unbranded line in diameters they request. Do not bring plastics that flake.

Health notes
- Tetanus up to date. Discuss Vibrio and marine wound prophylaxis with a travel medic. Bring a post-dive rinse plan for your skin; don’t do it in their gardens.
- Ear health: halocline crossings can make Eustachian tubes fickle. Equalize early and often.
- Post-dive fatigue is real here. The “green hush” plus small, continuous corrections wears you down. Schedule recovery before making big decisions back on your own side.

Communication basics (learn these before arrival)
- Rope-tap “I’m on your line,” “Passing,” “Help,” and “Cutting now.” Also hand signs for “hot,” “fresh,” “weight moving,” and “elder’s lane.”
- Proximity speech: mouth close to ear, one hand on the same line, other hand braced. Your bubbles (if any) go leeward. Even then, fewer words are better.

Things not to do
- No scooters, no spearguns, no strobes, no fishing, no loud pingers, no throwing weights to test depth, no “quick swims off the path.”
- Don’t ride counterweights or hitch to rising water-bellies.
- Don’t “improve” anything without being asked. Your idea of better may undo a season of learning in a single tug.

If something goes wrong
- Lost the path: stop, get neutral, clip your spool to the last sure line, and expand in a tight search, tapping at slow intervals. Sound travels poorly; the rope will bring help faster than your voice.
- Entangled and can’t free yourself: three slow tugs on the nearest line, pause, three more. That’s the local “come to me” call. If breath/time is short, indicate “cut” with your tool in hand and a held-out palm; they will show you where you can safely sever.
- Vent burn: retreat to ambient water, flood the suit to cool. Don’t run for a fresh-fall veil; abrupt density/temperature shifts can shock you silly. Treat on the ridge in calm.
- CCR failure: switch to bailout. Bubble etiquette ends where survival begins. Honest damage, promptly reported, is forgiven.

What it will feel like
- The first time you slide through a falling sheet of clear, sweet water in a green sea, your lungs will flinch and your brain will swear you are upside down. You aren’t. Touch the rope. Taste will change; pressure will kiss your ears; bubbles (if any) will jump. Then the world will steady, and a plaza will open like a soft-lit bell around you and you’ll hear the hum of a long net.
- A keeper will test your knots with a glance. If you pass, you will be shown how to stand where warmth goes, how to keep your wake away from tender things, how to hear a rock’s pop and say, “We leave this span for the day.”
- You will come away with your shoulders a little looser and your impatience suddenly loud to your own ears. This place makes patience the only speed. Let it set your metronome while you are there.

Simple checklist
- CCR, bailout, spares; mask/hood; cut-resistant suit; gloves; booties.
- Two cutters; reel/spool; low-lumen lights; slate; haptic clip.
- Med kit for cuts/burns; hydration gels; inline filter; electrolytes.
- Matte wraps; tight kit; decon done; no scooters, no strobes.
- Rope-talk basics memorized; invitation confirmed; rendezvous fixed; emergency plan agreed with keepers and your support.

If you arrive with quiet gear, a tidy profile, and the willingness to be taught by lines and water, the ridge will accept you. Everything else will be a lesson in how weight, warmth, and time can be shaped by patience into a place.
