Orientation
- You’re about to walk into a single organism wearing the ruins of a small works as jewelry. Think “living factory-cathedral,” warm and perpetually just-after-rain. Surfaces are filmed with water; sound is continuous; time keeps a slow, confident tempo. Straight lines and right angles are not hostile—here they’re honored and used.
- Treat the place as one body with lungs, plumbing, and a nervous patience. Your best preparation is to slow down until you can feel its breath and adjust your own.

Before you go
- Health and biosecurity:
  - Tetanus up to date. If you’re prone to mold or damp-induced asthma, bring your inhaler and consider a mask you can tolerate in humidity (lightweight, valved, or surgical—not a tight N95 unless you absolutely need it).
  - Fungal/dermatophyte prophylaxis: foot powder, antifungal cream, breathable socks. You will be damp.
  - Decontaminate on both sides. Brush, wash, and heat-dry footwear and clothing to avoid carrying spores/seeds in or out. No outside seeds, soil, or plant cuttings; no pets.
- Headspace and timing:
  - Budget more time than you think. The place will ask you to move at sap-rate. Plan a hard turnaround time and set a gentle alarm; it’s easy to lose hours.
  - Go in small groups (2–4). Designate a quiet lead and a sweep. Practice hand signals and low, steady calls.
- Conditioning:
  - Practice walking slowly but decisively on uneven, springy ground. Practice standing still and listening for minutes at a time without fidgeting.

What to expect
- Climate: mild heat, close humidity, constant condensation. Air smells like tea, cut cane, rain on metal, a memory of oil. Not swamp-rancid.
- Ground: springy knit of roots and rhizomes with anchored mosslike mats; below that, saturated, unseen water. Expect occasional hidden soft spots and flexing “floors.”
- Architecture: living columns with buttresses and low vaults; spans that are beams grown around air; old concrete/stone lines and pads that the plant keeps as edges. Translucent membrane “windows” of phloem occasionally stretch across gaps. A central brick pump house with slow, belt-driven flywheel.
- Sound: overlapping drips on leaf “drums,” soft cyclic chuff of the plant’s lungs, a distant, minute-by-minute click/creak as a flywheel takes another degree. The place does not answer quickly to abrupt noise.

Risks in plain terms
- Constant wet film = slip and drop hazard; gear fogs; skin softens and chafes.
- Hidden voids and sinking spots below the root mat; springy floors telegraph but you must listen with your feet.
- Slow, strong moving parts (plant-belts, flywheel): easy to underestimate. They won’t yank you, but they will not stop for you.
- Subtle pressure changes with the plant’s breath; can unsettle those with sensitive ears or anxiety.
- Allergen and microbe exposure typical of warm, wet, high-biomass interiors.
- Entrainment: the place’s tempo can seduce you into staying longer than is wise.

What to wear
- Feet: amphibious approach shoes or light boots with supple, siped rubber soles. Avoid stiff lug soles that scuff or puncture the living mat. Neoprene or wool socks; pack a dry pair. Aftercare: rinse, dry, antifungal powder.
- Legs: quick-dry, non-crinkly pants that brush off moisture and don’t snag easily. Avoid noisy fabrics.
- Torso: breathable wicking base, light softshell or vented rain shell with DWR; you’ll get filmed regardless—choose comfort over waterproof bragging rights.
- Hands: thin, grippy gloves (synthetic with textured palms). Pack a pair of nitriles in case you touch resins or biofilm and want a barrier.
- Head: wide-brim hat or soft hood; steady dripping will find your scalp. Glasses wearers: anti-fog treatment.
- Colors: low-key. The plant already plays with chevrons and signage; don’t compete.

What to carry
- Navigation/timekeeping:
  - Watch with gentle alarms; optional haptic metronome to check you’re not creeping into “plant time” too far.
  - Simple compass. GPS may be patchy under vaults; maps are useless inside. Your best compass is the humidity gradient and the feel of air movement.
- Light:
  - Headlamp with warm, low-lumen settings and diffuse beam. No strobes, no harsh flood. Red/amber is kindest.
- Poles:
  - One smooth-tipped walking staff to test ground. Rounded, no spikes.
- Dry management:
  - Roll-top drybag; zip pouches; silica gel packs; anti-fog wipes; microfiber cloth. Expect to wipe optics often.
- Water/food:
  - Bring your own water. If you must collect, take only slow drips off inert surfaces, then filter + carbon. Avoid boiling and flame. Cold-soak food; use flameless heaters if needed.
- First aid:
  - Wound-cleaning kit (sterile saline, iodine), waterproof bandages, antihistamine, anti-chafe barrier, antifungal cream/powder.
- Misc:
  - Lightweight sit/kneel pad (closed-cell foam) so you don’t bruise living spans or soak yourself while resting.
  - Picaridin insect repellent (unscented). DEET is messy in constant wet and can harm plastics.
  - Small hygrometer/barometer if you’re nerdy; it’s satisfying to sync with the breath cycles.

What not to bring or do
- No fire, flames, smoke, or hot stoves. The plant closes pores and “remembers” offense for hours.
- No drones, no loudspeakers, no high-lumen floods. Rotor wash disrupts local weather; sharp light and noise clash with the tempo.
- No chalk, tape, paint, or adhesives to mark routes. The plant will either mimic or erase them.
- No knives or prybars for “testing;” no climbing on living beams; no tugging at belts or flywheels.
- No perfumes, strong deodorants, fertilizer salts, or anything likely to leach.
- Don’t leave ropes, straps, or straight-edged objects behind. They will be adopted.

How to move
- Entering:
  - Feel for the boundary: the air goes softly warm and wet, the light breaks into a million reflections. Pause there. Breathe. Let your clothes darken a shade. Then step in.
  - Where a cilia-curtain hangs, don’t force it. Announce yourself in a low, even voice and wait; you’ll feel the air “listen” and part over a minute or two.
- Footwork:
  - Step on the tough, small-leaf mats; avoid glossy, translucent panels and obviously saturated dark patches. Test before committing weight.
  - Spread load; avoid jumping or stamping. Keep to old straight pads and edges when you find them; the plant has already decided those are good places to bear weight.
- Pace and sound:
  - Move at half-speed. Talk softly and continuously rather than calling out. Your breath and voice are cues; the place likes steadiness.
  - If you drop something, let the drips finish their phrase before reaching—sounds are not just decoration here; they’re plumbing cues.
- Touch:
  - Back of fingers before palm if you must brush aside leaves; palms stick and smear the water film.
  - Don’t press on buttress junctions or cross-braced fibers; they are working members.
- Resting:
  - Sit on old concrete or stone when possible, or your pad. Avoid leaning against belts or membrane walls.

Etiquette with the machinery
- The pump house is a guest-right place. If the doorway hairs don’t part, wait and speak again. Inside, stay clear of the belt path. Never oil, clean, or “fix” anything. Don’t block the sump outlets. Keep scarves, loose hair, and straps stowed.

Reading the room
- Signs you’re stressing it:
  - Drip chorus thins or desynchronizes.
  - Leaf bowls tip their water away from you repeatedly.
  - The slow chuff becomes hesitant or shifts phase from your breathing.
  - Your match-lit equivalent (bright headlamp burst, sudden noise) yields a long, stubborn quiet.
- When you notice any of the above: stop, go quieter and slower, or backtrack to a drier, brighter gradient.

Navigation and finding your way out
- The edges are marked by shallow canals/ditches where the saturated mass meets open water. If you need to leave, walk toward cooler skin, brighter light, and less overlap of sound.
- Old human lines—straight footings, squared pads, porcelain insulator runs—form reliable corridors. The plant keeps these as boundaries; follow them rather than cutting diagonals.
- If you feel air drawing in around you (in-breath), wait for the out-breath before crossing narrow spans; everything slackens slightly on the exhale.

Water and food
- Don’t drink freely from the place. The tannins and resins are real, and there’s a ghost of machine chemistry. If you must: slow drips off inert stone, then filter through ceramic + carbon and UV. Better: carry what you need for the visit and eat simple, odor-light foods.

Wildlife
- Expect little that needs you: perhaps a pallid eel in a run, a moth like a dull coin, a single frog syllable. Don’t attempt to feed, photograph with flash, or handle. If a bird appears, it is nobody’s business but its own.

Emergency thinking
- Slip/fall into a soft spot: don’t thrash. Spread out, let the mat rebound, roll toward tougher, small-leaf textures. Partners: extend the smooth staff; pull slowly with steady pressure.
- Caught clothing in a slow belt: go with it for a beat to slacken tension, then reverse the twist or cut the garment. The belt will not speed up; you have time if you don’t panic.
- Light fails: sit. Listen for the nearest drip drum and follow the brightness it implies; water seeks open shafts. Or wait; your eyes will take the place’s measure.
- If you feel claustrophobia or pressure panic: match your inhale to two cycles of drip, exhale over three. The plant’s breath is longer than yours; borrowing its count helps.

Aftercare and exit
- At the humidity boundary, stop. Brush and wipe down gear; check soles, cuffs, and seams for dust-fine seeds and film. Bag waste; nothing leaves but stories.
- Wash and thoroughly dry feet and any skin that stayed wet. Treat hot spots and folds. Launder warm; dry hot or freeze to kill hitchhikers.
- Expect to feel “slow” for a while afterward. Don’t rush straight into a noisy demand. Drink water; savor the lingering taste of tannin and iron; let your tempo reset gently.

A compact checklist
- Soft-soled amphibious footwear + spare socks
- Quick-dry clothing, light shell, brimmed hat
- Thin grip gloves + nitriles
- Smooth-tipped walking staff
- Roll-top drybag, silica packs, microfiber, anti-fog
- Warm, low-lumen headlamp
- Water (carry) + carbon filter backup
- First aid incl. antifungal, antihistamine, barrier cream
- Watch with alarms; small compass
- Lightweight sit pad
- Unscented picaridin
- Decon kit (brush, bags)

Final mindset
- You’re not just visiting a place; you’re entering a habit. Offer steadiness, patience, soft edges, and you’ll be shown halls and breaths you can feel in your bones. Try to hurry it, and it will simply continue at its own speed until you remember how to move again.
