What it is
- A low-roofed, tide-cut limestone labyrinth where the ocean breathes in and out through stone. Cool, wet air; almost no light; sound that piles on itself until it is its own weather. Every surface is alive or shaped by long water. Space is tight. Tides and storms re-write the floor plan.

What it feels like
- You will hear before you see. Pressure waves from surf, pebbles rolling, drip-networks, wing-burrs, bat-colony murmur, the small, continuous work of invertebrates. The dark is textured and damp. The ceiling presses, the floor moves—literally, with living carpets. You will be a large, clumsy mammal in a place optimized for smaller bodies and for touch and sound.

Mindset to bring
- Move like a guest in a packed house. Go slow. Assume each handhold and foothold is someone’s home. Trade sight for proprioception. Accept that the room can change because the sea decides. Humility is safety gear here.

Timing
- Go on a very low swell with neap tides, at the beginning of a long low-tide window. You want slack low, not the turn. Give yourself double the time you think you’ll need to get out before the tide returns.
- Avoid storm days and the first big swell after a calm period—sneaker sets reach far into cave systems.
- If there’s a major bat colony, consider timing your visit to when most adults are out foraging (after dusk) only if the tide and swell allow a safe, short, non-penetrating look; otherwise go at a time of least disturbance and keep far from the roost chambers. If pups are present, don’t enter roost areas at all.

Personal condition
- Not for claustrophobes or those who panic with loud, continuous noise. Practice five minutes of slow breathing in a closet with a fan on high; if your body can’t settle, choose another destination.
- Tetanus booster up to date. Rabies pre-exposure series strongly advised if you’ll ever be in bat spaces. If immunocompromised, prone to severe infections, or with open wounds, skip this site—saltwater Vibrio, guano-borne fungi, and general microbial richness are not your friends here.

What to wear
- Helmet with a chin strap; low-profile to avoid catching the ceiling. Add a soft brim/visor to deflect drips.
- Sticky rubber footwear with wide, soft contact (approach shoes, canyoneering boots, or felt-sole river boots). Avoid studs or cleats that shred living crusts. Neoprene socks for warmth.
- Durable, non-absorbent layers: thin neoprene or synthetic base with a abrasion-resistant outer. No cotton. Bring a light shell to break the chill of constant damp.
- Smooth knee and elbow pads to crawl without grinding.
- Gloves: thin neoprene under a cut-resistant, non-textured overglove. You want grip without sawing life off the rock.

What to carry
- Lights: three independent lights per person. Primary headlamp with a very low setting and warm/red filter; secondary headlamp; small handheld flood for brief, careful scans. Spare batteries in a dry bag. Keep beams short, diffused, and aimed down. No strobes, no flash on wildlife.
- Hearing: musician’s earplugs (attenuate volume but keep fidelity). You need to hear shape and surf timing without fatigue.
- Air awareness: a compact multi-gas meter (O2, CO2, H2S). Sea-cave air is usually flushed, but pockets can go sour near guano heaps or dead ends.
- Respiratory protection: a light P2/N95 or, better, P100 for any foray near dry guano shelves; keep it bagged and clean when not needed.
- Navigation: a small spool of thin guideline to run and retrieve (never fix to living encrustations), or a buddy line between travelers. Paper and pencil for a sketch map. A watch with vibration alarms for tide countdown. GPS won’t help inside; a phone can record inertial breadcrumbs in airplane mode if you have such an app.
- Safety: short length of rope for handlines, two non-locking carabiners, compact first-aid kit (sterile water, pressure bandage, hemostatic gauze, steri-strips, tweezers, broad-spectrum topical antibiotic), emergency bivy sack, chemical heat pack. Whistle is largely useless in the roar; establish touch signals.
- Water and food: carry your own drinking water in a sealed bladder; do not drink from the fresh lenses—they’re microbially rich and easily ruined. Pack small, sealed, non-crumbing food; no wrappers loose, everything double-bagged.
- Decon kit: stiff brush, dedicated cave footwear/clothing bag, and an approved disinfectant per local white-nose–syndrome/fungal protocols (check current guidance before travel).

Biosecurity, both ways
- Before: arrive with completely clean and dry gear. If you’ve been in other caves recently, follow decontamination guidelines to the letter. White-nose syndrome in bats and a variety of cave fungi/bugs hitchhike on fabric and mud.
- After: decon again before entering another cave or returning home. Wash and sanitize soles, knee pads, gloves, and any rope. Do not wash cave mud down storm drains.

How to move
- Keep the group tiny (two or three). Establish hand-on-shoulder or short buddy-line travel in the darkest constrictions. Pre-arrange taps: one tap stop, two tap go, three tap trouble.
- Step only on bare, already-polished stone or loose, lifeless cobble. If every route crosses living mats, turn back.
- Three points of contact, always. Test each hold. Expect sudden air slaps when waves hit angles—brace before corners.
- Crawl don’t clatter: your knees and palms distribute weight better than boot edges and hurt fewer animals.
- Minimize light and voice. Your light is a tool, not a torch to “see” everything. Use the low setting, shield with your palm, and sweep slowly.
- Do not touch roosting bats or nesting birds; don’t aim beams at them; don’t linger beneath guano drips. If the colony stirs because of you, you’re too close.
- Avoid stirring bioluminescent pools for fun. One gentle touch to witness is enough; repeated disturbance wastes their energy and draws predators.

Hazards to respect
- Tides and swell: your exit can drown. The cave tells you this in the pitch of the roar and the reach of wet stains on walls. If you’re below the freshest, glossier tide line when time is short, you’re in the wrong place.
- Sneaker waves: a long quiet can hide a set that charges far in and blasts air through constrictions. Keep one eye (and ear) on the surf rhythm; assign a watcher when near entrances.
- Slippery life: barnacles and mussels slice. Anemones and limpets crush easily and are impossible to “un-step.” If you’re about to plant a foot on anything that reacts, don’t.
- Cuts and infections: treat every nick immediately; rinse with clean water, disinfect, cover. Watch for signs of marine infection (escalating redness, heat).
- Air: if the meter shows low O2 or high CO2/H2S, back out. Headaches, dizziness, or a sweet-rotten note in the smell are also warnings.
- Guano: fungal spores can cause serious lung infections. Mask up if you must pass a dry pile. Don’t sit or crawl through it.
- Bites and stings: centipedes, crustacean spines, and the occasional unseen fish or eel in pans. Don’t put your hands where you can’t see; don’t wade blindly.

Sensing and orienting
- Let your ears acclimate before moving. The high, bell-like drips usually mark fresh seep lines and safer footing; the flat thunder marks open connections to the sea. The sudden, tight slap of air means a constriction ahead—crouch.
- Feel for temperature and moisture gradients with the back of your hand; fresh air drafts often outline exit options.
- Learn the floor’s “voice.” A whisper means fine pebbles; a seethe means living carpet. Choose the quietest step.
- Darkness here is data. Spending a minute with lights off will teach you where the space opens (the roar softens) and where it pinches (pressure rises on your skin).

Etiquette in a living maze
- Touch less, stay shorter, step lighter. You are loud and large in a place that runs on small exchanges. Minimize your footprint to the time it takes to pass once.
- Take only the memory. No shells, no guano, no photographs that required disturbing wildlife to get.
- Keep your chemistry to yourself: no bug spray, sunscreen, soaps, or leaky batteries in this watershed. If you must apply something, do it before you approach and cover skin with clothing.

If something goes wrong
- Trapped by rising water: find the highest dry bench above the freshest tide stain and wait out the cycle (6 hours can feel very long; that’s what the bivy and warm layer are for). Conserve heat, dim lights, share body warmth, and keep boots on.
- Injury: stop bleeding, clean, and immobilize. You likely can’t be reached inside; self-rescue back to the mouth is your plan A. This is why you warned a shore contact with a timed check-in and a “call for help if we are not out by X” instruction.
- Disorientation: stop moving. Mark your spot. Breathe until you can feel the surf rhythm again. Use your line or backtrack by touch to last known anchor.

How to leave it better
- Pack out everything, including micro-trash and your own waste. If you must urinate, do it below the marine splash line where the next tide will reclaim it; everything above is a nutrient-poor system where your input matters too much.
- On exit, inspect your gear for hitchhikers—amphipods in cuffs, snails on soles—and return them gently to the splash zone.
- Report hazards you noticed (unstable ceilings, new collapses) and significant wildlife observations to local stewards if the site is monitored.

A short packing list recap
- Helmet; sticky-soled footwear; neoprene socks; abrasion-resistant clothing; smooth pads; cut-resistant neoprene gloves
- Three lights with warm/red options; spare batteries
- Musician’s earplugs; P100 mask (guano zones)
- Gas meter
- Short rope/handline; small guideline spool; two carabiners
- First aid; emergency bivy; heat pack
- Water; compact food
- Watch with tide alarms; paper/pen
- Decontamination kit; dry bags; change of warm layer
- Buddy you trust, and a shore contact who knows your plan

If you can accept the rules—sound over sight, touch over stride, patience over conquest—this place will teach you to read a room that is alive down to its seams. And then you’ll leave, quietly, before the sea remembers you are there.
