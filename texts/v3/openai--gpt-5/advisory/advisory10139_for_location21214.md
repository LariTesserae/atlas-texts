Field guide for a hot nave in the thin air

What you’re walking into
- A high, cliffside basalt chamber saturated with dry heat and dense, anoxic, acidic gases. Think hot autoclave crossed with a very slow tidal pool of heavy vapor. The “air” pools like syrup in basins and gutters; sound dies in it; water is functionally absent.
- Surfaces are alive. The floor is a living conveyor of fingernail‑sized grazers; walls host clamp‑feeding plates; the high black hosts hanging sacs that drop beads of molten salts. Predators move by temperature map, geometry, and timing, not by chase. Signals are slow pressure waves and thermal gradients. Noise, sparks, breezes, and liquid water are “loud.”
- Someone or something has farmed here: three leaning columns with incised grooves that focus gas pulses to shelter the very small. Anchored glass threads arc across the void—some intact, many snapped. Outside, sail‑creatures shutter the mouth when winds fail, turning the place into a muffled kiln.

Primary hazards (assume all apply)
- Atmosphere: Dense, oxygen‑free, likely CO2/SO2/HCl/HF mix; hyper‑dry; corrosive; immediately incapacitating without a sealed suit. Even a breath will damage your lungs/eyes.
- Heat: Ambient radiant heat and hot gas; rock conducts heat unhurriedly but relentlessly. You can burn through your boots just by standing still.
- Chemistry: Chloride/sulfate rimes, molten salts, acid gases; HF is possible. Skin/eye contact is dangerous; equipment corrosion is rapid if unprotected.
- Gravity and geometry: Overhung drop, parapet of living bodies, snapped glass tethers, hidden notches that “remember” traps.
- Biology: Everything senses by heat and pressure. Your body heat, breath currents, and light make you a beacon. Molten salt drops from above. Predators reset geometry rather than pounce; if you trip a trap, there’s no second chance.
- Dynamics: Periodic “breaths” every few minutes. External sails may close the mouth without warning, spiking temperature and cutting your exit.
- Ethics/impact: Every inch is occupied; one footfall crushes a city. Water, lint, microbes, and noise from you will rewrite local rules for hours to years.

Go/no‑go in one minute
- If you cannot guarantee a fully sealed, acid/heat‑resistant suit with independent life support and thermal control, do not enter.
- If you cannot keep your external thermal signature within a few degrees of ambient (or be willing to stay at the lip only), do not enter.
- If you cannot decontaminate completely without water, do not enter.
- If you are prone to altitude sickness, claustrophobia, panic in heat, or you lack precise foot placement under zero visibility, do not enter.

The low‑impact way (recommended)
- Stay outside the mouth, on inert cliff rock that is clearly not colonized (use magnification to check). Do not stand on, brush, or shade any of the exterior “sails.”
- Use a long, thin, chemically inert borescope/probe (sapphire window; PTFE/FEP sheath; Inconel backbone) to look and sense from the threshold.
- Sensing stack (passive first): mid/long‑wave thermal imager rated for 200–300 C scenes; millimeter‑wave radar for structure and motion; passive ion/acid gas sampler with sealed micro‑ampule collection; narrowband visible camera with extreme low‑light sensitivity. Avoid floodlights and sonar.
- Log at least one full pulse cycle (10–20 minutes) before you even think about a deeper look. If sails start to fold, retract and leave.

If you insist on going in
- Suit and life support
  - Full positive‑pressure, sealed suit with outer shell of fluoropolymer (PTFE/FEP) over ceramic‑coated aramid or basalt fiber; all fasteners and rings in Hastelloy C‑276 or Inconel 625.
  - Helmet visor in sapphire or ALON; avoid standard glass (HF risk).
  - Closed‑circuit rebreather or SCBA inside the suit. No exhaust to the chamber; all moisture stays inside.
  - Active cooling: liquid loop with phase‑change buffer plus a tethered radiator left in the cold, thin outside air. Without an external heat dump you will overheat. Put a quick‑release on the tether and a timer on your internal heat budget.
  - Padding and soles in ceramic or PEEK; avoid rubberized soles (melting/chemistry).
  - Intrinsically safe electronics; all circuits potted; no exposed metal edges; anti‑static everywhere.
  - Minimal external convection: no fans that vent; no flapping webbing. Baffles around any necessary intakes/vents for suit internals.

- Tools and materials
  - Mobility: two or four‑point ceramic “stilts” or footplates with rounded, thumbnail‑sized pads to distribute weight; place only on dead rock verified by camera—if you can’t identify any, don’t step.
  - Line: PTFE‑sheathed Inconel micro‑cable for handlines; anchors placed only in pre‑existing bolt holes or clearly lifeless, flaking basalt. No new holes.
  - Light: head‑mounted, dim, narrowband visible (blue‑green) at the lowest usable lumen level; use only as a last resort. Prefer amplified low‑light and thermal. Cover light with a sliding shutter so you can vanish instantly.
  - Navigation: mm‑wave map overlay on your visor that updates passively; mark “do not step” zones for fissures, notches, and driplines beneath ceiling sacs.
  - Comms: bone‑conduction or hard‑line to a partner outside; RF may be absorbed/scattered by dense gas. Keep your voice low; better, use click‑codes through the line.
  - Sampling (only if permitted by your ethics): collect only loose dust already fallen, snapped glass thread ends that are already detached, or one of the mysterious marbles if you find one outside living flow. Use ceramic scoops and PTFE vials. Never wet anything.
  - Emergency kit: PTFE patch tape, ceramic scraper (for flicking off molten salt), calcium gluconate gel (HF contingency; do not apply inside the chamber unless to save a life), thermal burn dressings for after exit.

- Movement rules
  - Arrive during a period when the exterior sails are open and stable. Stage everything outside; rehearse your exit route.
  - Before you cross the threshold, stand down your external temperature: let the suit’s outer surface equilibrate to ambient by pausing at the lip. Do not step in radiating hot or cold patches.
  - Move only on a held breath of the chamber (between surges), or keep your pace steady enough to avoid creating pressure waves. If a surge comes, become a statue: let it pass. Many things freeze during pulses; match them.
  - Keep your outline small; limbs tucked. Every gesture is a weather event here.
  - Avoid:
    - Fissure lines with salt blooms (feeding lanes).
    - The driplines beneath ceiling sacs (watch for tiny glowing beads in your thermal feed).
    - Floor notches and any repeating groove geometry (old trap memories; predators cue to them).
    - The inner faces of the three leaning columns (they are functional infrastructure for farming; altering laminar flow harms the smallest grazers).
    - The parapet at the overhang—it’s a living migration track and nursery; also the highest body density.
  - Never sit or lean on a wall. Heat transfer through contact can spike and cook you or the wall’s inhabitants.

- Light, sound, and breath discipline
  - Lights off unless absolutely necessary. If you must use light, keep it tight, low, and short. Never sweep light up to the ceiling sacs.
  - Absolutely no open sparks. Ground yourself and your line. Your suit should be anti‑static; even so, avoid brushing filaments or dangling threads.
  - Your breath, fan hums, and pump pulses all count as signals here. Use DC pumps; avoid choppy PWM; add pulse dampers to fluid loops to eliminate periodic signatures.

- Time in
  - Assume you have 15–20 minutes of safe internal heat budget per incursion without a robust external radiator; with a tethered radiator in cold outside air, you might double that. Monitor internal core temperature; if you rise 1 C above baseline, back out.
  - Respect the local clock: learn the pulse period at the lip, then work in multiples of it. Plan your exit to start no later than two pulses before your worst‑case heat limit.

Contingencies
- If the sails start to close: Exit immediately. The chamber will heat and quiet further, and your radiator may lose its temperature gradient.
- If you trigger a predator reset (you’ll see a sudden, precise motion, not a chase): freeze. Do not back into a notch or under a sac. When it resets again, back away along your own footprints.
- If a molten salt bead lands on you: flick it off with the ceramic scraper in one fast motion. Do not brush with fabric. Expect a chemical burn to the suit’s outer layer—inspect for breach later.
- If you step on the living mat: stop; lift; reassess. Do not “shuffle clear”—that makes a wider swath of damage and disturbance. If you’ve crushed, you’ve already done harm; prioritize leaving the fewest additional footprints.
- Suit breach (acid/heat): retreat now. Outside, rinse with dry gas if you can; only use liquid decon (HF antidotes, water) once you are well away and will not drip into the habitat.
- Disorientation in the dark: anchor, mark your location on the line, and wait for one full pulse to re‑orient by the movement of grazers in your thermal feed. Never wander.

Leave‑no‑trace and ethics
- Do not introduce water, organic lint, skin flakes, or microbes. Your suit must be clean‑baked or plasma‑sterilized pre‑entry; filters single‑use and contained.
- Do not alter grooves, threads, or parapets. Those are structures with function, not scenery.
- Do not take living specimens. If you must collect, restrict yourself to already detached abiotic pieces (fallen glass thread ends, black “sail dust,” a single marble if it lies outside any living lane).
- Park no instruments that change flow, heat, or chemistry. If you deploy a logger, make it passive, small, chemically inert, and time‑limited with a retrieval plan.

Approach and exit (outside the mouth)
- You’re at altitude. Acclimatize. The outside is thin, cold, and windy, the inside is dense, hot, and still. Your body hates that contrast; plan rest buffers.
- Approach on dead rock only. The exterior sails are living; if they are folded flat across the mouth, do not peel them back or wedge yourself through.
- Anchor your outside radiator and lifeline to the cliff in pre‑existing placements. Keep the line taut but not singing in the wind.
- Stage a partner outside. They watch the sails, wind, and your tether, and they time the pulses on your behalf.

What to pack (short list)
- Suit as specified above; redundant seals; sapphire visor; ceramic‑soled boots.
- Closed‑circuit life support; redundant O2; no exhale to environment.
- Tethered external radiator with quick‑release; cold‑side fins for thin mountain air; thermal straps inside suit.
- Passive sensors: thermal, mm‑wave, low‑light camera, pressure/flow logger; minimal active LiDAR as backup; all intrinsically safe.
- Inert handline and anchors; ceramic stilts/plates if you must bear weight inside.
- Ceramic scraper; PTFE patch kit; calcium gluconate gel (for HF exposure after exit); dry‑gas blower for decon.
- Fluoropolymer vials and ceramic scoops for passive sample retrieval (dust only).
- Altitude kit: acetazolamide if you use it, but don’t rely on it; warm clothing for the outside wait; hot drink thermos—you’ll want it after.

Before you go in
- Dry decon your suit and gear; bake if you can. No moisture cargo.
- Practice moving like a piece of furniture: slow, precise, no flourish.
- Memorize one full pulse pattern from the lip: duration, lull, surge; note when ceiling sacs inflate/deflate relative to pulses.

On departure and aftercare
- Reverse the route, stepping where you stepped. Do not shake yourself off at the mouth; you’ll shower the entrance in foreign debris. Use the dry‑gas blower away from any living surface.
- Bag and seal everything. No trail‑mix of salts or dust back to some wetter world.
- Inspect the suit for acid etching, hot spots, or micro‑breaches; neutralize externally, rinse only in a place where runoff cannot reach soil or water.

Reading the room while you’re there
- Heat is the language. If your own thermal map shows sudden, neat diagonals or repeating arcs lighting up across the floor, that’s the farmer’s structure focusing the pulse—stay clear and appreciate from afar.
- If your mm‑wave returns outline dangling threads in a catenary arc above you, move out from under them; snapped or not, they’re part of spanning infrastructure and they shed dust when disturbed.
- If your pressure logger shows a deep, long hold—a “choking” pause—expect the whole chamber to become exquisitely sensitive. Do not move then. The smallest grazers will orient bellies‑up to ride free ions; treat that as sacrosanct.

A last word
- This place is old and crowded; your presence is a weather front. The most skillful visit is the one in which the chamber does not “notice” you at all. If that sounds impossible, stand at the threshold with your sensors and let the breath of the mountain count out your time there. You’ll see more by being less.
