What your body will feel first
- A deep, disciplined cold just above and below freezing, utterly saturated with moisture. You won’t see droplets so much as threads and skins of water that form, flow sideways, and vanish.
- Constant low-frequency vibration. You won’t hear it so much as carry it in your ribs and teeth. It can make you nauseated, anxious, and clumsy if you don’t prepare for it.
- Resistance from the ground. Everything is coated, slick, alive, and slightly springy; silt can turn to sieve under your weight.
- The air will try to take your heat and your exhale and use them. The place will answer your breath with rime. Creatures will come to taste that. You want to give it nothing.

Prime hazards in plain words
- Infrasound and standing waves: Sound here isn’t background; it shapes matter. Make the wrong sound in the wrong spot and you can loosen the ground beneath you or call something that eats wavefronts.
- Reflections behave like doors: Breaking a perfect mirror-surface can move you toward steeper temperature gradients and hungrier things. Never step into a specular reflection.
- Ubiquitous biofilms and stinging cilia: The “water” is full of charge-harvesters that can flash-freeze skin and electronics. The “mud” is a sieve of mouths and bristles. Treat all surfaces as reactive.
- Exhalations of warm brine and gas: Regular, forceful, and sometimes corrosive. Composition unknown; assume low oxygen and possible H2S/CO2. Their timing matters to your route.
- Electricity as appetite: Slow, creeping charge seeks edges and contrasts. Anything that leaks EM or static will be investigated, colonized, or drained.
- Nothing is empty: Any niche you make—footprint, drip, pause—will be filled by organisms or fields within minutes. Don’t linger in one geometry.

How to dress your soft human body
- Suit: A sealed, flexible drysuit rated for saturated freezing conditions with an omniphobic, non-stick outer skin. Choose materials that don’t chatter or crinkle; quiet matters. Outer layer should be matte and non-reflective.
- Thermal: Internal active heating with heat-exchanger that recycles exhalation heat into your core. Keep the suit’s outside skin near ambient to avoid creating strong thermal gradients.
- Respiratory: Closed-circuit rebreather with moisture and CO2 scrubbers; no free exhale. Condense and retain your water vapor. Use a throat mic or bone-conduction comm; keep your mouth shut to the world.
- Hands/feet: Tactile, sealed gloves with abrasion resistance and sting-proofing; insulated boots with compliant, high-friction silicone soles. Fit optional snap-on “matting” pads that distribute weight and damp vibration.
- Electrical discipline: Wear a suit-integrated dissipative layer (10^7–10^9 Ω to environment) so your potential equalizes slowly without arcs. Keep all sharp edges and antennas covered. No dangling leads.
- Eyes/face: Anti-fog visor with polarization and variable contrast to read films, flow-lines, and Chladni patterns. Add a narrow-band notch filter tuned to the dominant vent tones to reduce disorientation.
- Hearing: Infrasound-filtering ear protection that preserves situational low-frequency awareness through processed feed, not direct air. You want your balance intact, but the raw bass can scramble you.

What to carry
- A staff that doesn’t sing: Collapsible probe with a soft, non-resonant tip to test silt, film thickness, and charge without pinging the soundfield.
- Roll-out path: A thin, non-reflective, open-cell mesh you can lay across mirror-films to cross without puncturing them. Wide enough to avoid concentrated loads. Retrieve as you go.
- Scatter medium: Small supply of sterile, inert, ultra-matte microbeads or lattice flakes to kill dangerous reflections ahead (use sparingly; this place occupies anything you gift it).
- “Quiet crampons”: Clip-on microspikes made of compliant composite. Metal-on-stone is a bad noise here.
- Tether and winch: Quiet, low-resonance line to a partner or anchor. Hand winch with vibration isolation, not a motor that hums into the ground.
- Field sensors: 
  - Infrasound/FFT analyzer to read cycles and see the rails of damp/dry in real time.
  - EM/ESD meter to watch creeping charge and avoid becoming a lightning rod for slow lightning.
  - Gas sniffer (H2S/CO2/O2) inside your suit as a sanity check only; do not open-circuit.
  - Thermal imager to visualize warm exhalations and “breathing” ground.
- Comms and nav: Fiber tether for data to your team. Shielded, low-leak radios may work poorly; don’t count on them. Dead-reckoning IMU plus a map that updates to vent rhythm; GPS-like tools are often fooled by the endlessly similar plain.
- First aid:
  - Frostnip-to-frostbite protocol; chemical warmers for insulated use only.
  - Eye wash sealed within suit system; never break seal.
  - Treatment for sting-like burns and contact reactions; antihistamines and pain control.
  - Anti-nausea meds for infrasound sickness.
- Decon kit: Suit-safe rinse and wipes to remove biofilms from seals and joints before they colonize them.
- Power: All electronics double-shielded in a Faraday enclosure with filtered feedthroughs. No exposed leads. Carry a sacrificial decoy pack if local fauna insist on “drinking” your charge; keep it physically isolated.

How to move and when
- Learn the cycles before a footstep. Watch at least three full vent breaths (quietening thunder ~17 minutes to a hoot, then inhale) and a fog-belt reversal (every ~3 hours) from a safe dolomite spine. Build your crossing to those beats.
- Step only on the “old paths”: the hardened dolomite ridges that don’t weep, and your roll-out mesh when you must cross films. If your boot sinks more than an inch, back out along your own footprint immediately; don’t twist.
- Kill the mirror. If you must cross a specular film, dull it first with mesh or scatter; never put your weight through a perfect reflection.
- Be deliberately arrhythmic. Don’t fall into a metronome. The small hunter that strikes into tomorrow listens for predictable intervals. Vary stride length, pause lengths, and any tool taps.
- Stay quiet in the way that matters. Avoid sharp, narrowband sounds: no clacking poles, no metal cups, no zippers against plates. Your suit and gear should not provide edges for the sound to grip. Speak only via comm.
- Avoid crossings of strong tones. Where two basslines braid and sheets of water hang in the air, go around by a wide margin. Those sheets shatter into frost and fog that can flash-freeze your gear.
- Don’t block a valve-forest or a dermal slit. If you feel the ground swell like a sleeping thing, you’re on a bellows. Move off the grain of bulging, never across it.

How to see and listen well
- Read the floor. Chladni-like lines of damp/dry make rails you can follow, but they’re also highways for things that harvest the wavefield. If the pattern sharpens and your chest hum deepens, step out of the rail.
- Watch the weeping. Rock faces with thicker “tears” often mark a recent exhale; they can slick into traps underfoot. Finer beading suggests safer, older flows.
- Look for the hair-ice combs. They lift and sweep at “noon” and “dusk,” regardless of sun. Avoid getting downwind during a sweep; they rake silt and can scour suit seals.
- Filter the hum. Use your infrasound analyzer’s visual to keep you oriented without letting raw bass bully your inner ear.

How not to feed the place with your power
- Keep your lights dull and diffuse. Visible light is less interesting here than charge, but any lamp that creates sharp edges or warm spots builds gradients that things will graze. Use fiber-fed, low-EM, broad-beam illumination.
- Bleed static slowly. No rapid discharges. If you feel your hairs rise in the suit or see iridescent filaments reaching for you, freeze, let your dissipative layer do its job, and back away over your own prints once the glow gutters out.
- Keep batteries and coils inside shielding always. Never open a pack. Never “test a spark.”

Interacting with inhabitants without becoming a process yourself
- Don’t touch the bladders with cilia. Their defensive flash can frostbite through thin insulation and crash electronics.
- Give gliders above vents a wide berth; your turbulence and charge plume look like “food.” If they tack toward you, cut motion and wait; most will drift off once your gradients flatten.
- The winnowers and scrapers will find your footprints and drips. That’s not an attack; it’s maintenance. If you feel something reorganizing your boot tread, lift and relocate; don’t kick.
- If a spinal mound that grazes on standing waves turns toward you, you’ve made a wave. Still yourself, lower your profile, and let the field settle. Don’t try to outrun a geometry-eater on its own geometry.

Navigation and wayfinding that works here
- Treat distance as time in cycles, not kilometers. “One breath” is a unit. Plan legs that end before a hoot.
- Aim from ridge to ridge. The plain pretends to be flat, but there are always low dolomite spines. String your route spine-to-spine.
- Drop no breadcrumbs. Anything you leave becomes habitat quickly and may mislead you on the return. Use your inertial track and fiber tether markers you can retrieve.
- Beware of “mirrored roads.” Bands that look like wet steel may be condensing conveyors. They’ll carry your scent and static far. Cross them once, perpendicularly, on mesh if you must.

Camp and vehicles
- Don’t bivouac on the open plain. If you must stay, bring a tripod platform with compliant feet that rest on three separate dolomite spines. Isolate it acoustically from the ground. Better: operate in sorties from a shelter on higher, drier mineral ground.
- Avoid rotorcraft and anything that howls. Wash and tone set the factory in motion. If you need lift, use ducted fans in counter-phased, low-amplitude operation well away from the deck and land on prepared platforms only.

Emergencies and what to do
- Breakthrough into sieve-mud: Stop. Inflate your boot pads to increase surface area if you have them. Place mesh in front of you and crawl onto it. Do not thrash; you’ll ring the bell that summons cleaners.
- Flash-freeze sting: Don’t rub. Warm the zone from inside the suit with staged, controlled heat. Check electronics for brownouts; reboot from backup power only after the glow in the films around you subsides.
- Infrasound sickness (nausea, panic, tunnel vision): Get to a spine, sit low, engage ear protection with more aggressive filtering, and synchronize your breathing to your suit’s metronome—then desynchronize it by small random shifts after a minute. The point is to reassert your own rhythm without offering it to the field.
- Charge-creep swarming your gear: Power down nonessential circuits. Present a sacrificial, shielded decoy battery on the ground five meters away; back off quietly while the fauna attends to it.
- You punctured a perfect reflection and the world sharpened: Retreat along your exact approach path without stepping into any new mirrors. Throw scatter to kill fresh specular patches behind you if the air grows teeth.

Etiquette and ethics
- Sterilize in and out. Pressure-wash and UV-bake gear before entry; decon your suit and mesh before you leave. This ecosystem is complete without you; don’t gift it stowaways and don’t take souvenirs.
- Don’t feed with heat, sound, or charge. If you give the place a reliable subsidy, something will evolve (fast) to claim it.
- Sample remotely or not at all. If you must take a sample, do it from loose, already-mobile films at the very edge of your operating area using sterile, sealed tools. Replace volume with sterile, inert equivalent to avoid creating a niche.

Behavioral rules to tape inside your visor
- Quiet is not silence; it is not making edges for sound to grip.
- Dull the mirror or don’t enter.
- Vary everything you do enough that tomorrow can’t hear you.
- Step where the stone is oldest and driest.
- Watch three cycles before committing to one.
- Leave no vacancy; take nothing’s place.

Pre- and post-visit routine
- Before: Acclimate to controlled infrasound in a simulator for an hour a day for three days. Dry-run your rebreather and condensation loop. Practice walking arrhythmically with compliant soles on slick surfaces.
- On arrival: Spend one breath observing. Map the vent line, the fog-belt motion, the positions of valve-forests and rails. Pick a spine-to-spine route. Sync your team’s timers to the hoot.
- After: Decon everything. Inspect seals for hair-ice scoring and biofilm growth. Archive your infrasound and EM recordings; navigation next time will be more about recognizing a process than a landmark.

What it will be like, if you do it right
- You will feel like a misplaced warmth in an enormous, careful factory that politely ignores you as long as you don’t jam a gear. Your breath will stay yours. Your footsteps will be forgotten while you’re still making them. Everything around you will continue its cycles as if your hour there were a blink. That is success.

If you cannot commit to sealing your breath, dulling your edges, and keeping your own rhythms private, do not go. This plain makes everything into a process. You are no exception.
