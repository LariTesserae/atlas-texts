Orientation
- What it is: A newly built, lime-plastered cistern under a courtyard, completely flooded with warm, moving, high‑pH water. It’s lit from a square oculus above where water falls through a wooden grate. Terracotta lips, weirs, floats, shutters, valves, and grates shape the flows. It is loud. There is no air space inside.
- Why it exists: To condition/clarify water, cool air above, cure lime, and harvest salts—an instrument as much as a container. Young algae are just starting; almost no animal life. Edges are crisp; mechanisms are tuned.
- What this means for you: You will be fully submerged. Currents change as floats move. Surfaces are hard, sharp-edged, sometimes slick. The water is caustic enough to sting eyes and dry skin. Entry/exit depend on people above.

Can you go without breathing gear?
- Briefly, yes—if you’re a calm, competent breath‑hold swimmer with a topside tender and a retrieval plan. But the safest way to actually experience the place is with a small, redundant air source (e.g., a pony bottle or “spare air”) and a lifeline. There is no breathable pocket inside, and the only usable opening is the oculus/grate above.

Before you go
- Permission and team: Get the caretakers’ consent. Arrange a topside team who can open/lift the grate, manage flow shutters if needed, tend a line, and haul you out. Establish fail‑safes before anyone enters.
- Timing: Midday gives the most spectacular light and the strongest inflow; dawn/late day are calmer but dimmer. If it’s your first time, go when flow is modest so you can understand the currents.
- Health check: Avoid if you have eye injuries, very sensitive skin, open wounds, or dental work that vibratory shock bothers. Asthmatics and those prone to panic in enclosed spaces should observe from above.
- Don’t plan to drink: The water tastes chalky; the pH is high. Bring your own potable water for after.

What to bring (and why)
- Breathing and safety
  - Low‑volume freediving mask or full‑face swim mask to keep alkaline water out of eyes and sinuses.
  - Mini air source (3–6 cu ft) with a simple regulator, or a small rebreather if trained. A snorkel is largely useless here.
  - Backup light and a tiny slate/grease pencil for timing/notes; light is good at noon, less so otherwise.
  - Line and retrieval: 10–15 m of thin static line, a comfortable sit‑harness or chest sling, and carabiners. Pre‑rig a belay from above. Agree on rope tug signals.
  - Cutting tool for entanglement emergencies only: blunt‑tip line cutter or shears secured to your person. Do not casually cut site ropes.
- Skin, eyes, and ears
  - Full‑coverage swim skin or thin wetsuit/rashguard and water shoes. The suit protects against alkalinity and sharp edges; shoes grip tile.
  - Neoprene gloves to handle slick tiles safely and avoid skin oils leaching into the water.
  - Saline eye wash and a small bottle of dilute vinegar (food‑grade, 1:10 in clean water) for post‑immersion neutralizing rinse. Avoid contact lenses.
  - Soft earplugs or a thin hood to slightly damp sound; underwater sound still conducts via bone.
- Carry minimal metal: Stainless or titanium hardware only. Leave copper/brass jewelry at home. Avoid loose straps, tassels, or anything that can snag.
- Recording: A compact action camera with a short tether works; strobes will glare harshly off white tile.

How to enter and exit
- Entry: From the courtyard, have the team partially lift or hinge the grate, lower a short ladder or a knotted line past the fall zone, and control inflow if possible. Keep the retrieval line clipped to your harness before you go in.
- Inside you’ll see an underwater stairway to the grate; it’s not an exit by itself. The bottom outlets are finger‑spaced grates—do not try to pass them.
- Exit: Signal, return to the underwater steps beneath the oculus (the least turbulent launch point), and have the team haul/lift as you climb into the opening. Never rely solely on your own strength against moving water to re‑ascend; use the line.

How to move once inside
- First minute: Find the “station” zone—usually the open middle where opposing flows cancel. Take a moment to map the room: locate the terracotta lips (fall lines), the two brick weirs (“altars”), the tethered amphorae near the upper walls, the chain corner, and the base grates along the far wall.
- Currents: Expect sudden shifts when floats open or close shutters. New jets can appear along the upper walls. If a current grabs you, don’t fight head‑on—slip sideways across it to the still zone or shelter in the lee of a plinth or weir.
- Surfaces: Tiles and plaster edges are crisp. Use open palms, not fingertips, to avoid cuts; keep fins (if any) away from edges. The sunniest side is slick with young algae.
- Sound: The chamber “presses” with noise. You’ll feel water‑hammer pulses on teeth and sinuses; keep your jaw slack and avoid pressing your bite together against impacts.

What not to touch or change
- Don’t block lips or weirs, jam shutters, or shift amphora float lines—even a small change alters the system’s job.
- Don’t scrape salts or lift the reed mats, cane basket, or the potted plant. They’re doing work you may not see.
- Don’t pry at the thin lead skins tucked under lips. They’re placed to last, not to be handled.
- Keep hair braided/contained; keep loose straps short. Avoid the chain if the current is lively.

Etiquette and conservation
- Be clean: No sunscreen, oils, soaps, or powders on your skin or suit. Rinse yourself before the visit if you can. The water is engineered to stay clear.
- Leave no fibers: Avoid cotton towels, wool, or anything that sheds. If something detaches (a strap end, a broken zip tie), retrieve it.
- Observe as a guest, not a bather. This is working infrastructure, not a spa.

Hazards and how to handle them
- Disorientation: If you lose bearings, look for the brightest square above; that’s the oculus. If light is low, hand‑follow a single wall until you reach the steps, or follow your guideline back.
- Being pulled toward the outlet grates: Flatten yourself, angle sideways, move along the wall to the lee of a weir or behind a plinth where flow slackens. Reduce your profile; don’t try to force fingers between bars.
- Sudden flow shift (float opens): Roll with it to the middle, then reassess. Signal if you want the team to ease inflow.
- Eye/skin sting: Close eyes, exit on your next safe breath, rinse with clean water and then a dilute vinegar splash. Moisturize skin afterward; alkalinity strips oils.
- Cut tile shard: Don’t leave it loose on the floor. Park it flat on a plinth so caretakers can remove it later.

How long to stay
- Breath‑hold only: Think in short, purposeful descents of 30–90 seconds with surface recovery at the oculus (outside the grate) before the next drop—if the crew can safely lift the grate for breathing between dips. If the grate must stay down, only do single, brief visits with a hard return time.
- With a mini air source: Limit to 10–15 minutes to avoid fatigue and skin stress. Warm water still drains energy in current and noise.

Aftercare (you and your gear)
- Rinse you and your kit with neutral fresh water, then a brief dilute vinegar rinse on suit, mask, and shoes to neutralize alkalinity. Rinse again. Dry completely.
- Eyes: Saline wash. Skin: gentle acidic moisturizer helps. Expect some dryness/tightness.
- Inspect for micro‑cuts; alkali can delay noticing them. Cover once you’re dry.
- Log the conditions (time, flow level, any changes in sound) for future travelers and the caretakers.

If you can’t or shouldn’t enter
- The view from above is honest and beautiful. Watch caustics crawl the walls, listen to the stacked voices of the lips, and feel the cool draft the cistern exhaled into the courtyard. You’ll understand much of its “instrument” without touching a thing.

Summary
- Treat it like a living machine: alkaline, loud, precise, and purpose‑built. Go with permission, a plan, a topside crew, a lifeline, and eye/skin protection. Move deliberately. Touch lightly. Change nothing. Leave the water’s room as articulate as you found it.
