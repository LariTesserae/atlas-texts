Orientation

You’re heading into a young, engineered basin where everything—climate, water, power, transit, even agriculture—moves by pressure and sound. Light stays near-twilight. Dew is the primary water body. Structures are light, far apart, and tuned. The air is rich, warm, and thick with ultrafine “aeromass” and whispering resonators. The ground is sharp glass and fresh basalt; the sky behaves like a cooled hand. This place is loud to the bones and quiet to the eyes, and your main job is to be present without adding a note the basin didn’t ask for.

What it feels like

- Visually: Endless dim, gray-green depths. Silhouettes instead of solids. Bridges that look too thin to trust until you step on them and feel their confidence through your feet. Slow shadow-bands drifting overhead like the slats of a gigantic blind, scrolling weather across surfaces.
- Tactile: Surfaces are either slick or bitey—gloss-black rock, toothy moss, glass-grass edges. Everything hums. If you place your palm on anything, you’ll feel layered tremors: city, weather, biology, all speaking in pressure.
- Auditory and infrasonic: An organ in the landscape playing a chord that includes you. There’s a key set by basalt pipes, infrastructures layering countermelodies, biology doing choruses at dawn and dusk. The audible is dense. The subaudible can make your breath hitch if you aren’t protected.
- Climate: Warm enough to loosen shoulders, never scorching; damp in a stingy way with bursts of sudden, localized rain that fall as curtains a block wide. Dew writes itself onto edges the shadows choose. Temperature steps down fast when a stripe crosses.

Core hazards at a glance

- Sound/pressure: Infrasound can cause nausea, dizziness, panic waves, arrhythmia in sensitive visitors. Certain bridges and cliff interfaces are resonant; stray whistling or rhythmic footsteps can tip them.
- Falls: Long, honest drops beneath filaments and arcways; paths are narrow and dew-slick.
- Edges: New basalt and glass-grasses cut. Many surfaces are sharp in a way your eyes won’t judge correctly.
- Weather pivots: Shadow-bands can dump a square of cold and rain on you in seconds; localized microbursts.
- Retuning: Parts of the valley are periodically retuned; landslides, sharp changes in path stability, new shears open where last week was safe.
- Biological: Pollen and ultrafine plankton are abundant. Not bitey, but your lungs and sinuses might disagree. Predators (husher-cats) use moving null-fields; they turn your hearing to cotton without warning and strike by reading echoes.
- Transit corridors: Pressure corridors carry gliders and manta-wings. They do not stop for you.
- Silence holes: Overpowered hush tech can carve “acoustic vacuums” that destabilize structures or vehicles.

What to bring and wear

Your hush

- Counterphase hood/veil: Local-style “eating-mat-thin” cloak that pins to the back of your skull or collar, tuned to create a narrow, near-field personal hush without throwing a big silence bubble. Must handle:
  - Strong attenuation in the 0.5–20 Hz band (infrasound) and fatigue-free damping at 20–200 Hz.
  - Bone-conduction transparency for comms and local taps.
  - Automatic key-tracking so your bubble doesn’t beat against the valley’s tone.
- Spare hush band and passive ear protection for sleep and emergencies.
- Vestibular stabilizer: Behind-ear patches or a wearable that gently counterdrives your otoliths. Reduces motion nausea from infrasonic swells.

Clothing and skin

- Quiet kit only. No jangling hardware, no flappy straps, no crinkle fabrics. Soft-fastened packs with baffles. If your gear sings, you will, by accident, join the instrument.
- Humidity-smart base layers (hydrophobic outside, wicking inside), quick-dry and low-friction.
- Arm and shin gaiters rated for microcuts; glass-grass will slice you the way paper does—small, numerous, irritatingly effective.
- Textured, non-marking soft soles with built-in damping (avoid carbon fiber plates, hollow heels, or anything that can resonate). Micro-siping and deployable microspikes for wet basalt.
- Light gloves with palm grip and fingertip tactility; you’ll be reading with your hands.
- Low-lumen, warm-amber or dull-green headlamp with fog mode (wide, non-sparkly beam). No flash. Keep your beam narrow and close; respect the dimness.
- Eye protection with anti-fog and a gentle contrast boost for twilight. Optional near-IR viewer set to low output for night navigation without warming dew films.

Breath and bio

- Ultrafine particulate/pollen mask with low acoustic profile. Look for a filter that doesn’t whistle; check exhale valves for silence.
- Saline for nose and eyes. Humid warm air plus aerosols equals sticky sinuses.
- Broad-spectrum anti-itch/antihistamine tabs if you’re pollen-sensitive.
- Water strategy: Carry a small, silent soft flask and electrolyte tabs. Bring a pocket dew-lick tube with a sterile tip if you plan to sip from condensation safely; don’t suck off shared hyssop throats.
- Food: Calorie-dense, odor-light, non-crinkling rations. The local “aeromass” feeders are meant for migrants and are safe, but don’t count on them for all meals. Packaging should be fabric or soft elastomer, not crisp foil.

Tools

- Safety harness and a compact fall-arrester. Slim, non-clanky carabiners with textile safeties. A three-meter silent tether with dampers.
- Palm translator for the rail-tap lexicon (haptic glove or bone-conduction reader/writer). Practice ahead.
- Pocket “tides” reader: a modest pressure/infrasound meter that tells you when a node is ramping and when a corridor is live.
- Soft bivy sling or hammock rated for double-tie; quiet line for ridgelines.
- First aid: skin glue, hemostatic gauze, antimicrobial film, tiny splinter puller, steri-strips. You’ll get microcuts.
- Non-propulsive optics/camera only. No drones, no rotorcraft, no fans.
- Vibration-damped case for electronics; the valley will rattle cheap solder like loose teeth.

Arrival and orientation

- Decon: You’ll likely hit a hush-house at the rim. They’ll dust you, heat-pulse your fabrics, and seed your hood with the current key so you don’t throw beats at the city.
- Health check: If you have migraines, Menière’s, arrhythmia, seizure history, advanced pregnancy, or fresh ear infections, declare it. The locals may limit your access or insist on stronger hush.
- Briefing: Learn the day’s chord schedule and retuning windows. They’re published tactilely. You’ll practice name exchange in sign and rail-tap, footfall etiquette for bridges, and corridor right-of-way.
- Calibration: Techs will trim your hush bubble small and personal. Bigger fields are regulated. Do not bring your own wide-area silence gear.

Moving through the city

- Step weight: Move smooth and slightly arrhythmic unless a guide tells you to match dampening cadence. Synchronized groups can set bridges gonging.
- Clip where posted. Most filaments accept a quiet textile clip-in. Do not sling lines across pressure corridors.
- Read with your hands. Rails and nodal plates hum schedules, warnings, and routes. If you can’t parse, ask a local to translate the pattern; don’t guess.
- Obey the emptiness. Spires are a kilometer apart for a reason. Don’t short-circuit gaps by rigging your own lines. Don’t linger under arc-lattices when the pipes feed.
- Light discipline. Keep your beam tight and warm. No camera flash. Reflective jewelry off.
- Rain curtains: If a band moves in and the air goes metallic-cool, you’ve got ten seconds before a square of rain collapses. Step under a lintel or hyssop throat awning; they’re built to take it.

Water and food

- Dew appears where the shadow writes it, not where your map says. Trust the bands. Read the hyssop towers; the auxiliary throats ping audibly before they spill.
- Don’t harvest from communal condensers without an invitation. Your guide will show you the migrant feeders; they taste like cold bread and kelp for a reason: neutrality.
- River etiquette: It’s narrow and deliberate. Rush beds and sedges drink at night. Don’t trample the lip; it’s stitched habitat and hydrology both.

Communication and etiquette

- Speech is fine inside your hush, but outside you is instrument space. Keep your mouth small. Whistling is taboo and, at certain interfaces, dangerous.
- Names and greetings: palm taps on rails, small sign, and bone-conduction pulses. A visitor’s safe default is a two-beat touch then a pause—don’t invent patterns.
- Doors may be interference curtains. If a doorway looks like air, assume it is a tuned field and wait. The local host will open it or retune it for you.
- Don’t retune anything. Ever. Even “just a bit for a better photo.” Fines are heavy and accidents are worse.

Wildlife

- You’ll hear more than you’ll see. Don’t chase shifts in pollen vortices; those are striders.
- Gossamers look like fluff; they’re animals. Don’t breathe them deep for fun; keep your mask on in pressure sinks.
- Manta-wings: watch the air like a highway. If the infrasonic “floor” suddenly tilts in your bones, a wing is surfing. Step back, keep your hush small, eyes down.
- Husher-cats: nocturnal, visible as trembles in active dust, and as sudden absence of subaudible. If your bubble feels bitten smaller and your ears go numb, get to a human-lit alcove or a guard hush band station. Don’t run noisy; move smooth and big, keep your back to a surface so your echoes can’t be mapped from all sides. Travel with a local at night.

Shelter and sleep

- Double-tie your hammock when the city chooses a new chord (you’ll be notified by node). Clip your pack; retunes have thrown careful visitors before.
- Sleep with passive plugs plus hush on low; full silence can be disorienting here.
- Hospitals and sanctuaries maintain traveling hush almonds—pockets of portable quiet. If you feel overclocked, borrow one rather than overdriving your own gear.

Health management

- Hydrate gently. You will sweat less than you think and still lose salts. Small sips plus electrolytes.
- Skin care: rinse microcuts, seal them. Moist, warm air plus fresh stone is a culture plate for surprise infections.
- Sinus rinse nightly. Your dreams will thank you.
- Motion/emotion: Infrasound can make your heart feel like it’s having feelings that aren’t yours—unease, sudden tenderness, cheap dread. Anchor with breath and haptic metronome. If you’re spiraling, step into a hush pocket; ask a guard for a band.

Law and limits

- Silence is regulated. Keep your hush bubble small (head-and-shoulders). Large-area nulls require a license.
- No fans, rotors, or propulsive drones. No loudspeakers. Musical instruments by permit only, and usually only at the rim.
- Emptiness is conserved. You can’t string your own lines across voids, build ad hoc nets, or camp in pressure corridors.
- Harvesting: Aeromass feeders are communal; hyssop dew and pitcher-throats are managed. Ask before you drink or pick.
- Sound crimes: Whistling at joints, stamping rhythms on certain spans, or beating on columns can be prosecuted as sabotage.

Do

- Ask a local to set your footfall cadence the first time you cross the long arcways.
- Keep your hands on the rail when you can; let the city tell you its schedule.
- Plan your walks by the shadow stripes, not by clock alone.
- Carry a small gift that makes no sound: a good quiet cloth, a finely damped hinge, a weave that breathes without singing.

Don’t

- Don’t whistle near any interface of bridge and cliff.
- Don’t test echo. The valley does not need your clap.
- Don’t trail straps or hard objects that can rattle.
- Don’t stand in a pressure corridor to watch manta-wings. You won’t hear them coming; you’ll feel them too late.
- Don’t lay flat on fresh basalt during retunes; micro-shears move like spilled glass.

Moments not to miss

- Basalt pipe rim at a feeding: you’ll feel the chord bloom like weather inside your ribs.
- Dusk by the river when katydids turn their wings into keyboards and the valley rains metal.
- A hyssop tower after a cool shadow drops two degrees: the pinging dewfall is like glass threaded into thunder.
- A sound-reef shift: the sky’s fabric thickens, the gossamers feed, and you’ll taste a clean, sea-cold note at the back of your tongue.
- A maintenance spire breath: a single bright needle note that resets your sense of scale.

Exit protocol

- De-aerosol and de-spore at the rim. Your clothes will have caught phononic dust that can hum in the wrong world.
- Retune your hush to neutral before you leave; harsh beats in a quiet customs hall are a miserable way to make friends.
- Check your gear for resonant souvenirs. The pretty vibrating trinket on your pack cord might be a structural brace you didn’t notice borrowing.

Finally

Move like a guest in a cathedral whose god is pressure. Keep your hush close, your weight light, your curiosity in your hands as much as in your eyes. If you do it right, dew will fall from your outstretched palm and you’ll feel, for a breath, that the city has made room inside its chord for you.
