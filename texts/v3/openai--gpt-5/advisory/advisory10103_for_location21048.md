What you’re walking into, and what you’re not
- The “room” is a vesicle in young volcanic glass: a cavity the size of a helmet or two, sealed except for hairline couplings to a hot, pulsing plumbing behind it. It is pitch-dark, knife-edged, anhydrous, chemically aggressive, loudly alive with pressure and infrasound.
- It is also full. Every surface is layered in slow-moving, salt-solvent life. There is no spare ledge, no safe corner.
- A human body does not fit here physically or ecologically. Think remote presence. You go to the mountain; your instrument goes into the vesicle, if at all.

Before you go: mindset and ethics
- Treat this as a functioning micro-factory that happens to be alive. Any disturbance—light, water, new gases, pressure changes, an object wedged in the wrong throat—can crash a process you do not understand.
- Aim for listen-first, touch-never. If you must touch, touch once, with forethought, and be ready to reverse it.
- Never introduce water. The local chemistry will react violently and the life here has no defenses against it.
- Your best “visit” is through the rock: sensing, imaging, and if you must, peering through a sealed window that does not exchange mass.

Getting to the mountain
- Expect high, cold, thin air above most clouds. Nights are hard vacuum-dry; days are razor-bright. Winds scour and can carry acid fumes downflow.
- Typical hazards: infrasound you feel in your bones; occasional hot, poisoned breaths from cracks; unstable glassy scree and blades of new crystals.
- Bring a high-altitude mountaineering kit, but add volcanic PPE: fully encapsulating, positive-pressure suit; SCBA with redundancy; acid- and heat-resistant outer layers; helmet with infrasound-damping ear protection; goggles that seal under a positive-pressure hood; gauntlets with ceramic or aluminized backs for radiant heat.

Breathing and personal protection
- Do not rely on filters. The ambient risks are low oxygen, CO, SO2, and chlorine. Use self-contained breathing apparatus only.
- Suit materials should resist hot, dry halogens and sulfur species. Favor ceramic/external aluminized proximity layers over polymers near vents. Keep the suit cool: phase-change cooling vest or closed-loop liquid cooling.
- Infrasound can make you nauseous and disoriented. Wear active infrasound attenuators or heavy, well-fitted plugs combined with over-ear muffs inside your hood.

Approach and site setup
- Work upwind and upslope of any fumarolic noise. Anchor yourself; new glassy slopes behave like ball bearings under crampons.
- Establish a clean, dry staging bubble. Everything going near the cavity must be baked dry and kept in an anhydrous purge.
- Use a tripod or boom to keep your body well clear of any crack. Radiant heat and sudden slams of gas are real.

How to “enter” a space that small and crowded
- Default plan: don’t. Use through-rock sensing:
  - Passive seismic and infrasound geophones epoxied to the exterior to “hear” the organ and resolve pulse structure.
  - Thermal mapping on the rock skin to find the vesicle’s position by faint heat tides.
  - Muon or cosmic-ray radiography if you have the time: builds a density map of voids without drilling.
- If you must see inside, use a sealed window rather than a hole:
  - Laser-bore a millimeter-scale tunnel from a cool exterior face, stopping 1–2 mm short of the cavity. Fuse a sapphire or diamond micro-window across that final membrane with a cold-weld or ceramic braze so that you never exchange gas.
  - Behind the window, mount optics rated for 300–500°C radiance. Use a periscopic fiber or rigid borescope kept in an argon-purged sleeve.
- Absolute last resort: endoscopic probe insertion through an existing throat.
  - Only if you can confirm a naturally ventilated pathway large enough to accept a hair-thin probe without blocking it.
  - Probe materials: all-ceramic or glassy carbon body; sapphire window; platinum-group or gold traces only where unavoidable; no elastomers; no halogen-sensitive polymers. Everything electrically rounded and antistatic.
  - External surface preconditioned in a dry halogen stream so it isn’t the “freshest face” in the room that everything rushes to scrape.
  - Pressure-compensating design so it neither inhales nor exhales when pulses hit. The device should “ride” the pressure waves rather than fight them.

Sensing without breaking the music
- Light: use as little as possible. Continuous illumination will be an “event” in here. If you need it, favor very dim, narrowband near-IR and shield it so no radiant spill warms the walls. Turn it on between pulses; shutter it off before the next slam.
- Vision in the dark: exploit native luminescence. The zinc-sulfide spurs will flash when the walls flex. Use a highly sensitive, cooled sensor tuned to that green corona and to faint red from the crack; integrate across pulses.
- Sound and motion: couple an accelerometer to the exterior rock and synchronize all internal imaging to pulse phase. You will learn more by phase-locking to the mountain’s breath than by brute-force video.
- Fields and charge: a distant electrometer antenna can read corona events even through a window. Internally, avoid pointy conductors that will seed arcs.
- Chemistry: never sniff by ingesting gas. Instead, line the inside face of your window with inert, reversible sorbent microarrays and read them optically; or sample exterior plumes away from the cavity and infer.

What to pack, field edition
- Personal: fully encapsulating suit with SCBA; spare cylinders; liquid cooling; thermal gloves; helmet; infrasound protection; mountaineering kit; headlamp kept for exterior only.
- Instruments: geophones/infrasound mics; IR thermometer and thermal camera; gas sensors for SO2/CO/Cl2 set to alarm externally only; anemometer for exterior cracks; laser rangefinder for fissure mapping.
- Clean-tech: bake-out oven or hot case to dry-prove probes; dry nitrogen or argon purge system; desiccant lockers; clean ceramic tools.
- Optics: sapphire-windowed borescope with micromanipulator boom; radiation/heat shield; low-level IR illumination with tight shutters; cooled low-noise camera sensitive to green and deep red.
- Materials kit: ceramic braze or cold-weld patching for a micro-window; high-temperature, halogen-resistant epoxies for exterior sensor mounting; sacrificial ceramic spacers.

How to behave around them
- Don’t block the valve. The pinch-point where bodies act as a living shutter is sacred infrastructure. If you obstruct it, you will collapse local flow regimes and likely kill a belt.
- Don’t create the freshest surface. Anything new and clean will be mobbed by scrapers. Precondition tool surfaces with a thin, already-spent salt glaze.
- Don’t carry charge spikes in. Use antistatic paths and rounding; pre-neutralize via a corona brush before approaching the window. A single sharp point can seed unnatural discharges.
- Don’t push the rhythm. Time your actions to the trough between pulses. Insert, focus, withdraw only when the mountain exhales gently, never at the hard beat.
- Don’t leak anything. Not breath, not humidity, not trace oils. Go dry or don’t go.

What you can expect to sense
- The dark will feel dense, like a held breath. You’ll “see” best with your hands on the rock and your gear tuned to motion and charge.
- Heat will come in slow tides rather than steady burn. The exterior rock where you work may be only warm; inches in, it’s kilnlike.
- The sound is an organ of stone. You will feel a standing note through your bones, with sharper pulses that make your gear shiver. Secure anything that can rattle.
- Inside, when you finally glimpse it, movement is patient and communal. Belts of bodies slip in unison; a micro-thunderclap passes and a thousand intake mouths glide open like a field of anemones that decided water was unnecessary.

If you’re tempted to take a sample
- Don’t, unless your ethics committee and conscience both say there is unique, irreplaceable value.
- If you must, sample only detritus from outside the cavity or from a distant, already-spent surface. Anything living you pull will carry molten salts that turn to corrosive acids in air. Double-bag in dry argon, stow in a heated, dry box. Assume extreme chemical hazard, not biological compatibility.
- Never add water to “revive” it. Water is a weapon here.

Failure modes to plan for
- Gas slam: a sudden, hotter, richer pulse can blow back through a hairline and turn your boom into a bellows. Keep standoff distance; use breakaway mounts.
- EMMI/ESD: piezo spikes can induce currents. Shield electronics, isolate grounds, add surge suppression.
- Crack growth: the vesicle wall can craze or spall. Don’t load the rock locally. Spread any external clamp or mount widely with compliant ceramic pads.
- Weather flip: the whole mountain can go quiet. That’s their famine. Don’t mistake your own interference for a natural lull.

Aftercare and decon
- Your gear will be loaded with reactive salts. Keep it hot and dry until you can disassemble in a fume hood. When you finally allow moisture in, do it under controlled scrubbing so you don’t make a chlorine sauna.
- Air out nowhere near metals you care about, lungs, or animals. Neutralize with appropriate bases under ventilation, being mindful of heat release.
- Log exactly what you did, when in the pulse cycle you did it, and what changed. Share it. The next traveler should have your rhythm map, not guesswork.

When to go
- “Day” and “night” here are about the organ’s steadiness. Watch the infrasound and exterior gas heat for 24 hours at minimum. Go when the pulse is loud, periodic, and forgiving. If it’s irregular and thin, you’re visiting during their famine; stay outside and listen.

What not to bring
- Water, alcohols, oils, or anything that outgasses them. No snacks in the suit. No breath leaks. No soft plastics that will soften, drip, or react with chlorine.
- Curiosity that demands touching. Bring curiosity that can listen.

What you owe the place
- To leave the music playing the same tune it was playing before you arrived.
- To resist the human habit of making space where there isn’t any. Here, crowding is function. Your job is to witness without asking for room.

A last image to hold in mind
- You are not visiting a cave. You are pressing an ear and an eye to the wall of a beating kiln where life has found another solvent and another clock. If you keep your own breath out of it, it will show you how much can be done without water, light, or haste. That is your souvenir.
