What this place is
- A living utility chamber under a city: heat exchange and/or water movement, running 24/7. Industrial bones with a cave’s skin. No streets, no horizon, only service corridors and bolted doors. You come as a guest into someone’s workplace and a bat colony’s home.

How it feels
- Hot, wet, metallic. Humidity that hangs on you like a wet shirt. The floor sweats, the rock sweats, the pipes sweat. Light is amber and tired, with the occasional hard-white arc that reminds you every surface is live with purpose. Sound is layered: low pump thrum in the bones, fans, the fine telegraph of tens of thousands of bats, a constant rain of drips, an occasional hiss. Everything changes slowly; you won’t.

Before you go
- Permission and escort: This is an active plant. Arrange access with the utility operator. Expect a safety briefing and a minder. Solo wandering is a bad idea and likely illegal.
- Health check:
  - If you are immunocompromised, asthmatic, or pregnant, skip this one. Guano and damp industrial air are not your friends.
  - Tetanus booster current. Consider pre-exposure rabies vaccine if you travel often around bats, though you should never touch them. Discuss leptospirosis risk (rats, standing water) with a travel-medicine clinician.
  - If you have balance issues or severe claustrophobia, reconsider. There’s no horizon here and the floors are deciding themselves.
- Timing: Avoid dawn and dusk unless you are specifically there to witness the colony’s movement with your escort’s blessing. Those are guano-rain windows and high-bat-traffic times. Midday at the shaft is a hush that belongs to heat-and-smell navigators; treat it like a sanctuary.

What to wear
- Feet: Non-slip, dielectric boots with soft rubber soles and decent tread; ankle support. No metal shanks. Gaiters if you don’t like guano freckles.
- Clothes: Lightweight, long-sleeved, quick-dry layers that don’t hold water; long pants you don’t mind staining with rust and scale. Nothing that swishes loudly or snags easily.
- Head: Bump cap or hard hat with a short brim you can tape a lamp hood to. A light hood or cap for guano at bat rise/return.
- Hands: Grippy, non-conductive work gloves; thin nitrile liners in a pocket for anything slimy/oily. Leave rings and watches at home.
- Eyes/ears: Anti-fog safety glasses. Earplugs that attenuate without isolating (you still need to hear shouted brevities and alarms).
- Respiratory: At minimum an N95; ideally a P100 elastomeric if you’ll be anywhere near guano stacks or dust, especially during bat movement or cleanup. Fit matters.

What to bring
- Light, hooded: A warm, dimmable headlamp (2200–3000K) with a physical hood/baffle so the beam doesn’t brush the ceiling. Hand torch as backup with a diffuser. No strobes. No drone lights, no flash photography.
- Dry protection: Small roll-top dry bag for electronics; spare silica gel packs. Microfiber cloths and anti-fog for lenses. Everything you own will bead—plan for it.
- Comms: A wrapped radio/phone (cloth or rubber sleeve) to cut glare and noise. Keep sounds discreet. Consider airplane mode unless your escort says otherwise.
- Hydration: One sturdy, sealable bottle with a sport cap. Electrolyte tablets that don’t crumble. No open cups.
- Food: If you must, non-crumbly, sealed bars. Eat away from the sump and kiosk. Pack out all waste immediately. Bread grows legs here.
- Notetaking: Synthetic paper or a wax pencil slate. Regular paper turns to paste. If recording audio, muffled mic to avoid bat-attracting highs.
- Small towel and a spare pair of socks. Damp creeps; dry feet save days.
- Compact first aid: disinfectant wipes, a couple of large plasters, blister kit. Nothing loose that will turn to mush.
- Decon kit for exit: 70% alcohol wipes or a small sprayer; a trash bag; spare shoe brush. If you’ve been in other caves, follow full decontamination to avoid moving fungi between worlds.

How to move in the room
- Slow is fast. The floor is still deciding what’s high and low. Step where the aggregate shows, not on the ashy algae sheen. Test metal grates with a toe before you commit weight.
- Three points of contact on ladders. If the bats lift while you’re on one, hug in and wait. Their weather has right of way.
- Keep your lamp hooded and pointed down. Light draws wings. Don’t sweep the ceiling; don’t backlight the colony.
- Mind the rails. Rats use handrails as highways. If a rat needs it more than you do, let it pass without drama.
- Don’t touch valves or spin wheels. Every valve leaks by design here, and rags are doing quiet work. Your hand isn’t the fix.
- Give the kiosk wide berth unless you’re a customer, and even then, keep it brief. Cash dry, coins better.
- Stay a body-length from live switchgear and porcelain insulators. If the air is visible with a blue-white whisker, you’re too close.

What not to do
- No flash, no drones, no speakers. Your playlist is someone else’s hazard.
- Don’t shake anything that ties into the ceiling or the beams. The ceiling belongs to them.
- Don’t collect. Not a scale, not a soda-straw stalactite thread, not a beetle wing. A soda straw takes ages to grow and an instant to end.
- Don’t feed anything. Not the rats, not the fish, not the frogs.
- Don’t smoke, ever. Especially at the shaft at midday.
- Don’t step into or sample the sump. It is not a wishing well and it doesn’t forget.

Reading the room
- Pumps speak in cycles felt through steel. If you notice the beat slow or stutter, tell your escort immediately. The story here isn’t sudden flooding; it’s “warm becoming hot” and that’s worse for the machines and the life around them.
- Sodium lights give you amber dusk; the thin lines of cold LEDs mark ladders and hazards. Trust those lines; they were placed by people who do this daily.
- Rust advances seasonally; paint bubbles. If a railing foot looks like a sugar cookie, don’t lean on it.
- Drip rings and soft tufa lips around the sump mark where not to place chairs, cameras, or yourself. Those are tiny shorelines with a long memory.

Wildlife etiquette
- Bats: They are heat and motion and law here. If you feel the colony tensing (a pressure shift more than a sound), freeze your light and still your body. If you are hit by guano, that’s weather, not offense. Wash later.
- Amphibians: Frogs and blind salamanders linger by warmth and pipe mouths. Watch your step and your camera bag. Amphibian skin is chemistry fragile; keep repellents and sunscreen off rails and decks.
- Insects and others: Roaches flow like liquid; cave crickets spring far. Don’t swat in panic; your flail will send you skating. Millipedes are slow burners; admire, don’t poke.
- Rats: They know timings and escape routes better than you. Don’t corner. If one looks unwell, give it a wide berth and tell your escort—lepto and other diseases live where they do.

Working with people
- This is someone’s day shift and someone else’s night. You are moving through their “courtyard.”
- Communication is short and functional. Learn a couple of clear hand signals and keep your sentences compact. If someone shouts “Hold!” you become a statue.
- If offered tea at the kiosk, pay, thank, step aside. Don’t linger without buying. Don’t block the view to the pool; it’s where eyes rest between tasks.
- Curiosity is welcome when it’s quiet and doesn’t slow the job. Questions wait until the wrench is back on the rack.

Photography tips, if allowed
- Let your camera acclimate in a sealed bag for 20 minutes to avoid lens fog. Carry silica packs.
- No flash. High ISO, fast primes. Brace against rail uprights but test for slime first.
- Cover glowing screens. Bats and insects are screen-magnets.
- Keep lanyards short; anything that dangles becomes a wick for water and oil.

Heat and hydration
- It will feel like being inside a warm rain that won’t end. You won’t notice sweat because everything is wet already.
- Sip often; don’t chug. Electrolytes matter more than volume. Plan short, frequent pauses in clear, dry-footing spots.

If something goes wrong
- Power flicker or a hard-white arc: freeze. Lights back to the floor; eyes to your escort. Wait for an all-clear.
- Slip: Sit where you land if you can; sudden stands turn small slides into big ones.
- Wildlife contact (bite/scratch): Wash immediately with soap and water, then antiseptic. Report. Seek medical care for rabies risk assessment.
- Inhalation/dizziness: Mask up, move to a drier corridor with your minder. Heat exhaustion is sneaky here.

Leaving well
- Decon footwear and anything that touched floors or guano. Brush, then wipe or spray with 70% alcohol or a manufacturer-recommended cave-safe disinfectant; contact time matters (five to ten minutes).
- Seal dirty clothes in a bag. Launder hot, dry thoroughly. Don’t wear the same boots into another cave system in another world without decon.
- Hydrate again. Expect to smell like wet coins and machine oil until you shower.

Packing checklist (tight and true)
- Permit/access details; escort contact
- Non-slip dielectric boots; long sleeves/pants; bump cap/hard hat; gloves; safety glasses; earplugs; respirator
- Hooded warm headlamp + backup; spare batteries in a sealed pouch
- Dry bag with silica; microfiber cloth; anti-fog
- Sealed water bottle; electrolytes; non-crumbly snack
- Synthetic notebook/wax pencil; wrapped phone/radio
- Small towel; spare socks
- Compact first aid; hand wipes
- Decon supplies; trash bag; shoe brush

What you’re there for
- To feel the city’s under-surface learning to be a cave and the cave learning to live under a city.
- To watch soda-straws learn gravity and pumps learn patience.
- To stand one rung below a weather change made of bats and know that in this room constancy is king and your job is to keep from nudging it.

If you remember only the rules they teach with their bodies, you’ll do fine:
- The ceiling belongs to them. Keep light low. Don’t spin what a rag can solve. Step where the floor admits it’s a floor. Paper turns to paste; bread grows legs. If the pump slows, you call. And at the shaft at midday, you let silence be its own machine.
