What you’re walking into
- A high, wet karst that never really dries. The stone weeps; the air carries water, ammonia, and fine organics; light is lemon-clear when it isn’t fog. Every surface is a negotiation between flow, film, and gravity. Sound is constant: underfalls like a slow drum, machinery softened by mist, the whisper-chord of fences, and the evening surge of wings.
- The city is grown as much as it’s built. Gutters rule everything. Law is acoustics and hydraulics first, paperwork second. Animals are not background—they’re infrastructure. Dusk is a moving wall of life. Nothing goes fast except mistakes.

Mental prep
- Slow down. This place is paced by drip and breath. Hurrying reads as dangerous and juvenile.
- Expect to be damp, always. Dry is a technical achievement that costs somebody else work.
- Learn to listen. Locals judge health by tone: a bright, quick ping from a gutter means good flow; a dull note means clog or suffocation.
- Accept that “ownership” is about responsibility, not exclusion. You’ll be assigned a flow-line to mind or asked to contribute to one if you stay more than a few days.

What to pack (and why)
- Feet and hands
  - High-traction footwear with siped rubber soles and good edge feel; no hard mountain lugs. Add low-profile microspikes for polished inclines.
  - Two pairs of thin, hydrophobic liner socks and two pairs of wet-tolerant wool socks. Rotate; dry at warm vents when possible.
  - Lightweight, grippy gloves (nitrile-coated knit or gecko-texture) for wet railings, with a second pair of chemical-resistant gloves for plant/industrial visits.

- Body and shell
  - A hooded rain cape or smock that sheds and channels water without creating broad drips into public gutters; thigh-length is practical. Avoid umbrellas—they make eddies and can be fined if you block flow-lines.
  - Midlayer that insulates when wet (wool or hydrophobic synthetic). Even warm humidity can chill you over hours.
  - Corrosion-proof belt kit and carabiners (titanium or ceramic-coated). Always have a short lanyard for clipping to safety lines on exposed terraces.
  - Compact mesh face hood for dusk. It’s not for biting—most flyers won’t—but to keep a million curious wings out of your eyes.
  - Helmet with a narrow brim (to shed constant drip) and integrated ear protection you can feather open; commotion is constant but you still need to hear fence chords and bells.

- Lungs, eyes, skin
  - A comfortable half-mask respirator with ammonia/salt-rated cartridges. You won’t need it constantly, but you’ll be grateful near kilns, foundries, or downwind from dense roosts.
  - Tight-fitting anti-fog goggles. Bring extra anti-fog wipes; lenses fog the second you stop moving.
  - Barrier cream (lanolin/petrolatum mix) for feet and hands; apply twice daily. Antifungal wipes/powder for skin folds. Alcohol gel is less useful here; carry a small iodine-based topical for cuts.

- Tools and sensemaking
  - Waterproof notebook (ceramic or polymer pages) and a grease pencil. Paper dies here. Locals keep Sluice Books; you’ll want your own.
  - A small, soft-tongued bell or tuning fork at the city’s reference pitch (buy one at the gate if you didn’t bring it). It’s how you test a line without shouting.
  - Headlamp with a warm, low-output setting and a shield. Blue-white light scatters in fog; bright beams disorient flyers. No flashes at dusk.
  - Compact desiccant-free electronics in IP68 housings. Conformal-coated power banks. Hydrophobic port plugs. Expect anything unsealed to fail.
  - Biodegradable, low-foam detergent and a pocket oilcloth; oil keeps the damp out of moving parts and is a standard gift.

- Trade and goodwill
  - Useful smalls: titanium needles, ceramic bearings, anti-fog treatments, mildew-proof thread, silicone gaskets/O-rings. Bells tuned to common intervals. Good oil.
  - Don’t bring seeds, live plants, or fluffy fabrics that shed. Green life is alien here and a biohazard.

Getting there and first contact
- Approach along marked terraces or by rail. Updrafts are constant; don’t try parachute antics. The rim can look flat when it isn’t—hire an edge-walker if you’re not sure-footed.
- There’s usually a gate ledge with a flow office. You’ll register, get your visitor’s chime, a gutter map for your quarter, and instructions for where to stand at dusk. Many cities issue a “sluice visa”—you contribute a few hours to gutter-scribing or mat-brushing instead of a simple fee.
- Quarantine: boot brush, cloak rinse, and a UV or steam pass for luggage. Expect a polite sniffing by filter-crews and a look into your pockets for seeds or powders.

How to move without getting hurt or fined
- Keep to walk-lines. They’re slightly ribbed, shallowly cupped, and signed with a duller sheen. Anything mirror-smooth and faintly concave is for water, not you.
- Never straddle a gutter to rest. Your body will create a warm, dead eddy; the stone hates dead air and will flake. Fines for eddies are real.
- Clip in on outer terraces and any platform painted with chevrons. People fall less here not because edges are safe but because everyone assumes they are not.
- At dusk, freeze your position at the one-minute bell, put up your mesh hood, and face inward. Shepherding sirens mean “we’re bending the air”; you don’t want to be the obstacle the tide bends around.
- Don’t touch the harmonic wires. They look like handrails and hum. They aren’t handrails. They steer the flocks and can sting.

Health notes you’ll be grateful for later
- Skin: maceration is the enemy. Rinse, oil, air when you can. Change socks at mid-day. Dry feet near warm vents (there are communal grates for this).
- Ears and lungs: a nightly saline rinse helps with ammonia/fog irritation. If you’re asthmatic, inform your host; they’ll route you away from kiln drifts and find you a room with an intake baffle.
- Cuts: clean and seal immediately. The films here will colonize any opportunity; locals favor iodine and resin dressings. Ask for “weeper’s lacquer” at a clinic.
- Sleep: the city is ocean-loud. Bring soft earplugs you can still hear bells through, or ask for a room near a “quiet wall” tuned to cancel the fence chord.
- Immunity: if you’re immunocompromised, reconsider. The place is gentle but saturated with micro-life.

Etiquette that matters (and what it maps to)
- First rule: Flow belongs to the plateau. You borrow, guide, and give it back cleaner. Don’t dam, pool, or siphon for personal convenience.
- Mark what sings. If you hear a gutter change tone—bright to flat, or starting to “sing” at a new pitch—note the marker number and tell the nearest flow post before night. People die when notes go unreported.
- Don’t feed the nets at dusk. Throwing scraps into the air looks charming once; it breeds roosts that tear channels apart. It’s a big fine and you’ll be remembered for it.
- Keep the rails humming. If you have a balcony or edge assigned to your lodging, don’t silence its wire. Silent edges confuse flight and invite crashes.
- Fire is privilege, not right. Open flame is essentially banned. If you must heat or burn, you do it in a baffled stack. Smokers switch to chew or steam pipes.
- Leave the mats alone unless you’re working under instruction. The gray, the lacquer domes, the white fans—they’re livelihood and living tissue, not curios.
- Slug-bulls have right of way. If a wall moves, you move less. Don’t push, prod, or “play” their louvers. If you get pinned, go limp and ring a soft bell; a herder will free you.
- Children’s law: the hour before dusk is quiet around intakes and lifts. Don’t stage photos or block alleys then.

Money, work, staying longer
- Currency exists, but social credit is literally maintenance. Contribute to a sluice line, and you’ll be welcomed again. Expect to be invited to a Sluice Walk: combing and re-scribing gutters with a chitin rake while someone keeps time with a bell.
- If you stay a month, you’ll be given a small line in a Sluice Book—your piece of the city’s circulation to mind. It’s an honor and a subtle leash.
- Good gifts: oil, quiet bearings, tuned bells, strong corrosion-proof needles. Bad gifts: seeds, furs that shed, powders that float.

Food and drink
- Water is everywhere but owned by flow. Lodgings allot you “drips” per day; use what you need, then return it (graywater goes to mats).
- Expect umami-heavy meals: fermentations, fungal trays, glider and bat byproducts, insect protein, mineral cheeses grown in louvered caches, occasional slug-bull organ meats on feast days. Greens are rare and imported. If you need roughage, ask for “sieve crumble.”
- Don’t eat street curios off films. Market food is safe; wall sheen is not.
- Alcohol exists in low, steady forms—sours, fog wines, fungal spirits. Hydrate anyway.

How to see the place without stressing it
- Tours worth doing:
  - A condenser field at dawn: water falling like hair through whickering microturbines.
  - A kiln line under baffle hoods; ask to hear the “cooling prophet” tick at night.
  - A dusk shepherding from a tuned balcony; ear against stone, feel the whole building sing.
  - A Sluice Book reading: you’ll hear a neighborhood’s last decade told as changes in tone and weather.
- Photography: keep lenses warm against your chest; wipe with oilcloth not paper. No strobes at dusk. Ask before shooting inside song-rooms or near active fences.

Things that will get you in real trouble
- Blocking a fall or stuffing a dead space to be “cozy.” The stone will spall and people will see you do it.
- Tossing anything off an edge. It will land on a mat or in a fall, and your consequences will be immediate.
- Touching or detuning harmonic fences. If flocks smash into an intake because you leaned your pack on a singing wire, that’s on you.
- Ignoring a singing gutter. Not reporting can be treated like leaving a gate open into traffic.

If weather turns or something goes wrong
- Fog-bursts: step onto the nearest ribbed walk-line, turn your back to the fall, and wait. Pools vanish down swallow holes fast; don’t try to “outrun” the pattern.
- Sirens shift suddenly: flatten to a wall, clip in, and make yourself part of the geometry the shepherds expect.
- You slip into a blind pit: shout once, then listen. There’s usually a ladder within arm’s reach or a chain car that will pass. Ring your bell in a slow triple; that’s the “assist needed” rhythm.
- You see an injured flyer at dusk: leave it unless it’s tangled in a fence. Tell the nearest post; trained hands will free it without wrecking the chord.

Staying comfortable in constant damp
- Accept you won’t be truly dry. Aim for “warm and not squishing.” Oil moving parts. Rotate clothing around vents. Don’t fight the air; borrow its patience.
- Build a personal ritual around sound: morning fence chord check, the lowing sigh of roosts settling, your own cup pinged once before sleep. Locals will smile; you’re listening.

A few phrases (and how to use them)
- “May I test this line?” Ask before you bell a gutter in someone’s care.
- “This gutter sings different at Marker [number].” The right way to report a change.
- “My flow runs here today.” Polite claim when rinsing or airing clothes over a minor channel.
- “I won’t make eddies.” Useful when you’re about to set up a tripod or sit on a step.
- “Your chord holds.” Compliment for a well-tuned balcony or fence.

Leaving no trace
- Return water to water. Return organics to mats. Return noise to tone. Anything else you brought in leaves with you.

If you fall in love and stay
- Your bones may take on a softness and a gloss in your imagination, as if the place were sanding you smooth. People here tune their homes like instruments; they tune their days like gutters—comb out the dead, let the flow brighten, and wait for the slow strength to grow. If you stay long enough, you’ll learn to measure your life by the clarity of your lines and the way your walls sing back when the wind breathes. That’s how you know you’re no longer visiting.
