What you’re walking into is not a cave and not a subway. It’s the city’s second set of lungs and guts: cold, wet, loud, orderly, and always almost-changing. Prepare for it like a workplace you respect, a wilderness you don’t fully understand, and a machine that does not care you are there.

Essentials at a glance
- It is cold-damp, near-freezing. You will chill fast when you stop moving and sweat won’t dry.
- It is loud. You must hear to navigate by sound, but the amplitude will fatigue you and can damage hearing.
- It is wet, conductive, and alive with moving machinery. Assume any puddle could be energized and any quiet could break into force and vapor.
- The “weather” runs on a schedule. Tides of air, water, and pressure move with predictability until an upset, when they don’t.
- Light is thin and positional. Fog makes halos; black spaces swallow beams. Expect your visibility to change without warning.
- Distance is deceptive. You can walk an hour between “rooms” that feel human-sized.

Before you go
- Get permission and the day map. This space runs on posted schedules: fan reversals, purge windows, sump surges, blowdowns, and local oxygen routing. Photograph or print the current map at every entry point; clocks can drift between sectors, so note the local time source used on postings.
- Ask for the visitor safety brief from Facilities/Control. You want: designated safe corridors today, current lock-outs/tag-outs, pump/sluice timing, known buckled decks, and the sector emergency signal set (tones, light codes, and what silence means there).
- Buddy up. Solo is for the drones. Bring a second person or agree on a control-room check-in cadence at fixed waypoints.
- Pre-condition your gear to cold-damp. Put cameras and optics in sealed bags; let them equilibrate in the entry vestibule to prevent instant condensation.

What to wear
- Base and insulation:
  - Wicking base layer (synthetic or wool) + thin fleece or active insulation. Cotton will bite you.
  - Vapor barrier socks over liners if you’ll be long on wet grating; swap socks at warming booths.
- Shells and protection:
  - Waterproof-breathable shell with sealed seams; longer cut to shed curtains of condensate.
  - Cut-resistant, chemical-resistant gloves with thin liners; keep a dry spare set.
  - Safety helmet with a brim and chin strap; mount a low-profile headlamp and an anti-fog visor or sealed goggles.
  - Hearing protection that preserves intelligibility: filtered earplugs or electronic muffs that compress impulse noise but pass alarms and speech.
  - Anti-slip, cold-rated, dielectric boots with aggressive tread; add detachable low-profile traction if ice forms, but avoid spikes that can catch in grating.
  - High-vis with reflective tape; choose anti-static fabrics to reduce sparks near dusty, dry sections.
  - Fall arrest harness with twin lanyards and screw-lock carabiners; clip where there are rated anchors. At minimum, use a short tether in exposed galleries.

What to carry
- Sensing and safety:
  - 4-gas monitor (O2, CO, H2S, LEL) on your shoulder, always on. Set vibrate alarms.
  - Personal oxygen escape hood or small O2 rebreather for transiting thin-air segments and blowdowns.
  - Half-mask respirator with P100 + organic vapor cartridges; goggles that seal over it. Keep dry.
  - Non-contact voltage tester and an insulated probe/pole for checking suspicious puddles and grating.
  - Compact thermal camera or IR thermometer for spotting iced pipes, hot motors, and safe handholds.
- Light:
  - Two intrinsically safe headlamps (one warm tint cuts fog better) + a hand light. Carry chemical light sticks for passive marking; no flames here—ever.
- Navigation and comms:
  - Sector map printouts in a waterproof sleeve; grease pencil for annotating wet metal.
  - Inertial/breadcrumb tracker that doesn’t need GPS; leave no adhesives near intakes.
  - Hardline or through-metal comms if available; radios are fickle under concrete. Bone-conduction headset under the helmet works well.
  - Whistle is useless in this roar. Use light codes and tactile signals with your partner.
- Warmth and sustainment:
  - Insulated bottle with hot, unscented fluid; bite valve cover to keep out biofilm.
  - High-calorie, non-crumbly food in crushable packaging. No wrappers loose—anything that gets into an intake is an alarm and a fine.
  - Chemical heat packs rated for low-oxygen environments. Warming booths exist, but you queue behind maintenance.
- Tools and odds:
  - Short strap or tool tethers; nothing should be free to drop through grating.
  - Small towel or absorbent wipes; nitrile spares; anti-fog wipes for optics.
  - First aid: cold injuries, lacerations, eye wash ampoules, wound cleanser that works in dirty water contexts. Tetanus shot up to date.
  - Dry bags with desiccant. Batteries die fast in cold—keep spares inside your jacket.

How to move and read the place
- Let sound lead your sight. Every function has a voice. Learn:
  - Turbines: long, sinuous pitch shifts on schedule—good orienting beacons.
  - Belts and chains: rhythmic clatter from a distance; don’t cross under a load path.
  - Rails’ carrier hum: a steady, almost-musical tone. If it drops or detunes, an “accident tide” may be forming. When the hum dies, you move to shelter immediately.
  - Sluice coughs and hydraulic snaps: clear the edge; spray and suction follow.
- Footing, edges, and rails:
  - Test every unfamiliar grating with your weight on the near edge first; scan for buckling and frost rime.
  - Keep three points of contact on ladders; rungs can be glazed with condensate or ice.
  - Do not trust paint; trust the chevrons. Hydrophobic chevrons point where water will shuck—expect fast runoff along their lines. Hydrophilic threads direct drips; don’t stand under a threadline unless you want a cold shower.
  - Handrails can hum with wire-spider webs. They catch lint and, sometimes, moths—harmless, but they’ll wet your gloves and distract your grip.
- Air and alarm literacy:
  - Oxygen feels “thin” before you know you’re hypoxic. Your 4-gas monitor is truth. Don your respirator or hood when O2 dips under 19.5%.
  - You’ll feel pressure waves—louvers “breathing” overhead. When a major purge begins, visibility can go to milk-glass in seconds. Back to a wall, lower stance, visor down. Wait for the tide to pass rather than walking blind over grating.
  - Silence is the fist. If the background machine-choir drops out abruptly, you are inside the pre-blowdown window. Shelter in place:
    - Get off centerline paths and away from edges and spills.
    - Kneel in the acoustic shadow behind a bulk transformer housing or a wind baffle.
    - Seal goggles and respirator; clip your tether to a solid post.
    - Wait for the cold cloud and alarms to peak and recede; check O2 and then move.
- Light discipline:
  - Point lamps down, not into fog; use warm low mode to cut backscatter.
  - Do not strobe unless you are signaling your partner in close quarters; some sensors interpret rapid light changes as alarms.
- Distance and pacing:
  - Accept that a “block” here can be a kilometer of terraces. Factor that into your warmth and hydration plan. Mornings (right after a reset) are the crispest; by late shift, everything will be wetter and slicker.

Schedules, tides, and timing
- Day tide (~20 hours): a flex of airflow to dump heat upward; expect higher wind speeds, dryer gusts, more noise. Good for covering distance if you’re warm and steady on your feet; bad for working near edges.
- Week tide: bioreactor dump—wet, warm, sweet-metal yeast smell. Filters shed cake on conveyors. Surfaces are slippery; go slower.
- Accident tide: unscheduled, heralded by loss of carrier hum, then alarms and blowdown fog. You’re not outrunning this; you’re out-waiting it in shelter.
- Micro-climates: heat spills cause mirage shimmers over thin films; hoarfrost blooms when black air hits chill stacks. Treat every glimmer as a clue: either traction is changing or visibility will.

Water and biology
- Water is everywhere and almost none of it is safe. It’s loaded with salts, metals, and life optimized for steel and plastic. Do not drink from sumps or condensate lines; don’t let cuts bathe in it. Rinse skin with your own water and cover.
- Biofilm is slick, iridescent at grazing angles. Avoid stepping where metal furs like frost.
- If you see fungi caged on motor housings, leave them. That heat-waste is someone’s politics and someone else’s hazard control.
- Fauna are marginal and fragile. Pale sump fish and white eels are signs the water’s routes are connecting in odd ways; don’t touch. Vent-swallows, if you glimpse a charcoal comma, are adept at not being there—don’t chase.
- Insects and moths hatch in panels when warm; your light is a lure. That “Do Not Feed The Moths” sign is more than a joke: it means don’t make food crumbs or light-pools where maintenance needs grit-free intakes.

Drones and etiquette
- Maintenance drones are the locals. Beetle-low, glass-eyed, felt feet: give them right-of-way and don’t interrupt their squeegee runs or AntiCrust scrubs.
- The black galvanic moss on panels is doing a job—soaking salts, not ornamental. Don’t brush it, don’t step on panels it’s seeded on.
- Do not leave tape, adhesives, or stringers that could mis-teach a dripline. This place writes water’s path into its skin; ad hoc edits ripple badly.
- Pack everything out. Food wrappers in a still corner are less likely to trip an intake alarm, but they will, eventually. Use a pocket trash pouch.

Food, warmth, and rest
- Warming booths exist—red coves with eight-minute heat rations. They’re your reset. Use them to swap socks and gloves, drink, check your plan. Don’t overstay; rations cut off and the next crew will need them.
- Eat dense, moist bars or gels that don’t shed crumbs. Crumbs feed moths; moths find panels; panels trip sensors. It’s a loop you don’t want to start.
- If you must rest away from a booth, find an acoustic shadow close to path lights, never on the grating edge, and set a timer; cold creep is real.

Photography, notes, and memory
- Cameras fog. Keep them bagged until they’ve temperature-matched, then work quick. Use amber filters to tame LED smear.
- Metal beams make good notebooks. Grease pencil beats markers in the damp. If you borrow the local habit—initials and coordinates—add a date and “visitor” so your breadcrumb doesn’t get mistaken for a work order.
- Don’t flash into dark water; you’ll blind yourself on the reflection and miss the actual edge.

Emergency playbook
- Slip/fall: arrest with your tether; call it in; do not try to scramble up slick panels without checking for voltage.
- Chemical splash: rinse with your water, then find the nearest eyewash or drench; report the leak—there’s likely a misprogram or seal going.
- Hypoxia: mask up, hood if necessary, follow the blue emergency ribbons to the nearest stair or booth. Breathe slow; panic burns oxygen faster.
- Lost: stop. Kill your main light, listen. Turbines, conveyors, and the day-tide will tell you where you are. Match sounds to your annotated map. Call Control if you have it. Backtrack using your own tactile markers, not the facility’s driplines.

When to go, where to stand
- Best window: early in the sector’s “morning,” after resets, before purge cycles stack. Fewer drips, crisper footing, clearer air.
- Worst window: just before a scheduled purge in your level or a fan reversal above; all the little weathers come alive at once.
- Best vantage: the lip of a detention hall just after a sluice opens—the room sings, the floor becomes a sheet of moving glass, and you understand how the city breathes. Stay well back; the suction is subtle but real.
- Best silence: behind a transformer housing in an acoustic shadow. It’s not quiet; it’s a different roar you can rest inside.

Attitude to pack
- Treat it like a living schedule. You’re not sightseeing so much as synchronizing: with fans, with drains, with the carrier hum and the soft drum of water under the grates.
- Be small, decisive, and kind to systems. The place is tuned for certainty; you are the variable. Leave fewer drips than you found.

Checklist on the stair
- Helmet + chin strap; goggles down
- 4-gas monitor on; O2 20.9% here?
- Respirator fitted and ready; cartridges dry
- Headlamp warm tint on low; spare accessible
- Harness clipped; tool tethers secure
- Hearing protection in, still hearing alarms?
- Map photographed; time sync checked
- Partner check: signals, route, abort points
- Heat packs, water, food, spare gloves, socks—packed
- Comms link tested

Walk in ready to listen. In this under-world, sound is sight, schedules are weather, and water writes the map every day. If you align to those, you’ll be fine—and you’ll get to hear the city inhale and exhale from the inside.
