What you’re walking into
- Not a cave in the usual sense; a small, undecided chamber that is still practicing being itself. Bright without a source. Cool, dry-to-damp in pulses. Everything is provisional: rock, light, water, even the ways creatures move.
- Rules run a little slant: light clarifies or loosens what’s real; shadows can briefly become routes for small things; water keeps the temperature-memory of earlier hours; gravity follows rock-grain more than “down”; erosion edits itself like a restless writer.
- You are far larger, louder, wetter, and more decisive than most things here. That makes you the main hazard in the room. Prepare to minimize your footprint and your edges.

Before you go
- Health and scent:
  - Bathe with unscented soap for a day or two beforehand; avoid perfumes, deodorants, strong detergents. A leech here hunts scent, not blood.
  - If you shed a lot of dander, wear a light head covering. Trim loose threads and Velcro fluff from clothing so you don’t seed the place with lint.
- Mental prep:
  - Practice moving and looking slowly. Stare less; notice more. Learn to keep still without tensing.
  - Breath discipline helps. The chamber “breathes”; you’ll feel it. Box-breath (4 in, 4 hold, 4 out, 4 hold) keeps your body from syncing unpleasantly to the room’s tremor.
- Ethics:
  - Treat it as living and new. You are not there to test what it can become by pushing on it. Observe, pass lightly, leave no trace, take nothing.

What to wear
- Color/finish:
  - Matte off-white or soft gray from head to toe. This keeps you from throwing sharp, high-contrast shadows that might open unintended passages or spook the fauna.
  - Avoid black, glossy, or high-saturation colors.
- Layers:
  - Thin, breathable insulating base (it is cool, with brief damp pulses), topped with a smooth, softshell that won’t snag on calcite floss.
- Footwear:
  - Soft, non-marking soles with excellent friction and feel (approach shoes, felted river sneakers, or split-sole dance shoes with textured rubber). No lugged soles or microspikes—you’ll abrade young stone and crush microhabitats.
- Hands/face:
  - Thin nitrile liners under lightweight cotton gloves: reduce oils, sweat, and salt transfer without leaving synthetics behind.
  - A light, breathable face veil or mask to baffle and diffuse your exhalations so you don’t re-write the room’s humidity in one spot.
- Eyes:
  - Neutral-density or variable ND sunglasses with high optical clarity; a mild amber secondary pair for glare-fatigue. Polarized lenses can help, but don’t rely on them—the light isn’t behaving like surface glare.
- Hair/loose items:
  - Tie back hair; stow cords. Silence everything with elastic wraps or tape. No jangling metal.

What to bring (and what not to)
- Bring:
  - One small, white, translucent diffuser panel (A4 size). Not for making more light—there’s enough—but to soften any shadow you can’t avoid casting.
  - A dimmable, warm, diffuse headlamp wrapped in tracing film or milk-plastic, for emergencies only. Keep it off by default.
  - Paper notebook and pencil. Avoid solvent markers and strong glues (scent).
  - Small, soft kneepads and a square of clean, white cloth to kneel on. You’ll spend time low.
  - Silent timepiece with haptic alarm (no ticks, no chimes).
  - Distilled water in a tightly sealed bottle for you only; a collapsible cup so you don’t drink from basins. Do not pour or drip any into the chamber.
  - Compact first-aid with hemostatic gauze (in case a calcite floss slices you) and saline wipes.
  - Lint roller and a sealable waste bag for decontamination at exit.
- Do not bring:
  - Tripods or trekking poles with spikes. If you must brace, use your body and the knee cloth.
  - Aerosols, bug spray, chalk, flagging tape, food with odor, crumbly snacks.
  - Nets, sample vials, or forceps—unless you carry a research permit and a conscience ironclad enough to leave them unused.

Arrival and acclimation
- Stop at the threshold. Let your eyes adjust to everywhere-at-once light. Feel the room’s breath: cool, near-silent, with a slight whisk at your skin when it exhales.
- Stand still for a full breath-cycle. This lets the chamber notice you, and it reveals what’s fixed right now and what’s wobbling toward a choice (you’ll see edges sharpen, then soften; basins bead or clear).
- Check for life in your intended path. In this place, “empty” fills if you watch a moment longer.

How to move
- Step selection:
  - Aim for uncommitted flats—places that aren’t clearly ledges, shelves, basins, nests, flossworks, or trails. If a surface looks like a “first draft,” treat it as still-thinking and keep off.
  - Re-use your own footprints when you backtrack. Your first pass already taught the stone and biota how to tolerate you; don’t make it learn twice.
- Speed and posture:
  - Move like you’re underwater: smooth, continuous, no staccato. Bend your knees; keep your center low. Don’t surprise the room with sudden decisions.
- Hands:
  - Keep them close to your body. Don’t steady yourself on walls or near not-yet-stalactites. They recoil; you’ll teach them the wrong lesson.
- Gaze discipline:
  - Don’t fixate on a single bright patch; when you do, a counter-shadow condenses elsewhere, sometimes as a usable “line.” That can invite things through. Scan in soft arcs, pausing briefly in many places rather than drilling into one.
  - If a dim line starts to form from your shadow, change your angle or lift the diffuser panel behind your shoulder to break it into harmless shade.
- Breath and sound:
  - Whisper only if needed; silence is the native medium here. Even zippers are loud. Time your small movements with the room’s exhale; things are clearer then, but predators prefer it too—be alert.

Reading the room’s rules (and staying on the right side of them)
- Light edits reality:
  - Bright saturation “decides” edges; dimness fattens possibilities. If you need a surface to stay what it is while you pass, bias it brighter with your body position or a held reflector—but don’t create hard, straight shadows elsewhere while doing so.
- Gravity is polite:
  - Feel for the rock-grain lines—subtle urges where your balance “wants” to run. Creatures follow those lines. So should you. If your foot feels mysteriously light or heavy in a way that disagrees with down, adjust along the perceived grain, not against it.
- Water remembers:
  - Basins are time-keepers. Don’t drink. Don’t trail fingers unless you accept that your nerves may feel a wrong-hour cold that travels up your arm and sits oddly in your gut. If you accidentally touch, stop. Let your body re-sync before moving.
- Shadows are passage:
  - Avoid laying long, uninterrupted dark lines along floor-wall seams or across the spiders’ frameworks. If your gear or body casts one, shuffle or rotate to break it.
  - Never deliberately “draw” a doorway with your shade. You are too large to use it; others are not.
- Erosion is undecided:
  - Crisp edges can blunt under your foot mid-step; powder can crust up again by the time you lift it. Place each foot as if you may have to commit to that surface twice.

Coexisting with residents
- General:
  - Do not feed, do not touch. Let approaches happen to you; if something tastes the air near your hand, hold still and let it finish its sentence.
  - The structures—shell spindles, pebble rings, floss frameworks, floating silt dikes—are not art installations. They are ongoing negotiations with the room. Give them generous clearance.
- The quiet swarmers (white isopods, glass shrimp):
  - Your breath and sweat-salts are magnets. Keep hands gloved; keep your mask on.
- Salamander:
  - It reveals itself between looks. If you see a “comma” of animal, treat that area as occupied, even if, a breath later, it’s stone again. Don’t step there.
- Snails with square plans:
  - If there’s a droplet forming above one, it’s hunting the echo of that fall. Do not disturb the droplet. Pass well outside its future cone.
- Spiders with calcite floss:
  - Their frameworks aren’t sticky but are braced and knife-clean. They slow air—your sleeve may stall. If touched, back straight out along the approach vector; don’t sweep sideways and scissor the frame.
- Leeches that ride droplets:
  - They follow scent and condensing moments. When the air cools a hair and beads form, stay still; let the patter pass. Don’t present a wet target: keep sleeves dry.
- Centipede that moves when not watched:
  - If you see a “row of ridges,” assume it’s the centipede. Keep it in your direct attention while you back away. Don’t blink yourself into quick glances; commit to a steady regard. If you have a companion, trade attention cues (“mine,” “yours”) before shifting your eyes. If solo, use your peripheral only when you’re already at safe remove; a mirror is unreliable—attention, not photons, seems to matter.
- The plum-white bat:
  - It drinks silence and warmth. Your heat draws it. If it slides nearer without moving, go still. Don’t exhale toward it.

Timing your visit
- “Morning” (cold, white, sharp): best for clarity; webs are taut, basins crisp. Move with precision. Droplet falls are more singular; leeches less active. Good for a first entry.
- “Midday” (soften and sweat): more condensation, leechier moments, shrimp lively; spider frameworks extend in the quiet before bright—give them room.
- “Evening” (crisp and dry): fungus vents appear, leaving salt punctuation. Do not step near new rings; they mark yesterday’s breath cooling in place.
- The room occasionally flares with impossible brightness, revealing a high seam like a lens. It will pass in a breath. Do not chase it with your eyes; let it come and go from the corner of your vision to avoid inducing a migraine of the soul.

If something goes sideways
- Glare overwhelms you:
  - Close one eye. Look into your cupped palm to reset. Swap to amber lenses. Keep your head still while your stomach catches up.
- Your pulse syncs to the room and you feel floaty:
  - Crouch. Put the back of one gloved hand against your sternum, feel your own rhythm, and count four slow breaths. When the chamber exhales next, stand and leave a few steps of space between you and where you were standing.
- You nick yourself on calcite floss:
  - Step back to clear any frameworks. Sit on your knee cloth. Saline rinse, pressure with gauze. Keep blood sealed—scent is attention here.
- The centipede is too near:
  - Lock eyes (conceptually) with the ridge-row. Announce (even silently) “I see you.” Back away heel-to-toe. Only when there’s clear stone between you and the nearest grain-path should you look elsewhere.
- You’ve accidentally formed a long, crisp shadow along a seam:
  - Lift the diffuser behind your shoulder; rotate your torso slowly until the line breaks into blotches. Step to scatter it further.

Photography and notes
- No flash. Ever.
- Shoot from stable kneels. Use high dynamic range; accept that edges may shift between bracketed shots. That’s part of what you’re documenting.
- Don’t shade a subject with your body; edge-clarity may change the thing you’re trying to record. If you must control contrast, use the diffuser to add light, not remove it.

What not to do (even if you’re curious)
- Do not pluck a not-yet-stalactite to “see it recoil.” It’s already decided against you.
- Do not trail a finger through a basin to watch time prickle your skin. If you do, don’t do it twice.
- Do not stack, line, or “assist” the chamber’s buildings. Your symmetry is not its symmetry.
- Do not bring in dark cloth to “test” a human-sized shadow passage. The room is not for you to leave through; it is for smaller negotiations.

Leaving and decontamination
- Retrace steps along your own prints. Leave the room at an exhale; edges are clear and goodbyes are simpler.
- At the threshold:
  - Lint-roll sleeves, knees, and boot-uppers; check for mist-like eggs, salt rings, floss threads.
  - Wipe soles gently with a dry cloth; bag all lint and wipes. Seal your waste bag.
- Quarantine gear for 72 hours from other biomes in your multiverse kit. The rules here hitch rides quietly.

Why go at all
- To learn the discipline of gentleness. To watch a place practice becoming itself, and to be patient enough not to nudge it. To feel a world where light and choice are the same muscle, and where even your shadow has consequences. If you do it well, you’ll leave with nothing but changed attention—and the room will keep none of you. That’s the right exchange.
