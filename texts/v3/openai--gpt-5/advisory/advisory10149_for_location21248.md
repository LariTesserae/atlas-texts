What this place will feel like
- Expect to be held. The water is warm and soft, the light green-blue and grainy, and everything wants you to slow down. Sound wraps close; your own wake will be the loudest thing you hear. Time will dilate because nothing rushes here.
- You will have altitude without sky. You are on a drowned highland—your body understands “cliff,” “ledge,” and “drop” even though you’re buoyant. Vertigo is real and oddly pleasant; it can also be dangerous.
- Plants run the show. Rock is newborn and sharp; life has already begun to laminate, web, and curtain it. Almost everything you touch will try to adhere, cushion, or very gently digest you.
- Big animals are rare, almost ceremonial. If one passes, that will be your day. Most of what moves is small and only visible when you change how you look.

Before you go: training and mindset
- Learn to be slow. Practice hovering motionless in water and moving with fingertip sculls and tiny fin flicks. Your goal is to make disturbances smaller than a falling leaf.
- Buddy discipline: stay within arm’s reach and line-of-sight at all times. Practice haptic communication (touch signals) and tether etiquette; sound doesn’t carry far here.
- Vertigo management: rehearse focusing on instruments when your inner ear disagrees with your eyes. Learn to park yourself on a “perch” and watch, instead of chasing views.
- No-touch ethic: assume every surface is living or becoming living. Practice the habit of braking with water, not with hands or knees.
- Patience as a sense: plan to spend long still stretches, letting details rise out of the scene on their own schedule.

Environment brief you should internalize
- Pressure may be deceptively “light.” This is a highland ocean—perched water with density layers and internal weather. Bubbles are lazy and big up here; there’s likely a lower-than-usual hydrostatic pressure for the depth your eyes guess. Treat it as altitude diving plus stratified water.
- Layers shift. Thermo- and halo-clines drift through like weather, sometimes bringing internal “rain” that thickens the water. Visibility, buoyancy and gas behavior can change over minutes to hours.
- Geology is fresh. Basalt edges are glassy and knife-sharp, warmed by sun and still adjusting—flakes and plates do let go along the escarpment.
- Chemistry is busy. Many plants exude acids and gels to eat the rock; make sure nothing you wear is vulnerable to mild acids or enzymatic goo.

How to go: two safe approaches
1) In a 1‑atmosphere micro-sub or soft-hover “glider”
- Best for non-divers or anyone wary of unknown pressure regimes. Look for electric, low-wash thrusters and panoramic, optically flat viewports.
- Advantages: no decompression issues, no gear-snag risk, you won’t bruise the forest with your wake, and you can park at the brink to watch edge-theater in comfort.
- Ask for: blue-green lidar mapping, downward and forward altimeters, and a “drift mode” that holds station with millimeter-per-second corrections.

2) As a swimmer in a full-coverage hydrosuit with a closed-circuit rebreather
- Only if you are a practiced diver who’s comfortable with tethers and trim in fragile environments.
- You want extremely gentle finning, line management skill, and the ability to stay off the bottom for long periods without fidgeting.

Life support and pressure management
- Use closed-circuit rebreather (CCR) with two independent bailouts. It keeps your bubble signature minimal and your wake quiet.
- Gas choice: bring a trimix that keeps narcosis low over an unknown pressure range. Let your support AI or dive computer select setpoints dynamically from live ambient pressure and oxygen sensors. Do not run pure O2 or high setpoints near the plateau—plant thickets can spike O2 locally.
- Decompression: treat this as altitude diving in a stratified medium. Use algorithms with altitude compensation and wide safety margins (low gradient factors), and budget slow, patient ascents along the same path you came down. If you cannot get confident pressure data, choose the micro‑sub instead.
- Redundancy: two independent pressure/depth sensors, two computers, physical slate with a simple schedule, and a per-buddy analog SPG/bottom timer as last resort.

Suit and personal gear
- Suit: full-coverage, warm-water suit rated for abrasion and mild acids (smooth exterior, no exposed stitching); integrated hood and full-face mask. Gloves and boots with Kevlar or ceramic-dotted soles.
- Cutters: carry two—blunt-tipped shears and a ceramic line cutter—for bubble-vines and tape-leaf curtains. Practice freeing yourself without thrashing.
- Buoyancy and trim: low-profile wing/BC with redundant bladder; short, soft-edged fins or small forearm sculls to reduce wash. Avoid scooters unless you have a micro-DPV with feathered thrust and shrouded props.
- Lights: low-intensity, warm-balanced fills to restore reds for photography; a close-up red light for macro that plants are less likely to “notice.” Keep beams soft; bright blue blasts will make the forest stiffen.
- Navigation: 
  - Spool a thin guideline from a bare-rock anchor near your drop point; never tie into living mats.
  - Wear a blue-green optical comm/beacon, a low-power acoustic pinger, and a small haptic tether to your buddy.
  - Instruments: ambient pressure/altimeter, dissolved O2 sensor, conductivity/salinity probe, temperature probe. A wrist display that shows gradient changes will warn you when an internal boundary drifts in.
- Skin and mucosa: do not expose. Full-face mask keeps plant saps and mineral tastes out of mouth and eyes. If you must open the mask, rinse with your own clean water loop, not local.
- First aid: rinse bottle with neutral water, pH-indifferent gel for minor stings, occlusive dressings, and a spare fingertip glove layer for tears.

Getting there, getting around, getting back
- Entry/exit: have a fixed “bright corridor”—a pre-surveyed approach path marked by a hovering beacon above bare rock. No plan here should rely on a freewater emergency ascent; your exit is the same door you came in or your sub.
- Stay high on the plateau at first. The brink is theatre—watch from a safe stand-off. If you go to the edge, clip a guideline to inert rock well back and keep a meter or two of vertical clearance so a sudden lean in the plants doesn’t nudge you over.
- If a wall of whiteness (an internal cloud) moves through, stop. Let it pass. Your instruments may twitch; your buoyancy may too. This is normal. Breathe, wait, and watch how the bubble chains lean before you resume.
- Do not shelter under overhangs of fresh basalt plates. Calving happens without warning, and you won’t hear it from far away here.
- If a leviathan rises: hold position, angle yourself side-on to minimize profile, and let it choose its line. The water it pushes will be a warm sigh more than a shove. Enjoy it, but don’t chase.

Touch and wake etiquette
- Assume the mats are building load-bearing architecture—baffles, terraces, shelves. Your fin wash is erosion on the scale that matters here.
- Do not brush bubble garlands; they are the forest’s breath and scaffold. Do not pop gas pockets for fun.
- If you must stabilize, touch bare basalt with two fingers and lift again. Never grab ribbons, tapes, or chimneys; they’re often load paths grown from their own excretions, and they break clean.
- Your best posture is a quiet hover with your hands occupied in gentle sculling and your knees slightly bent, feet high.

What not to bring or do
- No dangly gear, clips, or nets. Everything stows smooth and tight.
- No strobes on high or white floodlights raked across the brink; bright windows from above already change plant posture, and you don’t need to add sudden noon.
- No sampling unless you hold explicit permits. This forest is very young and busy. Even your fingerprints are a disturbance.
- Don’t taste the water. It’s rich in plant saps and new minerals; you don’t know the enzymes.
- Don’t wedge anchors into cracks softened by roots; they will tear wider than you think. Use micro-weights or removable hooks on dead, glassy faces only.

Reading the place in real time
- Bubble mood: where pendants lean, the “afternoon” current moves there. Bubble size and speed tell you about local pressure and viscosity—bigger and slower means “higher” in this ocean’s sense; smaller and brisker, you’re under a denser lid.
- Plant posture: if fan-groves fold or mats tighten, a brighter window has passed overhead; hold still and watch the afterimage ripple.
- Rock voices: faint mutters through your bones mean warming or cooling plates adjusting. Back off from sheer faces when you feel that.
- Small-life cues: comma-creatures hop at leaf edges where microflow concentrates; starbursts open when you shade them. Use your body as a moving, very gentle cloud to see them perform.

A day-plan that works
- First hour: acclimatize on a broad, bareish ledge. Practice not touching, watch how your wake curls into the fuzz. Note a few “habits”—a ledge that exhales, a bubble-vine that pearls faster than its neighbors.
- Middle hours: ease toward the brink along your guideline. Park 2–3 meters back and a meter up. Let the edge-theatre play; this is your best chance at a passing grazer or the brush of something large from below.
- Last hour: drift home along the same line, climbing very slowly through the brighter corridor. Give your body time to forgive any pressure games the layers have played on it.

Photography and keeping a record
- ISO high, shutter slow, and brace your camera with your breath. The light is soft; your subject is movement by patience.
- Use soft red fill for macro; it’s “invisible” to most of the forest and brings back colors you’d otherwise never see.
- Write down behaviors, not just scenes: “this fan folds by degrees when the water brightens,” “this ledge exhales more in the afternoon.” You’ll start to recognize place by habit rather than view.

Health and recovery
- On exit, rinse everything in neutral water. Don’t carry gels, fronds, or silt into other worlds or back to your room. Quarantine your gear for 24 hours.
- Treat minor acid stings with copious neutral-water rinse and an occlusive layer; don’t try to guess the chemistry with household neutralizers.
- Expect a pleasant fatigue and a lingering “quiet” in your head. Hydrate, eat salt, and sleep.

If things go sideways
- Entangled: freeze, slow your breathing, then work methodically with your cutters from the snaggiest point out. Signal your buddy to take gentle tension off the vine, not to yank.
- Visibility drops to arm’s length: stop, clip to your guideline, and wait. Internal clouds pass. If you must move, reel home hand-over-hand with your buddy counted in.
- Suit breach: press the leak flat with a palm or patch and abort along the line. These are warm waters; you have time if you don’t thrash.
- Computer disagreement: trust the more conservative schedule or your slate; call the sub.

Why you’re really here
- To witness very new rock making an agreement with very new life. Plants here are engineers building a city of tongues and shelves hour by hour. Your job is to keep your wake from erasing their lines.
- To learn a different meaning of “high” and “quiet.” This place is altitude without sky, weather without wind, a forest that drinks light and makes gas pearls for stairs.
- To be reminded that patience is a form of movement. If you do it right, you come back with the particular habits of three ledges and a single bubble-vine, and everything else you saw arranges itself around those.

Pack list, concise
- 1-atm micro-sub/glider, or: full-coverage acid-resistant warm-water suit + hood + full-face mask; CCR with two independent bailouts; redundant depth/pressure/PO2 sensors; analog backups.
- Short, soft-edged fins or micro-DPV with shrouded props; buoyancy wing with redundant bladder.
- Two cutters; low-intensity lights (with red macro option); blue-green optical comms; low-power acoustic pinger; haptic tether; guideline reels and inert anchors.
- Environmental sensors: O2, temperature, conductivity; wrist nav with layer alerts.
- First-aid micro-kit; neutral-water rinse; spare gloves; repair patches.
- Camera with diffused lights; slate and pencil.
- Decon kit and drybag; warm layers for after.

Above all: arrive unhurried, leave unhurried, and let the place set the tempo. It will show you more if you behave like a slow, polite weather system than like a visitor.
