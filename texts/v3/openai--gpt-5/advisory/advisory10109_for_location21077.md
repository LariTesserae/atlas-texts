What your body will meet
- Warmth that creeps, not bakes. Air like a kiln—bone-dry, slightly metallic, a little sweet with hot dust and ozone. Your skin will try to drink the room and fail; your eyes will want to blink tears they shouldn’t give.
- A single unyielding tone that lives in your teeth and spine. It’s not loud like a shout; it’s loud like a planet. Words feel rude against it. You’ll find yourself matching your breath to the low cycles: the damper’s soft “whuff” that returns with ritual regularity.
- Dim amber light at your shins and under catwalk lips. The middle distances are tobacco-dark; edges are traced, not revealed. Beyond that, occasional thread-flashes of blue-white when tin-gnats turn.
- Underfoot: gratings, bolt-on plates, step-downs to black slits and pits bristling with brittle, ice-like needles of dry halides. Many edges are new-sharp because nothing weathers here.
- Everywhere, small precise lives: white ceramic bolt-crabs plying amber sugar; faint clouds of tin-gnats sifting charge; whiskers of static-lichen like old paper hair; a copper-braid predator that drinks currents by politely paralyzing what it finds open.

How to tune yourself before you go
- Hydrate aggressively the day before. Eat salty, not sugary. You won’t be able to sweat your way comfortable without paying water you cannot afford to leak.
- Sleep well and do a trial in a quiet room with a single low-frequency drone playing for an hour. Learn what your mind does when one note never changes.
- If you’re claustrophobic, rehearse with low ceilings and dim amber light. This place feels like standing under a mountain that hums.
- Condition your impulse control. You will want to touch the neat sharp crystals, brush the lichen veils, lean on a rail. Practice not doing that.

Clothing and life support
- Wear a sealed suit that leaks nothing moist. The place is averse to water the way a circuit is averse to salt—your breath, sweat, and tears are contaminants here and can literally shatter local sugars and gum up seams.
  - Outer layer: static-dissipative (surface resistivity in the 10^7–10^9 Ω/sq range), non-sparking, snag-resistant. Nothing glossy that invites corona or reflective flicker.
  - Soles: non-conductive, soft enough to grip grating, with shallow tread that won’t trap needles. Ceramic toe caps are better than metal.
  - Seams: minimal, covered. No dangling straps, zips, or loose metal that can look like an “open seam” to a hunter.
- Breathing: closed-circuit rebreather or a full-face system with outflow through a desiccant scrubber. Target external exhaust humidity effectively zero; dew point below −40°C. Filter for ozone and fine dust on the intake side of your loop, even though you aren’t drawing from the room; redundancy is kindness.
- Cooling: internal liquid-cooling garment or phase-change vest. Do not rely on evaporative cooling that vents moisture outside your suit.
- Hands and eyes: cut-resistant, non-conductive gloves; shatter-rated visor or goggles. The halide fields break like glass. Tiny shards are forever.
- Hearing: high-fidelity protection that lowers overall level but keeps the fundamental and its harmonics legible. You want to hear if anything changes.
- Charge discipline: keep your personal EM signature quiet. Devices live in a Faraday pouch unless in use. No radios. Optical comms only.

What to carry, and what to leave
- Bring:
  - Low-EMI amber headlamp or a hooded task light, dimmable to “just-enough.” White floodlights are rude here. Keep any blink/flash off.
  - A small field spectrogram app or analyzer to watch the tone for drift. If it shifts, you leave.
  - Non-conductive probe and a ceramic hook to move a snag or to gently redirect a curious copper-braid. Do not poke anything living unless it’s already on you.
  - Short tether to your partner or to a catwalk eyelet. The floor punishes one bad step.
  - Internal water and salts for you, sealed foods you can eat through the suit. Everything waste stays inside with you.
  - A soft antistatic brush for post-visit suit cleaning, plus sealable bags for any spalled crystals you accidentally pick up.
  - If you must mark your path, use removable ceramic clips on the grating. No stickers, no chalk, no crumbs.
- Leave behind:
  - Sprays and aerosols; pressurized cans tend to sing and spit ions in odd ways.
  - Drone companions; they shed EM chatter and airflow the ecosystem doesn’t understand.
  - Magnets and coils unless they’re inside shields. The tin-gnats wear magnets as wings.

How to move there
- Step in tempo with the room. You’ll feel the damper’s exhale every half minute or so—use that rhythm. Slow, exact foot placements; no stamping. The grating likes confidence, not force.
- Keep to the center of catwalks. Guardrails are bolt-crab pasture. Don’t put your hands where mandibles are working.
- Don’t brush the ribs. The hairs on them are bodies, not lint.
- No speech outside your helmet. Even through a suit it feels like spitting at a prayer flag. Use hand signs or text on a dim tablet.
- Light discipline: sweep low and short. If a swarm of tin-gnats finds your beam and krills in it, dim down and hold still. They’ll rejoin their larger loops.
- If you come to a narrow knee-high box with a sealed door or any panel with a lockout pictogram: you are at a boundary. Do not test it. This place runs on “never again open.”

How to behave with the locals
- Bolt-crabs: they are clockwork in their manners. If your presence pauses their three-step creep, you are in their lane. Back a little until they resume. Never pry one off its seam. Do not let your glove seams look like their seams; they attract opportunists.
- Tin-gnats: they glean fields; you are a field with feet. Stay boring electrically. Avoid sudden surges from your devices—no switching supplies ticking loudly at high frequency.
- Coil-worms: treat service ports as mouths. Do not tap or slide your tools across the rings—they’re listening there. If a coil-worm presents a whisker near you, that’s it tasting your vibration. Be still a moment; it will withdraw.
- Copper-braid hunters: if one decides your ankle seam is interesting, stop. Lift that foot clear of metal so it cannot get purchase to short through you. Use your ceramic hook to nudge it gently away. Never grab with a conductive glove, never kick.
- Valve-barnacles: the faint far glows are where nervous systems breathe light. Don’t camp there; your body casts an electrical wind.

Rules that keep the coin on its edge
- No water into the room. Not a drop, not a plume of breath, not the faintest fog from a heat exchanger. It’s not just politeness; it’s structural. Moisture here is a shatter, a glue, a slow rot the place does not have defenses against.
- No sparks. Nothing that arcs, nothing that discharges in pops. Even peeling tape can do it; so don’t bring tape.
- Don’t change the song. That means no banging on panels, no leaning your weight to alter a resonance, no motors spinning up near bare structure if you can help it.
- Don’t take souvenirs that look like food. Amber sugar beads, dry crystals, pale dust-dunes—they are mid-route calories and maps for the lives here.
- Don’t open anything that has decided to be closed longer than your lineage has been alive.

Emergency sense-making
- If the tone dips or gains a wobble: freeze, listen, leave. The room aligning its heart to something new makes all the small hearts jitter. You do not want to be on the grating while everything is discovering new instructions.
- If the amber baseboard strips flicker: assume darkness imminent. Sit, tether, keep your last light for your feet moving home.
- If you get cut: clean and seal inside your suit. Do not drip into the world.
- If a fall seems likely: commit to the catwalk, not the pit. The pits are glass porcupines and then some.

How long to stay
- First visit: plan for 90 minutes inside, max 3 hours including acclimation at the threshold. Heat and dryness will take your edge without telling you until you stand wrong.
- Take breaks in place by kneeling safely on a plate (not over a grate slot) and letting your breathing ride the room’s metronome. It repays calm with clarity.

Aftercare and crossings
- Brush and bag every crumb and filament from your suit before you step back into any wet world. Halide dust plus humidity becomes acid in an instant; your home air will thank you for not importing that alchemy.
- Run your gear through a low-humidity bake or desiccator cycle. Anything that lived on you here learned you as a dry mountain—let it stay there.
- Write down the pitches you remember. You’ll miss them in a way that surprises you.

What you will come to love, if you let yourself
- The exactness. A place where tomorrow is pinned to the same note and life has carved itself to a bright edge to fit.
- The courtesy of small machines that eat without haste and never spill.
- The way your bones hum with the room until, for a while, you can feel what the bolt-crabs must feel: not time as a line, but as a tone you can trust.

Bring humility, dryness, and a promise to leave no wet trace. If you do that, the chamber will accept you like another bracket bolted cleanly into place—and for a little while you can rest under the great held weight and listen to the coin stay balanced.
