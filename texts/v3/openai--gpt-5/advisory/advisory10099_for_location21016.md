What it is, in plain terms
- A sealed, skyless salt-and-gypsum chamber: hyper‑arid, warm from stored rock heat, utterly still, totally dark.
- Air exchange is minimal and slow; sound dies quickly; there is no free water.
- The floor is a brittle, tessellated salt crust with dust-filled seams. Walls are pocked and draped with delicate gypsum formations. Sparse, slow-growing mineral-microbe films live under ledges.

Primary hazards (ranked)
1) Desiccation and heat stress: near-zero humidity strips moisture from skin, lungs, eyes; sweat evaporates instantly and silently.
2) Air quality: possible CO2 pooling in the basin, low O2 in pockets, salt/gypsum dust irritation to airways and eyes.
3) Fragile terrain and ethics: your footprints, scratches, and breath can mark or alter formations for centuries.
4) Orientation and psyche: absolute dark and silence; time perception drifts; easy to lose bearings if you move away from the basin’s rim or into fissures.
5) Corrosion/contamination: salt dust invades gear, wicks moisture later, and corrodes metals and electronics.

Go/no‑go checklist (before you step in)
- Companions: minimum of two travelers; one stays within line‑of‑sight and line tether at all times.
- Time cap: 90–120 minutes inside on a first visit. Longer stays dramatically increase dehydration and air quality risks.
- Weather/topside: ensure no external event could force you to linger (this place gives you no margin).
- Purpose: decide if you’re here to observe, map, or document. Don’t improvise “projects” once you’re in; overlong stays are where mistakes happen.

Personal kit (choose durability and dust-sealing over comfort)
- Respiratory and face
  - Full-face respirator with P100/HEPA filters, or at minimum an N95 plus sealed chemical-splash goggles.
  - Optional but excellent: a heat-moisture-exchange (HME) mask insert (used by desert runners). It traps some of your exhaled moisture and tempers inhaled air, protecting your nose/throat and minimizing condensation on formations.
  - Carry a compact O2/CO2 monitor with audible alarms (placed low near the basin floor for testing, and one on you).
- Lighting (redundant)
  - Three independent LED lights per person (headlamp + chest/helmet backup + handheld); warm-neutral 4000–5000K helps with glare on pale salts.
  - Diffusers/softboxes to reduce harsh reflection. Avoid hot halogens/incandescents.
  - Spare batteries in dustproof bags; wipe gear before swapping to keep salt out of contacts.
- Clothing and skin protection
  - Lightweight, breathable layers that fully cover skin; synthetic, non‑shedding fabrics.
  - Sealable hood or cap; neck gaiter.
  - Barrier balm for lips and hands; light, non-scented moisturizer applied beforehand. Don’t slather—minimize shedding.
  - Sealed goggles (no vents) to prevent ocular dryness.
  - Tyvek or similar over-suit if you intend to crawl/bump against walls; reduces fiber shedding and contamination.
- Footwear
  - Soft, grippy rubber soles; no metal cleats. Avoid leather (it desiccates and cracks).
  - Gaiters to keep dust out.
  - Move slow; use trekking poles with rubber tips to test plate edges and seams.
- Hands and tools
  - Thin, dexterous gloves (nitrile under light work gloves) to avoid leaving oils on formations.
  - Do not carry metal tools unless essential; if you must, keep them bagged. Salt finds metal.
  - Line and anchors: cave reel with low‑shedding static line; use freestanding cones or removable sand anchors if needed—no drilling, no permanent bolts.
- Hydration and nutrition
  - Water: 0.5–1.0 liters per hour per person; small, frequent sips. Use bite‑valve bottles to avoid splashes; keep valves capped when not drinking.
  - Electrolyte mix skewed away from sodium (you’ll incidentally ingest some from dust); favor potassium/magnesium balanced solutions.
  - Food: non‑crumbly gel packs or soft bars. No powders; no wrappers that shed foil or microplastics.
- Health kit
  - Saline nasal spray or xylitol/gel for post‑visit recovery (not to use inside unless an emergency).
  - Lubricating eye drops (single-use vials; again, for after).
  - Small pack of gauze, tape, and blister care; expect skin to dry and split more easily.
- Navigation/recording
  - Survey app or notebook in a sealed pouch; graphite pencil (pens dry out); lens cloths.
  - Camera with dust seals; keep lenses capped except when shooting; avoid flash bursts near delicate whiskers.
- Comms and safety
  - Radios will likely be unreliable; use rope tugs/hand signals.
  - Whistle is of limited use in this acoustic; still carry one.
  - Small foil bivvy only for emergency; never plan to wait out trouble here.

How to enter and move
- Pause at the lip: test air at multiple heights for CO2/O2; if CO2 > 0.5% (5,000 ppm) or O2 < 19.5%, shorten or abort the visit. CO2 can pool in the basin. If readings worsen as you descend, back out.
- Temperature check: if rock feels hotter than your skin, expect faster dehydration. Shorten time.
- Establish a path: pick the most durable line—usually plate centers, not dust seams and not near microbial films under ledges. Once chosen, reuse your own steps on exit to minimize new disturbance.
- Footwork: slow, deliberate, balanced. Expect plates to creak or “ping”; avoid prying edges up with your toes or poles.
- Distance from walls/ledges: keep at least an arm’s length. Your exhale and body heat can alter delicate whiskers over weeks if repeated; a single touch can snap them now.
- Silence and light discipline: dim lights, broad beam; no shouting. If you must communicate, use signals or murmur close. Sound shocks feel larger here.

Breathing, body, and time
- Breathe through your mask/respi to conserve airway moisture. Mouth-breathing unprotected will punish you within minutes.
- Expect thirst not to “feel” urgent at first; schedule sips every 10–15 minutes.
- Your skin will lie about sweat because it vanishes instantly. Use time— not sensation— to pace effort and water.
- Cap your visit by plan, not by how “fine” you feel. People often feel fine right up to a headache and a nosebleed.

Ethics: treating a place that barely changes
- This chamber records everything. A footprint, a scratch, a droplet—their signatures can last lifetimes.
- Don’t touch walls or draperies; don’t “test” the softness of halite with a thumb; don’t take souvenir plates or snap whiskers “for science.”
- Keep 1–2 meters from microbial films. Your breath adds CO2 and trace moisture that can bias their chemistry.
- No food prep, no urination, no spitting. All waste is packed out, including microtrash and used wipes. If you must relieve yourself, use a sealable container with absorbent and double-bag it.
- If you photograph, mind your stance: tripod tips and knee pads can etch the crust. Use broad pads if you must kneel.

Sensing and learning without breaking things
- Note the acoustics: a single stone tap is a datapoint; don’t repeat it everywhere.
- Study tessellations from a distance; shifts at seams tell you about contraction history. Photograph, don’t pry.
- Microbial “meadows” are often just a shimmer at certain angles. Side-lighting helps reveal without touch. Record their positions; revisit only from the same path if you ever come back.

Contingencies
- CO2 spike: you may feel air hunger, mild panic, or headache. Climb toward higher ground immediately, reduce exertion, switch to backup air if you carry any, and exit.
- Nosebleed or airway pain: stop, rehydrate, and if bleeding persists, pad and exit. Don’t use sprays inside unless needed to control bleeding.
- Slab shift or plate break: freeze, assess stability, then slowly redistribute weight. Backtrack along your entry path.
- Light failure: sit, breathe, and bring up the second light calmly. Panic magnifies dehydration and error.

Decontamination and aftercare (back at base)
- Brush then rinse salt dust from boots, clothing, packs before they hit humid air; otherwise the dust will wick moisture and corrode buckles, zips, and electronics.
- Wipe gear with slightly damp cloths, then dry thoroughly.
- Hydrate steadily over the next 6–12 hours; a liter with balanced electrolytes is typical for a short visit.
- Nasal and eye care: gentle saline irrigations and lubricating drops can prevent next‑day headaches and sandpaper eyes.
- Log exactly where you stepped and stood. If you return, reuse that path.

What not to bring or do
- No candles, stoves, incense, or smoke. Ventilation is not your friend here.
- No adhesive trail tape or paint marks. They fail and shed. If you must mark, use retrieval reflectors or a guideline you’ll take out.
- No drones. Rotor wash is a hurricane in this world; dust will coat everything and you’ll snap whiskers for a novelty shot.

If you must extend your stay (scientific work)
- Use a low‑flow, closed‑loop rebreather or at least a CO2 scrubber mask to reduce chamber gas burden.
- Stage sealed water and spare filters at the entry lip, never on the basin floor.
- Wear a clean-room over-suit and booties you can bag out and discard.
- Pre-sterilize sampling tools; sample only from loose debris already detached; document removals; take milligrams, not chunks.

What it will feel like
- Warmth that does not move. Your body heat seems to go nowhere; your sweat disappears like a thought.
- The dark is full and close but not threatening—more like thick velvet than void.
- Sound is a rare event. Your breath, damped by your mask, becomes more a presence in your chest than a noise in the air.
- Every step feels consequential because it is. The place has finished deciding what it wants to be, and it will remember you.

A simple ritual that helps
- At the threshold: touch your glove to your own chest—“I leave nothing”—and to your pack—“I take back all I bring.” It focuses you.
- On exit: turn, dim your light, and let the chamber’s dark re‑settle. The room had no sky before you; it will not miss your lamps. Let it close again exactly as you found it.
