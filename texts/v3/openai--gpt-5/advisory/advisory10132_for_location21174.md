Field briefing for the plant-cathedral under cold sky

What you’re walking into
- A living, engineered nave grown by a cold-adapted superorganism. Every surface is plant or plant-adjacent; even “rock” is scaffold the plant is teaching into hardness.
- Air is near freezing and at or near saturation. Your breath will bead on you; your body heat will be collected and repurposed. Nothing dries.
- Sound is infrastructure. A patient, chest-deep hum shapes water, times the room, and—along with latticed tissue—decides where light and droplets go. Your noises matter.
- Light is imported through biological waveguides tuned to deep blues and the rare polar day or star-glow. It is dim and green-blue with sudden pale flickers of bioluminescence.
- Fauna are components, not guests: ring-worms sifting microfilms, anemone-moths fanning dew into being, a comb-fingered mouse grooming drops, snail-choirs building shells by sound. You are the visitor.

Mindset
- Move like a pilgrim, not a tourist. Everything here is slow, deliberate, and continuous. Your job is to not become a weather event.
- Expect your senses to be retuned. The hum will feel like weather in your bones. Gravity will feel slightly opinionated. Your internal level will argue with your eyes.

Personal kit checklist
- Base: thin merino or synthetic that stays warm when clammy.
- Vapor barrier layer (VBL) top and socks to stop evaporative heat loss in 100% RH; accept some dampness as normal.
- Insulation: light but continuous—active fleece or aerogel-lined vest that tolerates wet. No puff that collapses when soggy.
- Shell: quiet, non-crinkly softshell that sheds condensation without broadcasting droplets; no Velcro, no jangly hardware.
- Head/face: insulated cap and a heat–moisture exchanger mask to warm and reclaim your exhalations and keep visible plumes minimal.
- Hands: thin liner gloves under waterproof, grippy overmitts. You’ll want dexterity without bare-skin heat signatures.
- Footwear: insulated boots with wide, soft, high-friction soles (felted or gecko-like microhairs). Avoid spikes, cleats, or sharp lugs that score tissue. Add short gaiters; expect beading.
- Traction staff: a light composite staff with a broad, soft tip for probing and three-point balance. No metal ferrule.
- Quiet kit discipline: wrap anything that can click or clank. Tape loose buckles. Ditch zippers that buzz.
- Light: dim, hooded amber or deep-red headlamp with diffuser and physical shroud. No blue-rich beams, no strobes, no lasers, no flash.
- Hearing: passive ear protection that attenuates highs but lets you feel the low hum. Avoid active noise-canceling; it emits signals.
- Comms: keep it simple. Hand signals. If needed, tethered throat mics to wired earpieces. Avoid open speakers and drones.
- Navigation: inertial logger for your own record; don’t expect RF to behave. Bring a few freestanding, inert ceramic tripods with reflective tabs for temporary, non-contact waymarks—and collect them on exit.
- Thermal management: small chemical warmers kept sealed; only deploy inside your clothing to avoid heat plumes that attract attention.
- Optics: anti-fog goggles; camera with hydrophobic-coated lens and big battery (cold tolerance). No flash; embrace long exposures.
- Medical: hypothermia kit, blister care, pH strips, sterile eyewash, mild base (bicarbonate) to buffer accidental contact with acidic weep, standard first aid.
- Motion comfort: meclizine or ginger for inner-ear dissent. Move slowly regardless.
- Waste: double-sealed bags. You’re a closed system here; nothing you shed should join the metabolism.
- Water/food: carry your own sealed water and dense food. Do not drink from the habitat. Do not feed anything.

Approach and entry
- Pause outside to cool your exterior. Open layers to let your surface temperature drop a few degrees so your first exhale and heat signature aren’t treated as events.
- Step through during a quiet between pulses. The hum cycles; after each low wave, wait half a beat—water is less mobile then, tassels are often retracted.
- First breath inside through your exchanger mask. It protects you and keeps the nearest weather-stations from over-sampling you.

Movement and gait
- Three-point contact. Probe with the staff, then step, then let the floor finish remembering you before shifting your full weight.
- Favor the denser “benches” and visibly reinforced paths. Avoid bright, glassy sheens and lacework edges that look fetal or recently healed.
- No stomping, no skidding. Let your soles meet the microcode of hydrophilic/hydrophobic patches without scraping.
- Move on the rests between hum pulses. When the low pressure rolls through, vertical threads and films mobilize: wait it out.
- Treat icicle-threads overhead like live wires. A snap rolls sound around the nave; freeze in place when you hear the spiral, then resume.

Light and sound discipline
- Keep light low, warm, and hooded. Deep blue is the plant’s preferred currency—don’t spend it. Amber/red won’t ride their waveguides and is less likely to trigger adjustments.
- Speak only if you must, close together, and softly. Even whispers can push skin-water; consonant bursts are little pumps. Hand signs are best.
- Silence your tech. No active sonar, no lidar bursts, no music. Your footsteps and heartbeat are enough stimuli.

Heat and breath
- Your heat is a commodity here. Insulate it, slow it, keep it inside your layers. Hearth behavior draws tassels; that’s not dangerous, but it’s disruptive.
- The mask makes your exhale a slow exhale. If tassels touch it, hold still; they will taste and leave.
- If you feel phantom breezes, it’s not wind; it’s animals or moths shunting air over tissue. Don’t fight it; shift position a half step and let them finish.

Hands and curiosity
- Do not stroke columns. They learn your heat and respond with water and growth you didn’t mean to ask for.
- Don’t scrape “almost-rock.” It will regrow, but you’ll trigger the weeping and spend someone else’s sugars.
- If you must lean, use your staff or a pad to distribute contact and interpose insulation.
- No samples. Leave snails, worms, moss-that-isn’t, and tassels to their weather. Your pockets stay empty.

Reading the room
- The baseline: a steady low hum that resets every few seconds, xylem popping like soft glass, faraway grit whispers, applause of magnified insect chitter.
- If the hum drops two octaves and the walls go darker and slicker, the calibrator is near. That long, slow arm is not hostile, but the system will borrow photons and cold from you.
  - What to do: stop, close your stance, lower your profile. Dim your light completely. Tuck hands close, chin down, and become a small, slow stone.
  - What not to do: don’t flee, don’t reach, don’t present a hot palm to any surface. Let it pass. The aftersign is a fine rim of microfrost and a slow relighting of vines.
- If the tassels lower en masse and droplets start to move sideways, you are inside a seasonal “song” moment. That is infrastructure in motion. Freeze, breathe shallowly through your mask, and wait for the set to complete.

Wildlife etiquette
- Ring-worms: translucent bands at root bases. Step wide. Their feeding lanes are water lanes.
- Anemone-moths: sideways, clear-fanned, glittered with frost. If they beat toward you, you are a dew problem; stop until they settle elsewhere.
- Comb-finger mouse: upside down, collecting droplets. It may approach your heat pocket and sing. Do not feed; enjoy the company.
- Ear-lobed snails: in shadow under ledges, chanting. Give a meter. Their ripples are building, not asking.
- If something you can’t see makes the plant hold its breath, you do too.

Navigation and timing
- Assume maps age by the day. The plant grows load paths, heals scratches, thickens throats.
- Place only freestanding, inert waymarks where there is bare, self-supporting substrate; retrieve them on return.
- Keep line-of-sight with your party. Gravity’s bias will make levels feel wrong; trust your staff and soles more than your ear.
- Plan to move slowly: 300–500 meters per hour is ambitious here. Build in long listens.
- Choose visit windows when the surface sky is likely to offer brief brightness if you want to witness a relight—otherwise, embrace the long darkroom day.

Hazards and responses
- Slip-and-soak: sit-fall more than head-fall. Keep your center low. If you do go down, roll rather than skid; friction scars.
- Cold creep: you won’t notice sweating, only the after-chill. Swap to dry liner gloves halfway. Add insulation early, not late.
- Acid weep: a damp patch may sting. Rinse with your clean water, dab with mild base if needed, cover. Eyes: eyewash, then goggles back on.
- Overstimulation: the sound can be too much. Pause, sit on your pack (insulated side up), close eyes, breathe through the mask, and count pulses to 20. The room will feel quieter after.
- Disorientation: if your ear and eye fight, stop. Plant your staff, square your feet on a firm bench, let three pulses pass. Your vestibular system will retune.

Fieldcraft that helps the room help you
- Mirror the tempo. Take one step per pulse, or one step per two pulses. The architecture expects cadence.
- Offer no loose droplets. Wipe condensation from exposed gear with a microfiber and tuck the cloth back inside your layers; otherwise, the room will send tassels to tidy you.
- Be a weak light source. Think moon, not headlamp. If your camera has a pre-focus assist beam, disable it.
- Let the plant finish what it starts. If a water thread begins to pull a bead across your sleeve, hold still; breaking it is wasteful. It will let go when it is done.

Exit and decontamination
- Backtrack on your own softened path where possible; it will be erasing itself with courtesy, but while it remembers you, you are lightest there.
- At the threshold, stop and cool again. Shake off but do not snap droplets; let them be recaptured.
- Outside, bag every scrap—lint, peel, thread. Wipe soles to remove microfilms. If there’s a decon protocol in your hub-world, follow it: you carried memory on your gear whether you meant to or not.

What it will feel like
- Like walking inside a single, slow organism that learned architecture. The hum will sit in your chest like a tame animal. Water will behave as if it knows whose idea it is. Your breath will belong to the air more than to you.
- You’ll discover how loud zippers are. How bright your own heat is. How patient gravity can seem when the floor answers your step with a gentle, living rebound.
- If you’re lucky, a photonic vine will relight above you with a faint northern glow, and for a moment you will see the whole nave as if it has remembered summer. Don’t do anything big in that moment. Just stand in it.

Bottom line
- Go sealed, go quiet, go slow.
- Don’t spend their light, don’t pump their water, and don’t ask the room to remember you more than it already must.
- Let your trace be a story written in how the next drop chooses to fall, and not in anything you leave behind.
