What you’re entering is a flooded, working room that still believes it’s a room. It keeps the discipline of a factory and the patience of a garden. Treat it as both.

Snapshot of the environment you’re stepping into
- Medium: warm, clear water throughout; no pockets of air. Expect good visibility, low turbidity, and gentle scheduled currents.
- Depth/pressure: “High” in the tower, not abyssal. Plan for recreational-to-moderate depths (10–30 meters equivalent). Pressure is noticeable but not hazardous if you plan like a careful diver.
- Light: strong, uninterrupted full-spectrum illumination, likely with some UV components; internal “noon” that does not end.
- Biology: exuberant plant and algal growth on every surface. No fish, no invertebrates, no animals at all. Many species are engineered, some are biosensors.
- Machinery: pumps, louvers, traveling cranes, pruning arms, nutrient windows that open/close, pollination jets. All are slow, scheduled, and quiet. Power is live.
- Surfaces: everything is coated in living matter; slick, soft, or velvety. Honeycombs, meshes, and baskets fill the voids where soil would be.
- Air you might be tempted to imagine is not there. The room remembers air only as geometry.

Before you go: skills, mindset, permissions
- Be able to move underwater with precision. If you don’t have buoyancy control that lets you thread delicate spaces without touching, don’t enter physically—use telepresence.
- Read and accept that you are stepping into a functioning agricultural organ. You are not a customer. You are a potential contaminant. The ethics here start with humility.
- If there is an operator, caretaker AI, or visible control authority, ask for a visitor window. If there isn’t, at least abide by basic lab biosecurity: take nothing in, take nothing out, leave no bubbles, leave no prints.
- Decide your mode:
  - Best: telepresence ROV with soft-prop thrusters and a neutral lighting package.
  - Safer human: sealed lightweight exosuit or full-face CCR diving, neutrally buoyant, no loose gear.
  - Do not: open-circuit scuba spraying exhaust and noise through an engineered water column unless you are explicitly cleared to do so.

What to bring
- Life support
  - Closed-circuit rebreather (CCR) tuned for silent, bubble-free operation. Setpoint 1.0–1.2 ppO2 is comfortable at these depths. Carry bailout gas and know your bailout plan.
  - If CCR is not an option, a full-face mask with exhaust diffuser and nitrox (28–32%) to give you margin and reduce narcosis at the upper end of the likely depth.
  - Integrated comms if you’re with a partner; otherwise, accept that you are essentially alone with your own head.
- Suiting
  - Thin wetsuit or skinsuit and hood; the water is warm. Drysuits overheat here unless you know your personal thermals. Choose smooth exterior materials; avoid Velcro fields and snag-prone textures.
  - Gloves you can feel through but that protect from resin, abrasive meshes, and biofilms. Non-snag booties with soft, wide treads.
- Movement and safety
  - Low-profile fins and a frog-kick habit. No flutter-kick.
  - A small spool and cookie markers for your own orientation; do not tie off to living structures unless absolutely necessary. Prefer steelwork that’s already part of the architecture.
  - Two cutting tools: a line cutter for emergencies and trauma shears. Use only to free yourself—this room builds itself with green ropes.
  - Compact handheld light with a warm beam for close inspection; keep it dim. The house lights are enough.
- Hygiene and biosecurity
  - Pre-entry: full rinse, then suit-up in a clean environment. Wear a hair cover under your hood. No cosmetics, sunscreens, or insect repellents; they will leach.
  - Post-exit: oxidizing rinse (peroxide-based) and UV cabinet exposure for all gear, followed by a freshwater wash. Treat it like leaving a BSL-2 greenhouse and a coral nursery at once.
  - Waste management: in-suit collection if you’ll be long; do not contribute nutrients here.
- Miscellany
  - A transparent slate and grease pencil for note-taking underwater. Cameras if you must, but use housings with rounded edges and soft handles.
  - A small microfiber swab on a stick for gently clearing a corner of a display if you need to read it. Do not scrape broad areas.
  - Personal ID/locator beacon sealed, but set to passive unless needed; you don’t want to ping through maintenance systems.

How to enter
- Look for internal service access within the tower rather than coming in from open water. If you must approach from outside, ride the lee of the tower; keep clear of warm effluent plumes that rise along its flanks—they can be surprisingly strong near vents and will telegraph your presence with a streamer of bubbles if you’re on open-circuit.
- Expect a flooded lock or a maintenance hatch with signage. If you see “Loading Bay,” you’re near human-scale access. Everything is underwater, but the geometry of ladders and catwalks remains useful for navigation.
- Before crossing a threshold, pause. Watch. This room runs on cycles. Count a few minutes. Learn the tempo of its bubble trains, arm movements, and louvers.

Moving inside without leaving a wake
- Trim yourself mid-water, horizontal, a meter off the living. Your best touch is no touch.
- If you must steady yourself, choose bare steel that peeks through or the thickest, oldest-looking mats. Touch with two fingers, slowly, and let go as if you’re lifting your hand from a sleeping animal.
- Frog-kick only. Pull-and-glide if you have to. Every fin stroke is a weather system for spores.
- Keep your exhalation (if any) diffused. Bubbles will lodge under fronds and fault readouts if you’re careless.
- Never swim blind into curtains of growth, under hanging cranes, or between paired rails; those rails are tracks. The machine moves slow, but it does move.

What can hurt you here (and what you can hurt)
- Entanglement: meshes, cable catenaries, vines trained as structure. That is the primary hazard. Carry your cutters, keep your gear tight, clip nothing that dangles.
- Pinch and shear: guillotine windows on nutrient racks, scissor pruners, pollination combs. They are deliberate and slow, but they do not see you. If you hear or feel a nearby thrum change and see a status light wink, give rails and slots half a meter of space.
- Suction: intakes behind grilles on duct faces and planter bases when bubble trains begin. They’re gentle, but avoid hugging them. If your slate pulls a little, you’re too close.
- Electrical: the system is built for wet, but don’t pry at panels or disturb cable looms. If you sense a tingling on exposed skin, leave the area calmly; it means insulation is failing somewhere you can’t see.
- Chemistry: nutrient-rich water can carry algal toxins and plant exudates. Protect eyes and skin; don’t taste, don’t sample. If you break a mat and feel a slick or a smell leak into your mask, move upslope, give it space to dilute.
- Heat: you can overheat silently in warm water under constant lamps. Pace yourself. If you feel floaty and content and time seems to blur, that’s your cue to back away and cool down.

Reading the room
- Screens under a glaze of algae still speak. A slow orange pulse walking a perimeter is airflow management—expect a soft current to shift leaves (and you). Hourly bubble chains along a planter base mean a “dawn” for that crop; visibility will sparkle and tickle your cheeks. Darkening nutrient racks behind closing windows signal a weekly recycle—stay clear of those racks for a while.
- Plants are instruments. A fern underside glowing blue has something to say about nitrates; if you see colors shift along a hedge, it’s mid-measurement. Don’t block the lamp with your body.
- The analog clock matters mainly to you. It confirms you’re not alone in perceiving time here. Use it to anchor yourself; stillness makes minutes elastic.

Where to be and where not to be
- Good places: the long visual corridors beneath ceiling trusses; along catwalks (treat them as lines, not sidewalks); near, not under, the cranes; by the far-end window, where outside is a sheet and you can watch rising motes without getting into them.
- Avoid: the immediate mouths of ducts; between paired rails near the ceiling; deep within dense woven planters where a maintenance arm might travel; any recess that feels warmer on your glove than the room water—that warmth is active process.

Etiquette, or how to be the right kind of ghost
- Don’t feed, don’t free, don’t fix. The urge to help is strong in rooms that look abandoned but are not.
- Don’t scrape off “just a little” algae to see a warning stripe. The moss has rewritten the language here on purpose.
- Do not harvest. A leaf you pocket is a line cut from a scored piece of music.
- Your bubbles (if you have them), your shed skin, your impatience—they all leave fingerprints. Make a deliberate choice to be boring.

If something changes suddenly
- A tone, a light sweep, a coordinated louver shift: the room is about to do something. Freeze, observe, back three slow kicks into open water and let it happen.
- If visibility drops from spore release, stop moving. Let the slow snow fall past. Disturbance makes it linger.
- If you are in an active track when a mechanism begins to move, don’t race it. Move perpendicular, out of the corridor, and wait.

Getting out and shedding the room
- Retrace. Resist the urge to “find a new route out” through denser growth; the shortest path between you and the exit is the one you already proved is safe.
- Decon yourself and your gear thoroughly. Oxidize, UV, rinse, dry. Check Velcro, zippers, fin straps, camera foam for clinging filaments. They will try to ride you to other worlds.
- Journal right away. The room is quiet enough that your memory will smooth it into a single moment if you don’t pin details.

Alternatives to getting wet
- If you’re not a diver or you don’t trust your discipline, you can still have a perfect visit:
  - Park by the far-end window and let the outside depth be your sky replacement.
  - Send in a palm-sized ROV with soft shrouded props, a macro camera, and a light no brighter than your living room lamp.
  - Or stay in the tower’s adjacent corridors and watch through interior panes, if any remain. The clock will still tick for you.

What you might feel
- Relief at the honesty of the light. A pressure behind your eyes that isn’t pain but gravity written in water.
- An ache to hear a fin flick that will not come. The place does not lack for life, but it has declined our usual definition of company.
- The sense that you are visiting an organism that stretches far beyond this room, spinal and patient. You are in one bright alveolus of a lung that breathes by schedule.

A few final don’ts
- Don’t assume “recent workmanship” means “safe to climb.” It means “not yet scuffed.” Age is not the hazard here—habit is.
- Don’t time your visit to “maintenance day.” You don’t know when that is, and you don’t want to be inventory.
- Don’t bring souvenirs across realities. This room was built to exclude animals. If you carry one back in, even microscopic, you become the story of why the next visitor can’t come.

Pack for reverence, precision, and silence. If you do it right, you’ll leave no wake, and the room will keep doing exactly what it was made to do, on, and on.
