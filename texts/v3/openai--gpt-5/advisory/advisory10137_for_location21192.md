What you’re stepping into is a cool, saturated, vertical city grown out of a cloud-forest ridge. Everything flexes, everything sheds water, and most of what looks like “structure” is alive and expects to be treated that way. Prepare for wet air, narrow footing, sudden white-outs, and a culture that listens to water the way desert people listen to wind.

Big orientation before details
- Climate and feel: cool and perpetually damp, with mist most days, quick squalls some afternoons, and brief windows of gold light near dusk. Not swamp-stagnant; everything is in slow motion flow. You will be wet more often than you are dry, but rarely cold if you keep moving.
- Terrain: slippery wood and root, sway-bridges, buttresses like natural ramps, short ladders, ropeways overhead, sudden weeping cliffs. Horizontal distance is small; vertical distance is everything.
- Sound as map: drip pitches and animal calls cue weather, work, and hazard. You navigate as much by ear as by sight.
- Culture in one sentence: never fight the forest—tie, feed, guide, and adjust.

Conditioning and practice before you go
- Balance and legs: practice slow walking on a 10–15 cm beam, barefoot if you can, with a light pack. Add short ladder bursts. Train three points of contact on uneven surfaces.
- Breath in cool humidity: do a few hikes in a rain jacket at cool temps to learn how you vent heat without getting chilled.
- Knotwork you should know cold (slipped versions that release when wet): water bowline, taut-line hitch, trucker’s hitch, constrictor, reef knot, girth hitch with a drip line, and a quick-release lashing.
- Listening drill: sit by a fountain or gutter and practice hearing changes in flow; get used to distinguishing background from a new note cutting through.

What to wear
- Layers that love damp:
  - Base: thin merino or high-wicking synthetics; long sleeve to keep the perpetual bead off your skin.
  - Mid: light fleece or wool that insulates when damp.
  - Shell: a soft, quiet, vented rain cape or poncho that sheds mist without trapping sweat; stiff crackly shells are noisy and confuse the local soundscape.
- Trousers: quick-drying, with stretch. Avoid stiff, abrasive fabrics that scar bark and fray moss.
- Footwear: soft, sticky soles that flex and grip wet wood and root (approach shoes, river-trek shoes, or amphibious hikers). No hard-edged lugs, no metal tips. Bring 2–3 pairs of thin wool socks; rotate and dry on your body heat.
- Hands and head: light grip gloves (thin, tacky palms) for ladders and wet rails. Brimmed cap to keep bead-line out of your eyes. Avoid loud colors on big surfaces; keep one bright scarf for emergencies and cover it when frogs sing after noon.
- A light, warm layer for evenings; mist steals heat fast when you stop.

What to carry (keep it light, keep it quiet, keep it sealed)
- Pack: small, close-riding, without hard corners; everything in dry bags. Use broad, soft straps; no frames that bite into living rails.
- Hammock sleep kit: breathable hammock with integrated bug net, broad tree-friendly straps, short underquilt or insulated pad, and a quiet dripline tarp. Never screw hardware into living wood.
- Light: headlamp with warm LED and red/amber mode; low-output is your friend. Bring spare cells in a waterproof pouch. Tiny task light for hands.
- Water: a compact, manual filter that does not shed microplastics (ceramic or hollow-fiber with carbon) and a collapsible bottle. Do not dip from bromeliads; those are apartments, not canteens. Ask where to draw from the gutters/cisterns.
- First aid: antifungal cream/powder, blister care, wound-closure strips, broad fabric tape that sticks when wet, antiseptic, antihistamines, your inhaler or EpiPen if needed, rehydration salts, a space blanket.
- Skin and bites: unscented, non-aerosol repellent you can keep on your clothing; itch relief. A fine head net if you’re midge-sensitive. If you treat clothing, do it at home; don’t spray here.
- Tools: small knife that resists rust (ceramic or marine-grade steel), a few soft shackles or slings for hanging gear without chewing bark, a whistle you won’t use until taught the local call-codes.
- Protection for devices: waterproof pouches, silica gel, a pencil and waterproof paper for when your phone fogs. Power is unreliable; a hand-crank works better than a solar panel under a cloud ceiling.
- Hygiene: a small, fast-dry cloth; unscented, true-biodegradable soap in tiny amounts (ask where to use it); nail clippers. Menstruation: plan for high humidity—either a cup you can boil under supervision or enough cloth you can smoke-dry with your host’s help; pack out disposables.
- Gifts that don’t break the place: a tin of good tea or black pepper, a few rustproof needles, waxed linen thread, a magnifier lens the lacquerer can use. Salt is welcome but already traded; offer it sparingly and ceremonially. Best gift is labor and quiet attention.

How to move without making trouble
- Step on what wants your weight: roots, mats, barked ramps; avoid bare rock—it’s treacherous and culturally rude unless you’re on a marked stair.
- Three points of contact. Keep your weight over the midline of any path. On ramps and walkways, the upslope side is built to take the shove; favor it in a torrent.
- On ladders: never stop on a rung to sightsee. If you must pause, step off at a niche or a marked pull-out and call “clear.”
- Ropeways: loads have right-of-way. Don’t cross under a moving travois; help settle swaying loads with an open palm and bent elbow as locals do.
- Market alleys: narrow, shoulder-brushing. Tuck straps and swinging items. Yield to porters; you are recreational weight—they are livelihood.
- When something in the sound changes, you change with it. If dripping shifts to a purl, look up; if it becomes a hiss, shutters are about to move.

Daily rules you adopt on arrival (and mean it)
- Tie everything twice and assume it will be moved by something other than you.
- Lift shutters when birds go quiet; a predator wave is passing.
- Take down bright cloth when frogs call after noon; wet is coming.
- Feed the bridges: add a ring of compost to the main living lines when asked. A bridge is a citizen; you help feed citizens.
- Show ants respect. Never kick through a column. Use the woven barriers; ask where to set new ones. If army ants come, close up and step aside—they’ll clean and leave.
- Fires are small and in their stone pockets only. If you bring a stove, you use it there or not at all.
- Don’t interrupt water. Gutters, cisterns, drip clocks, and moss beds are this city’s skeleton and nervous system. Don’t “fix” a leak; find the sluicemaster.

Weather literacy in five cues
- Cloudbank shoulders up: voices lift, shutters loosen, gutters get unstrapped. Prepare to be interior for a time.
- Hiss between buttresses: mist has turned to micro-drizzle—slippery increases; slow your feet.
- Birds go silent in a wave: lift shutters, check small animals, mind your fingers.
- Frogs call early (after noon): secure bright cloth, check overflow lines, expect paths to carry water.
- Drip pitch goes lower and more numerous: cisterns filling; listen for the hour gong.

Eating and drinking
- Expect steamed, stone-baked, and leaf-cooked foods. Fruits, roots, pliant greens, breadfruit thumps at once when it’s ready. Meat is rare and not offered casually; eggs sometimes come amber and tiny.
- Accept what is handed with two hands; decline gently only for allergy or belief, and give a reason.
- Water is abundant but alive in the ecological sense. Draw from named channels or shared skins; filter out of courtesy to your gut, not as a judgment on theirs.

Health in unending damp
- Feet: change socks at mid-day; dry them under your shirt as you walk. Treat hot spots early. Keep nails short.
- Skin: rinse sweat salts off; keep creases dry at night. A dab of antifungal where straps sit goes a long way.
- Lungs: the air is clean but wet. If you are asthma-prone, keep your inhaler under your shirt to stay warm.
- Cuts: clean immediately; cover with a dressing that will stay on in damp. Tetanus up-to-date is assumed.

Staying and sleeping
- You’ll likely be in a hanging guest niche or a host’s spare hammock. Ask for tie points; never cinch hard against living cambium. Use broad straps; use drip knots.
- Night sound is a tapestry: frogs, bats settling, water easing. Sleep with low-profile earplugs that preserve detail; you need to hear a call.
- Hang everything. Ropes, not floors, are your cupboards. Nets descend on counterweights; learn the pulls so you don’t drop a neighbor’s seed basket.

Technology etiquette
- Keep devices quiet and dim; the city’s guidance is acoustic.
- No drones. No floodlights. No metal spikes. No adhesives on bark.
- Photographs of people and sacred work (sluice rigging, bridge feeding, drip clocks) by permission and from a step back. No flash.

Trade and contribution
- Currency is attention, hands, and timing. If you can hold a ladder foot, mind a gutter during a squall, stand a “three-hand” steady on a rope travois, or sing a call-and-response that fits their rhythm, you are already paying.
- If you bring goods, favor small, repairable, rustproof things that don’t introduce new materials streams: needles, thread, a lens. Offer them to craftspeople rather than as tips.

Hazards to name and defang
- Slips: most likely cause of injury. Move slower than you think you need. Keep your center stacked and your hands free.
- Army ants: not malice, just momentum. Get feet off the moving line; they pass.
- Weeping cliffs and tufa: gorgeous, slick, honeycombed. Unless a local invites, you don’t go there alone.
- Sensory overload: the sound never stops. Plan a quiet ritual—tea, breath work—for two ten-minute breaks a day.
- Humidity and gear rot: air your kit daily; silica gel lives in every pocket. Wrap metal in waxed cloth.

Arrival and first hour script
- Approach when light tilts—early morning or late afternoon—when the ridge shows itself between cloud pulses.
- Stop before the settlement edge and listen for a break in the calls. Announce with a simple up-call; a trader or child will answer with the local pattern. Repeat it back.
- At the market fig, remove your pack, stand still for three breaths, and let the boards return your weight. Offer your name, where you came from, and what you can carry or tend.
- You’ll be taught the house rules quickly; mirror them exactly before you improvise.

Signals you’ll hear (ask your host for the local words and echo them)
- Up/Down on a ladder.
- Slack/Tight on a line.
- Flow rising/Drop the sluice.
- Quiet bird (predator).
- Bright off (take down cloth).
- Load above (ropeway moving).
- Ladder clear (you can climb).

What not to bring
- Umbrellas; they snag and drip unpredictably.
- Trekking poles with carbide tips; they scar and slip.
- Hard boots, crampons, or anything that bites wood.
- Smelly soaps, perfumes, loud plastics.
- Aggressive insecticides and leave-behind synthetics.

A day, so you can picture it
- Dawn is animal edges and warm breath in cool air. Ladders creak, mortars start their soft thud, market ropes descend with herbs and bowls.
- Midday the mist lifts a little; trade moves along the top of old buttresses that hum faintly with sap. You carry a basket once, then twice, learning to step over the ant barriers without breaking them.
- Afternoon a cloudbank shoulders in. You help unstrap gutters, ease a travois back into its rut when a gust swings it, and feel that small glow of the boards taking and giving your weight.
- Evening drains slow. A bright swarm happens and burns itself out in color. The drum marks the hour when the basin’s float crosses the notch. You hang your socks, you check your knots, you feel triumphant and temporary.

Mindset, last and most important
- This place is not built against water and growth, it’s built with them. If you can let yourself yield, listen, and move on their terms, you will be welcome, and what you carry away will be more balance than gear can buy.
