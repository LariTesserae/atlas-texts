What to expect and how to move through it

You’re going to a very high, very old plateau where the sky is never quiet. Think of a stone table the size of a country, rimmed by cliffs that drop for kilometers, baked pale and loud enough that speech falls apart in the air. The surface is mostly bare rock, with life tucked only into the wind-shadows—the lees—where mist collects and drips. The wind uses the land as an instrument: there are notes, throats, resonators, and pressure-lines that can knock you down, lift you, disorient you, and deafen you.

Arrive ready to be small, quiet, and patient. You will travel at the seams of day, feel with your feet, read the plants, and keep to the lee. If you only remember one dictum: when in doubt, drop low, face the wind, and wait.

Pre-visit preparation

- Acclimatize to altitude
  - Target: spend 2–3 nights at progressively higher elevations (2,000–3,500 m) before ascending the plateau proper to reduce acute mountain sickness risk.
  - Consider acetazolamide if you have a history of AMS. Ascend slowly; hydrate, but don’t overdo water in the wind (electrolytes matter).
- Train your ears and head
  - Practice gentle equalization (Toynbee, Frenzel, and yawning) until you can open your Eustachian tubes at will. The wind’s pressure changes are constant; you must equalize without forcing.
  - See a clinician if you have chronic sinus/ear issues; barotrauma is a real risk.
- Desensitize to noise
  - Spend time with steady loud low-frequency sound (subwoofers, surf, industrial hum) while using filtered earplugs so you learn to feel through your bones while protecting hearing.
- Learn to move low and quiet
  - Drill three things: controlled prone drops, low crawls with pack, and diagonal moves across gust fronts toward a lee. Practice “freeze and anchor” on a command.
- Hands-talk and glance-talk
  - Bring a compact set of prearranged hand signals with your partner: stop, low, gust front, throat nearby, reverse, water, rest, danger left/right, help, I’m OK. Combine with simple ribbon semaphores on your pack straps (more below). Do not plan to rely on voice.
- Hire or meet a local guide if that’s an option in your multiverse route
  - People live in the lees. They read the wind by skin and lichen. If you can, learn from them; pay with what doesn’t sing: strong nonmetal cordage, fine needles made of bone or ceramic, high-quality waterproof cloth, salves, fats, or pigments. Ask first before gifting metals.

Gear: what works here

Keep everything low-profile, quiet, and bound. Nothing should flap, chime, or whistle.

- Footwear
  - Sticky, soft-rubber approach shoes with good edging and smear, low volume, no squeaky components. Add thin, flexible knee pads for crawling and sliding stops.
  - Heel lock lacing to prevent blisters when you must brace against gusts.
- Gloves
  - Thin, grippy stone gloves (goatskin or synthetic with textured palms). Moist-leaf work gloves for the groves (non-snag, non-abrasive).
- Clothing
  - Smooth, close-fitting, non-flapping layers. Long sleeves and hood. Sun-high UV means UPF fabrics even in wind-coolth.
  - Windskin: an anorak cut like a wetsuit for air—matte, tight, with minimal vents. Any zipper pulls are taped. No hoods that become sails.
  - Light insulation for night lulls (thin puffy that compresses flat).
  - Neck/face veil or buff to keep moisture in your breath and stop grit.
- Eye/skin
  - Wraparound goggles (clear and dark lenses). High SPF mineral sunscreen; lip balm; nasal saline spray to combat desiccation.
- Hearing
  - Filtered musician earplugs (custom if possible). They reduce amplitude but allow pressure equalization and let low-frequency information through. Over-ear muffs are backup only in camp; do not walk in muffs (you need the feel).
- Harness and lines
  - Sit harness or low-profile caving harness with two belay loops.
  - 30–40 m of low-stretch, high-strength cordage (UHMWPE/Dyneema) as soft shackles, daisies, and tethers. Prefer fabric soft-links and toggles; avoid metal hardware that can sing or clang. Pack some tubular webbing for bollards.
  - Short leashes (1–2 m) to partners during crossings. Everything leashed to you or the pack. Loose is lost here.
- Anchoring kit
  - Soft protection: slings for horns, bollards, and around natural features; wood/bone/ceramic toggles for jam cracks; a couple of sandbags to make deadman anchors in pans.
  - A roll of friction cloth or rubberized mat for emergency self-arrest on slick slab.
- Shelter and sleep
  - No tents with poles that hum. Bring a low, wedge-cut bothy bag (group shelter) and a compact storm tarp with curved edges and many tie-out points for damping, used only in deep lee.
  - Bivy sack for you, breathable and water-resistant. Thin closed-cell pad (insulation and lightning posture).
- Water and kitchen
  - Collapsible ceramic- or silicone-lined bottles/cups with lids. Quiet, low-flame stove with a silent burner head. Cook only in strong lee or in a local hearth with permission.
  - Fog kit: hydrophilic mesh panel and drip line to a container. Also carry two simple cups to leave beneath known drips when you bed down.
  - Filters: 0.1 µm hollow fiber filter plus activated carbon stage. Avoid skimming bromeliad cups—they are homes. Favor tinajas and drip basins. Pre-filter through clean cloth if there’s plant silt. Electrolyte tablets.
- Navigation and sensing
  - Analog first: quality compass, altimeter/barometer watch (to track pressure trends), and paper with graphite (ink clogs when wet).
  - Micro-anemometer is optional; many spin and whine—wrap its vents in mesh and use sparingly in lee.
  - “Lee cards”: small ribbons on your chest straps that lift when pressure drops; a fringe on your forearm tells gust direction without you looking up.
  - Vibration notebook: a thin leather card to pencil the local tones as dots and bars; it will help you recognize repeatable patterns (like the locals’ notations).
- Lighting
  - Headlamp with diffuse cap to avoid hard-beam flicker through fog. Red mode for groves.
- First aid
  - Ear barotrauma kit (decongestant spray used sparingly, sterile gauze, analgesics), eye wash, blister care, antifungal cream for macerated skin, broad-spectrum antibiotic ointment, elastic wrap, triangular bandage, space blanket.
- Power and electronics
  - Solar panel only if it can be rigidly mounted in camp lee; otherwise, charge before you go. Electronics ride in padded, dustproof bags. Mics will saturate; don’t count on audio. Bone-conduction headsets may let you “hear” recorded notes in lee if you must, but you won’t talk over the wind.

Things to leave behind

- Whistles, flutes, drones, anything that makes new notes. Do not add voices to a place that keeps them.
- Loose metal, carabiners that clang, pot lids that ring, crinkly wrappers. Tape and wrap everything that could rattle.
- Tall staffs, trekking poles with flags, kites, umbrellas, wide-brim hats. Anything that wants to be a sail or a lightning rod.
- Drones and UAVs—turbulence will eat them, and their whine is an offense.

How to travel there

- Approach and timing
  - Ascend by the switchbacks early—arrive on the rim for the first light lull. The “seam of day” is your travel window: a soft vacuum where most beings cross from one green pocket to the next.
  - Midday is radiant and roaring; you travel only between very close lees if you must. Dusk may grant another brief lull; night can be dangerously still with sudden microbursts.
- Reading the world (borrowed rules)
  - If you can see the scrubgrass show its pale undersides and flicker upright, the gust-train is changing; don’t step onto open slab.
  - Watch the black lichen; when you see fine white dust loft, pressure is shifting. Get low and wait in a lee.
  - Do not whistle. Do not sing out. Use hands, shoulders, and ribbons. Even your pack straps can speak if you tie them right.
  - Leave cups in the shade under dependable drips; do not expect streams.
  - Move with dawn; plan your leaps between lees, not long crossings.
- How to move
  - Before you step off a lee, clip a short leash to your partner. Agree on the next lee. Count down with fingers; move together.
  - Out on slab: three points of contact. Angle your path so the wind hits you slightly from the front; you want to feel it in your chest, not your flank.
  - If a gust front arrives (you’ll see grit lift like a sheet and feel static in your knees), drop prone, face into it, elbows and toes down, fingers in fissures. Exhale—being full of air makes you more buoyant to lift.
  - To escape a lateral pull, crawl diagonally downwind toward the nearest shadowed alcove; big sideways fights waste you.
  - Never investigate a singing hole from above. If the stone begins to speak in a narrow, pure tone at your feet, back away. Throats can lock into a note that lifts.
- Where to step and not
  - Avoid the silver-blue cushions on bare rock; they are slow-growing and snap under a thumb’s weight.
  - Tafoni honeycombs look like helpful steps but crumble under load. Test with knuckles, not boot.
  - Keep off the rims of bowls draped with vine mats; those are living harvesters. Step on stone.

Water, food, and cooking

- Water is caught, not found
  - Favor tinajas, leaf-drip basins, and your fog mesh in a lee. Skim the surface gently; let silt settle.
  - Never empty bromeliad tanks; they are entire worlds. If you must take a sip in an emergency, take a single small draw and replace it later with your own collected water as thanks.
- Treat all water
  - Filter and carbon. Iron and mineral runs can taste sweet-metallic; that’s fine in small amounts. The ungulates lick seam tears—humans should not. Avoid any orange-stained seeps unless you can taste safely in the tiniest amounts.
- Eat what you bring
  - The plateau doesn’t have the biomass for you. Carry dense foods that don’t crinkle: waxed packets, cloth-wrapped bars, flat breads, cured meats, nut pastes. No open cooking smells in groves; cook in your lee or at a shared hearth if invited.
- Salt and fat
  - The wind strips you; replace salt and calories. Keep salted nuts or bouillon to add to hot water.

Camp and shelter

- Site selection
  - Choose a deep, braided lee where at least two windbreaks overlap. If the moss breathes softly and the noise is surf-far, you’ve found it. Avoid any place where a pure tone persists.
- Pitching
  - Anchor tarp edges low and curved, multiple points, with damping lines (shock cord segments). No straight edges to hum. Use bollards and toggles; check all knots for creep in vibration.
  - Leave cups under drips in shade. Set fog mesh high enough to thrash without striking rock; stabilize its spine with a damped cord.
- Fire and light
  - If you see ashy, soot-smoothed alcoves, you’ve found a used hearth. Ask or leave a token before you use it. Flames in gust fronts are lethal and wasteful; this is a place of coals and patient heat.
- Night
  - Cold arrives quickly under clear sky. Keep earplugs in while sleeping; equalize before you drift off.

Navigation and orientation

- Distance lies
  - The plateau’s scale and clarity flatten distance. Plan by lees, not by kilometers. “Two lees west, one lee north” is a valid plan.
- Use tones as landmarks
  - The big undertone that speaks three times a day in long hollows can be your clock. The steady note of a throat during a particular fog is your beacon—if it’s not too strong to be safe.
- Marking without speaking
  - Biodegradable ribbons tucked in fissures on the lee side—not out in the wind to make a new voice. Stone arrows are fine if you scatter them on your return. Never carve.
- Instruments
  - The barometer’s trend is more useful than its absolute number. A fast fall means veils and sudden tones; a snap rise can mean a gust wall.

Etiquette and people

- Assume somebody lives within earshot, though you can’t hear them
  - If you find fog-nets, basins blackened by use, fat-smeared cairns, or bridges that cross wind, you’re in a tended place. Do not tamper. If you must use water, replace it. Leave gifts discreetly: a coil of silent cord, a pot repaired, pigment in a tight jar.
- Don’t add notes
  - The cairns with carved lips turn shrieks to softer tones; smearing fat and pigment is someone’s practice. Don’t whistle; don’t tie flappers; don’t string humming lines.
- Speak with hands and shoulders
  - Keep your gestures compact. Raised palm: stop. Fist to chest: I’m anchored. Two fingers to eyes then point: look there. Flat hand pressed down: get low. Index finger tracing a curve: gust front. Palms together then apart: split and meet at the next lee. Fingers combed through hair: humidity change. Thumb to ear, open mouth: equalize.
- Ask before photographing or recording
  - Some consider the wind-games private. Many things that look like toys are instruments.

Wildlife and plants

- Keep to the stone margins of groves
  - The dwarf forests are thick and fragile. Brush no more than necessary; oils from your skin can damage lacquered leaves.
- Do not break ant-lines
  - Those quiet gutters are their roads through the noise. Step over; do not stomp.
- Leave pollinators alone
  - The dawn glider and night moth are rare; your lights will confuse them. Red light in groves, always.

Safety: when things go wrong

- If you start to lift
  - Drop immediately. Flatten toward the wind. Arms tucked, elbows down. Unclip anything that can catch air if you can do it without rising.
- If you are spun by a tone
  - Get out of the throat’s axis. Crawl sideways with one hand sliding on the rock to feel for the edge where the note softens. Don’t try to stand until the tone widens.
- If you can’t reach a lee
  - Make one. Use your body and pack as a windbreak for your head and chest. Lay the friction mat under you. Wait for the gust train to pass; they come in sets.
- Ear pain or bleeding
  - Stop all movement. Sit in the deepest available lee. Equalize gently; do not force. If bleeding, keep ear down to drain, pad loosely, and rest. Evacuate at first safe lull.
- Lightning
  - If a hot rainstorm rides the plateau and you smell ozone: lose the high ground and isolated towers. Crouch on your pad, heels together, hands off the ground. No metal above shoulder height. You’ll see steam pour from hollows after; wait until fog lifts to move.
- Lost in fog
  - Stay with your partner by leash. Move only if you can keep one hand on rock and one eye on ribbon-sign. Better to bed down in place and drink from your cups than walk into a throat or rim.

What you will learn to feel

- Bass in your bones before you hear it. Your kneecaps know a resonator is near.
- Static on your scalp a few seconds before grit lifts. That’s your warning to get low.
- The “held breath” of the dawn lull—air goes soft, greens seem to lean out, your own exhale sounds too loud.
- The way lichen dust lifts like snow when a front is about to change its mind.
- The difference between a singing hole that’s about to take you and one that’s safe to pass by: purity of tone, steadiness, and the way it steals the edges from your touch.

Leave it better or at least untouched

- Pack everything out. Do not harvest wood or break stone to build.
- Replace any water you take from communal basins and leave two cups shaded for the next traveler.
- If you soften a shriek with a smear of fat and pigment, understand you’ve stepped into a local pact; leave a note (a token, not words) and expect to be known when you return.
- Don’t chase phantoms. This place makes you want a witness. Be your own.

A suggested first journey

- Day 0–2: Acclimatize below the rim. Train your hand-talk and equalization. Bind all your gear quiet.
- Day 3 pre-dawn: Climb the switchbacks to reach the rim at first light. Spend the lull making a short, safe crossing to a known lee within sight of the rim. Set a conservative camp.
- Midday: Stay put. Learn the tones of your bowl. Watch lichen and grass. Set fog mesh. Leave cups in shade.
- Dusk lull: Practice a short hop to the next lee and back, leashed to your partner. Return before full dark.
- Night: Sleep with your face in the moss if you like; wake with your hair wet. Listen with bones, not ears.
- Day 4 dawn: If all is well, plan a two-lees loop. If the undertone in the long hollow speaks different, change plans. This is a place that rewards listening over distance.

Gifts to bring, if you meet the people of the lees

- Silent strength: skeins of high-grade fiber cord. Tough, fine cords that don’t sing in wind.
- Pigments bound in fat in waxed pellets. Not powders that dust away into the plateau’s lungs.
- Ceramic needles, awls, or fine bone tools. Obsidian if it’s not common here.
- A repair: re-bind a fog-net with your cord, glaze a chipped cup with clay slip you offer to fire with them. Ask always; this place is bound in rules as much as in fiber.

In the end

You will be humbled by loudness and gentled by the moss. You will plan by lees and by lulls. You’ll learn to judge distance not by how far a thing looks, but by how much quiet lies between. You will not add a note. You will come home with a few tones you can hum only in your throat, too soft for the wind to keep, just enough to find your way back.
