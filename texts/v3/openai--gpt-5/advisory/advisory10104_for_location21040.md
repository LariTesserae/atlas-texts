What you’re going to step into is not “a cave you can visit.” It’s a living instrument, a wind-worked city that treats sound like weather and dryness like gravity. Prepare as if you’re entering a machine you must harmonize with rather than resist. Your success will come from what you keep quiet, what you tie down, how you move on a beat, and how well you learn to listen with your skin.

Brief picture you should hold in your head
- The room is a giant pipe with its own noon, its own wind, its own seasons. The wind is the law.
- Sound has mass here. Shapes that fight the flow are punished; things that conform survive.
- Liquids are hot, rare, and dangerous to the work. Your own body’s moisture will be eaten from you; anything wet you carry becomes a problem unless sealed.
- People speak by touch. Privacy is made with calm, not with walls.

Before you go: conditioning and practice
- Noise acclimation: Spend a week sleeping with high-level broadband noise (safely, with ear protection) and practicing calm-breathing under it. Learn to do tasks with white noise roaring so you don’t panic.
- Rhythm training: Practice timing steps to an external slow pulse. You’ll move on beats and rest on beats; clumsy, off-beat bodies get hurt.
- Knotwork: Learn two self-tie waist knots you can set and release with one hand. There, novices wear left-hand knots, veterans right-hand. You’ll be expected to signal your status honestly.
- Tactile etiquette: Practice hand-to-hand, palm-to-palm communication with a partner. Learn to ask permission before contact, to offer a wrist or forearm first, and to keep touch brief and clear.
- Hydration discipline: Train yourself to sip by schedule, not by thirst. The place hides how fast you dry out.

Core kit: make yourself quiet, tethered, and self-humidified
- Microclimate hood and respirator: A soft, close hood with a humidifying rebreather that keeps your inhaled air at 30–50% RH and filters charged dust. Aim for something that captures your exhaled moisture so you aren’t venting liquid into the room. No dangling tubes. Bone-conduction transducer inside the hood for private listening if you need it, but don’t expect to understand anyone by air.
- Ear protection you can feel through: Custom-molded, pressure-equalizing musician plugs (–15 to –25 dB flat attenuation), plus a second over-ear layer for Surge Hours. Do not fully occlude; you need to feel the pressure beats in your skull. Overreliance on active noise-canceling will fail here.
- Eye protection: Wraparound, anti-static, lightly tinted goggles. The light is relentless and sidelong; you’ll squint without tint, but you still need to read microtextures and hand signals.
- Skin and airway care: Solid wax-based barrier sticks for lips, fingertips, heels; a tiny vial of inert oil for nostrils (not a spray). Do not bring watery lotions; they flash away and leave you more raw.
- Clothing: A close, non-fluttering skinsuit with textured outer panels that break flow into small scallops. Nothing that flaps or makes its own song. Hair braided or capped. No capes, no loose scarves.
- Feet: Thin, split-toe, high-grip soles that transmit vibration. Bring sacrificial footwraps; your skin will crack if you go fully barefoot on day one.
- Hands: Half-finger gloves with grippy palms; bare fingertips or thin callus tape for tactile talk. Keep nails filed smooth—jagged edges will sing on resin and get you noticed in the wrong way.
- Tethers and anchors: Two redundant quiet lanyards (ceramic or composite hardware, not ringing metal), a broad waist belt with ambidextrous quick-releases, and a handful of expanding ceramic wedges for impromptu anchoring to scalloped surfaces. Learn to clip and unclip by feel.
- Shade-baffle: A collapsible cone or fan of slats that throws a wind-shadow and a little acoustic lee. This is your “room” anywhere you stand it; it also keeps your kit from skittering away during surges.
- Quiet weight: A pouch of dense, non-ringing counterweights (stone or composite) for stabilizing yourself or your work. Weight matters more than glue.
- Tools: A small nonmetal joinery kit—pegs, wedges, cord loopers, a resin-safe scraper. One soft-headed hammer. A ceramic-blade knife. Avoid sparking metals; if you must bring metal, pad and wrap it so it doesn’t sing.
- Food and water: High-calorie, non-crumb bars and gels in soft, non-crinkling packs you can open with gloved hands. A closed hydration bladder routed inside your hood. Do not expose water to the room; drink unseen.
- Waste: Sealed waste bags and absorbent pads. Pack everything out. Nothing here rots; if you leave it, it becomes someone’s problem forever.
- Electronics: If you bring them, choose conformal-coated, gasketed devices with no speakers, no fans, and no flapping straps. Lanyard every item. Cameras work; mics mostly don’t. Expect static and saturation; don’t count on radios.

Arrival and first hours: how not to be flung, dried, or rude
- Come with a local if you can. If not, approach along the wall terraces; never step into the central channel your first days.
- Tie in as soon as you step from a still place into the working air. Use your novice (left-hand) knot openly.
- Don’t speak into the open. Offer your wrist; if they accept, they’ll cup your palm-to-palm and you can murmur bone-to-bone through your hands or use simple taps. You ask permission before any touch.
- Keep all metal muffled. Do not tap, clang, or test echoes. Here, sound is not entertainment; it is stress.
- Keep your liquids hidden. Don’t share water. Don’t pour anything on anything. A spill can fossilize a tool or foul a plant’s work.
- Follow their day. Move quickly in the Hollows; tether and tend in the Surge. If you see everyone clip on, you clip on.

How to move
- Read the floor. Where dust forms delicate chevrons that point, you stay in the dull zones, not on the glossy channel. Your soles will learn the safe edges.
- Move on the beat. Step during the softening half of the throb; rest during the hardening. If a bridge hums, match it or wait. When in doubt, freeze and feel.
- Stay low and narrow. Carry long things aligned with the flow. Keep your center of mass inside your tether triangle.
- Ladders and pendulums: Climb when the pendulum swings toward you; hold and breathe when it swings away.
- Don’t chase glitter. The plant “flowers” are riding pressure nodes; a step into the wrong note can take your feet out from under you.

Eating, sleeping, and washing
- Eat under your shade-baffle, clipped in, with your kit bag weighted. You will be surprised how fast an unopened pack tries to leave.
- Sleep with ear protection in, hood on, tether clipped slack, and your shade-baffle casting a little calm around you. Expect your first night to be light and staccato; your body will learn the rhythm on night two.
- “Washing” is mechanical. Scrape dust. Wipe. Do not spritz. Oil your cracked places and reseal your hood.

Health and hazards to watch
- Dehydration sneaks: metallic breath, nosebleed, sandy eyelids, headache. Sip to schedule, not to thirst.
- Sound injury: if your teeth buzz or you feel nausea, you’re taking too much. Add your second layer of ear protection and step into a calm crescent or your own shade-baffle.
- Airway and eyes: keep the hood on. Remove goggles only in a deep lee and only briefly. Contact lenses are a poor idea.
- Static: discharge to the railings with the back of your knuckles; don’t “zap” with fingertips you need for speaking.
- Falling beams: when you feel a snap in the pressure that isn’t part of the room’s breath, you crouch and cover your crown. Locals will already be in motion; mirror them.
- Predators are inversions: you’ll notice a wrongness of quiet more than a shape. If a corner is “too calm,” you don’t investigate alone; you tell someone.

What not to bring
- Anything that flaps, whirs, or rings: umbrellas, capes, keychains, bell-trimmed gear.
- Aerosols and perfumes: they vanish instantly and announce you in all the wrong ways.
- Adhesives and glues: most will fail or fossilize at the worst moment. Expect to lash, peg, and wedge instead.
- Open flame: no. The flow will snuff it or spray it; soot fouls the plant combs.
- Crumbly foods and crackly wrappers.

Trade and gifts that make sense
- Quiet baffles and slatwork that fold small but cast big, stable lees.
- Composite clamps and ambidextrous quick-release buckles that don’t sing.
- Dense, non-ringing weights. Smooth ceramic bearings that turn without hum.
- Pattern knowledge: plans for crescent and scallop geometries that “throw” calm. Carved or etched on plates—no paper; paper shreds into trouble.
- A set of tactile phrase tokens: small, textured tiles encoding basic messages and maps of the chamber’s pressure lines. Offer them to a teacher, not as trinkets to kids.

Etiquette: how not to be the storm inside their storm
- Ask before entering someone’s calm. A lee is a doorway; pause at its edge and let your presence be felt through the rail before you step in.
- Voices are intimate. If someone offers you a mouth-to-ear, that is trust. If your hood prevents it, offer your temple and bone-conduction puck with your hand cupped over it; it’s the nearest respectful equivalent.
- Don’t “quiet” a space for your own comfort without asking. Calm is currency; reshaping it is construction, not a mood.
- The plants are not scenery. They are the law. Don’t pick, prod, or brush through thickets. If you must cross, follow a gardener.

A likely three-day arc
- Day 1: The room punishes you. You’re thirsty, loud, and slow. Stay lashed, stay to walls, let a guide carry your time.
- Day 2: Logic emerges. You begin to step on beats and can feel the Hollows’ invitations. You still overreach; don’t.
- Day 3: You’ll sit in someone’s woven lee, palms flat to the humming floor, and catch yourself grinning. You’ll understand why their children run to “almost-quiet” the way shore-children run to surf.

Leaving without leaving a scar
- Decontaminate dry: scrape and brush; never rinse. Check your kit for resin that has started to set; once it fossils, it’s forever.
- Return borrowed calm: if you’ve placed a baffle or counterweights, take them away when you go. Don’t leave strange leeward geometries behind.
- Make a last pass with your eyes for anything small you might have shed. In this place, what you forget becomes pattern.

If you carry only one rule in with you, let it be this: the room remembers. Every loud edge you present, every loose thing you let go, every unasked calm you cast will be taught forward into the city’s next hour. Tie yourself in. Move on the beat. Ask with your hands. Let the instrument play, and don’t insist it be quiet for you.
