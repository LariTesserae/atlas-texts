Orientation: what you’re walking into
- Think of a newborn hydrology plant built for a climate that doesn’t quite exist yet. It runs on light and on the breath of clouds. It is high, bright, cool, thin-aired, and made almost entirely of engineered surfaces that are still crisp from fabrication.
- The place behaves like a machine trying on the habits of a landscape. Some rules you know (gravity, daylight, straight lines) hold, but only locally, and only as long as you move with conviction.
- Silence is normal here. Motion is slow. Processes stretch into hours and days. You’ll do better if you arrive with a patient metabolism and a respect for ambiguity.

Best season and time to arrive
- Fog-bank weeks are the gentlest: glare is reduced, animals are active but calm, and condensate is generous. The catwalks bead with dew and you can read “down” more easily by watching the drips.
- On “bright” days, light has the density of a push. Go early in the cycle before the skyplane ramps to full clarity. Avoid the long “blinks” if you rely on vision for balance; the sudden valley-wide dimming can steal your horizon.

Personal conditioning and mindset
- Altitude: This air is cool and thin, and it tastes of ozone. Pre-acclimate if you can. If you cannot, plan a conservative first 24–48 hours. Move slowly, keep your exertion low, and measure your breath instead of your progress.
- Pace: Everything here operates on long gradients. Adopt a three-beat movement: look, decide, step. Don’t dither between paths; your body’s commitment picks a geometry and keeps you from being split by it.
- Attention: Expect micro-illusions—catwalks that are “both” directions until you choose, stairs that fold space, shadows that are more than shade. Curiosity is good; touching the anomalies is not.

Clothing and personal protection
- Eyes: Category 4, full-wrap UV/IR-blocking visor with a deep-tint ND filter and polarization you can rotate. Light here can feel like pressure; protect for brightness without assuming heat. Bring a fog-ready clear shield for the bank-days.
- Skin: High-SPF, broad-spectrum sunscreen; cover as much as possible. The air is dry but the light is not forgiving. Long sleeves and neck protection help keep ozone off your skin.
- Hands: Cut-resistant, dielectric gloves with good tactile feedback. You’ll be grabbing rails that host eels, burrs, and condensation; avoid bare contact.
- Feet: Non-sparking, dielectric footwear with soft, high-friction soles that grip ceramic and polymer. Consider removable micro-spikes or gecko-like forefoot pads for wet plates, but do not step on condenser faces unless marked as pedestrian-safe.
- Layers: Windproof, breathable shell over insulating mid-layers. You will rarely be warm, and when light blinks, the apparent temperature changes by perception more than by degrees.
- Respiratory: Lightweight half-mask with ozone-rated filter and a low-flow supplemental O2 option if you’re altitude-prone. The mask also spares you the resin tang.

Core kit
- Harness and lanyards: Full-body harness with dual-tether shock-absorbing lanyards. Clip whenever you cross open grating or stand near a “down” that doesn’t agree with your gut.
- Rope and anchors: 30–50 m of static line, non-marring rail clamps, and soft shackles. Don’t drill, don’t glue—clamp. The structure is alive with function; never pierce it.
- Navigation aids: Inertial logger, analog watch set to your own circadian, chalkless clip tags or numbered, non-adhesive micro-clamps to breadcrumb safe routes. A bright fabric handline between partners beats radios here.
- Sensing: Pocket droppers with distilled water and glycerin for “down” tests; a small IR thermometer; a photometer; a TDS/conductivity pen for water safety. A handheld magnet is more trouble than it’s worth—leave it; the mites herd magnetite and don’t need your interference.
- Comms: Haptic/bone-conduction sets with line-of-sight optical or coded-IR beacons. Sound dies fast; shouting is for yourself, not your partner.
- Light: Headlamp with baffled hood and a very narrow beam; keep it pointed at your feet. Wide floodlights confuse the instrument kites and erase your own shadows, which you need for balance.
- Water and food: Carry your own water for the day and a way to make more without flame (manual pump filter plus a small field still). Meals that rehydrate cold; nothing crumbly or sugary that could gum condenser textures.
- Shelter: Compact bivy, insulating pad, and a blackout scarf or hood. “Night” may not announce itself; make your own.

Moving through a place that bends rules
- Choosing direction: If a catwalk seems to extend in two sensible ways, pause. Drip-test: express a bead onto the grating and watch what it chooses. Step with your lead foot in the bead’s decision and commit your weight. Don’t pivot back-and-forth; that’s how ankles and minds get confused.
- Stairs that lie: If a stair delivers you where you began, mark the landing with a clamp tag and take a different descent that breaks symmetry (switch railing side, change cadence, clip to the opposite trunk). You’re teaching the place which version of itself you’re using.
- Edges and vents: Treat light vents like ducts: don’t walk through panes of brightness or let them cross your eyes unfiltered. Treat any “empty” span above a terrace as occupied by wires; assume a line you cannot see is present and taut.
- Contact rules: Rails host eels; touch with a knuckle tap first, then grip with gloved hands. Step only on designated mesh. Condenser plates are not paths unless the path is explicitly built into them.

Water, damp, and what not to drink
- Dew and sumps contain dissolved metals and salts. If you must harvest, do it from runoffs marked with service access, not from functional plates; prefilter through a cloth, then carbon, then boil or distill. The “mirror-silver” look is pretty and occasionally mercurial—test with the TDS pen; if it reads like a battery, walk away.
- Never take from the ghost-funnels or cups; those are living systems, however plasticky they look. If you find a feral maintenance absorber, observe it; don’t borrow from it.

Animals and how to be among them
- General rule: They are quiet by necessity. Match them. Move slowly; keep gestures small; lower your center of gravity when watching to avoid reading as a threat.
- Foilwing gliders: They ride pressure you won’t feel. Don’t cast shadows across their sills; they spook and tear. Keep your visor from flashing; polarized lenses help.
- Rail-eels: Assume the handrail is occupied after dripfall. If one brushes your glove, let it pass; the lubricant is neutral but will make you slick. Do not wipe it onto plates; clean your glove on your own clothing.
- Beetles and mites: Don’t drop metal dust or shed filings; seal tools. Your magnets and your crumbs are ecological events here—avoid both.
- Fur-less nesters: Warm pump-casings are their nurseries. If machinery hums, assume a family lives above it. Give braced bays a wide berth at fog’s peak; it’s their feeding hour.
- Threadbirds: Leave cable crossings intact and tensioned; don’t tighten “messy” places you think you’re helping. What looks like chaos is their pantry.

Machinery etiquette and hazards
- This is not abandoned. It’s commissioning at a rate slower than you. Anything that looks static can move later today.
- Never camp under fans, belts, or baffles—even if they haven’t moved in recorded memory. A centimeter a day is still a centimeter while you sleep.
- Punched-code tags are language; photograph, don’t touch. Frostbloom and condensation on ducts tell you contents; avoid hot-frost paradoxes (cold lines that “sweat heat”)—those carry whatever “stray light” is, and it plays poorly with optics and skin.
- Lockout/tagout is not yours to do. Instead, route yourself so that if something starts, you are not part of its clearance envelope.

Geometry seams and other temptations
- Pencil-thin moving darkness is not a shadow. It’s a seam. Don’t put your hand in it. People who do come away changed in small, hard-to-measure ways—words just out of reach, time feeling braided. If you’re a collector of odd sensations, fine; if you need to keep your baseline, keep clear.
- Manuals that switch language mid-page are warnings that the local logic is shifting. Stop and reassess your path. It’s not for you today.
- Gutter water that climbs is not for touching or tasting. Mark and admire.

Communication and group travel
- One to three travelers is ideal. Bigger groups disrupt the place and each other.
- Establish haptic signals before you start: two taps stop, three taps step, squeeze and hold means clip now. Sound won’t carry reliably.
- Tether lightly in exposed spans; untether when paths fork in “both” directions or your lines will argue the geometry for you and drag you sideward.
- Drones: Only slow, whiskered micro-drones with collision sense. Never fly above terrace height; sky wires and kites own that column of air.

Health, safety, and small first-aids
- Hypoxia and ozone: Mild headache and throat rasp are normal early on. If your thinking starts to ladder—jumping steps—get low, hydrate, and mask up. If you start to laugh at nothing or lose words, that’s not charm; that’s your brain asking for O2 and shade.
- Cuts and scrapes: Burrs on panel edges are new and mean. Carry a small file or emery to tame what you must touch; otherwise work around. Clean any cut; don’t trust sterilizing ozone to do the job for you.
- Balance and vertigo: If perspective doubles, sit. Clip in. Close one eye and read your bootlaces; when your heartbeat slows, stand and choose again.
- Sound hunger: The quiet can be unnerving. A soft metronome in your pocket or a bone-conducted heartbeat loop can keep your nervous system from inventing alarms.

Fieldcraft that pays off here
- Carry a pocket of bright thread or flagging to hang as wind telltales (clamped, not taped). They’ll tell you more than your instruments will about the microflows the gliders trust.
- Keep a small log of drip ticks: time a bead along an east-facing plate in the morning and at noon. The change tells you where “down” is going to be this afternoon.
- Photograph the same seam daily. Commissioning is geology here: it moves slow but moves. You will see it in a week what you would miss in an hour.

What not to bring or do
- No open flame. Resins and ozone make poor company for sparks.
- No adhesives, paints, or solvents. Everything here is a surface with a job.
- No magnets, ferrous swarf, or snacks that shed sugar dust. You’ll confuse more than the mites.
- Don’t tidy tension nests, don’t scrape lichen, don’t wipe rails clean. The wildness is functional.

If things go wrong
- Getting looped: If a path returns you to its start twice, change a variable you control (pace, which foot leads, which hand trails the rail) and try only once more. If it loops a third time, retreat to your last clamp-tag and choose a dramatically different route.
- Blinding bright: If the skyplane snaps to full clarity and you can’t see edges, go to ground: clip in, sit with your back to an upright, visor down, and wait ten minutes. Do not walk.
- Injury: There is very little echo here; your voice won’t find help. Your best rescue is your partner and your own redundancy. Keep a bright reflective panel in your pack; light as substance reflects well, and distant teams can spot your shimmer.

Leaving well
- Pack out everything, including the threads you hung and the tags you set. Close clamps; don’t leave them to ring like wind-chimes on the rails.
- Wipe your boot-soles on your own kit before you leave. The array is young; don’t carry its beginnings into somewhere that won’t know what to do with them.

What it will feel like
- You will feel held at arm’s length by a place that is busy becoming itself. Your breath will be a little too loud in your head. Your shadow will sometimes disagree with your plan. The animals will accept you if you are mostly a temperature and not a noise. The machines will ignore you if you ignore the urge to help.
- At some point a droplet will choose to fall sideways and you will accept, quietly and without argument, that “down” is a conversation. That is the right way to be here. Move with the array’s patience, and it will read you as weather, not as a problem.
