What you’re walking into
- A world-sized high table of stone, cold and thin of air, so old it has worn itself smooth. Light looks normal up close and untrustworthy at distance. Water is shy and precise: fog, rime, dew, black still pools, short-lived morning runnels. Wind is a sentence fragment; silence does most of the talking. Distances slide with the moistness of the day. There are no animals at all. Cities live low and deliberate, woven into fog basins, built of stone, fiber, and knots. Fire is rare and purposeful. The place edits anything that isn’t careful.

Mindset to bring
- Move gently, place things exactly, accept that the ground and the map negotiate with each other.
- Expect quiet to be part of the weather. Your noise is an intrusion; your weight is a decision.
- Plan by moisture, not by miles. Fog shortens the world; clear air stretches it.
- Assume you are a biosecurity risk. This ecology has no animals. Arrive clean, leave no living hitchhikers, introduce nothing.

Body realities
- Altitude: Thin enough that your breath frosts at noon. Treat it like high-alpine travel.
  - Acclimatize if you can: 1–3 nights at an intermediate elevation before entering cities.
  - Bring acetazolamide for prevention, dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergencies (HACE/HAPE), pulse oximeter, and know the symptoms.
- Cold: Sharp, dry-cold in the day; radiative and rime-heavy at night.
  - Moisture condenses on you and then freezes; manage condensation as seriously as temperature.
- UV: High and clean. Protect eyes and skin even when you feel cold.
- Dehydration: Insidious at altitude and in cold. You’ll lose water to breathing and to managing layered clothing.

Navigation and physics that don’t quite stay put
- Paths fade if unused; the land forgets.
- Distances compress when fog runs and lengthen in brittle clarity. Counted paces won’t behave.
- Water will sometimes flow up what your eyes say is “up,” then pretend it didn’t. Trust local route customs and stone cords more than your gut.
- Sound can step out for a beat and return. Don’t rely on shouting or whistles across fog-lakes.
- Nights: set a bowl down and it’ll crawl a finger-width north. Stake and tether everything; wake slightly moved and assume it’s not just your dream.

How to dress and carry yourself
- Layers that still insulate when damp: wool and high-loft synthetics. Down only if protected from rime in a waterproof/breathable shell.
- A windproof, lightly insulated shell that sheds rime and doesn’t snag fine fibers.
- Face: buff or mask to warm/humidify inhalation and to spare the dew-nets your stray breath-ice.
- Hands: liner gloves for knot-work; big mitts for idle time. Bring a spare dry set always.
- Footing:
  - Dual footwear strategy: soft, moss-kind soles (felted or crepe-soled overshoes) for vegetated mats; removable microspikes for bare rime-slick stone. Learn when to swap; don’t crunch the lichens.
  - Gaiters that seal well; moisture rises from the ground.
- Eyes and skin: glacier-grade sunglasses (Cat 3–4) with anti-fog; high-SPF sunscreen and lip balm that won’t crack in cold.

Shelter and sleep
- If you camp between towns (ask first), use a double-wall tent with generous vents and a frost liner; everything that can frost, will.
- High-R sleeping pad stack (closed-cell + insulated air) and a vapor-barrier liner to keep your insulation from drinking your breath.
- Stake with cords to rock or pre-drilled ring-stones; the plateau nudges things overnight.
- Keep a dry bag of “sleep-only” layers; accept the discipline of dry/wet clothing cycles.

Water and food
- Water exists but on the plateau’s terms. Don’t freeload from public fog-wells without being invited; they’re civic organs.
- Carry initial water in insulated bottles; keep lids and threads unfrozen by stowing upside-down in sleeves.
- Treatment: bring a cold-capable strategy. UV pens work well if you keep the batteries warm; chlorine dioxide works but is slow in cold; filters can freeze and fail—treat them as precious and never let them freeze wet.
- Hot drinks matter more than you think; carry a heat-exchanger pot. Stoves: liquid-fuel or inverted-canister systems perform best in cold; use tiny, respectful flames and vent well. Fire is etiquette here.
- Food: Plan for plant-forward eating. Local fare is moss-garden edibles, fungi, and curated mats—mineral-rich, hearty, subtle. Bring B12 if you’re staying long; electrolyte salts for altitude diuresis. Choose compact, no-cook or quick-soak foods to spare fuel and water.

Tools that belong
- A hygrometer and a simple sling psychrometer: learn the town’s dew tables; they plan days by them.
- Barometric altimeter; don’t trust distance, trust change.
- Compass and sun compass: use as cross-checks, but defer to local anchors and cords when they disagree.
- Headlamp with cold-proofed lithium cells; red mode for fog. Keep batteries on your person to stay warm.
- Knife/awl for fiber work; polishing stone. If you bring metal, keep it small, precise, and gift with tact.
- Repair kit that functions glove-on: tapes that stick below freezing, silica gel, zipper lube that doesn’t ice.
- Notebook that tolerates rime and a grease pencil; pens sulk in this air.

Moving through it
- Travel windows:
  - Pre-dawn to mid-morning fog: world shortens; heavy lifts and long errands happen now.
  - Midday: clear, brittle, distances stretch; don’t overcommit.
  - Late afternoon: fog-lakes go walking; navigation gets treacherously attractive. Err on the side of homeward.
  - Night: everything laces itself with frost; only move if you’re practiced and invited.
- Gait: place feet flat and broad; step stones over green when possible; distribute weight with poles. Your print can last until the next thaw—walk like that matters.
- If you hear the torn-paper rip of a bedrock seam opening, stop. Let the land finish its sentence. Give seams a wide berth afterward.

Etiquette and taboos
- Ask before touching any net, condenser-bower, lattice, ring-stone, or cord. They are water, map, and memory all at once.
- Do not move stones casually. Rings anchor neighborhoods to themselves. A single “tidy-up” can unmoor a street over a season.
- Doors are low and sealed for a reason. Enter slowly, close things fully, and mind the draft mats; they’re arranged art, not expendable.
- Fire only where you are shown. Smoke and heat patterns are tools here; random flames break them.
- Sound is a resource. Keep your voice close. Singing at twilight has a function; don’t improvise a compliment unless you’ve been included.
- Tools carry lineage in their knots. Admire, ask, don’t copy without permission.
- Waste: pack it all, bodily and otherwise, unless directed to a fungal digester. Never foul a black pool.

Health specifics
- Hypoxia: pre-med if needed, climb high/sleep low principle if you’re making any vertical changes within the plateau. Descend (toward towns; sometimes they’re “lower” because of fog) if severe symptoms hit.
- Cold injuries: avoid white-numb patches; rewarm once, properly. Keep feet dry-warm via rotation: damp set for moving, dry set for resting.
- Skin: lanolin or silicone balm for knuckles and cheeks. Rime will chap you like desert sun in reverse.
- Respiratory: spore pulses ride chill breezes. If you’re sensitive, wear a light mask during wind shifts or when condensers are combing the sky at night.
- Fungal foot/skin care: dry time daily, antifungal cream, and disciplined hygiene.

Staying with cities
- Expect circles within circles. The center is a quiet pool. Don’t lean over it like a tourist; it is watched.
- Work trades are the coin of courtesy: helping hang morning nets, comb fiber, resharpen adzes, re-tension walls as frost tightens them.
- Sleep half-underground, warm and close. Condensation rules apply indoors too—hang your damp with care. They will show you where to breathe your steam so it feeds, not harms.
- Food is small, exact, generous in its way. Bitter greens with mineral hum, broths made from fog-water and fungi. Eat what is offered; notice the geometry of where you set your bowl.

Trade and gifts that don’t skew the place
- Offer small, exact tools: a handful of stainless needles, a ceramic blade, a non-freezing spirit level, a tiny loupe, spare lithium cells with a simple solar trickle mat, a stout thimble. Present them with lineage: who taught you to use them.
- Knowledge trades: a knot from your world with a story; a way to read wind with a vane tweak; a moisture log. Avoid anything that shortcuts their relationship with fog and stone (no hydrophobic sprays for nets, no motors).
- Accept that what you’ll receive may be placement—a stone set for you, a path persuaded to hold a little longer.

What not to bring
- Seeds, insects, pets, leather with living oils, untreated wood: anything that might smuggle a whole other answer to the plateau’s old question.
- Loud devices, drones, broadcast beacons. This place doesn’t consent to being mapped that way.
- Wheels meant to roll. They’ll mock you and then injure you.
- Big fires, big smells, big gestures.

Fieldcraft tricks locals will teach you if you’re worth teaching
- How to read a dew-net’s “breath” by the tremor in its threads.
- How to choose the hour when a stone is lighter than it should be.
- How to set black rock among green so a patch exhales warmth after dusk.
- How to lay a cord so that a street remembers its angle to the center.

Emergency sense
- If you are lost and the air is drying, sit and wait for damp. In fog the world shortens and the right place is closer.
- If you break a net, say so at once; silence costs more water than shame.
- If the bass hum crosses the plateau, pause. The air is changing its mind. Plans should too.

A minimal packing list to start right
- Health: acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine, ibuprofen; personal meds; blister/fungal kit; lip and skin balms; pulse oximeter.
- Clothing: wool/synthetic base layers; mid-loft insulation; windproof shell; two hats; liner gloves + mitts; spare socks; vapor barrier socks if you know how to use them.
- Footwear: warm boots + felted overshoes + removable microspikes; gaiters.
- Shelter: double-wall tent, frost liner, two pads, -15 to -25 C capable bag (adjust for season), VBL, robust stakes and cords.
- Water/food: insulated bottles, UV purifier + chemical backup, heat-exchanger pot, cold-capable stove + fuel, compact no-cook foods, electrolytes, B12 if long stay.
- Tools: knife/awl, repair kit (tapes, cordage, patching), lighter + storm matches, headlamp + lithium cells, small solar mat, power bank.
- Instruments: compass, barometric altimeter, hygrometer, simple thermometer, pocket level, notebook + grease pencil.
- Etiquette/gift pouch: handful of precise metal/ceramic smalls, a knot you can teach, a story to tie it to.
- Biosecurity: fresh-washed gear, heat-treated or new textiles, sealed tote for anything suspect, waste bags.
- Optional: dew heater for lenses, camera in a dry bag with silica, quiet music + headphones for private use.

How to arrive
- Signal a city at the edge of its ring-stones in the time between first standing fog and its run; stand where your breath won’t fall on a net.
- Offer your name and lineage as you understand lineage—teachers, not employers.
- Take off your spikes, show your soft soles, and ask where your breath should go.

How to leave
- Return what drift you borrowed: stones you moved, cords you tensioned, warmth you basked in by adding a small task to the sum.
- Step back along stone where you can, and where you cannot, step light on the green.
- Expect to dream of levelness for a few nights. The next place you visit will feel tilted until you forgive it.

If you remember one thing
- Here, correctness is not a rulebook, it’s a relationship: with cold, with water that prefers edges, with stone that has almost finished deciding what it is. Prepare to be exact, quiet, and kind, and the plateau will make room for you when the fog runs.
