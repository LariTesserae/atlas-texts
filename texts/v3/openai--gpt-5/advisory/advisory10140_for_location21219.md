What you’re walking into
- A narrow maintenance ledge opening onto a vast, vertical recirculation shaft: constant, laminar, high-velocity airflow; bone-dry; very low humidity.
- Strong static electric fields and carefully maintained chemical gradients in the moving air (acid/base, oxidizer/reducer) that “feed” the non-biological flora coating every surface that doesn’t flex.
- Low, steady sonic environment: a powerful, unwavering fundamental hum from the fan body and alternator harmonics layered over it. Communication by voice is impractical; lip-reading and hand signals work best.
- Light is dim and fixed (cold service diodes and emergency strips). Surfaces are overgrown with sharp, friable, conductive, and sometimes piezoelectric accretions.
- Real fall hazard. The main shaft is an abyss with a ripping draft; your ledge is pocket-sized, with a single aged handrail and a catwalk that’s partially encrusted.

Mindset before you go
- Think cleanroom meets climbing. Your job is not to disturb the gradients. Move slow, disturb little, leave no moisture, leave no trace.
- Expect sensory dominance by a single, unwavering note. If you’re noise-sensitive, acclimate beforehand with steady brown/pink noise sessions and practice breathing calmly against constant sound.
- Train with your partner(s) on hand signals and tethers in gloves. Practice “silent comms” until it’s automatic.
- Accept that if the note changes noticeably or the breeze temperature/texture shifts, you abort. Stability is life here; a drift in tone is an omen.

What to wear (materials matter)
- Suit: full-body, low-shedding, antistatic cleanroom coverall with integrated hood. Aim for fluoropolymer or fluoropolymer-laminated fabrics (PTFE/FEP-laminate) or PEEK-fiber blends. Avoid cotton, wool, Velcro, and anything fuzzy; do not wear anything that flaps.
- Respiratory: closed-circuit rebreather or SCBA with internal humidification, fully sealed. Do not rely on ambient air—composition swings (acids/bases/CO2/oxidizers) will punish lungs and mucosa. Keep it leak-free; even breath condensation is an unwanted reagent here.
- Eye/face: full-face mask with anti-fog coating rated for acid gases; add a secondary face shield if you anticipate brushing growths.
- Hands: layered gloves—inner cut-resistant liner, outer chemical-resistant fluoropolymer (FFKM/fully fluorinated rubber) or multilayer laminate. Keep fingertips dexterous enough for clips and signals.
- Footwear: dielectric, anti-slip boots with smooth soles. No exposed metal shanks, eyelets, or traction cleats that can snag growths.
- Hearing: high-NRR earplugs under slim electronic ear muffs. The hum still arrives by bone, but double protection eases fatigue. Bring a spare set.
- Harness: sit/rope harness with a fall-arrest lanyard. Prefer aramid (Kevlar/Twaron) or UHMWPE core with a PTFE jacket; ceramic or polymeric (PEEK) hardware where possible. If you must use metal connectors, use anodized aluminum or titanium and keep them shrouded in dielectric sleeves.

What to carry (keep it minimal, sealed, and inert)
- Lifeline/tether: static line anchored inside the hatch to a rated point. Use twin-tether technique so one is always clipped.
- Comms: hardline is king. A thin armoured optical fiber spool between teammates gives reliable full-duplex text/low-bitrate voice. Radios are iffy inside metal ducts and static fields.
- Light: two intrinsically safe headlamps plus a chest light; warm-white or amber beams. No strobe, no UV. Sealed housings with conformal-coated electronics.
- Sensors: pocket E-field meter; multigas (CO2, O2, H2S, acid gases) for belt-worn curiosity, but do not let measurements tempt you to “test” the air by removing your mask. Optional contact mic with a fluoropolymer footpad to couple to bare metal if you find any.
- Tools: as close to none as you can stand. If needed, bring only non-sparking, ceramic or polymer tools in a sealed roll. Wrap every hard edge in PTFE tape. No blades unless mission-critical.
- Trail aids: a few non-adhesive PTFE “shoes” or shims to spread load on suspect grating without crushing growths. Small fluoropolymer wedges can help prop a cable bundle gently without touching flora.
- First aid: chemical burn kit (external use only), saline eyewash and water strictly for post-exit use in a secondary decon zone. Inside the shaft: no free liquids.
- Spares: glove liners, earplugs, mask seals, tether sleeves. Pack in rigid PTFE or PEEK containers.
- Don’t bring: drones with rotors, fans, balloons, powders, paper, cloth tape, gels, alcohol wipes, free water, food you plan to chew, or anything that sheds fibers.

Movement protocol
- Clip in before the hatch opens. Keep at least one tether to an interior anchor until your second tether is on the handrail anchor, then transition.
- Enter like you’re moving through thin oil: slow, compact, no sudden gestures. Minimize your cross-section to the flow. Keep kit flush to your torso in smooth bags.
- Feet: test before you trust. Probe the grating lightly with a smooth boot edge. Treat bright lacy growth as friable and sharp; treat velvety mats as conductive.
- Hands: touch only bare, load-bearing metal—and expect very little of it to be truly bare. If you must touch a rail, use a dielectric hand pad or glove palm inserts. Never palm a crystal edge.
- Distance: maintain a hand’s breadth from any “plant.” Their matrices can bridge charge through you; some will crackle and sting; a few may arc if you span potentials.
- Turbulence discipline: do not wave, flap, or swing gear. Turbulence here is chemistry. Keep your wake small.
- Three points of contact always. Never step into a space you haven’t visually cleared. The drop will take you faster than your reflex.
- Time on ledge: keep it short. The dryness and sonic load are deceptive; fatigue arrives fast once you relax into the hum.

Communication and coordination
- Pre-brief silent signals for: stop, back, anchor, hazard (edge, fragile, arc, slip), status (OK, low on time, abort), and “tone change.”
- Throat mics and lip reading help, but assume you’ll rely on hand signals and light pips. If you use light codes, keep them dim and close-in.
- Agree on a “note check” every few minutes: everyone pause, breathe, and confirm the hum is unchanged. Any disagreement = retreat.

Interacting with the environment (ethics and safety)
- Don’t harvest. These growths are a working equilibrium with a machine. A souvenir becomes powder outside anyway, and your removal perturbs fields set for years.
- Don’t clean. Don’t scrape. Don’t knock plates loose, even “just a little.” Detached material becomes reactive meal downstream.
- No free water or alcohols. Moisture will catalyze corrosion and phase changes the system was never meant to see.
- Heat and light: keep lamps cool and directed; avoid leaning hot electronics onto surfaces. UV can do chemistry here—skip it.
- If you must “listen” by contact, interpose a thin dielectric pad and accept a less pure signal. Don’t bare-metal your skull to a rail.

Sensing and savoring without harm
- The garden is audible. Stand still, feel the rib-deep fundamental, and turn your head slowly to catch the glassy overtone shift. Let the note write your sense of time.
- Observe species by texture and geometry—velvet charge-lichens at the leading edges, shard-fans in the lee, bright capacitive “succulents” around gauges. Learn without touching.
- Watch how everything tends to a thickness and holds there: that’s the feedback system made visible. It’s the closest thing to weather you’ll see.

Red lines and abort criteria
- The fundamental pitch wavers or beats against a new low tone.
- Air temperature or “texture” at your cheeks changes abruptly.
- You see corona, hear crackle beyond a gentle tick, or taste sudden sharpness through your mask’s charcoal.
- Any anchor, grating, or rail feels spongy or shifts under test.
- Your suit, mask, or tether takes damage; a glove tears; a seal hisses.
- You drop something large enough to tumble the flow—or you feel yourself treating the abyss like scenery instead of hazard.

Decon on exit
- Close the hatch before you unclip. Rest where it’s quiet; take your time.
- Dry decon only at first: low-velocity vacuum, soft antistatic brushes, tack-free wipes. Pay attention to soles, glove backs, and harness webbing.
- Bag and quarantine: any dust, shavings, or crystal crumbs go into sealed PTFE vials. Don’t open them in humid air; let a lab handle disposal or study.
- Only after gross dry decon do you use liquids (outside, in a controlled area). Rinse hardware with compatible solvents, then water, then dry thoroughly.
- Inspect seals, tethers, and outer garments for micro-cuts from crystals. Retire damaged kit.

If you’re tempted to sample
- Don’t, if you can help it. If mission-critical, collect only free flakes that have already detached and lodged safely. Use a nonconductive spatula, deposit into pre-purged PTFE microvials under a portable dry-nitrogen hood.
- Log exact position, orientation, and tone snapshot at collection—context is the specimen’s soul here.
- Expect samples to transform on exposure to humidity; store and analyze under dry inert atmosphere.

Contingencies
- Sound stops: leave immediately. The ecology and deposits will start to relax toward a new equilibrium (or powder) and your assumptions just expired.
- You take a shock: step back, check for pinholes or burns, reassess your path. Tiny arcs can puncture suit films unnoticed.
- Sudden brightness or ozone smell: you’ve got arcing or mixing. Back out the way you came, slow and small, keeping your wake minimal.

Why go
- To feel a human-made climate that never storms, where time is the persistence of a single note and life is chemistry tamed into lace. It’s an austere profusion—an ecosystem of patience—that rewards attention and punishes hurry.

A final etiquette
- Be brief.
- Be smooth.
- Be dry.
- Touch nothing you don’t have to.
- Leave with your awe and your data, and give the rest back to the note.
