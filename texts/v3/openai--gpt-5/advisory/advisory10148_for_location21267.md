What it is like
You’ll be stepping into a living district that behaves like a thoughtful machine. It’s quiet, cool-skinned, deliberate. Dew is its currency and patience its clock. Nothing is quite still, but nothing hurries. Walkways are grown, not built. Water moves like a decision. Light feels earned. Expect your own pace and voice to feel too loud at first.

How to arrive
- Approach from the pale outside, across the lean sedge and salt film. You’ll see the silt berms with buttress roots: those are the edges and the invitation. There will be one or two obvious entries where the cambium-thickened paths begin.
- Pause at the edge. Knock or tap—twice, then once—on a buttress or handrail and wait a breath. It’s both courtesy and a practical notice; sound travels through the wood ahead of you.
- Brush your boots. Better: arrive with soles already scrubbed and seed-free. They are vigilant about foreign spores and grit.

Best season and time
- Any cool season is kinder. Nights bead, dawns drip, and the Condensary performs. In hotter stretches, the aisles still hold their damp, but edges go hard and bright.
- Dawn is the show: dew shedding down the runged dripways into the small basins. Late afternoon brings the dry wind that combs only the outer baffles. Nights are dim and moth-quiet and respectful.

What to wear
- Shoes: soft, non-marking soles with fine siping. Nothing with cleats or sharp edges. Rubber tips on sticks or poles if you use them.
- Clothing: light layers that handle damp without shedding lint or microfibers. Smooth weaves in cotton, hemp, lyocell, or tightly knit synthetics that don’t fuzz. Avoid Velcro and anything that pills; fuzz clogs the reedlike filters by the basins.
- Warmth: a thin insulating layer for night; radiative cooling can surprise you even though the air looks wet.
- Hands: light gloves help on resin-lipped rails and ladders. The resin won’t harm you, but it will bite cheap metals and leave a faint tack.
- Colors: low-key tones keep you from distracting workers and moths. Save the neon for other worlds.

What to carry
- Water: bring your own to start. You can request measured fills once oriented, but do not drink from any basin or cup on your own. A narrow-neck bottle with a tethered cap is best; nothing that invites a spill.
- Salt: tiny electrolyte tabs. The air keeps you cool, so you forget you’re losing minerals.
- Light: a headlamp with a true low setting and a warm or red mode. Blue-white beams at night pull every moth for a quarter aisle.
- Cloth: a flat-weave towel or bandanna for dew and bench-film; a small chamois or anti-fog wipe for optics.
- Mask if you’re sensitive: the mill pods smell of tannin and a little solvent; the mildew on an old belt can tickle lungs. An N95 is overkill for most, perfect for the reactive.
- Notebook and pencil. Ink can bead. Graphite behaves.
- A soft-bristle brush to clean soles before stepping onto a walkway; a spare to gift.
- Earplugs if total quiet unnerves you. The district whispers: belts skate, valves tick, water threads down. It’s more present when you sleep than you expect.

What not to bring
- Ultrasonic repellents, high-volume speakers, or musical instruments you intend to play through handrails or trunks. Growth here answers to tone. Unmeant requests still count.
- Aerosol bug sprays, harsh solvents, bleach wipes. They bruise the place and confuse the valve-trees.
- Drones and kites. Air is a design choice; don’t crowd it.
- Spiky tripods, cleated canes, hard-soled boots. They leave scars.
- Pets. The green is not provisioning animal corridors; predation here is plant-shaped and patient.
- Loose glitter, fleece that sheds, any fabric that litters microfibers. The reed filters keep the district’s memory clean; don’t gum it.

Etiquette with the living city
- Keep to the cambium-thickened bands and the visibly planed paths. The open ground between is intentional—air lanes, not shortcuts.
- Touch lightly, briefly. Rails and posts will flare a little under frequent contact; your fingers will be read as vote and request if you linger and rub.
- Speak softly. Three people at normal volume under the Condensary feels like a breach because it is.
- Don’t harvest dew, leaves, or pith. The cups you see at the Condensary lip are communal cisterns. The city catches breath for itself.
- No open flame. The gasifier pods contain their blue fire; yours is a hazard.
- Ask early, accept slow. If you’re commissioning a grown shelf, a grafted hook, or even a hammock point, a steward will set weights and scaffolds and send tones. Results come on wood time and will be good.
- Step around active traps and carnivorous cups. They’re no danger to you, but don’t spring them; a day’s protein matters here.
- Photography without flash. If you must light, use diffuse and warm and avoid pointing it at blooms or the white undersides of condenser plates.

Health and comfort
- Hydrate deliberately, not constantly. The air feels quenching and tricks your thirst; sip, salt, repeat.
- Skin: the dew is clean but mineral-laced. If your skin minds iron and a whisper of salt, rinse with your own water at day’s end and apply a plain barrier cream. Avoid perfumed lotions; they pull moths.
- Lungs: most people breathe easier here than outside. If you’re reactive to spores, keep the mask handy in the mill pods and in the cool powerhouse hollow with humming dynamos.
- Cuts: the resins here are mildly antiseptic but not yours to use. Carry your own plasters and leave the valve-trees alone.
- Sleep: hammocks are happiest. Many guest platforms are pre-cleared; ask permission to tie in. Nights are quiet enough that you’ll hear the deep inward click when a valve-tree consents to move water. It’s not a problem; it’s the rhythm.

Getting around
- The grid is real but softened by growth. Where a path curves unpredictably, you’re seeing old training swallowed by the plant. Keep to the polished, darkened lines—the places that remember feet.
- Rail: the meter-gauge loop is not a toy. Trains are few, slow, and corked quiet; look for the light on the car rather than the noise of it. Stand clear of the laminated rails; they are slick when beaded.
- Bridges and platforms are held by catenaries of timber that look too light and are exactly right. Trust them with respect. No bounding, no hopping the lips.
- Dripways are not stairs. The rungs look tempting. They are for needles of runoff, not boots.
- If you’re lost, put your palm to a handrail and listen. Low sounds ride the wood. Follow the burr of a generator, the faint clack of a press, or the water talk down and out; they lead to people.

Work rhythms you’ll feel
- Foggy mornings: faster belts, more workshop lights, busier pods.
- Clear runs: handwork and maintenance surface—polishing, set-screw checks, scaffold nudges, tone tests.
- After the brief hard rain (every few weeks): the district gleams, the cisterns brim, the freightway smokes a soft resin where ropes run, and everyone checks anchors and lips. You’ll be asked to stay off certain spans until they stop shedding.

If you want to help, and how to pay
- They accept time, attention, and parts. Useful gifts: soft brushes, resin-friendly lubricants, belts in old standard sizes, warm low-lumen bulbs, rubber pole tips, fine stainless that won’t rust but also won’t bully the wood.
- Money is taken edgeward (there’s a ledger near the old metal nameplate where stewards settle with outsiders), but inside you’ll get further offering to hold a tone probe, retie a weight, or wipe dew from a gauge.
- Don’t ask the plant directly unless you’ve been shown how. Work through people; they know what they’ve already asked for and what the green is willing to answer.

Rules in practice (you’ll see them as habits)
- Take your sound off the wood unless invited. Music stays airborne, low.
- Light only what you need to see. Night belongs to moths and the small white blooms.
- Space is allowed to be space. Don’t fill every bench and landing with your body and your bag.
- Change requests get made early in the day and never in a rush.
- Pack out every crumb and fiber; there are designated collection jars for stray fuzz and coil-scrap near the mill pod doors.

Hazards and how to avoid them
- Slip risk: dew-slick planks, resin-lipped rails, and the smooth undersides near the Condensary. Move with the patience the place teaches you.
- Traps: insect cups and folding leaves—fascinating, sticky. Keep clear and don’t poke.
- Electricity: dynamos hum and wires in resin sleeves mind their business. Don’t bridge terminals, don’t drape metal necklaces over living conduit.
- Weather pulses: when the brief hard rain arrives, a thousand small falls become many—not dangerous if you’re under canopy, but don’t be on a dripway or the lip of a basin.
- Edges: off the berms the plain is bright and brittle. Don’t step out in midday without eye protection and water or you’ll feel baked hollow in minutes.

Food and water
- Bring sealed, low-crumb food. Eat over your cloth. Wipe the bench when you’re done.
- Water is a procession here, not a buffet. There are designated sips for visitors once a steward knows your face. They’ll show you the little wooden lip that never quite overflows.
- Don’t rinse cups, hands, or tools in any basin. Every cistern is part of the district’s mapped drip.

Respect for the few animals
- Waders arrive after rains; watch with quiet. Don’t push them along drip lines to see where they go.
- Frogs happen in a pair of basins for a few weeks. Enjoy their sound, don’t hunt their egg collars with your curiosity.
- Lizards on condenser racks own those rails the way you own your shoes. Give them right-of-way.

What to do with your senses
- Ears: press them to a handrail. You’ll hear a jointer bring two panels flush three bays away, and the layered gentleness of work.
- Nose: catch the sweetness of tannin in the mill pod, the metal whisper at the tea-colored cistern linings, the cool sap of the powerhouse hollow.
- Skin: feel the air change at aisle mouths, the radiated heat stored in root-berms, the film of dew at dawn. It’s how the place speaks to bodies.
- Eyes: stand under the Condensary at first light and watch a million small decisions go to ground. At noon, look up: the crosshatched leaf-shadow is its own cathedral.

If something goes wrong
- If you slip into a basin or foul a dripway, stop moving. Call, softly. Help will find you faster than thrashing will help you. They know their own hydraulics.
- If you trigger a small trap with a sleeve or a cuff, don’t yank back; breathe, ease free, and let a steward apply a neutralizing wash.
- In lightning: pods, not walkways. Away from grafted phloem runs and handrails.
- In injury: there are kit boxes near the pod doors—clean bandages, not plant resins. Use them and leave a note in the ledger.

A last word on tempo
Your first urge will be to match a city’s usual stride. Don’t. This place does not withhold urgency as a test; it simply does not possess it except in dew and weather. If you ask it something, ask early and accept the slow yes. If you listen, listen to the low. If you work, keep your oil gentle and your magnets kind. If you stand in the middle and wait for a single face to assemble, it won’t. It will instead give you a moving center: a hush of drips, a click consenting, a belt’s soft skate. That’s the right welcome.
