What this place is like
You’re headed into a cistern-hall that has become a village—a hand-built, hand-polished, always-breathing room where light, water, sound, and work are braided into one system. Think of it as a monastery for water and air, tuned over generations until even the drips are information. Nothing here is accidental. Everything is doing a job.

Expectations and sensations
- Temperature and humidity: Warm as a body, not stifling; humidity shifts by hour. Mornings may bead dew on lashes. Certain hours smell like boats and pitch; other hours like lime and fresh rain.
- Light: Squares and circles of daylight dropped through whitewashed shafts; some open, some shuttered. Midday can verge on bright. Dusk is hooded lamplight, all downward. No glare, but strong contrast—like walking inside a net of time.
- Sound: A constant, breathing tone; wind organs in the walls; plinks, rings, and hums from drip-forests; soft scrape of brooms; birds’ thin calls; frogs’ bass at dusk. During rain, it becomes a drum and a river at once.
- Surfaces: Terracotta, lime plaster, polished wooden handholds, reed mats. Floors feel slightly silky under bare soles. Steps are grooved where water runs; hand-hollows are oiled smooth from use.
- Life: Swiftlets near the light shafts; geckos along the ribs; a huntsman spider you should let be; frogs stationed like sentries on channel lips; the quick punctuation of a ferret’s bell. Fish thrum up the runnels when rains come.

When to go
- To see the system “thinking”: early morning (dew chimes forming and falling).
- To see it bright and active: midday (light patches crisp, maintenance and cooking underway).
- To feel the room gather itself: dusk (lamps hooded, frogs begin, drip-notes separate, counting knocks on gourds).
- Avoid first fierce downpour if you’re new. The hall is safe, but water surges require practiced choreography.

How to arrive
- Arrive unhurried, hands visible, shoes in hand. Wait at the threshold until someone gestures you in.
- Offer a short greeting and a visible willingness to be put to work. Here, help is the currency.

What to wear
- Feet: Plan to be barefoot or in soft, clean, quiet soles used only indoors. No grit-tracking footwear. Bring a small mat or cloth bag for your outdoor shoes.
- Clothes: Breathable, quiet fabrics (cotton, linen, light wool). Layers you can add or shed. Long sleeves and trousers cut close enough not to snag on ladders and hung cords.
- Head/neck: A light scarf to baffle drafts and to screen your breath near delicate condensers.
- Pack: Soft-sided, non-rigid, nothing that snags. Keep your profile low; the ceiling bristles with drip-needles.

What to bring (useful, welcome, and harmless)
- Time and hands: The most valued offering here is a task done attentively.
- Consumables that match their tech: clean-burning lamp oil (sesame/olive) in a stoppered bottle; beeswax; hemp or linen cord in modest lengths; a few fine, non-rusting needles; spare gourd or two with drilled hanging holes; simple cloths for wiping and drying.
- Repairs: Small tin of pitch or pine resin, a bit of ash or lime for quick mortar touch-ups, a patch of fine mesh for a weir.
- Personal gear that won’t offend the system:
  - A hooded, warm-toned light you can point strictly downward (ask before using at all; their lamps are enough).
  - Earplugs for sleep, though try one night without—sound is part of the language here.
  - Unscented soap, a small towel, foot powder, antifungal cream if you’re prone to it. Amphibians live here: keep all chemicals well away from water.
  - Insect protection: long sleeves, a head net if you’re bite-sensitive, picaridin-based repellent used far from water (never DEET or permethrin near channels).
  - A light hammock sheet or sleeping cloth if you plan to stay; they have slings and anchors, but bring your own fabric.
- Gifts that speak their dialect of making: a good soft broom head, a coil of reed or rattan, a small bundle of clean rags. If in doubt, offer labor first, objects second.

What not to bring
- Anything crumbly, glittering, shedding, fragrant, or adhesive. No aerosols. No food that flakes into channels. No drones. No hard-soled shoes. No bright headlamps or camera flashes.
- Avoid plastics that squeak and scratch; avoid magnets and gadgets that hum—this place holds its own music.
- Don’t bring live plants, seeds, soil, or pets. Decontaminate your gear if you’ve been in other wetlands; amphibian disease is real across worlds.

Non-negotiable rules (learn them before you touch anything)
- Never block a water channel, even for a heartbeat.
- Never silence all the drip-plates at once.
- Keep one lamp banked at the mouth of the sleeping niche.
- In the east alcove, the frogs feed at dusk and dawn; do not startle or crowd them then.
- The small stone on the fourth weir from the west stays where it is. If you don’t know which weir that is, don’t touch any.
- Do not reach for cords or counterweights unless someone indicates which one and why. Everything ties to something else here.
- Ask before photographing; no flash. Sound is a tool; don’t add noise for novelty’s sake.

How to move and behave
- Slow down. Walk where the grooves tell you. Use the hand-hollows; they are invitations and instructions.
- Keep to the terracotta floor; reed mats catch dust—do not step on them.
- Mind the ceiling forests: hundreds of hanging funnels and cups that you must not jostle. Duck gently; keep your pack low.
- Speak softly. Listen first. The room is speaking already. Learn its base note, then notice the changes.
- If water begins to hurry: get clear of the runnels, step to higher stone, and watch the closest keeper for cues. There is a practiced dance to unpinning weirs and opening shutters; you may be asked to pass a pin, hold a gourd, or simply move out of the way.

Understanding the “language of the hall”
- Drip tones: Higher and faster means a colder or newly moistened surface above; slowing or flattening means a pan is nearing its even state or needs tipping. People will hear this the way you read a clock.
- Shell whistles at shaft mouths: a northward wind and a turn toward rain; this will cause a flurry of rope work overhead.
- Gourd strikes: counting, timing, sometimes calling a neighbor without shouting. Don’t imitate until invited.
- The ferret’s bell: not for rats; for people. If you hear it, make sure your face and hands aren’t where a ferret will jump.

Food, water, and sanitation
- Drinking: There will be designated vessels for drinking water; do not scoop from runnels. Ask for “cup water” or “mouth pans.”
- Eating: Expect grains, fungi from another room, maybe fish when permitted. Offer to help hull, sweep, or tend lamps in exchange for a bowl. Don’t feed wildlife.
- Hygiene: There is a rhythm to washing; runoff is directed. Ask where to tip graywater. Keep soaps away from frogs and channels.
- Waste: Pack out what you brought in. Human waste is likely handled in a separate corridor; do not improvise.

Sleeping
- Hammocks hang in niches; ask where to string yours or borrow a sling. Keep your belongings in a bundle hung from a peg. Food seals tightly or it becomes a rat’s lesson.
- A lamp must remain banked by the niche mouth; if you’re on lamp duty, learn the wick and hood etiquette.
- Nights are sound-rich; expect the frogs’ base note, slow breathing of vents, an occasional gourd pulse.

Safety notes
- Slippery when freshly wet; the grooves help but don’t rush.
- Wildlife: Geckos and huntsman spiders are friends; don’t handle them. Don’t touch amphibians with dry hands; better not at all. Snakes are turned back with the snake-stick; if you see one, call a keeper, don’t intervene.
- During heavy rain, watch the channels. Surges are beautiful and sudden. Keep loose cloth and straps away from moving water.
- Falling hazards: Ladders lean in odd places; look up before you step. Don’t stand under a lifted shutter.

Learning and helping
- Easiest ways to be useful your first day:
  - Sweep dust toward reed mats without lifting it into the air.
  - Carry heavy, gray mats to the sluice when asked; keep their dirty water away from drinking pans.
  - Mind a drip-forest while someone fetches tools: call softly if a line stalls or a cup overfills.
  - Pass pins and pegs to a rope team on cue.
  - Sit by the old woman who times basins and mirror her motions.
- If you play an instrument or keep time, don’t show it yet. First learn how they do timing with gourds and drops.

If you’re sensitive to sensory load
- Bring a thin eye mask for the occasional shaft of moonlight.
- Earplugs help, but try to wean yourself to a partial seal—the room’s “breath” is part of navigating it.
- Sit with your back to a pillar if you need an anchor; the hand-hollows are made for resting, not only grabbing.

Etiquette shortcuts
- Shoes off, hands washed, voice low.
- Ask, don’t guess. Gesture to a cord, then to yourself with raised brows—someone will nod or redirect you.
- If you bump a funnel or a gourd, stop and make eye contact with the nearest resident; they will retune it or show you how.
- Offer first: “May I sweep?” “Where may I sit?” “What must I not touch?”
- Give thanks in labor. Leave a thing better than you found it: a coil wound neatly, a mat squared, a basin tipped just so.

Good offerings on departure
- A neatly coiled length of cord with a simple splice you’ve learned to tie.
- A cleaned, patched mat you beat yourself.
- A small bottle of clean lamp oil, handed to the person who kept the night flame.
- Your attentive memory: be ready to recite two or three rules aloud to prove you understood the room.

Why come
You’ll leave with a different sense of time: not the counting of clocks, but of drops, breaths, and hand-to-hand adjustments. This hall is an education in how a place can be intelligent without machines—discipline woven through clay, rope, light, and patience. Come prepared to be porous and precise, to listen with your skin, and to disappear—briefly—into the work of keeping water company.
