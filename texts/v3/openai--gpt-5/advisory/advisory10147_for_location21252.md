What you’re walking into
- A high, hot, saturated world where water is everywhere and almost nothing is dry. Think tepui plateaus gone mythic: black sponge-stone, permanent mist made on-site, combs of thread-thin superstructures droning along the mesa edges that bend wind, gather clouds, and soften gravity. Life is layered, carnivorous in places, delicate in others, and patient everywhere.
- Expect relentless humidity, heat that arrives via stone and steam, infrasound from the combs, sound that bends around corners, and a constant bias in the air that makes you a little lighter and holds breath and cloud close to the ground.

Core hazards to respect
- Heat stress in 100% humidity: sweating cools poorly; dehydration and hyponatremia both are risks.
- Slips and falls: algae films, spongey footing, invisible comb threads, altered gravity at edges, and optical/sound illusions in mist.
- Corrosion and rot: steel, untreated leathers, and adhesives fail fast.
- Biology: fungi, molds, small stinging/biting insects, possible contact toxins from amphibians and plants, unknown microbes in tank-waters.
- Electromagnetics and infrasound: faint charge around combs, low notes in the chest—can unsettle the nervous system, confuse electronics, and erode focus.
- Culture and tech: the combs and the slow local machines are not ruins; they are working infrastructure. Treat them as you would an active dam or temple.

Before you go
- Heat acclimatization: 10–14 days of daily exercise in warm humid conditions if you can. Practice moving slowly, hydrating by schedule not by thirst, and salting your food.
- Fungal defense: start a daily skin/foot care routine two weeks prior (antifungal wash or wipes, zinc oxide for high-friction zones).
- Cardio is less critical than heat discipline; breathing is surprisingly easy up top, but don’t count on the physics being kind every day.
- Medical prep: up-to-date tetanus. Pack antihistamines, epinephrine autoinjector if you’re allergy-prone, oral rehydration salts, topical steroids for rashes, antifungals (clotrimazole/terbinafine), broad-spectrum antibiotic, sterile strips, hydrocolloid dressings, and a small suction tick/leeches tool.
- If you have implants, pacemakers, or insulin pumps, get device-specific guidance for high-humidity and EMF exposure. Infrasound can provoke anxiety—have a plan if you’re susceptible.

How to get there and move around
- Arrival: best is to portal in or come with locals who understand the lift lines the combs create. Helicopters and ordinary drones struggle in the dense mist and pressure quirks; gliders and kites can work if guided by someone who “hears” the fields.
- On-foot travel: plan short stages. Mornings are the kindest. Around midday the wind climbs and the combs announce themselves—find shelter then, move again late afternoon.
- Stay on rock and established mats. Every step on the living carpet leaves a signature longer than your trip.
- Edges: tie in. Always. Microgravity zones can make a normal stumble a long slide. Use light, nonconductive tethers clipped to your belt and a partner.
- Sound lies. If you’re following a waterfall by ear, you can lose it in twenty paces. Mark your route visually.

Clothing and personal kit
- Fabrics: thin, hydrophobic synthetics that don’t hold water; merino can work for odor but expect it to stay damp. Bring duplicates of very light base layers so “wet” can alternate with “less wet.”
- Cut: loose, not flappy. Long sleeves and pants reduce bites and skin maceration.
- Footwear: soft, high-friction soles (felt or gecko rubber) that grip wet rock without studding delicate mats. Drainage is more important than waterproofing. Bring two insoles sets; swap midday. Toe caps help against hidden stone.
- Socks: thin synthetics you can rotate. Pre-tape heels and toes with kinesio or leukotape. Apply antiperspirant or zinc oxide to feet morning and night.
- Hat: wide, stiff brim for drip management.
- Hands: thin, textured gloves for constant wet grip; a second pair for camp.
- Eyes: two sets of lenses—low-UV, warm-spectrum headlamp or lantern for nights so you don’t scramble the local insect traffic, and the local sap-hardened UV lenses if you’re offered them (best way to see the cliff “starfields” and some comb threads).
- Ear management: carry soft earplugs that cut mid/high but let bass through; the infrasound is information here, but the insect engine can fray you.

Tools and materials that survive
- Metals: titanium and ceramic hardware, gold-plated connectors. Stainless still “velvets” here; bring it only if you’re happy to lose it. Avoid bright steel entirely.
- Polymers: PTFE, PEEK, HDPE, UHMWPE lines; aramids (Kevlar, Vectran) are fine. Avoid natural rubbers that will biofoul; silicone is stable.
- Fasteners and structure: prefer mechanical joins over adhesives; most glues creep or mold in continuous wet.
- Packs: simple, drainable, with roll-top closures and few zips. Double-drybag critical electronics with desiccant inside and keep sealed until needed.

Electronics and power
- Everything must be IP68-rated or better, conformal-coated, and stored warm to reduce internal condensation. Warm gear fogs less.
- Charging: solar is unreliable under gauzy sun; bring kinetic chargers, fuel cells rated for wet environments, or plan to barter for local charge time if such exists. Wireless charging pads reduce corroded contacts.
- Radios and GPS: compasses can drift near mineral veils; GPS may work, but radio lines bend in odd ways. Use low-frequency comms for reliability and rendezvous by time/landmark, not by “follow my ping.”
- Keep cables short; the comb fields can tickle long conductors. Coil, cap, and stow.

Navigation and field-sense
- Carry a pocket pendulum and a small plumb bob. They show you comb-biased gravity. If the bob leans, your leap will land strange—shorten your stride.
- A fine mister bottle is your friend. A quick spray at ankle height can light invisible threads and field lines in the air.
- A handheld EMF meter and a cheap contactless voltage tester can warn you when you’re nearing a comb strand.
- Map by sights and smells. Make high-contrast, biodegradable chalk marks on bare rock only; do not tag plants or combs. Better yet, travel with a local who reads the hum.

Water, food, and cooking without insult
- Water is “everywhere” but not safe. Do not drink bromeliad tanks or pitcher plant liquor—rich in larvae, microbes, and alkaloids. Collect sheet-flow or fresh drips from plain rock.
- Treatment: gravity ceramic filter + activated carbon + UV pen. Boiling is taboo and hard in this wet; you can pasteurize by sealing water in a dark bag and setting it on warm stone for a few hours—ask locals how they confirm it; they have tricks.
- Electrolytes: add salts to at least one liter per two liters plain water. Eat salty broths, not just sweet gels. Hyponatremia here is sneaky.
- Food: plan light, high-salt, low-cook menus. Dehydrated foods rehydrate well; bring spice/sour to fight heat fatigue. If you accept local meals, expect steam-and-stone cookery and benign fungi/greens. Many frogs and bright plants are toxic—don’t forage unless taught.
- Fire: open flame is an insult. Bring flameless heating packs if you must, or learn the hot-stone method in a ceramic pot. Alcohol or hydrocarbon stoves are both rude and dangerous around comb mists.

Health maintenance in saturation
- Skin: rinse, dry what you can, barrier cream on pressure points, powder sparingly. Change out of expedition clothes in camp even if the camp set is still damp—clean-damp beats dirty-damp.
- Feet: midday sock/insole swap; elevate at rests. Treat hot spots immediately. For trench-foot tendency, short “airing drills”: shoes off 5 minutes every few hours under a net.
- Bites/stings: pressure-immobilize, then antihistamines. Avoid scratching—skin infections bloom fast here.
- Contact toxins: many amphibians and plants carry them on their skin. Wash hands after touching anything living; don’t touch your face. Nitrile gloves for handling unknowns.
- Sleep: a hammock with a breathable underpad and a high, well-vented bug net. Tents turn into steam ovens. Pitch under a comb-sheltered overhang only with permission and never slung from the threads.

Living with the combs
- See them first. Use side light and fine mist; a simple wand waved slowly can pull beads from the air onto otherwise invisible threads.
- Don’t pluck, touch, or sling gear from them. They cut like wire, conduct faint charge, and are part of an active system tuning weather and gravity bias.
- Expect their “voice” to notch down at night and surge around midday winds. If the bass deepens into a slow drumbeat from the cliff, anchors are stretching—stay back from edges.
- Your body will feel a soft hand on your shoulder at times: that’s the field bias. It can make you sluggish or oddly buoyant. Let your pace follow the place; rushing here fights the rules.

Etiquette and the human layer
- Huts are spaced with intent. Approach slowly; look for the dish of pebbles catching drips at the threshold. Don’t rearrange it. If invited, you can add a pebble.
- Trade, don’t tip. Things that matter here: ceramic tools, titanium needles, stories, songs that do not clash with the comb’s register, good cordage. Bright steel turns to velvet—don’t offer it.
- Ask before taking UV “starfield” walks along the cliffs; the night ecology is busy and easily spooked by the wrong lights.
- Never mock the machines. The slow minds in roots and mud are present-tense neighbors. Don’t “fix” anything unless someone asks you to, and then do it the slow way, with them.
- Fire is a story-thing. If you absolutely must burn, do it inside a local narrative—by invitation, in a ceramic hearth designed for it. Otherwise, heat stones.

Waste and trace
- Pack out anything that won’t rot harmlessly. Even metals that corrode can poison seams; don’t leave them. Human waste: follow local practice; often this means designated seep-edges where microbes are already abundant, or sealed carry-out bags if you’re moving between huts.
- Oils and anti-corrosion: the place “recognizes” its own. Buy or trade for the local plant oils cured under comb tones; they protect gear and are culturally correct. Wipe down at day’s end.

Daily rhythm to plan by
- Early morning: best for walking and seeing. Mist is gentle, sounds are honest.
- Late morning to afternoon: wind climbs, combs answer, edges grow treacherous. Work near camp, visit pools, listen.
- Evening: heat slides “to the side,” insects retune. Cook with stones, rinse gear, feet care, mend.
- Night: sudden and real. Bass note drops; UV life switches on. Move only with consent and the right lenses.

Simple field tricks
- Comb check: hold a strand of spider silk or a hair at arm’s length—if it tugs sideways or beads, there’s a field line in front of you.
- Gravity bias test: drop a short cord with a washer; note the lean. If it never quite points down, keep three points of contact.
- Slime sense: shine a light at a low angle across rock; rainbow-sheened algae are slicker than matte black films.
- Rust audit: inspect metal twice daily. Wipe and oil. If it “velvets,” retire it before it fails under load.

What not to bring
- Open-flame stoves, accelerants, fireworks, or bright white-blue headlamps.
- Heavy leather boots or belts; they mildew and slip.
- Long dangling metal lines or antennas.
- Bright steel tools you care about.
- Single-point lifelines—always twin, nonconductive tethers.

Emergency thinking
- Your #1 rescue is your partner and the next hut. Travel in pairs or threes. Share your day plan with the nearest people.
- If someone overheats: shade, strip to base layer, douse with cool drip-water, elevate legs, salts when alert. Don’t force water if nauseated.
- If someone falls near an edge with comb bias: do not jump after. Anchor, test gravity with your pendulum, approach crawled and tethered.
- Comms failure: agree on time-based behaviors. “If we don’t link by sunset, each returns to last hut.” Don’t chase ghosts in the mist.

A compact checklist
- Wear: light long-sleeve, light pants, soft-grip shoes, hat, thin gloves.
- Carry on you: UV-safe headlamp, mister bottle, pendulum/plumb, tiny EMF wand, knife in ceramic/titanium, salts, ORS, antifungal wipes, zinc oxide, bug net hood, twin nonconductive tethers and carabiners, whistle with a warm tone.
- In pack: hammock + net, two base layer swaps, sock/insoles swap, ultralight rain cape, ceramic filter + UV pen, high-salt snacks, flameless heaters or plan to cook with stones, repair kit (ceramic/titanium needles, UHMWPE cord, tape), med kit, spare gloves, warm layer for night (damp-chill is real), double drybags, gold-plated charge cable, power bank.
- Trade goods: ceramic needles, strong cord, small titanium items, seeds from your world (only if asked), and stories.

Finally, mindset
- The place announces its rules gently, then enforces them absolutely. Move slow. Listen. Resist the urge to fix, dry, or dominate. Let your schedule take on the place’s long exhale: step, stop, look, sip, salt, smile. If you find yourself grinning at a droplet letting go of a bromeliad leaf as if it were a small planet falling into a bigger one, you’re doing it right.
