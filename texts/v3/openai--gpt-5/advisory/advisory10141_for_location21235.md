What your body will meet
- Air that has just rained and will never stop being after-rain: warm, dense, bead-wet. Expect a steady dewpoint near skin temperature, fogging anything cooler than you are.
- Ground that is not ground: vitrified ceramic and ribbed metals glazed by microfilms. There is no friction debt forgiven here—everything is a thin balance between surface tension and your steps.
- A noise floor of hush and metronomes. Your own zipper may feel like a shout.
- An ecology that is nearest to the surface: hyphae at seams, grazer-lint that looks like punctuation, filter petals bristling at drains, quiet small mammals above. Your footfall is geology to them.

Your task
- Leave no wake. Do not change the way water moves, heat distributes, or films feed. If you must be here, be here as a sealed, soft weight that touches little, sheds nothing, and departs without having given or taken.

Pre-arrival decontamination
- Personal: Shower with non-scented, residue-free detergent; skip conditioners and lotions. Trim nails and cover minor cuts. Avoid perfumes for a week beforehand; volatile compounds can alter foraging behavior in this place.
- Clothing and gear: Use new or autoclaved kit. Vacuum-seal until the moment you suit up. No cotton, no wool, no natural fibers; no felted items; no hook-and-loop that sloughs. Everything lint-free, low-shed.
- Biological: If you’ve recently visited soils, forests, or aquatic edges, assume you carry spores and eggs. Include a 24–48-hour quarantine in clean, filtered air before entry to shed hitchhikers.

What to wear
- Outer suit: Smooth-faced, non-shedding, fully taped, hydrophobic shell with minimal texture. Think a laboratory cleanroom suit redesigned for ship decks: slippery to water but grippy at the sole. Integrated hood. Pale gray or off-white to neither shadow-bait nor glare.
- Underlayers: Thin wicking base, plus a phase-change cooling vest if you run hot. There is no evaporation to save you; manage your own heat.
- Footwear: Soft, siped rubber soles with boat-deck lineage. Wide last, no heel. Gecko-microfibril patches at toe/heel for controlled adhesion on enamel and ribbed steel. No spikes, no studs (scratches seed new ecosystems).
- Gloves: Nitrile or PU-coated, textured just enough to hold your tools. Bring two sizes for layering if temperatures differ between strata.
- Face: Full-face respirator or quiet PAPR with P100/HEPA (and exhale filtering) to keep your breath from feeding the films and to keep their spores from feeding you. Anti-fog insert and hydrophilic lens coating are not optional.
- Eyes/ears: The respirator covers eyes. Skip earplugs unless the quiet unnerves you; you’ll navigate partly by listening for the regular clicks and whispers.

Pack, but pare it down
- Hard case, IP68, smooth-faced, with a lanyard. Anything soft-edged and fuzzy will come away carrying half a small world.
- Navigation:
  - Foot-mounted IMU/inertial nav linked to a wrist display. There is no sun, no slope you can feel, and the modules repeat.
  - High-resolution inclinometer (can read 0.01°) for “field-down” if you must choose a single direction. The gradient is a rumor, but precise.
  - Note-taking device sealed and wrist-mounted; camera trapping of enamel plaques and stamped numbers becomes your breadcrumb trail.
- Movement:
  - Walking staff with a silicone, non-marking tip for probing slickness and edges; foldable and leashed to you.
  - Compact sling or sit-pad with rubber feet if you rest; no fabric pads on the floor.
- Optics:
  - Weather-sealed camera with hydrophilic front element or a gentle lens-heater ring. Accept that fog is a collaborator here; harsh lights will write new weather. Use a low-power, wide, warm lamp only for close work under catwalks.
- Health:
  - Oral rehydration salts (you will sweat but nothing evaporates).
  - Barrier cream (silicone-based) for feet and hands before suiting.
  - Antifungal topical, broad-spectrum; sterile dressings; blister care.
  - Electrolyte tabs; antihistamine if fungal volatiles irritate you.
- Waste: Double-bag WAG system with rigid outer; all fluids and solids pack out. No urination into drains. This world already calibrates its salts.
- Tools:
  - Non-residue mechanical solutions only. No tapes, no powders, no oils. A slim, rounded pry for freeing yourself if a velvet trap tests your boot.
  - Fine mesh gear covers for vents/intakes on your suit or pack, to discourage salt-seekers.
- Power: Conformal-coated electronics, desiccant inside housings (they will saturate; plan short exposures). Magnetic connectors shrouded. Avoid opening anything here.

How to move
- Use what was made for feet: catwalks, inspection lanes, ribbed metal. Hazard stripes mean “step here.” Expansion joints are highways for microfauna; do not stride along them.
- Step like a librarian: roll from midfoot, place, test, transfer. You are a heavy droplet among ten thousand small ones.
- Avoid edges under condenser fins where drip-lines stay unnaturally perfect. That glossy precision can be a living latch.
- Never block a drain, even for a second. If you must cross one, do so perpendicular and light.
- Keep your heat to yourself. Don’t lean long against fins or louvers; your warmth writes a local weather the custodians must then erase.
- Mind the air roads. Membrane bats ride invisible updraft lanes between towers. Give them the height—don’t raise poles or throw anything into those paths.
- If the whole ceiling chooses rain at once, stand still. Your body’s wake in a synchronized shower is its own shout. Let the place finish speaking.

What not to do
- Do not wipe, polish, scrape, or “help.” The organisms here are the maintenance crew. Your idea of clean is a pollutant.
- Do not feed, chase, or photograph at distances that alter behavior. If a duct-marten watches you, it is counting your breaths, not asking for a handout.
- Do not sit on the ceramic. Rest on your pad or hang a sling from a tower rung without loading fins or blocking louvers.
- Do not open handwheels, hatches, gauges. They are moving exactly as needed because something lives to keep them so.
- No cooking, no flame, no vented heat. Pack cold food.

Reading the place
- Time: Accept that sunless noon. Carry your own clock and obey it. Simulate evening with your visor tint at the end of your watch.
- Direction: Numbers are the constellations now. Photograph enamel module names, arrow stamps, hatch IDs. Tag your inertial trail with these waypoints.
- Weather: A clean, unbroken drip line is a vein; a sudden matte patch on enamel means a gloss-eater worked; a too-clean square meter is a mouth. Walk around rain that looks arranged.

If you fall or are taken
- Slip into a basin: Spread weight, belly to glaze, and inch to a rib or joint. Use your staff to hook a grille; do not kick (scratches seed new colonies).
- Velvet disk adheres: Freeze. Slide the rounded pry under from the dry side, not the wet face. Do not twist. Flooding it with water only pleases it; oils are forbidden. Patience and a slow peel win.
- Suit breach: Tape is contamination. Instead, clamp a sterile compression patch from your kit and withdraw. You are now a leak.
- Lost: Stop. The world repeats to erase panic. Match nearest tower’s plaque to your last recorded ID. If you have none, pick a vector by inclinometer and commit; mark each decision by photographing every plate stamp you pass. Your inertial nav will tell you when you are spiraling—don’t let it.

Physiology and headspace
- Heat: You will sweat into a suit that cannot evaporate for you. Plan short sorties with cool-downs at your own air. Hydrate before you’re thirsty; electrolytes stave off the salt-hungry fauna that will test your zippers.
- Skin: Maceration comes early here. Air feet at your safe airlock between walks, powder only inside clean socks. Barrier cream before, not after.
- Mind: The sameness is a tuning fork. Some find prayer here, some a low anxiety. Give yourself markers—music on a timer, scheduled notes, a rule to stop at the fourth tower, not the fifth.

Encounters
- Filter-feeders at drains: They snap at shadow; keep fingers away. They’re not for you.
- Isopod processions: Yield. They are on time and carrying harvests that keep everything from felting.
- Midges and bats: Your lamp will gather midges; your lamp will then gather bats. Use it sparingly and low.
- Duct-martens: They step rivet to rivet. If one mirrors your pace, it is measuring your habit. Be boring.

Photography and collecting
- Shoot with warmth. Let your camera acclimate in the suit’s microclimate; quick exposure to the room makes a lens into a droplet.
- Macro wins. A square decimeter is a city. You don’t need a vista.
- Leave with pixels only. Seeds, shells, shed plates, even a fallen petal of a filter feeder—don’t take them. They are gears.

Ethic and exit
- You are an exhalation here; be the kind that leaves no residue. The animals are not ornamental; they are function. Every crushed isopod is a slower drain an hour from now.
- Decon on departure as strictly as arrival. Expect hitchhikers: eggs at zipper teeth, hyphal lace nested in stitching, isopods in vent creases. A UV cabinet for your suit (zipped) and a long shower after. Quarantine the kit for a week before mixing it with gear from wetter or drier worlds.

A suggested day
- Hour 0: Hydrate, suit, heat your optics. Record starting module IDs.
- Hour 1–2: Walk the inspection lanes only. Learn the cadence: the click far away, the whisper near. Practice stopping when the place “rains together.”
- Hour 3: Find a basin field. Watch a drip line for five minutes. If it changes, you caused it—back off.
- Hour 4: Rest in your sling at a tower. Log plaque names, their font, their age. Count the eel-mites polishing a gauge; you’ll see why this place is clean.
- Hour 5: Return by your numbers. If you aren’t a little in love with the hush by now, this place is not for you.

Remember the rule the animals write by living: keep the drains free, keep the condensers unrusted, polish the traffic lines with bellies and tongues, disturb nothing that holds the temperature. If you can make your body rhyme with that, you’ll fit. If not, look, listen, and leave early. This world rewards patience and punishes help.
