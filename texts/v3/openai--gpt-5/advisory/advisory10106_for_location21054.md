What it is, in plain travel terms
- A finished, functioning stone organ the size of a cupboard. Not a room to be in so much as a throat to pass through.
- Brilliant, shadowless white light delivered by a polished quartz vein: no flicker, no sun movement.
- A single, unwavering aeolian chord loud enough to press on your bones.
- Air as dry as powdered chalk, free of liquid water. Salts and gypsum everywhere, edges worn to satin by sound.
- Almost no spare space: packed with baffles, ribs, and honeycombed planes. The “floor” is close-set steps more like ribbing.
- Living crusts exist only in hairline seams and ledges. Nothing with blood lives here.

Should you go
- Only if claustrophobia is not your demon, and you can be a guest who leaves no trace and asks nothing of the place but to listen.
- Expect focus, hush (inside yourself; the place itself is loud), and an odd straightening of time. People come away feeling clarified or slightly emptied in a good way.

How to prepare your body
- Hydrate fully in the hour before. Avoid alcohol and strong diuretics for several hours. You will not drink inside.
- Protect mucous membranes: use a heat-moisture-exchanger mask (a high-grade desert/cold-weather breathing mask that recovers your exhaled humidity). This both saves you and spares the stone from your breath-water. Have it fitted before you arrive.
- Apply an unscented, very low-volatility barrier to lips and nostrils at least 30 minutes before entry so it flashes off before you go in. If you can tolerate it, skip balms entirely and rely on the mask.
- Ears: bring serious hearing protection. Two-layer is best: deep-seal foam earplugs (or custom musicians’ filters) plus over-ear muffs. You’re aiming to tame a steady chord without losing your balance; musician filters set to a moderate attenuation work well if you want to “hear the shape” without the pressure.
- If you’re prone to nosebleeds in dry air, rethink the visit or use a mask with internal absorbent pads. A single drop of blood here is a weeks-long scar in the salts.

What to wear
- Cleanroom-simple. Close-fitting, non-shedding, unscented, pale-colored clothing. A hood or skullcap to capture hair. No fibers fuzzing, no dangling cords, tassels, or jewelry. Tie hair securely; tuck all ends.
- Smooth-soled, soft, clean booties over your footwear (the kind used in microfabrication labs). No tread that will shed grit; no dark soles that leave marks.
- Thin, powder-free nitrile or PEEK-coated gloves. Your skin oils don’t belong here. Gloves also keep you from instinctive bracing on polished baffles.
- Integrated knee and elbow padding under the suit if you may need to crouch or crawl. Nothing with Velcro; it sheds. Use silent zips and smooth closures.

What not to bring
- Liquids, mists, sprays, or gels of any kind into the chamber. If it can bead, it will bloom salts and write itself on the place.
- Loose objects. Anything that can rattle, flutter, or dangle will be “tuned” by the resonance and may migrate into seams.
- Food, wrappers, paper, chalk, or markers. No notes inside; commit to memory.
- Tripods, selfie sticks, or bags. There isn’t the space or the moral room.
- Fans, drones, instruments, or speakers. The place makes its own sound and needs no help.

What you may bring (minimal kit)
- The mask and hearing protection already on you.
- A small, wrist-tethered, sealed camera or phone if you must, with lens hood and a neutral-density filter. Disable flash. Expect blown highlights; bracket exposures; shoot gently and rarely. Better: look.
- A slim haptic timer on your wrist set for a soft reminder to leave. Time smears here; you’ll lose count.

Etiquette translated from the place’s rules
- Do not shed. Bind hair, cover skin, seal pockets, still your clothing.
- Do not wet. Keep your breath, sweat, and tears to yourself. If you feel one coming, leave before it arrives.
- Do not add light unless you must for safety, and keep it low, narrow-beam, and cool. Light here is already an inhabitant.
- Do not lean or wedge into the vents or overhangs. Your body is a plug. Don’t be a plug.
- Do not speak above a whisper; better, trade hand signs. Your voice will not carry the way you think, and your breath will.
- Move as if dust is watching you and taking notes. Slow, sure, with the kind of patience that fits the place.

Getting in and moving
- Before the threshold: pause. Stand still for a minute to match your breathing to something quieter. Final check for danglers and grit on your soles.
- Entry will likely require a sideways turn or slight crouch; think “threading,” not “entering.” Keep elbows tucked and head low.
- Use the ribbed floor with a midfoot roll; don’t drag your soles. Touch walls only when necessary, and only with gloved fingertips on already-polished faces, never on the seam-crusts.
- Keep a fist’s distance from the quartz vein and the voiced edges (the thin throats and scallops where air moves). If you can see a blue-fixed shadow, that’s close enough.
- If the chord shifts or you feel your muffs vibrate with beats, you’ve occluded a key slit. Freeze, ease away, and let the place retune.

How long to stay
- First visit: 6–12 minutes inside. Set your timer. Long enough to hear the chord settle inside you, short enough to leave nothing of yourself behind.
- Veterans sometimes stay 20–30 minutes. Longer than that and your sense of time and thirst both go strange.

Safety and contingencies
- If panic rises, reverse gently the way you came. Do not twist in place; the geometry is unforgiving of flailing.
- If you drop something, do not swipe for it. Note where it rests. If retrieval can be done without touching seams or crusts, do so with gloved fingers; otherwise, accept the loss rather than comb the baffles.
- If you cough, sneeze, or bleed, cup everything into your mask or sleeve and withdraw immediately. Outside, bag and seal anything contaminated.
- If your ears ache or your balance wobbles, step back to the threshold and reset; adjust protection and re-enter only if it resolves.

What to attend to while you’re there
- The fixed blue of every shadow and how it doesn’t care what time it is.
- The way the quartz vein carries a memory of hands—minute waves of polish—that you’ll feel more than see.
- The single chord separating into its notes as your hearing acclimates: a high, a low, a middle that lives in your bones.
- The biocrusts along the grout lines: ash-pale at first, then resolving into green glass scales, with occasional ghostly “script” where an old skin folded back.
- The dust drifts tucked into leewards—a map of the room’s breath.
- How your own heartbeat makes a faint interference pattern against the tone when you hold very still.

Aftercare for you
- Rehydrate slowly once you’ve left the dry zone. Electrolytes help. Expect a small thirst debt and mild ear fatigue.
- Give your skin a lukewarm rinse before moisturizing; let your body return to wetness before you feed it oils.
- Ears may ring with a phantom of the chord for an hour. It fades. Quiet helps.

Aftercare for the place
- Inspect your suit and booties outside the threshold; brush off any borrowed dust back into the local air so it can rejoin its drifts. Bag your used PPE; don’t shake it out down the hall.
- If you think you left a trace, tell the next caretaker you meet, even if that caretaker is only a note for whoever comes after. Honesty is a form of cleaning.

If you can’t go in but still want to meet it
- Lean at the doorway; let the chord find your bones without committing your breath to the interior.
- Or come at a different hour and watch the same light’s certainty from a step away; your eyes are different even when the place is not.

Why this place matters
- It is a proof that something can be absolutely finished and absolutely alive. A lesson in patience you can feel in your teeth. Visiting is not about taking an experience; it’s about learning how to be the kind of traveler whose presence costs nothing.

Pack list, concise
- Heat-moisture-exchanger breathing mask, fitted.
- Dual-stage hearing protection (foam plugs + over-ear muffs or custom musician filters).
- Non-shedding, unscented, close-fitting clean suit with hood; soft clean booties; thin nitrile gloves; integrated knee/elbow pads.
- Small haptic timer.
- Optional: small, sealed camera with ND filter, flash disabled.

One last thing
- This place assigns purpose to everything in it. For the minutes you are there, give it your purpose too: be tuned until you are not loose. Leave when you feel yourself begin to bloom toward it. That’s your cue that it has welcomed you and is done.
