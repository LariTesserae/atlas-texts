Orientation in one breath
You’re going to a place that is finished in the way a cathedral is finished: nothing added, nothing wasted, everything held in a long, exact stillness. It is high; it is dry beyond dryness; light is a constant noon that clarifies rather than warms. Sound falls apart in the air. Distances behave until they don’t. Life is everywhere and almost motionless, tuned to shadow and position rather than voice and speed. You will not meet a storm. You may meet an edge you cannot see until you’re in it. Prepare to be patient, gentle, and very deliberate.

Skill prep before you go
- Practice moving slowly. Walks of three to six hours at “museum pace,” mindfully placing each step, help rewire your impatience.
- Practice silence. Spend time in an anechoic room or a heavy-snow forest; learn how your mind responds when echoes disappear.
- Shadow discipline. Learn to read and compare edges, double-shadows, and the behavior of tiny flags in a faint breeze. You will use shadow like a compass.
- Rope craft with torsion. Learn to put, keep, and read a precise number of twists per meter; learn how to lay line so it prefers a path and refuses a split.
- Graph thinking. Drill yourself to map by nodes and connections rather than distances and bearings. You’ll be building a route-net, not a contour map.
- Breath economy. Train with a heat–moisture-exchanger mask. Learn to nose-breathe at low volumes for hours.
- Quiet signaling. Agree on hand and posture codes with your partner; rehearse line-of-sight semaphores and shadow cards.

What to wear
- Full-body, dust-sealed clothing in pale, non-reflective tones. Think desert-stillsuit plus glacier whites, but matte. Fabrics should be smooth to shed flour-fine silica and salt. All closures internal or storm-flapped.
- Head: a helmet or soft shell hood with a wide, rigid brim that casts a readable, consistent shadow. Built-in eye protection with neutral-density lenses, high UV cut, and anti-glare coatings. Add a drop-down shade visor for reading micro-shadows without squinting.
- Face: a heat–moisture-exchanger mask that reclaims exhaled humidity and warms/cools intake air slightly. Bring two, rotate daily; salt is relentless.
- Hands: thin liner gloves under abrasion-proof outer gloves with good tactile feedback. You will be reading textures as much as touching them.
- Feet: wide, soft-soled boots that spread load (think gentle snowshoes or camel-pad boots). Soles should be tough against glassy edges and yet forgiving so you don’t crush the living crust where it is thin. Detachable over-sand pads for powder and agglomerated shell fields.
- Skin: oil-based barrier (silicone or squalane, not water-based moisturizers). Line your nostrils with medical-grade sesame or lanolin oil; use ocular gel drops that don’t need water. Sunscreen is still wise even under a cool noon.

Life support and water
- Assume the world will not accept “wet.” Carry a closed-loop hydration system. Best: an integrated stillsuit/reclaimer that captures sweat, urine, and breath moisture and returns potable water. Bring an independent, sealed water reserve you can drink through the mask without exposing liquid to air.
- Do not open containers to “pour.” Free water here can behave strangely, bead, or shed—at minimum you’ll lose it; at worst you’ll make a hazard and feed the wrong attention.
- Drink by schedule, not by thirst. Cool, unwavering air tricks appetite and thirst. Set a timer or use suit telemetry.
- Salt management: you will accumulate external salts without sweating them out. Wipe down with oil-soaked cloths; never with plain water. Electrolytes go in your reclaimed drinking water.

Breathing and acoustics
- Sound will not help you. Whistles and shouts go nowhere. Plan all communication by:
  - Tether signals (gentle tugs with pre-agreed codes).
  - Hand/arm semaphores at distance.
  - Laser/LED blink codes in the cool noon light.
  - Bone-conduction comms inside sealed hoods if radios falter. EM may be normal, but assume pockets of odd propagation. Redundancy matters.
- Carry a metronome or heart-rate cue inside your hood if silence makes you speed up unintentionally.

Navigation and staying oriented
- Bring and use:
  - A “shadow card”: a palm-size board with cutouts, pins, and a slit; used to compare local shadow sharpness and detect the telltale peel of double-shadows. When the card shows two edges where there should be one, you are near a seam or split.
  - A torsion line: 60–100 m of line with a counted twist (e.g., 13 turns per 10 m), knotted at primes. When laid on the ground, it “prefers” a single braid of the path and will show you where the world is trying to be two. You are borrowing a local trick; respect any native lines and never cross them with tension.
  - Inertial recorder and a topological logger: log nodes (cairns, gates, mats, unique boulders) and connections (which touch which, under what attention), not GPS points. Conventional maps will lie by tiny, insistent degrees.
  - Optical beacons: small, low-glare reflectors you can prop like cat’s eyes. Drop only along rock that is already scarred by passage; retrieve on return. If in doubt, don’t place; use existing cairns.
  - “Angle flags”: hair-thin streamers on wands to show micro-breeze and to betray places where air splits or refuses to move. Animals read this; so should you.
- Rules of thumb:
  - When distances are wrong, stop. You can’t win by pushing. Sit, take ten slow breaths, and re-read shadows and flags. Often the world will choose a single answer if you choose a single pace and direction.
  - Respect mats across seams. Step on them if they are clearly laid as bridges; do not move or re-tension them.
  - Between matched cairns, trust the line they imply more than your sense of “shorter.”
  - Ascents that feel level and descents that don’t pop your ears are warnings you are crossing attention layers. Move like you are threading a loom: slow, straight, steady.

Footing and edges
- Expect powder drifts, fused pans, brittle shell carpets, and clean drops. Poles with mushroom feet help you feel for the vertical slots that cut the tables. Test before you commit weight. Never hop.
- If a crunch sounds like breaking porcelain rather than shifting grit, back off; you are on a living reef of shells and plates.
- Avoid overhang lips glittering with salt. They shear unpredictably and everything below is made to receive, not forgive.

Interactions with life
- Every surface might be living or chosen by living things. Default to not touching. If you must, touch with the back of a gloved knuckle and withdraw.
- Do not walk through the slow curtains of web. A “wave” there takes seasons to pass; your tear would be a wound that lasts longer than your memory of making it.
- The floaters above are tethered by filaments so fine breath could cut them if breath counted. Assume poles, kites, drones, and masts can do harm. Do not fly anything, do not throw lines, and do not trail guy-wires under their ceilings.
- If a “mite carpet” turns to face you, stop. Let them turn away before you step past. Courtesy matters and also keeps you from stepping into something they’re warning you about.
- Never feed, water, or “rescue” anything. Here, “drink” and “eat” mean different things, and your charity could erase a grammar you don’t speak.

Human traces and artifacts
- Treat gates, mats, sails, and cairns as active instruments. They are measurements, prayers, and tools at once.
- Do not retie or “repair” anything unless you know the system. Rawhide is fossil now; replacing it with modern cord can overcorrect a balance.
- If you must mark, use minimal, removable signals: a single stone turned, a low-reflectance tag tucked under an edge. Erase on exit.
- If you meet people, you may not notice in a way you expect. Keep your shadow small, your hands open, and your speed slower than theirs. Wait to be attended to. Or not.

Camp and sleep
- There is no night, only your decision to stop. Bring a true-dark hood or sleep mask; bring soft ear covers, even though the air devours sound, to tell your body “now it is quiet for sleeping.”
- Choose lee sides of boulders where powder is shallow and the living crust is absent or already trampled. Probe before committing your weight. Lay down a ground cloth; do not stake if stakes would pierce seams or tethers in the stone.
- Cooking: cold rations or sealed-oxygen stoves. No open water, no steam. Keep all condensate inside your system.
- Power: solar is excellent under that fixed noon. Panels need dust-shedding surfaces; give them a gentle oil wipe rather than a dry brush to avoid launching fines.

Health risks and how to buffer them
- Desiccation without the sensation of heat. Schedule hydration. Occlude skin. Gel your eyes. Oil your nose.
- Over-breathing in thin, cool air. Use your HME mask and keep pace easy. If you feel “not getting enough,” slow down rather than gasp.
- Vertigo of mismatched distances and quiet. Anchor yourself with routine: hourly checks, written logs, a physical token you move from one pocket to another when you complete a loop.
- Falls into vertical slots. Test, tether, and never chase hats or beacons the wind and angles seem to carry.
- Microabrasion and salt rash. Wash with oil, not water. Swap base layers daily and bag the used; salt migrates and will bite on day three if you let it.
- Psychological pull to become “part of it.” That can be a gift, but set a timer for check-ins. If you find yourself delaying every decision “to match the place,” that’s your cue to turn back or to sit in shade and eat.

Tools that help here (and a few that don’t)
- Helpful: torsion lines; shadow cards; wide-footed poles; optical blinkers; low-glare reflectors; stillsuit; oil wipes; bone-conduction comms; inertial/topological logger; soft-soled wide boots; sleep-dark hood.
- Unhelpful to dangerous: loud hailers; whistles; rotorcraft drones; wheeled vehicles (they crush and slip); chalk (it vanishes into sameness and fouls crusts); anything that sprays or mists; tent stakes you plan to hammer.

Etiquette and pace
- Move like a librarian, not like a tourist. Announce yourself by existing, not by noise.
- Wait when you encounter anything that seems organized: lines of polished stones, mats across seams, matched cairns with a plate of bone—these are boundaries and balances. Read before you cross.
- If you feel watched, you are being considered. Offer your profile, then your front, then lower your eyes and make yourself a simple object. When attention withdraws, proceed.

Contingencies
- If you feel lost and distances disagree:
  - Stop. Sit on your pack. Slow your breath for two minutes.
  - Take out your shadow card; check for double-edges. If doubled, you’re on a seam—backtrack ten of your own body-lengths at museum pace, lay torsion line, and choose the braid the line prefers.
  - If single, look for the nearest two matching cairns or a mat across a seam; align and walk as if along a taught thread, not toward an object.
  - Drop a single reflector under a lip; note it in your topological log. Retrieve on success.
- If a floater dims above you, treat it as “right-of-way”: stop until it lightens. Your shadow is part of the conversation and could cut something delicate if you move.
- If you break a crust or plate and it was clearly alive, pause. Place the fragment back in the exact orientation. Step back the way you came and reroute by wider pads.
- If injured: shade, seal, and wait. Bandage with oil-occlusive dressings to keep your own water yours. Call by light, not by sound.

Decontamination and leaving well
- On exit, oil-wipe and vacuum your gear. The living flour of cells will try to travel with you by patience alone. Do not take them.
- Rinse suit internals and filters away from other worlds. Salt and silica are stowaways you don’t want to seed elsewhere.
- Return any reflectors and retrieve any torsion lines you placed. Leave only the memory of your weight where others have already remembered theirs.

What not to do
- Do not test how wetness fails here with a spilled bottle; you’ll only lose the bottle and write an error across a grammar you don’t understand.
- Do not “correct” the quiet with music outdoors; your speakers will sing to you alone and teach you nothing.
- Do not assume radio works because light does. Plan for it to fail softly.
- Do not follow an animal’s path; their attention braids and timings are not yours.

If you bring a gift
- Bring patience. And if you must leave a token, leave intent rather than mass: re-stack a fallen stone exactly as its neighbor is stacked, make pairs of dissimilar slabs sing to one another under a cairn, or sit and keep the wind-measure company without touching it. The place remembers that sort of thing in its own long arithmetic.

One sentence to keep in your pocket
- Choose one pace, one shadow, one line—then keep them until the world agrees again.
