What you are walking into is a pocket of patient water thinking in stone, with a human lattice fitted to it like a careful graft. It is small, wet, loud, and complete. Your job is to arrive as lightly as a new droplet: take shape from the room, fall once, make no new channels.

How it will feel
- Temperature: cool enough to nip fingers and nose, not cold; the wet will creep into sleeves and seams. You won’t shiver at first. You will after a while if you linger.
- Humidity: total. Your eyelashes will bead, your hair will darken, your throat will slick. Nothing truly dries while you are there.
- Sound: a constant, built structure—roar made of drips, hoots, hums. Your voice will become architecture if you let it. It’s beautiful and disorienting. Expect to feel both held and slightly lost in it.
- Light: shy. Fungal fox-fire bowls sketch edges; mirror-smooth crystals carry a dimness into corners. Your eyes will adjust if you allow them. Bright light will feel like violence.
- Space: a rounded cistern room, ceiling low enough to make you tuck your neck, floor sloping to a shallow lens of water. There are narrow ledges, slick ribs, hung ropes, tiny bridges, tuned membranes, spoon-ladders. The city is everywhere the water is not.

What to bring (and what to leave behind)
- Helmet with a soft, low-profile cap lamp that can be dimmed to sub-lumen glow and shuttered. Fit a warm-tone or red filter. Bring two silent spares, all with soft switches or covers to prevent accidental glare. No flashing beacons.
- Thin, warm layers that work wet: merino or synthetic base, light fleece mid, a quiet, thin shell. No crackly fabrics. No cotton. Everything snug, nothing dangling.
- Hands and feet: thin neoprene or wool liner gloves under nitrile for grip and to keep your skin oils off the rock; soft, non-marking, sticky-soled shoes (approach or felted stream shoes) with thin neoprene socks. Avoid lug treads that gouge and hold grit.
- Knees and elbows: low-bulk pads with soft covers help you settle onto stone without adding percussion.
- Pack: the smallest you can manage, body-hugging, everything tethered. Wrap metal objects in cloth so they don’t click. Use dry-bags inside, but do not open desiccant packets while in the room.
- Quiet water and calories: a small bottle with a soft bite valve, a little electrolyte, something to eat that doesn’t crumb or crackle (soft bars, gels). You will not want to chew loudly.
- Waste kit: a sealable bag or bottle for urine and any other waste. Nothing stays behind. Do not baptize the city with your biology.
- Health: a small CO2/O2 monitor with a silent vibrate alarm. An inhaler if you use one. A few blister plasters. A chemical hand warmer for the exit, not for use inside. Musician’s earplugs (flat attenuation) in case the hum becomes too much; they let you hear shape without being drowned by it.
- Navigation/communication: a watch you can feel (haptic/tactile), a soft pencil and waterproof card for sketch-notes, and your hands. No beeping devices. No radios unless they can be muted fully.
- Camera: if you must, a sealed, silent camera with lens hood and anti-fog. No flash. Accept fogging as part of the visit.
- Leave behind: fragrances, sunscreen, bug spray, chalk, tape, dyes, drones, tripods, trekking poles, candles, cigarettes, any flame. Leave behind the urge to collect, label, or fix.

Before you go in
- Scrub your gear of outside soils. Wash with plain water, dry completely, then heat-bake soft goods at low temperature if you can (to shed spores), and cool sealed. Do not bring cave diseases to a room that has kept its own careful biota.
- Eat, hydrate, and empty your bladder. Pack your waste plan.
- Agree on hand squeezes and touch-language with your companion, or decide to go alone. In there, sound belongs to the room; you will do better mouth-to-ear when you must speak, and mostly by touch.
- Decide in advance not to touch anything that looks intentional: ropes, wheels, cords, veils, spoons, gutters, mirrors, shelves, jars, the board of slate, the string with knots. Every one of these is a decision someone already made.

How to arrive
- Pause at the threshold and listen until you can pick out at least three repeating voices of the room. This isn’t ceremony—it’s calibration. Your heartbeat and pace will slow to fit the meter. If your light feels pushy, dim it more.
- Offer only the smallest knock if you must announce. One soft touch near your knee on the wall is enough—let the room translate you. Avoid “messages.” You don’t know the code.

How to move
- Three points of contact. Slow feet. Test every step with weightless pressure first; the polish on calcite is treacherous, and wet ropes swell and tighten—they may feel different from one end to the other.
- Keep to the human paths if you can read them—regular ledges, cut steps, braided lines. Assume each was set to carry exactly one body at a time. Travel in single file, spacing a body-length per person. If alone, pretend you are already the second person and step where the first would have stepped.
- Never step in the aqueducts, on spoon-ladders, or under a steady leak. Those are contracts with water; your boot is not a party to them.
- If you need to rest, choose a rock bulge without tooling or glaze, tuck in, and make yourself small. Your body can be another baffle, not a new channel.
- If you slip into the pool: it is shallow, and panic is the only deep thing. Roll to hands and knees, shuffle out along the slope you know. Do not thrash; do not push wakes into the gutters.

How to speak and be spoken to
- Let your light be your first language: narrow, low, steady; tip it toward your chest when you look at a living thing or a bowl of glow.
- If you have to share words, do it cheek-to-cheek, one palm as a cup, lips close. The room will take any spare syllable and make it its own.
- You can count time with the visible moving cord or the turning clay wheel if they are in view. Don’t touch—let your eyes do the measuring.
- If you hear the tone of the room shift to urgency (hum deepening to throb, hoots climbing and crowding), leave. Rain above has announced itself below. This place likes constants; respect the rare variable.

What not to do (the lines that matter)
- No flame. A lighter, a match, a stove: each is a problem—not just for air, but for memory. There were fires here once and never again; let that remain true.
- Do not open sealed jars or bags, even if they call to your curiosity. Dryness is the room’s currency and banked for delicate work. Your fingers are not a good investment.
- Do not drink the pool or the drips. They taste like time and stone and will not love your gut. Bring your own water and keep it private.
- Do not rearrange anything. A spoon out of sequence, a membrane retied, a rope untwisted—all will ripple into the chorus and the hydrology.
- Do not write your name. The only durable text here belongs to work.
- Do not feed or catch the pale creatures. They are punctuation in a long sentence; don’t turn them into an exclamation.

If you meet someone
- Assume they live at the scale of maintenance. They may tend the singing veil, check a knot, or blot a drip. Make yourself briefly visible, then smaller. Show your empty hands. Nod, then look away to give them back the room.
- If they gesture, mirror once and wait. If they offer a rope or a ledge, step where invited and nowhere else. Do not break their concentration with questions: this place speaks through adjustments.
- A gift, if any, is knowledge carried away, not matter left behind. At most, outside the pocket, you might leave a small quiet spool of braided plant fiber at the approach with a note that it is unasked-for and can remain unclaimed.

What to notice (and how to let it mark you)
- How the city doesn’t fight water, it phrases it. How every curve is biased so a droplet chooses a seamless way. How ropes are braided to swell tighter. How the loudness makes privacy. How time here is beads and cords, not pages and clocks.
- Stand by the wheel that only proves time. Watch the moving cord that hauls nothing. Feel what it does to the part of you that needs progress to be a direction.
- Read the rules in the architecture: doors that are curtains for sound and wet; shelves with lips so no pool finds a seam; mirrors that carry dimness the way a river carries silt.
- If you think you understand it, stop and let the understanding soften. This place is not a solved problem; it is a long agreement.

Safety notes for your body
- Limit your stay. An hour or two is a good human shape for time here. After that, cold and carbon dioxide will take their quiet share.
- Watch your breath. If your CO2 monitor hums your wrist or your head feels woolly, go. There is no heroism in being the heaviest thing in a light room.
- Rinse your mouth with your own water if the metallic taste of stone gets strong; don’t spit.
- When you leave, change into dry socks and a dry layer quickly. Let your joints warm. The hum will stay in your ribcage for a while; that’s normal.

When you go
- Look once more at the places you stood. If you left a muddy half-print, don’t wash it—let the room reclaim it the way it reclaims everything.
- On the threshold, pause as you did on the way in. Listen until your heartbeat is yours again and the chorus is the room’s again. If you feel the urge to say thank you, do it with your breath, not your voice.

If you must carry a phrase back with you, let it be practical and small: Keep the drip slow. Let the rope swell. Give the noise its room.

That is the shape of your visit. Bead to bead.
