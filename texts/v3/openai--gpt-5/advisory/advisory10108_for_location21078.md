What this place asks of you
- Go slow, go light, go quiet. The plateau is not empty; it is busy with patient work. Everything that makes it beautiful is also easy to bruise.
- Think of it as a city of water-habits, not a hike. Addresses are found in relationships—between drips, lips, winds. You learn one neighborhood at a time.

Big picture: environment and hazards
- Altitude and exposure: You are above any normal timberline. Days can be bright and deceptively cool; nights are cold. UV is fierce. Air feels vivid and clean but thin; expect altitude effects.
- Perpetual damp: The stone is always just-below-wet. Even in sun, film and fog reappear with the plateau’s “breathing.” Surfaces range from satin-slick to sugar-rough.
- Living stone: Those lips, rims, and shelves are active mineral structures. Thin “drummy” floors can be hollow. New layers are measured in microns and years—your boot can erase months.
- Daily “tide”: Once each day, a higher terrace overtops and a slow wash moves through channels. Not a flash flood, but enough to change footing and routes.
- Microclimate: Morning midges; occasional fog that turns corridors into white rooms; frost film before sun; a quiet that lets small sounds carry unnervingly far.

Before you go: conditioning and practice
- Balance and footwork: Practice moving on wet tile or polished limestone. Learn to weight feet flat, test before commit, and step “over” edges, not “on” them.
- Listening: Train yourself to count intervals (10–60 seconds) by ear without looking at a watch. You’ll navigate partly by drip tempo.
- Altitude readiness: Spend a night or two at intermediate elevation if possible. If prone to altitude sickness, consult a clinician about acetazolamide and conservative ascent profiles.
- Biocontamination discipline: Decontaminate footwear and soft gear beforehand (clean, dry completely for 48+ hours; no residual bleach or detergent). This world’s biology is thin and specialized. Arrive and leave clean.

What to pack (keep it minimal and reversible)
Footwear
- Sticky rubber approach shoes or soft-soled boots with siped tread; no aggressive lugs or spikes on active stone. Ankle support matters.
- Neoprene socks or vapor-barrier socks plus thin liners for warm feet in perpetual damp.
- Lightweight gaiters to keep trickles out.

Clothing
- Sun-first layers: long-sleeve UPF shirt, brimmed hat, neck protection. Favor fabric over creams.
- Insulation that works wet: wool or high-loft synthetic midlayer; light puffy for night (synthetic preferred; if down, protect it well).
- A very breathable waterproof shell; pants that dry fast.
- Thin grippy gloves (for slick holds) plus warm liners for night.

Navigation and sensing
- Simple altimeter/barometer watch and a small analog compass (electronics can fail; GPS may not exist here).
- Waterproof field notebook, pencil, and a tiny stopwatch function (or count in your head) to record “addresses” by drip intervals and wind habits.
- Small hand lens to read textures (and to keep you curious rather than careless).
- Headlamp with red mode; spare battery. Night fog reflects light back at you—red helps.

Protection and movement aids
- Trekking poles with rubber tips (no carbide). One collapsible probe pole for testing hollow floors.
- Two or three foam stepping tiles (shoebox-lid size) to bridge a fragile lip without point-loading it. Pack them out again.
- A few long slings/cordelettes to handline short downclimbs by looping around robust, thick stone—not thin lips; never use hardware that bites.

Shelter and sleep
- Bivy sack or single-wall tent that handles condensation well; tiny tarp awning to deflect drips. No stakes in stone—use water bags/rocks as deadmen on dry, robust ground.
- Closed-cell foam pad (also doubles as stepping tile if needed).

Water and food
- Filter + chemical backup or UV pen. The water looks clean; assume unfamiliar microbes. Mineral load is high—good for taste, hard on filters—carry spare elements.
- Electrolytes with magnesium/potassium; hard water + sweat over days can cramp you.
- Pack-in, pack-out meals. No crumbs; fauna are scarce for a reason.

Health and first aid
- Altitude kit: acetazolamide if prescribed; ibuprofen; nausea relief.
- Hypothermia wrap, blister kit, elastic bandage, SAM splint, sterile dressings, waterproof tape.
- Sunglasses (category 3–4), lip/skin protection by fabric; avoid lotions that will wash onto active stone.
- Head net for midges. Avoid chemical repellents near water or amphibians.

Ethics and impacts
- Step only on bare, robust stone. Avoid moss, liverwort, and the soft crumbly tufa margins.
- Never step on a rim/lip. Step over it or bridge with a foam tile; your foot can undo years.
- No soaps, sunscreens in water, or any biocides. Urine and feces: pack out in sealed bags.
- No cairns, no scratch marks, no drilled anchors. Leave no markers but your notes.
- Keep noise low. Amphibians, birds, and the place’s own acoustics depend on quiet.

How to move once you’re there
- First 24 hours: don’t range. Sit with a single terrace and learn its clock—when fog forms, when the daily wash passes, how many seconds between two notable drips, which corridors hum with wind at what hour.
- Choose routes by texture and sound. Satin flowstone = slick; sugar-rough = good. Drummy hollow = avoid load. A low letterbox hum means a constriction ahead: expect condensation and slipperiness.
- Time your walking: mid-morning to mid-afternoon, after frost has gone and before evening breath condenses again. During/near the daily wash, stay high and still.
- Test before trust. Probe suspicious floors and undercut shelves. Edge your weight onto ribs, not membranes.
- Keep your center low, steps short, pauses frequent. Place, don’t plant, your poles.

Navigation without a map
- Read the joints: long straight gullies often line up to horizon; use them as meridians but don’t camp in them—they carry sound and water.
- Make a “flow address” for camp and key junctions (example: “Two-count drip, western breeze at third whisper, raven pass ~late morning”). Note altimeter reading and one or two unique wall lichens as visual anchors.
- At night, don’t travel. Fog becomes horizontal rivers that erase distance. Sit in the clean starlight and listen—this is when you learn most safely.

Campsite selection
- Find a dry, thick-floored alcove above obvious runnels, with a ceiling that doesn’t pearl continuously. Check for drip freckles on the floor; if it’s freckled, it drips at night.
- Avoid basins and their parapets; avoid anywhere that “drums” under your heel.
- Set your little tarp as a drip diverter, not a wall; channel stray water away, not off a lip.

Water etiquette and collection
- Collect from flowing films or beads mid-run, not from pool edges where deposition is active. Skim rather than scoop; never scrape biofilms.
- Filter away from active rims. Spill zero. Dry your filter before packing so you don’t drip on the move.

Weather rhythm to plan around
- Dawn: frost film and midges—beautiful, treacherous. Watch, don’t stride.
- Midday: safest footing; long lines of sight in the straighter corridors.
- Late afternoon: third whisper of wind often brings fog threads through arcades; expect fresh condensation and slicking.
- Daily wash: once per day, often predictable after a couple of cycles. Treat like tide: plan crossings for the slack that follows.

Wildlife manners
- You’ll meet voices more than bodies. If amphibians start their bell-choir, step back; oils from skin/clothing and your CO2 plume can matter.
- Birds: ravens/choughs will size you up; don’t feed. If a falcon passes, pause and let the place remember itself.

If you must use rope
- Only for human safety, and only soft contact: sling thick, time-hardened columns or deep, load-bearing chock points. Never wrap or load a lip, and never place metal that bites or expands. Remove everything you add.

Photography and electronics
- Weather-sealed camera or phone; keep near ambient temperature to avoid lens fogging when fog forms. Microfiber cloths; silica gel in a dry bag.
- Minimal drone use (ideally none). The washboard acoustics and wildlife make it a bad neighbor.

Group size and communication
- Two or three people at most. Bigger parties multiply damage and noise.
- Prearranged soft signals (hand, cloth flag, a single-note whistle used sparingly). Sound travels oddly: echoes in gullies, vanishes in fog.

Emergency thinking
- You’re on your own. Carry a compact beacon if your multiverse kit supports it, but plan as if no one hears you.
- Know two dry exits to higher, thicker ground from wherever you walk.
- A fall is the main risk: manage traction, don’t overreach, keep packs light.

What not to bring
- Spikes, crampons, aggressive lugs
- Soaps, shampoos, DEET, permethrin near water
- Chalk, paint, dye, flagging tape, cairn-building ambitions
- Stoves that leak, candles, any flame you might set down on soft tufa
- Curiosity that needs to touch everything. Look, listen, note.

A simple first itinerary (3 days)
- Day 0.5: Arrive at the rim. Clean boots. Enter one district only. Make a sit-spot. Learn the local tide and two drips’ tempos. Sleep high and dry.
- Day 1: Walk out-and-back radii from your sit-spot, timing your turns to return before the late-afternoon breath. Practice bridging a single lip with your foam tile only where unavoidable. Record a “map” as a sequence of flows and corners, not distances.
- Day 2: Link two districts via a straight gully you identified yesterday. After the wash passes, shift camp to a new alcove with a different wind note; compare rhythms. Leave nothing behind but drier air where you stood.
- Day 3: Exit along your known meridian. On the way out, notice what the place changed already—one brighter sawtooth, one migrated trickle. This is the measure of your lightness.

Leaving well
- Dry and brush gear before descending. Don’t carry this plateau’s spores and films into the next world, and don’t take that next world’s seeds back here.
- Leave a page in your notebook with the plateau’s “address” for yourself: a few drip counts, a wind’s hum, a smell of warm stone. That’ll get you back better than coordinates.

If you remember only five things
- Step over rims, never on them.
- Move in the middle of the day; sit at dawn and dusk.
- Learn a place by its intervals, not by its vistas.
- Keep everything you emit—noise, scent, chemicals, crumbs—small.
- Treat the whole as someone’s careful home. It is. The occupant is water.
