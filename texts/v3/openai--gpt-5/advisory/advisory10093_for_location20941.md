What you’re walking into
- A sealed, heat-breathing bowl cut from rock that never learned the word “wet.” The air is bone-dry and chemically busy (sulfur, chlorine, acidic volcanic gases). The heat rises and falls in pulses like a lung. Wind matters more than gravity for what lives here. Anything that expects water fails here; things that bend, hinge, and regrow from shards survive.
- Lushness without juice: glassy, salty, sooty plants that open and close with the drafts. A rim city of terraces and wind-shaped halls—clearly made, not accidental—built to present as little defiance to the air as possible.
- It is loud (organ-deep bellow, whistling vents, crystalline chimes), light is sparse and predictable, and emptiness is an active element. This place has its own strict rules. If you step into it on human terms, it will strip those terms away.

Absolute constraints for a human body
- You are a water animal. Here, exposed water flashes away; exposed tissues burn, desiccate, or acid-scorch. Your moisture, your breath, your tears—none of them can meet the air.
- The air chemistry is hostile: expect SO2, H2S, HCl/Cl2 in shifting mixes, plus abrasive, saline dust. Assume oxygen-poor. Assume high radiant and convective heat during surges.
- The wind will take anything that doesn’t give way. Your profile, your tools, your posture—everything must be low, smooth, and ready to hinge.

How to be present here at all
- Remain a sealed, self-contained weather system. Nothing about you should mix with this place—no vapor, no crumbs, no droplets.

Personal system (suit) that works here
- Shell and shape
  - Full positive-pressure suit, smooth and teardrop-profiled from the chest up so gusts slip rather than grab.
  - Outer materials: alumina or zirconia ceramic plates or enamelled scales over silica/basalt fiber cloth; PTFE/PFA fluoropolymer barrier film under the ceramic; perfluoroelastomer (FFKM) seals. Avoid plain metals on the exterior; chlorinated heat will eat them.
  - Face window: sapphire or thick fused silica. Anti-fog on the inside only.
  - All fasteners and zips recessed and overhung so wind can’t catch; no dangling straps.
- Respiratory and pressure
  - Closed-circuit rebreather entirely inside the suit volume. Oxygen supply plus redundant scrubber (soda lime or lithium hydroxide cartridges rated for high temp). Positive pressure prevents seep-in of acid gas if a pinhole opens.
  - No exhaust to ambient. Every molecule you exhale stays with you until scrubbed.
- Thermal management
  - Active liquid cooling undergarment on a closed loop to a phase-change cold plate. Choose paraffin-PCM or salt-free metallic PCM (e.g., gallium alloy) packs; bring swappable modules. Target 2–3 hours per pack at moderate activity.
  - Radiators, if any, must be shrouded; don’t let them condense and flash water on the outside surface.
  - Reflective inner lining to even radiant heat spikes; outer matte to avoid glare.
- Skin and hands
  - Inner comfort layer that wicks and keeps salt away from skin; silvered or bamboo-charcoal fibers for odor/microbial control.
  - Gloves: aramid knit with ceramic dotting and a fluoropolymer outer. High tactile penalty is better than a glass cut.
- Boots and ground contact
  - Ceramic or glass-ceramic soles, microtextured; anti-static grounding strip inside the suit electronics—not to the outside world.
  - Clip-on low, wide “pads” if you must step on powder-dune slopes to spread pressure and avoid avalanches.
- Senses and sound
  - Triple hearing protection inside the helmet (passive plugs + earmuffs + active cancellation) but with an exterior microphone and filtered mix so you can still “hear” the lung’s cycle safely.
  - Bone-conduction cue channel for cycle alerts and teammate haptics.
- Tethers and anchors
  - Basalt/silica braided tether lines dusted with ash to dull. Ceramic carabiners. Low-profile ceramic pitons or clamp anchors that bite into ledge edges without leaving much scar.
  - Keep one short “dog leash” to your partner and one long line to a fixed point.

Instruments and tools to bring (keep them quiet, cool, and simple)
- Gas and heat: multigas sensors rated for SO2, H2S, HCl/Cl2; fast-response thermocouples; an acoustic level sensor; a simple vane-and-flag wind indicator you can read with a glance. Electronics potted and halogen-rated; sapphire or fluoropolymer windows.
- Navigation: inertial step-counter, short-range UHF radios, and—most important—tether discipline. Laser rangefinder with sapphire window for judging distances in shimmering air.
- Light: a narrow, amber LED band under a visor lip, baffled so no beam spills into the chamber. Use it sparingly; additional heat and glare are rude here.
- Sampling: only for loose drift dust and pre-shed shards. Ceramic scoop, fluoropolymer vials with locking lids. No prying, no clipping from living structures or city fabric.
- Repair kit: high-temp ceramic tape, FFKM patch disks, clamp sleeves for hose breaches, and a little pouch of insulating dust (harmless local equivalent) to smother hot spots on your own gear if something overheats.
- Comfort and bio: internal hydration bladder, high-calorie gels (no crumbs), urine diverter. Anti-nausea, bronchodilator, analgesic. Calcium gluconate gel is a just-in-case if you later suspect fluoride exposure from another route; better to have than not.

Preparation before you go
- Bake-out and dry-down: purge your suit and outer gear in a warm, dry cabinet until humidity is near zero so you don’t step in trailing a cloud of unwanted vapor. Wipe all external surfaces with a dry, inert powder (sterile ash or talc) to kill sheen that could change radiance patterns.
- Heat rehearsal: wear the suit hot on purpose in safe conditions to learn your physiological margins and cooling-time math. Practice kneel-low-brace postures and one-handed self-anchoring.
- Silent comms: rehearse a lean sign language and tether-tug vocabulary; practice working through gusts when speech is impossible.
- Route reading: study the chamber’s “sundial” light path from other travelers’ logs, if any, and decide which terrace shadows you will borrow and when.

How to move and where to be
- Enter on a terrace, in shade if possible, and clip in before you even fully stand. Stay under eaves and along the concave walls; convex promontories catch gusts.
- Keep your profile low. Kneel or sit during surges; stand and move during lulls. Don’t stride across empty spans; move from brace to brace—pillars, lintels, terrace risers.
- Never block a vent mouth, hedge the mouth of a corridor, or hang things where the drafts accelerate. The city’s apertures are lungs; your body is a rag in that throat if you forget this.
- Walk as if everything is edged in glass (it is). Assume every glossy ribbon once ran melting-hot and could again. The green and bottle-brown flow-glass on walls are do-not-touch lanes.
- Plants are inhabitants. Halite hedges, silica tassels, gypsum fans, ember-moss—do not brush, lean, or breathe on them. If wind pushes you toward them, go slack and rotate your shoulder shell to glance off a structural surface instead.

Timing and listening
- The chamber breathes in minutes. Learn the cycle before you attempt distance:
  - Bellow rising: temperature ramps; plants unfurl; dust lifts. Kneel, face slightly into the wind, one hand on an anchor.
  - Keening minor vents: small rivers of faster air open—use these to move sideways if you must, like slipping into a back eddy.
  - Lull: listen for the space to thicken and the chimes to stop jangling—this is when you cross gaps and re-clip.
- Watch the blade of day on the high vault. When it passes over your intended route, wall radiance jumps. Shade is currency; spend it wisely.

Etiquette with the built city
- These terraces are careful choreography. Don’t retune them by accident:
  - Do not wedge doors or baffles; if you lower a shaggy panel to shelter yourself, rehang it exactly as you found it.
  - Don’t polish railings with your gloves. That dullness is intentional; shine changes heat.
  - Ladders of halite skeletons are museum-delicate. If you absolutely must use one, distribute weight by hugging the uprights and climbing low.
  - If you find a pit that hoards cold, don’t lean over it breathing warmth. Think of it as a pantry with the lid off.
- Keep your own lights down. If it glows here, it does so because heat told it to, not because you asked it.

What not to bring
- Anything wet, porous, or shedding: paper maps, wool, cotton, leaky hydration bladders, gels with foil you’ll tear and drop.
- Metals that chlorinate or corrode easily when warm: bare steel, aluminum, copper fittings, magnesium alloys.
- Open flames, hot tools, sparking gear. Go intrinsically safe with sealed batteries and well-insulated contacts.
- Drones with props—your rotors will add a weather the chamber didn’t choose. If you must use a camera, fix it on a tethered boom and let the wind decide.

Emergencies and decisions
- Heat overrun: you feel swimming, clumsy, and your cooling packs are nearing phase-out. Get into shadow, kneel, back to a concave wall. Swap PCM if your plan allows. If not, abort and follow your tether home. This place rewards patience, not bravery.
- Suit breach you can’t see but can smell or hear hissing: positive pressure buys seconds. Clamp a gloved palm over the suspect area, drop to a sheltered recess (behind a half-wall, under an eave), and apply ceramic tape and a patch disk. If your rebreather chemistry alarms, assume acid ingress: abort immediately.
- Knockdown by gust: don’t fight spin. Turtle—curl, protect your faceplate, and let the armor take it. Expect to slide on crystalline dust. Your tether should draw like a compass needle toward safety; let it.
- Gas spike: outside sensors scream SO2/HCl. Your helmet should already be sealed and scrubbers running. If they fail, there is no safe naked inhalation here—retreat.

Decontamination leaving
- In a shadowed recess on the terrace, do a dry knock-down: brush crystalline dust away with a dedicated, stiff, ceramic-bristle brush. No compressed gas (adds weather), no rinses here.
- Bag samples and any loose bits inside double-sealed fluoropolymer pouches. Every hitchhiking shard can grow on a new edge elsewhere—don’t spread this ecology to worlds that can’t host it or be hosted by it.
- Full bake-back and acid-gas neutralization of your gear outside this world. Assume your suit’s exterior is lightly salted with reactive sublimates.

A first visit, hour by hour (suggestion)
- Minutes 0–15
  - Arrive into shade on a mid-terrace. Clip in two points. Go still. Let your sensor feed and bones learn the cycle. Orient to the light blade high above.
- Minutes 15–40
  - Short traverses along the terrace rims. Notice which apertures turn the draft into corridors; feel how the staggered halls quiet the roar. Watch a halite hedge whiten as new faces birth in a single deep inhale.
- Minutes 40–60
  - Choose one pillar forest and stay with it across two full breaths. See the silica tassels shiver, hear their chime turn on and off with the keening vents. Catch the ember-moss brighten as night returns across the vault.
- Minutes 60–90
  - Approach the city’s infrastructural edges—baffles, racks that harvest crystal seed. Touch nothing. Watch how an opened panel changes the voice of a corridor. Listen for habitual clacks: that’s maintenance in a place without hands.
- Minutes 90–120
  - Return to your original anchor path while your PCM still has cold. One last look from a halite-beaded aperture: notice what’s imperceptibly new—a slip-face advanced, a gypsum fan turned a degree, a rope shifted. Hold the silence during a lull. Then go.

On the chance of meeting makers
- If someone lives here now, you’ll meet the arrangement before the hand. Don’t tidy, don’t “improve,” don’t weigh a curtain with your own knot. If a mat is weighted, it’s tuned for a wind you don’t yet understand. Leave tokens and notes elsewhere; nothing should flap, glitter, or smell wet.
- If you hear a deliberate, rhythmic sound against the wind (a repeated clack or a patterned hiss), that’s the local equivalent of signage. Stop. Wait through a breath. Let the place answer first.

Why go at all
- To learn a new set of rules your physiology never had to carry: life that is porous and patient, that eats gas and hoards shade, that measures day in degrees and minutes, not light. To stand in a town designed for the hot breath of stone and realize that “lush” never belonged only to water.

The short list to check before you step
- Suit sealed, pressure-tested, baked dry
- Cooling charged, time budgeted with a margin
- Gas sensors on, spare scrubber cartridge stowed
- Tethers coiled, anchors reachable, partner clipped
- Maps in your head, not on paper; lulls and surges felt in your bones
- Promise to yourself: if the place says “no” (and it will, in wind), you say “not today” and leave it intact

Go ready to be a quiet object among other quiet objects. This pocket breathes without you. If you can pass through it without leaving water, heat, or argument, it will let you see how it moves.
