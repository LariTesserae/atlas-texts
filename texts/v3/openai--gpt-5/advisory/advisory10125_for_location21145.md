What your body will notice first
- The light never really arrives. Your eyes keep waiting to adjust and they never quite do. Accept the dim cream of it; it’s how the place keeps the sharpness off of sound and heat.
- Heat presses from all sides and from below. Even when it rains hard, the heat doesn’t leave; it just changes voice.
- Everything is wet with intent. Not romantic wet—industrial wet. Films, skins, beads, weeps. Every horizontal will be slick; every vertical will be sweating or weeping into its footings.
- Odors announce the chemistry: steel and limestone, mineral salts, a sweetish “meat water” note near loading aprons, occasional sulfur. None of these are decorations. They warn, they describe, they date the last maintenance pass.

How to move here
- Slow is safe. Every hurried step is an experiment you don’t need to run.
- Test surfaces with a pole or the side of your boot. The gray-black and orange bacterial mats are beautiful and treacherous; treat them like polished ice.
- Assume any culvert, sump, or gate can wake without visible drama. If there is a siren or a rubbed-throat horn, give it your full attention. Backwash and level changes happen by design and on schedule that is not yours.
- Keep to the causeway crowns and painted paths, faded as they are. Catwalks are honest but not forgiving.
- When the wind presses a band of ripples across a pan, read it as a map of where the slicks are thin and where they are thick.

Permission, people, and pace
- This is someone’s farm, even if it farms water and residue. If you can, check in at a pump house. Wear your good manners where you’d wear a hard hat.
- The tenders walk like farmers: patient circuits, known afflictions. Don’t block their lines of sight, don’t step on their chalk notes, and never “tidy” a staged spare part.
- Gifts that make immediate sense here: graphite grease in small tins, composite-toe boot inserts, nitrile O-rings, sacrificial zinc buttons, a coil of good paracord, strong coffee. They won’t ask, but you’ll see them smile.
- Speak low; sound travels warm here, and work concentrates by listening.

What to wear and carry
- Clothing
  - Long, light, UPF-rated layers treated with permethrin. The dim light lies; the UV still bites.
  - Quick-dry synthetics; cotton will sour and chafe. Avoid leather; it rots and salts stiffen it.
  - A brimmed hat that tolerates drizzle and salt. Neck gaiter for insects.
  - Thin, breathable rain shell for the first-hard hour of a storm.
- Footwear
  - Amphibious work boots with aggressive non-slip soles and composite toes. Puncture-resistant midsoles matter (hidden wire, barnacles, rebar). Neoprene socks keep skin from macerating. Gaiters help keep the salt slurry out.
- Hands and eyes
  - Cut-resistant, nitrile-coated gloves for rails and ladders; lighter nitrile disposables for notes and samples. Traction-grip is worth more than padding here.
  - Anti-fog, splash-rated goggles. Sunglasses with polarization for reading surface films when the light lifts.
- Respiratory and gas awareness
  - A four-gas personal monitor (O2, H2S, CO, LEL). Clip it high on your chest; check it twice a day. If it’s angry, believe it.
  - Half-face respirator with P100/acid-gas cartridges for chlorinated areas or sudden sulfurous pockets. Fit matters; practice before you need it.
- Insects and bites
  - Picaridin or DEET, plus a head net you won’t mind wearing for hours. A small battery fan strapped to a shoulder strap creates a personal wind that insects hate.
  - Antihistamines, hydrocortisone, and a couple of epinephrine auto-injectors if you know you tip toward anaphylaxis.
- Heat discipline
  - Two liters of water per half-day minimum, plus electrolyte sachets. Over-sweating and under-salting make the world tilt.
  - A light, packable shade cloth or umbrella; there’s precious little shade that isn’t working.
- First aid and biohazards
  - Waterproof kit: iodine or chlorhexidine, saline rinse, wound-closure strips, broad-spectrum oral antibiotics for skin/soft tissue infections (watch for Vibrio after cuts in warm brackish water), oral rehydration salts.
  - Tetanus up to date. Hepatitis A and typhoid are wise. Consider leptospirosis risk: treat any fever after rat exposure seriously.
- Navigation and comms
  - Offline maps; the geometry repeats and the horizon is a rumor. A simple compass saves dignity. A whistle and a personal strobe light for murk or night.
  - VHF/handheld radio if permitted. Power banks in drybags; salt eats contacts—carry contact cleaners and silica gel.
- Tools
  - Collapsible trekking pole for probing slicks and depths.
  - Non-contact voltage tester. In a place that is always wet, assume faults.
  - Headlamp with red mode; bats and insects prefer you that way. Spare batteries in sealed bags.
- Recording and optics
  - Waterproof notebook and pencil. Equipment and moods here change in increments worth noting.
  - Camera in a sacrificial clear bag for acclimation; let lenses warm slowly to avoid blooming with condensation. Microfiber cloths get salty fast—rinse with distilled water.

Health cautions you’ll actually need
- Heat is the climate, not the weather. Work/rest in 45/15-minute cycles when the air stops moving. If your sweat stops, you stop.
- Mosquitoes and midges are a property of the air. Protect skin rather than plan to shoo them.
- Cuts escalate fast in this water. Treat immediately, keep covered, and don’t get brave. Watch for signs of infection within 24–48 hours; seek antibiotics early if red lines or fever appear.
- Hydrogen sulfide is the quiet danger. At higher concentrations you stop smelling it before it kills you. Trust your monitor, not your nose.
- Confined spaces are off-limits unless you live here and do the training. A still sump with a pretty sheen can be an anoxic trap.
- Rats and bats: no touching, no rescuing. Rabies pre-exposure vaccine earns its keep in places like this.

How to read the site’s rhythms
- Learn the horns and the hums. One low, drawn-out note presages a gate’s decision. A softening pump throb means larvae will rise behind the screens within minutes.
- Near the weirs, fish shift layer by layer with gate breath. If you want to see the city think, stand there—but give the bars a respectful distance.
- After rain: one loud hour, then a shameless, exquisite quiet. That’s the best time to smell the whole recipe of the place and watch bubble films give up their ghosts.
- Dragonflies mark where the air is slightly faster; swallows tell you where the gnat curtains are thickest. Follow them to see, not to step.

Where not to be
- On, under, or beside any gate or outfall when alarms sound. Even a six-inch level change can take your ankles out from under you and make your exit someone else’s problem.
- On the shadowed lower deck of the sluice assembly without a harness; damp steel, algae, and low light collude.
- Under power lines during the crackling hours of a storm. The high hum means both heat and charge are leaning on the catenaries.
- In the runouts of culverts at dusk. Everything that hides there by day comes out to eat and to be eaten, and you are neither.

If you must stay the night
- Sleep off the ground. A cot or a jungle hammock with an integrated net keeps teeth and legs off of you. A light underquilt helps against the clammy bite that arrives toward dawn.
- Sodium lamps don’t fight the dark; bring your own pools of light and place them low. Expect moth riots in the edges.
- Food goes in rat-proof containers. They’ve learned every cheap latch. Don’t cook near sumps; fumes do strange things at night.
- A small fan, even for an hour at a time, will restore your humanity.

Food and water
- Do not drink or treat local water; it is a ledger written in salts and process residues. Bring what you need and enough margin to share.
- Eat sealed and simple. Crumbs recruit ants, gulls, and then rats in neat succession. Clean as if every flake is a promise to a hundred small mouths.

Etiquette with the living
- Don’t feed anything. Here, the economy is balanced on leakage and schedule; handouts teach bad timing.
- If a heron lands near you, you are in the right mood. Stay that way.
- Bats own the under-lips of bridges. Your bright white headlamp breaks their day; keep it red and brief.
- If a rat sizes you up from a rail, it’s not a threat; it’s a colleague doing a line check. Let it pass.

Weather and water contingencies
- Tropical rain here is decisive. Get where you’re going before it starts. After, the world becomes a calculus of slickness and hidden edges.
- Storm surge or king tide? Head for the highest causeway crown, not the nearest building. Long and high beats close and low.
- Lightning and open metal: put the trekking pole down, step away from railings, crouch small on dry ground if you can find any.

Exiting with grace
- Rinse your gear with fresh water before the salts set their teeth. Pay special attention to zippers, buckles, and any hinge that felt gritty today.
- Check your body for new nicks. Clean again. Mark anything that looks angry. If fever arrives after a wet cut or a rat splash, seek antibiotics; don’t bargain.
- Catalog what the place taught you: what hummed wrong, what wept more than yesterday, what lived in the oil-dark and what counted the hours by taste. This is a city of repetition; noticing its small changes is how you belong here, even briefly.

Small joys worth planning for
- Sit beside a flume when the wind skins the water and watch bands of motion slide like muscles under translucent flesh. It will feel, for a minute, like reading the site’s own breathing.
- Catch the moment when the pumps throttle back and the larvae rise. The midges write with themselves in the air; the dragonflies edit.
- At night, listen for fish knocking at the undersides of scum. It sounds almost like asking permission. They get it more often than not.

A last rule, because the place repeats it softly until you hear it: water takes every chance, metal pays for it, and maintenance is a diet. If you prepare with that in mind—gear checked, steps deliberate, senses open—you will do fine here, and the city will regard you as an animal that knows how to cross without making a mess of the schedule.
