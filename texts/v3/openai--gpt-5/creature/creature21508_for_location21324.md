They are level-boned. Whatever lives here has learned to press itself to stone and take on its manners. They do not rise much, not even in their thoughts. They keep to surfaces, to edges, to the places where a skin becomes a seam and something can be passed along.

First, the film. The primary citizens are not made of parts you can name without a lens. They hold the color of old coins and dry moss, of a night that hasn’t quite remembered to darken. In the dim before sun they rouse, and if you lay your palm flat to the rock you can tell where they are awake: a warmth that is not heat but doing. Mats of cyanobacteria and algae, little republics of lichens and fungi, colonies inside colonies—these are the plateau’s slow factories. They make sugar and habit from thinness. They keep water where water would not keep itself by exuding glues and latticework gums that bind dust to bedrock, building a felted crosshatch that does not look like anything until it is everywhere. By day they low-crawl from shade to shade inside their own thickness. By night they become cliffs and basins to dew, each bead met and held and traded to a neighbor. They are older than this height; they remember oceans.

Because there is film, there are grazers light enough to nibble it without peeling it off in sheets. You can meet them at dawn if you have patience the size of a pin: spider-shouldered mites the color of fog ash; springtails that resist names because they do not spring, they wick; dust-colored velvet mites fat on a year’s one feast that is the week after real weather. Beetles are the craftsmen here. They are matte and unostentatious, a family of small dark ovals with backs that cheat condensation. Look close and you’ll see the microtopography: a dotted runnel down each elytron, wax textures that coax dew to travel in a narrow path from the sky to the mouth beneath. Their faces are always damp by design. When the stone sweats, they kneel to it.

Under the edges you get the ones that traded eyes for advice from the air. Blind isopods with whiskers that read humidity the way others read light, each hair a question: wetter? drier? They commute along scentless highways that only they can feel, from pan lip to crack shadow and back, eating what is soft and leaving what is idea. Snails here are chalk with a memory of rain. They do not plead when the dry months arrive. They close the door with a bit of self and wait, gluing themselves to shaded undersides until another night behaves itself and brings a mouthful. Their coiling is not romance, it is a mathematics of keeping moisture in a smaller room.

Reptiles come as the day lifts. You might not see them because they are attracted to the kind of light that makes edges. Flat geckoes with toe pads like suede ease out from under slabs, and they lean forward with tongues tasting the lattice left behind by passing water and salt—this is the record they read: where a beetle walked; where a pan lifted and breathed; where a drop gave itself away. They press their lower jaws to stone to listen. Snakes here are thin of appetite and long of patience. They align themselves with the seam between warmth and its absence and inch alongside the boundary for hours, moving when the line moves, until something smaller makes the same mistake twice.

There are birds, but not many, and they understand whose country it is. They do not bring voices with them. They cut the sky, drop, and let their wingbones do the work of landing. They step like hunters who have been told the floor is glass and the room is full of sleeping glassmakers. Thin-billed rock-martins glue cups of lichen and fiber into the mouths of tafoni, anchoring them with spit and a calculus of balance, raising two or three puffed silences a year. In the dry season they spend their days guttering dew off the tips of rosettes for themselves and their infants; when the wet is an ordeal rather than a gift they break camp and ride gravity to valleys that are not their language. At night a small bat scours the open spaces between spires and shrubs, not with voice but with touch so tight it could be a held breath. It flicks its ears forward and corrects in the air as if a seam in the dark had moved.

Predators do not announce. Even the spiders have unlearned string as music. They put their nets in the lanes of migration you cannot see, sheets that look like wind or heat until they settle around something that had intended to go one way and gets redirected instead. There is a mantis here, but it is not architectural—no spines like flags, no flags. It is all crease and lean and expectancy, the color of the little shadows in rock that are not shade but shift. A centipede long as your hand and thin as thought will rest half the day with each leg placed just so, every segment a question mark finally answered at dusk when the stone releases its last, tender heat and the couriers start up again from their shelters.

There is a bigger resident, and whether you call it an animal or a system depends on how far away you stand. Imagine a soft thing thick with care. Knee-high at most, broad as a welcome, with staved ribs under an oval drum of a body and toes widened into pads. Its coat is not hair but a low nap of tiny, backward-facing scales that drink fog and lead it by capillary habit to the skin folds that run to its mouth. It walks with its head lifted the way a listener does in a crowded room; its eyes are reluctant to be involved, set small and sideways, but if you could see the inside of its skull you’d find the spaces for hearing meet and complicated. The bones of its hands and feet are fused and shingled in places where other creatures keep gaps, so that when it drums the touch goes in a clean line from ground to brain and back again.

These padfoots—call them that for now, though their feet are half their thought—keep their teeth in files and live on the green-black velvet of the crust. They rasp, not with greed but with ritual. They do not take a large bite; they do not pull and lift. They skim a breadth of mat, stop, skim another breadth a body length away, stop, move again. The way they eat makes islands of film and lanes of bare rock that leach and pulse differently, a chessboard the crust responds to with giddy slowness: new growth on margins; spores set free from pressure and taking; a veining that helps dew choose its path. You can watch a padfoot’s pasture spread over seasons as a slightly richer green in checkered bands, and then you can look again three decades later and find that the checkerboard has become a map of water in the stone, and that the pans within that country are fractionally deeper and fractionally wider.

They do not build with subtraction. Their making is coaxing—bending shrubs into long, low vaults by resting their backs and bellies in the same hollows until branches give up and agree. They tug the resinous ends of those shrubs with prehensile lips and smear that stick where a ring of pebbles needs persuading to stay on the lip of a pan. They set stones by shuffling, not lifting, nudging them along with the sides of their feet until they lodge just so. They bring their young to polished depressions worn vanishingly slow by elders’ toes and play them the afternoon news.

Because that is what the drumming is: a daily edition in pulses. As sun declines, each family chooses its instrument—concavities made by generations in a patch of rock that takes a good tone—and tampers its pads in sand to take off shine. Then they tap. Slow, spaced thumps that carry into places where light no longer goes. It is never the same pattern two nights running, but it rhymes with itself across years. Between households there is an etiquette of intervals. This is how you tell your neighbors you found the pan to the northwest three fingers lower than last winter; that the ants at the southern crack are weaving again; that a cup nest in the second cave has two chicks and so you will not pass near for a while. They announce births and deaths, long losses that arrive all at once and those that come shrinking. Sometimes they just play for the fact of playing and you can feel it in the way the air tastes on your tongue, a friendliness that travels farther in stone than in sky.

They nurse, you should know that about them. Their litters are small and often out of step with rain, because their bodies heed the dew and its precision more than they heed a storm that might be theater. The babies ride tucked between belly and thigh where skin is thin and warmth an easy loan. When they are a third grown they begin to press their chins to the ground and catch the bedrock hum at dawn like everyone else. Old females inherit thickets and certain corners of rock the way other species pass down jewelry. Old males keep a cartography in their bones: distances between pans, lengths of time between first warmth and first lift of warmth, the angle the morning light must take for this gnamma to kindle and that one to sulk.

Ants are the quiet industrious other. There are several lineages here, but the masters of dew are a pale kind that have learned silk. They seal cracks with a film so slender it is modeled on absence. By the morning it is downright visible with beads like tiny eyes along its length; by late day it is gone again into the dark, the harvest given back in drips and mists to their fungus beds. They farm underground gardens of gray threads that eat what the ants will not and turn it into what the ants can bear to smell. Their tunnels breathe through the crack and through the lungs of the stone itself; you can find a beaten path somewhere to a resin shrub, and along that path will be a few bright sticks of sap abandoned by padfoots and dragged antwise to gum the next season’s film. The ants stack pebbles into rings around shallow pans because rings complicate breezes, and anything that slows a wind here is a finger on a scale.

Others take advantage of that ring-thinking: nonbiting midges that own the half-hour over pans at first dawn, an occasion of lifting and setting just above the meniscus where they never find out that they have wings. They are eaten by what will not fly to meet them, mouths on sticks rising from transoms of stone—amphipods? not quite; perhaps a larval something that lives on the arithmetic of drip. The midgelets are not here the rest of any day. At noon even their ghosts have other problems.

There are engineers who are not given credit because they do not look like it. A squatty dark tenebrionid takes to pacing the same circle around the same thigh-high rock every dry season, polishing it with its belly and its knees, peeing on it with urine mined from calcium in the crust and the shells of their dead. Every decade that circle has risen, just slightly, into a little skirt the color of late milk. After enough lifetimes, that skirt becomes a pedestal and the pedestal a chimney that pulls mist in the hour before dawn right down its throat. Drips gather at its foot like an answer. If the pedestal slumps, a padfoot will stop on its way to noon and give it one press with a belly, recalling it to itself. This is what I mean by additions and polish.

Everything here is present tense with rules. Sound is stored as pressure in bodies and bedrock. Eyes are for surgery, not for broadcasting. Muscles aside, the most developed organ in almost all of them is an ear that has snuck onto a foot or into a jaw. Birds bring pad-keen tarsi; snakes put their quadrate bones flat to the warm and count arrhythmias. One family of small grasshoppers keeps atrophied wings held like badges and does its calling by tapping a foreleg the size of a thorn against its own sternum and then against the ground, the two notes together making a beat a lizard two meters away can feel in its ribs.

Violence is efficient and ungloating, because this is a country where you do not burn off energy on show. Most deaths are single moves: a scorpion that had spent the day as a smaller irregularity under a larger one decides exactly when to be a machine; a gecko opens a mouth as narrow as a rule and closes it on a beetle’s head with the gravity of a cupboard door. The padfoots die either of old age in a nest that is all smell and love and remembered thumb rubs on their brows, or all at once under a dropped rock when a human passes through looking for the prize of something that he does not know he is holding even when he lifts it. But that is rare. Mostly there are no catastrophes here, only maintenance, slight errors, correction. Even the rare rain is folded in rather than fought with: ants plug galleries that would drown; the padfoots abandon drumming for three nights and go out into it like farmers into a hailstorm, delighted despite the damage.

If you lift a stone—and you should not—you would find a civic order: a stratum of mites arranged like organs around the undersurface, each at its preferred tightness; a clutch of snail eggs sealed with a varnish that will drink dew until a week arrives that can carry young mouths; a line of isopods queued nose to tail along a moisture gradient as fine as a tone curve; a gecko’s shed skin stuffed like lint into a crack both to slow a draft and to smell familiar. Even the arriving-and-going is measured. When you lower the stone (you should), wait. In a quarter hour you will see a few ants come and work its edge as if it had always been that way. A quarter of a day later, it will be as if you had never been.

Vegetation lives on the small seconds. Cushion plants make a weather of their own and hold it so close their leaves forget how to be leaves and become more like sponges taught to look botanical. The rosettes’ backward teeth do real work, combing the night’s film from the air like a groomer with a fine hand, sending it down a taper of stem to a pith that is more cistern than plant. The shrubs are the few honest reachers. They dress their leaves in hairs because hair catches breath; they tie their branches by incremental agreement to the wind because agreement makes less damage than denial. If you follow the arc of an old shrub and feel how it leans, you can reconstruct a hundred years’ worth of prevailing insistence. The padfoots’ spines have written in them too: each callsus, each polished place on a rib, is a map of that shrub’s memory.

And still, everything you can see with the day’s naked eye is less than what is there if you lower yourself and make a body part into a tool. Put your ear to a polished hollow and hold it there until the grit on your cheek begins to consent. Do you feel it? The chamber below brushing cool at you, as if the ground were exhaling without moving a grain. That is a brood nest of ants breathing; that is a lizard’s slow heart under a ledge; that is an undersurface that kept one extra drop and is willing to loan it to the air for a minute. The bedrock hum at dawn is the hour when everyone decides whether they are brave. At dusk the veins of warmth begin their crawl downslope and the plain resolves: a procession of dots walking tall because the sun makes them long; the midge like a nervous thought; a hurrying no one-cares that is a tenebrionid; two padfoots at their bowl with one youngster between them, drumming to say: here; alive; water remembered; let the news carry.

Time is the talent here. Muscles learn not to twitch for six hours. Skins do not flinch at the touch of grit. Lungs trade in the small coin of shallow breaths, so that two steps do not spend what you cannot get back. Bones take responsibility for the signal and give it back without asking what it meant. Life is not forceful, and so it works. It goes for depth. It listens. It adds. It polishes. It takes the honest sun and makes something to eat from it. It takes the thin air and braids it into silk that can catch a season if that is all a night will give. It takes the memory of rain and raises young on it.

If you stand very still midmorning at the lip of a pan with its ring of dew-catchers set like teeth, you can begin to assemble the town from shards: a beetle like a word that refuses to be plural; a small lizard making the old decision with its eyes; a whorl of lichen like a spent coin; the negative space where a padfoot was and will be again this evening when it comes to say in drummed syllables all of us here. The plateau will answer in its oldest language a long, low yes: not spoken, but felt through whatever part of you can still be pressed to stone. And under that yes, the ground and all those that keep to it will continue arranging liquids into their homes—not by flowing, but by remembering the direction home has always been.
