What lives here first is breath. The throats of the ground are the oldest citizens, each with its practiced note. They keep their hours by tide and moon you cannot see, tune themselves against the teeth of the shelf, and breathe through everything else that attempts a life. In their congregation there is fellowship—one voice buckles to make room for another, a cough loosens a hum, a hoarse bass shelters a high wheeze. The city is their stitchwork on the skin.

After them come the layers themselves, mineral behaving like a slow animal. It creeps. It grows where it is fed. It answers habit and tending. Every lip that leans a little further out, every fringe that fattens, is the record of acts: hung fiber, patient brushes of hand to keep a drip-channel clear, a trimmed scab to encourage a curve. Stone here is susceptible. It takes suggestion easily. It is shy of stopping. It has been taught to behave like home, and it has learned.

Between breath and growth, the people make a shape for themselves.

They are short from the demands of balance and storage of heat. Their thighs and calves are taut from stairs that never dry and panes of wet that never forget. Ankles are thick and quick—joints schooled in small corrections. The feet are the most formal part of the body: soles roughened in childhood and tended with pumice like instruments; toes trained to spread and grip on the smallest ledge as surely as on a flat. The people are always barefoot or in thin fungus-leather with ridged grips; anything thicker lies to the bone about the state of the ground.

Skin is oiled the way tools are. Fish-skin oil and myco-fat rubbed daily into forearms, calves, and faces to keep the wet from bleeding you of yourself. Salt leaves its pale frost in fringes along elbows and hairline and is wiped away and returns. Hair is bound close in braided nets to shed mist; the brows are taught to angle drops away from the eye. Eyes themselves are wide in the iris and narrow in the lid: not for distance so much as near clarity, reading texture and temperature in what is close. The palate learns the air. A certain iron-sweet in the breath says a vent is mixing heavier brine into the spray; a certain bitter says a whale has been taken apart within a street’s span.

They bear the place on their backs and wrists. Laces of grown crystal sewn onto fungus-felt mantles show a person’s patience in care—names displayed not in color but in the fineness of an edge coaxed to continue, the way a drip obeys fingers decade after decade. Raised scars along the forearm echo the strokes of their signed name. A band at the wrist holds a family’s tactile mark: a bit of shell carved in fat-thick relief, a loop of bone that hums a recognizable pitch when struck against a rail.

They do not keep their mouths idle, but they keep them private. Lips habitually pressed—not in severity, but in attention. The tongue is a tool: to taste the boards of a bridge for the tang that says new growth is slick; to test the dryness in a tinder-box; to press to the bead of a carving and check if its trough is true. Voice is for emergencies and for rooms sealed twice over where silence is kept at a cost. The language they live in is hand and shoulder and the shape of a wrist in the air. Fingers speak constantly. They touch a lot, but not idly; every hand on a shoulder is a sentence, every brush across the upper back a grammar. In company you read along the floor as well as the face. Rails and steps burble with informed knocks. There are jokes that only make sense through the soles of the feet.

Children learn the alphabet by running their palms along carved bold letters in the community galleries, where stories are kept at a height a child can reach. They learn poetry by tracing it with both hands and feeling the weight changes it describes, the way a phrase can bank and slide like a person crossing a slick rib. They learn their way home by heat and the partner notes of pairs of vents, the same way we learn a mother and father’s voices. They can tell, blind, the instant their street’s pitch-clock leans into the next hour.

Work is tempered by the long patience of the medium. Lacewrights hang frames in the spray like fishermen casting nets, then go and do other jobs while a wall decides to be a wall. Bafflers tend the throats and keep them healthy, shaving mineral whiskers from the mouths so breath passes clean, sliding bone plates to adjust tone as the season of swells deepens. Trimplers—the fine carvers—keep doors from closing themselves on the future and steps from flowering into accident. Dripwardens adjudicate rights to a rack of accreting shelves and assign turns for snipping a new lip back from creeping over a walkway. Right, here, is not ownership so much as responsibility. You hold what you tend. If you go away for a year, your stair belongs to the one who wiped it and remembered its awkwardness at the elbow.

Food is the neighborhood of the city. Crab-herds lean long-handled hooks against the uprush and tell the time by the behavior of a single obstinate male who always wants to climb too high before slack. Eel-keepers work blind; they joke that sight is unreliable because it believes what it sees. Mushroomers in the cooler quarters cradle rafts of bone under their arms, feeling with their thumbs the swell and give of healthy mat. They bring in rope and felt, the smell of soil in a place that knows no leaves. There are fat-workers who smell like sweetness overlaid on the fish-sour of oil; careful people who put flame where flame can’t run; people everyone thanks and no one wants to stand beside in a gust.

There is a craft in growing dryness. Wind-harvesters stretch skins and tight-fiber in the edges of the most blasted lanes, and the passing air leaves its meager gift on them, a gleaning of less-wet held in oiled boxes. The product of that patience is used sparingly—tucked into drums whose heads must speak clearly in the belly, in small chests where letters thin as fingernails are kept from swelling, in a room where someone wants to hear a voice.

Their instruments are mostly the city itself. The great vents are organs under the open sky. Kids will string a loop of intestine across a narrow mouth to make a reed speak a new phrase for a day until mineral eats it. Choirs of bone plates in the throat of the Seventh Mouth can be slid by hand to let the midnight call arrive an hour early for a festival; everyone will smile to feel the joke run through the stone. There are performers of the weight-dance who are famous over twenty neighborhoods: you sit in your house and feel on your bench a measured sequence of impacts propagating along the arches outside, odd and perfect, an argument you witness without ever seeing a foot.

Clothing grows where it can. Felt is layered from fungus and softened with eel-sweat; leather is a composite taught to remember curves. People keep their shoulders and wrists covered for the language that lives there and leave their calves bare for grip and cleaning. The old sometimes wear caps with small downward points over the ears, less to keep wet out than to focus the tones of familiar vents inward; a comfort in age is making your streets louder in your own head.

They keep animals with an ethic of lanes. Everyone has learned the paths. The clipped-wing shear gulls circle tight in their own galleries, like smoke taught to be square. The slick whiskered newt-things—house-otters, really—have their agreed routes between drains and eaves, and they will take a scrap from your hand only under the hands’ word for friend. The octopus in her clay pot is not a pet but a neighbor; her door is turned away from the market and toward the lessened light. Children are taught to sign at the lip of her pool, and she will answer, or not, in the stutter of her skin, each such conversation a minor civic occasion. At night the pale geckos appear like a thought you almost had earlier; they are flickers of punctuation, clearing the air of the constant tiny swarm.

They make their art in patience. They coax braids of mineral lace into cloaks for a festival and then cut them free to wear, stiff and fragile, for a while, until they become roof tiles. They hang ribbons of mycelial cloth in shrines and tend them as if they were bruises of the city that must be healed with care. They paint in fish-skin oil in troughs of letters and in finger-wide bands along ribs where it can gleam back even when sun is a milk-saucer guessing at itself through the mist. Their most admired hands belong to those who can feel a new piece of growth and predict its finished shape, then talk with it for a year to coax it there.

Law is the applied science of weight. There are rules the way there are best grips: not imposed, learned. Never step across a mouth; step around it. Test with a staff even when you think you remember. Hold to the low side. Lay your weight where it will teach the step to bear it, not where it will break. Teach children in pairs; a single child is a temptable thing. Keep the birds’ lanes clear; they pay for their streets in watchfulness. If your neighbor’s dripline is building her a door in the wrong place, tell her before you trim it; if she ignores you, trim one mouthful at noon when all hands are home and ready to feel the shape change before someone walks into it in the dark. Every vent has keepers, and every keeper can be called by a knock-you pattern you learned before your own name was finished.

There is ritual because patience requires a calendar, and the pitch-clock is not quite enough. The days of the year are kept in a wall of thin bowls hung in a hot throat and fed to brine one by one as each full-tide arrives; the sound they make when they split is the day’s birth-cry. A child is named beneath a throat that suits them. You bring them at dawn, when the world is whitest, and hold them so the breath of that mouth fills their lungs; later, always, they will hear that tone in a chord and turn. Marriage is a built silence: a room is sealed and swaddled in felt and oiled along the seams until the constant voice of the place becomes a distant wool. Inside, two people speak in mouth-voice to each other for as long as the drums outside can keep the wet at bay. Many never do it again. Death is conversion. The body is lime-washed and placed under a chosen drip, and over months and years it is persuaded into shelf or bench. Joints teach the shape of a curve; a sternum becomes a little lip; the names cut in the soft tufa are read forever by hand when you pass. You are not “laid to rest” here. You become useful.

They have stories. One says the shelf is the back of an animal so huge it forgot to roll, and we are the itch-scratchers taught to soothe it. Another says the first of them were cloud-fishers who threw nets into a fog and hauled up emptiness that weighed like a baby; they rocked it on the breath of the Fifth Throat and made it a city. There is an old belief that every vent owns its laugh and will trade it only at a funeral; sometimes in the mist a mouth that has kept to comfort will break on a new chuckle like a stone finally deciding to confess it is a stone, and everyone will smile because they know someone has gone from person to page.

They do sometimes rage. It is not an angry place, but it is strict. Now and then a swell climbs wrong, a mouth takes a person who stepped across it without respect, or a ropeway sings beyond its safe note and drops. Grief is loud where everything else has been kept feeling-soft. They will build three silences, one after another, for the first unquiet days. They will spread their fingers on the door of the heavy room as they go in. When they come out, they will tune the vents low and carry eels to the octopuses and give the newt-things more offal than they truly love. They make amends to the place and to each other by setting their weight where it will best teach.

The old know this landscape the way a long-married couple know each other’s breathing. They can wake from sleep because the south lane has gone an eighth of a note down, because a baffle slipped two fingers’ breadth, because the market’s edge has grown too fast and the lip where you lean to haggle will take you into the digest of a vent. The young test themselves against ropes across the canyon, against the disciplined leap from rib to rib while feeling, with their belly more than their feet, the percussive talk coming up from those who have crossed before them. Lovers run phrases along wrists in the dark and teach babies their names by rubbing them into their backs.

As for beyond—the horizon is not a promise here, just a place where the same kind of breath is happening to people you don’t know. There is some small trade in copper and wood from calendars with leaves: travelers who come blinking with voices too loud and diaphanous clothes that drink salt and die. The city gives them stiffened tufa, felt that will not rot, a drumhead that speaks through a damp night. Sometimes one of the leaf-languages stays long enough to learn to feel a sentence with their hands, long enough to know the comfort of the pitch-clock beginning to mean home. Sometimes one of the shelf-speakers leaves, goes inland, dries out. The story says those who go looking for the clean horizon never sleep well again; without the ground’s breathing they cannot teach their weight where to fall.

What lives here, then, are breath and habit, animals that trust their lanes, hands that have made a language out of touch, eyes that are happiest with the nearby, a civility shaped by drip and patience and the refusal of corners to stay put unless coaxed. What lives here has learned to take noise as prayer and wet as kin. They do not keep the world off; they build with it until it holds them back. They count their days in bowls they let break. They believe, not as a creed but as a posture, that the ground under them is growing another page while they sleep, and the right thing to do in the morning is read it with your feet.
