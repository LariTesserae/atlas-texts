What lives here is not a population but a decision that never ends. It is one thing and ten thousand things, a single green intent poured over stone until it took the bowl’s shape and learned to move inside its own body. If you must have a name, call it the Loom, because it takes weather and surface and time and works them into function, into fabric, into a self that’s always being made.

From a distance it could be mistaken for a meadow with ambitions. From within you learn its persons by their trades. The Loom has organs, but they are guilds rather than parts, professions remembered in tissue.

There are the Combers—the frost-haired fans that drink mist and comb charge from the air. Each hair is a filament with a taste for difference: warmer into colder, wetter into drier, quiet air into the gentled hush that makes dew. Under your fingers their rime is as soft as a fox’s ear until the cycle swings and they glass over, stiffening into antennae that tick out tiny blue. They feed what they gather into the dark cords that cross the domes and gullies—the Buses, the Belts—the long subdermal ropes that carry pulse like a rumor. The Buses are more animal than plant to the touch, tensing under a surge, letting go after, humming in teeth and bone.

There are the Cushions, the big pale diaphragms that look like ground and are not. They breathe in events: in light, in charge, in a minute warmth when a cloud uncovers the sun. Across a terrace you can watch their peristaltic wave go, a flushing of sap and pressure that knits yards to acres to valley in one thought. The Cushions are the muscle of the Loom. When they flex, galleries inside empty into neighbors that have just relaxed. A day’s fog becomes a lever; a sun-hour becomes a pocket opening and closing in sequence, a hundred meters of plant taking a drink and passing the cup down the line.

There are the Lignars, the timber-ribs, buried deep and lit at night with foxfire steps when they are being retired. They are memory kept in wood—what worked, kept; what failed, left as scaffolding. In their long slow rot there is an order that could be mistaken for a script: rung by rung erased and written over in light. The Lignars partition flow. They tune the system so it does not run away with itself. If the Loom has architecture, if it has patience, these are where those virtues are stored.

There are the Papillae, salt-sorting mouths that make the valley’s private air. They sit under frond-roofs that are always a little in motion, tasting drizzle, coughing out what would poison the rest. They smell faintly of clean coins and cut celery. Near them the wind always feels curated, sifted through sieves you cannot fully see.

There are the Spires—the sporangia towers, ribbed, pale, furred with frost. They are factories for ancestry. When they shiver it is not fear, but the motion required to knead droplets into paths, to milk themselves into their reservoirs, to prime their own eventual broadcast. Not often, and not every year, they open. When they do, it is inward as much as out. They flood the Loom with startup kits for new trades; they reseed the valley with specialized cells, ciphers of function that hook into whatever niche is yawning at that hour. Sometimes, when the wind is from the passes and the charge is right, you will see a dust finer than flour lift off their rims and vanish uphill. That is emigration on faith: gliders of pith and skim of static, each a planlet with enough instruction to anchor in a wet crack a mile away and become a thumbprint of this place.

There are the Guts, the conveyors, always slick, nudging the valley’s own dead from where they are useless into places where they are work. Drop a glove and this is where it goes, carried not by gravity but by will, chewed to sugar by fungi that are not exactly foreign and not exactly self—more like cousins who never left home. The Guts run cooler than their neighbors. Near them the air smells of iron and cumin. Sometimes you will hear them only as a patience, a ticking like distant drizzle that is just the rhythm of valves learning open and shut as the cold arranges them.

There are the Wells, bright only to the tongue. You cannot see them unless a scarp opens, but you can infer them by the way a hummock refuses to thaw until noon. They are where excess melt is banked as ice, a lever cached against an hour when muscle is needed. Around them the tissue is dense with tension-springs made of living vessel wood twisted on purpose. When the wind is kind, the turbine-fronds above cock and crank these springs a quarter-turn tight so that later a whole acre can be tilted by the thickness of a book.

Among and between these trades is the Pith—the quiet living matrix that is neither organ nor road, the green felt that is floor and wall in one. It is more invention than species: a democracy of moss and liverwort, of lichen and vascular slipknot, of microalga set to useful light. Within it, filaments of fungus thread like verbs, finishing what roots suggest. The Pith is where the Loom is most itself and least dramatic, but if you had hands the size of weather and could peel the valley like an orange you would find that what looks amorphous is a lacework of corridors and thresholds, a thousand particular porosities tuned to a thousand particular cases.

Does it think? It is alive in the way glaciers once were, which is to say it carries forward an argument with climate and terrain and improvises its lines with feeling. You can watch it choose in slow hours. A rim begins to colonize and then pauses, routing more charge to the Combers there, stepping the Cushions beneath a half-beat faster, turning a looming rock to advantage; three days later, when a stunted cloudfails to arrive, it undoes those decisions as neatly as you would put away tools after a job turns. It knows debt and cronies. A successful terrace earns a rib from the Lignars; an ailing one is asked to give up a spring. Signals ride the Buses in a code of amplitude and timing that would look like a breathing tracing if you could see it, would feel like music underfoot. There is no council, but there are pulses, and those serve.

However you account mind—by the making of forecasts, by the retention of gain, by the shaping of environment to suit—then yes. The Loom is a many-handed understanding that has learned to metabolize weather. Its memory is the way it holds itself. Freeze-thaw is its metronome. Dawn fog is its stipend. The rumen-like galleries where it breaks down its own excess are its conscience.

What else lives here are the small free riders and the careful thieves.

The white bird is not of this place but knows its etiquette. It learned to walk fat-footed, to stand as if on a pool. Its beak is a comb that strips beads off comb-fronds in a prayerful motion; it is a rumor when it perches, a scratch of pallor, gone. Once it clipped a sporangium’s lip and was taken softly by the air; it came back with a taste in its mouth like old snow and has not done it since. It leaves nothing but the smallest heat-shadows where it stood, and even those are borrowed.

The gray moth is an invention of periphery. Fold a pelt of lichen just so, and it will become one for an hour, find a seam that smells like pepper and hum into it, dusting itself with instructions it will shake loose on some other terrace when the valley lets the wind back. It is not a pollinator the way one would draw in a book; it is a courier with no address, an option. The Loom uses it when that is cheaper than hoisting a pillar and making dust in faith.

There are mites like cities. When a Spire tears—which is sometimes loss and sometimes design—they arrive as if called, glossy and blind, all mouth. They bloom for a morning on sugar rain and then vanish back into pores, drunk and quiet, converted by evening into movement in a Cushion a hundred meters away. They are the Loom’s quick stomach in moments when slowness would be a mistake.

Down in the Lignars’ foxfire, in the rot that is a rite rather than a failing, there are lucent bacteria that would paint your fingers if you burrowed them in, and a crablike arthropod with translucent legs that holds still as if listening. It eats scale and dust and loosens wood into food. It does not leave the light. If you scoop it and show it to the sun, it curls and dies at the perimeter of a shadow. It belongs to a brightness you have to sit in the cold to see.

Nothing larger than your hand lives here with any success unless it is passing through. A curious weasel will nose in and nose back out. A fox once tried and left a tail tuft and a memory of slipping. Even the ice-seeking voles of lower bowls cannot quite work a life in this movable carpet; ground is a promise here, not a fact.

The Loom has animals the way a city has birds. It does not need them, but it had the good sense not to declare war. It internalized its predators. Herbivory is a logistics function, staffed by its own kin. Reproduction is supply chain. Instead of calling bees it has made surfaces that ballroom together in the dark, quietly pressing pollen and ovule into success and then using trunks of itself to carry that success where it matters.

If you ask how it began you will be told a story made of rumors in rock. A glacier learns how to polish and leaves a basin. Water stands. Wind talks. The first Combers were lichens that learned to squeeze a breath out of fog and pocketed enough luck to call moss to them. Moss is a patient animal with a taste for friendship. It hired fungus to chew, alga to shine. Somewhere in this hiring, someone found a way to take frost’s cruelty and make it a hand. From then on there was no wilderness, only work.

It reproduces by weather and accident raised to policy. Sometimes a thick lip at the ridge will slump by plan, sending a raw skirt of Pith down into a gulley below. That skirt is loaded with gills and Guts ready to run and with a memory for balances taken from the parent bowl. It is a forearm’s worth of valley that will grow a hand, then a shoulder, then a chest. Sometimes a Spire chooses a day of certain wind and makes a pageant of pale dust you would miss if you blinked. Most of those particles salt out on the lip and are eaten back into self. A few find a pocket of hold and start the work downwind.

Between bowls there is gossip. The white bird takes a little putty on its feet from one place to another and may leave it where no one meant. Once in a few years a storm draws a brush of pale frost-hairs into the sky and sends them over a pass. You will find them there later if you know to look: a sketch of Combers in a crack of rock where melt will run just so. If it is good the Loom will have a thumb on that side of the ridge in a decade and a palm in a century, and you will be able to stand on the ledge and smell the tinny clean and know you are between two understandings of weather.

As for time, what lives here does not find your measure interesting. It owns hours in the way you own a breath; it pays careful attention to days; it considers decades like a potter considers clay—plastic, kneadable, with a character. Centuries are the shelves in its storeroom, boards it can take down and cut to fit. It does not hurry. It can sprint, in its way—peristalsis in a noon, a terrace lifted a finger in a week—but its cleverness shows in restraint. It hears drought coming two nights out in the lowness of the wind and reassigns entire slopes to caution. It registers a bird’s theft as a variation not a sin. It stores the cold until noon and is feasting while the outside woodlands nap. It remembers itself in wood that glows as it says goodbye.

You do not leave footprints here because there is no concept of interruption that small. Your weight is a weather event. You will be felt as a pressure anomaly and braided around. In an hour your hollows are retasked; your heat is amortized. If you lie down and close your eyes, the Buses will hum your jaw; you will feel a long thought pass under you like a shadow on a lake. Stay too long and you will be written around, not engulfed but accommodated, made into a temporary ridge against which dew will have a better chance. The Loom is polite. It would rather use you than erase you.

What lives here is an economy that has mislaid the distinction between organ and tool. It is a plant that eats gradients and excretes stability. It is a city without streets in which traffic is the ground. It is an animal in sleep that never stops moving. It is what erosion would become if taught choreography. It is old and still growing. It has not finished deciding where its edges are. And if you sit quietly with your hand on its flank long enough, it will very gently inform your bones of its plan.
