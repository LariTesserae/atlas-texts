They are small and soft and certain of edges the way you are certain of breath. Not a species in the way you want to pin them, but a center—a narrow nation of one kind—and then its tolerated, negotiated periphery: the punctual springtails, the omnipresent mites, the passing thunder of a centipede. The city’s people are the ones who make a habit into a structure. Call them the Threaders until they earn another name from you. They never asked for one.

A Threader looks like nothing much in air. In air they would collapse. In their weather they are exact.

Body first: pale ivory, neither white nor clear but the color of a grubbed root’s heart. Their skin is cuticle, thin as fiction, laminated with films that sort water from air the way a sieve sorts grit from grain. Under a lens the cuticle shows two maps: islands of fur that throw off water like geese and lanes of naked hydrophile that drink a drop thin and hold to it. Along those lanes are setae—long, articulate hairs with their own hairs—each a fingertip and an ear. Six legs, because that is a workable number; the fore pair trimmed with combs and pads that can pinch, the middle pairs broad for standing on something that would rather bead than bear, the rear pair with hooks to set into woven sedge or an obliging hypha. The head is all reach: antennae longer than the body, each segment studded with organs that test temperature, taste tannin, hear distance through pressure. Where you would put a mouth they have—well, a mouth, but not for talking: two blunt, projecting pick-axes of chitin that can shave bark, nip hypha, worry a resin bead into compliance. Between them soft parts do the tasting and the silkiness: spit not silk, but a glue that sets in damp enough to be law.

Eyes? They keep them small. Facets are there, black as seeds, but they live in a place where sight is only a rumor carried by water. Their pupils—if you want to be generous and lend them yours—are on their antennae. They read by touch and by strain.

They carry in, each of them, a slung problem of cold. Their blood is syrup laced with alcohol; their hearts are not hearts but peristaltic decisions passing down a tube. When the pocket glasses over, the decisions slow; the syrup thickens with an order it learned before any of them were born. The ones who tend the freeze hum through their legs then, their bodies pressed long to the meniscus-ladders, singing into tension what the colony needs to remember about not breaking.

They make no sound on purpose that sails in air. Sound here is for roots. They bind a grass-straw to the main root where it fattens out of the peat, and they thrum. It is conversation by percussion and pressure, hands replaced by chitin edges, language by the spacing of vibrations. A slow pulse: the drip has slowed in the west cistern. A tight trill: move the shutter in nursery two. A double-beat and a pause: freeze. Three beats in a row are forbidden for reasons taught like religion: the red lightning learned once and came.

They build as if building were a reflex like scratching. Fungus threads are coaxed into place with a mixture of praise and food; the Threaders carry dung as if it were bread. They lay a hair of moss here, a glint of hypha there, bridging distances you would refuse to step over in a lit room. Resin is teased from spruce wounds, suborned with sweet sap until it behaves. They do not spin their own long silk, so they barter and rob: a spider’s molted thread becomes a cable; a spider’s current thread becomes—if they are quick and if the spider is out on business—a stolen kindness looped back on itself to make a hinge. They train surface tension like you would train a dog. The ladder that gives when you push and holds when you pull is a fence of hydrophile and hydrophobe pricks along a plane of water: each knot set with a grain of silt, a smear of crabgrass wax, a dusting of diatom shells polished by the haft of a vole-whisker until they recruit the meniscus to purpose. A step goes in with the tack of a fly’s foot; a step comes out with the certainty of rope.

They are not tall. There is no height in them to speak of. Their stature is patience, their reach is delegated: to the web they place, to the drip they assign, to the root they indent with minuscule, ceremonial pressure. They have hands only by metaphor and by multiplication: a crew of two becomes a hand; a crew of six becomes two. When a platform must be moved because the root surged a finger and would crush it, they unhook and hook with the same breath. One lifts the old knot with a palpal comb; one holds the slack in their rear hooks like a jaw clenched in a fight; one is already drumming to a neighbor five drops down: prepare to hold.

There are castes if you insist on seeing them, but they are not castes like wasps or ants make. They are learned and unlearned roles, and they move between them like water moves between shapes. Some carry the name of their work for a season and then lay it down like a skin. There are Drip-tenders: small, very small, with an extra fringe of hydrophilic hair along their thighs that will wick a bead into steadiness with one intimate press. There are Threaders in the strict sense: old for this place, scarred along the mandibles from too many years shaving spruce and gnawing roots without ever boring them. There are Blue-keepers, who cultivate the gnat larvae—they would never call it cultivate, but they choose the tusks of water where the larvae will string, and they defend that light with a fervor that just misses being prayer. There are Root-readers, who put one antenna and then the other to the main, then to the side-cords of mycelium, then to the stone itself, and they make maps no one sees, just acts on. There are Freeze-singers; there are Flooders, who when the weeping becomes a running and all menisci are tripping into sheets, take down the gates and open the sluices in the only order that keeps the whole plaza from turning into a rip. There are Scribers and their apprentices: the ones with antennae thinnest at their tips and the most patience for mistakes, who punch instructions into leaf-cuticle and know by breath and by damp where a promise will warp into a pun.

They do not write names. You cannot write names in a medium that swells with humidity and shrivels with drought and call them the same name twice. They write allotments and forbiddances. They score the law’s edges: not into the root; not through hypha thicker than a hair; no free droplet broken except where the gates are. Where you would keep a litany they keep a pattern of holes no wider than a mite’s shoulder. They read them by sweeping their fore legs along them like blind hands along a fence. In a thaw the text relents and grows generous; in a dry spell it tightens. Grammar is weather. There is a joy in that for some. For others it is only the daily problem of living in a language that cannot hold still.

Do they have families? If by family you mean a cohort that refuses to drown together, yes. They lay eggs in beads. Eggs are not beads but they become them: a clutch smeared into a lens and pinned by tethers of scraped fiber to a hyphal braid over a place where the drip is patient. The bead gives warmth and gives window. The window gives threat and gives beauty. Between these gifts the ootheca hangs and breathes, and the adults pass and lay a foot to it and leave a bit of spit that feeds the film and the fungus, and in a week you can see the eggs as a constellation inside the drop, little crescent moons hammered thin by growth. They hatch sideways, into a truth that is all edge and touch. The city makes a fuss but not a noise. A Blue-keeper loops a light over the nest and the nest is a lantern until the larvae eat their way loose and drop into the moss like another rain.

They eat what wet gives them: the sugars riding the phloem and the starch hammered into the old bark, the bacterial blooms that fatten where lens meets leaf, the gentle mosses in corners where it is allowed. They lick at dead summers with reverence: old sedge, old fern, the corpses of gnats who fell on their own bridges of surface tension and could not recover their faith in it. A beetle’s discarded wing-case is a harvest; it becomes a shovel; it becomes a roof-tile; when it finally softens it becomes a meal. They nibble threadbare at liverwort edges, and there is a whole code of fines and mitigations for when a liverwort complains and proves it by pulling its lobe back faster than it did yesterday.

Danger is constant but never theatrical. The harvestman is a walking precipice. The city’s defense against it is small and cumulative: nothing to catch a long leg except lines under tension everywhere, latticed with their trained water, so that when a leg slips the harvestman removes itself with the offended care of a nobleman whose sleeve has been brushed. The planarian is more a philosophical problem: an idea that tastes like meat. They deal with planarians by unfairness: resin beads dropped in cunning sequence that pull flat bodies down with the strength of simple physics. Mites are a riot one day, a workforce the next. The centipede is not enemy so much as weather with a direction; it can be bribed with the rumor of food drummed three platforms inland, but never more than twice: a third time, they know, teaches. The salamander’s snout is a godhead of thirst that they do not petition; they open the shutters and close the shutters and lie still and will the beads to stay round on the scooped roofs, and afterward the salamander-scent is everywhere and all work is done with that musk in the nose.

Trade is a flare, as it was said. Trade is a mat out, three beads wide, at the edge of a plaza the size of your thumbnail. Trade is a pellet of silt, because the silt came from a river’s laughter and must contain a strength no local mineral knows. Trade is phloem peeled with the whisker-gouge into a slice that still smiles sugar. Trade is light: a strand of glow-worm larvae’s threads stretched across a cradle, given and then retrieved when the larvae drop and pupate and dim. Children’s eyes—if you will let them have eyes—hold that green for an hour after, and they will belong to the Blue-keepers when they grow if they keep lifting their antennae toward that fading like grass does toward a ceiling gap where sun once passed.

They go out from the crack only under compulsion or because some lines of Threaders have the pheromonal itch for it. Those are the Foragers, and they are only sometimes allowed to make more Foragers. They learn outside light the way you learned to swim: by being thrown into it. They travel pressed inside the skins of droplets that roll along the underside of fallen leaves, great carriages swollen into their shape by gravity. They return with grit, with a rolled flake of diatom glass clenched in a mouth that will be cut for a day after. They locate spruce-bruises not by sight but by the sweetness leached through soil, and they milk pitch with a patience only a nation can summon: three Threaders to a bead, one to pull, one to thin with spit and sap, one to sing to the root that the stress is temporary, do not answer with growth that will crack us all.

They remember without long lives. Some die when the crack freezes too fast and the nets snap and the laws of glass enforce a new geometry no one wrote. Some die because the meniscus failed under their feet where the hydrophobe had worn off. Some die because a harvestman pressed one long toe down out of curiosity, not malice, to see if the plaza moss would bear. They do not build monuments except the archive of stone. They keep plates of shale and flake and chert in a tinderbox of moss on a high ledge and take them out one by one to teach the young: this fault means the roof will let go; this sheen means the drip will run this way and not that next season; this pitting shows where the salamander’s breath has etched history. They stroke each plate along its face with antennal tips so fine they can count, in the dark, the grains of a sand that made a stone.

They have festivals, but they are not roasted-meat festivals; they are not noise festivals. A still bead left holy for a week is visited and not touched, and the one who refrains from touching longest in a city that must touch to live is fed first from the new phloem. They sing to the hyphae when the hyphae refuse to grow where the city needs them to. The song is touch, of course: a patterned stroking with setae. The Blue Freeze—once, a glow that did not dim through a winter—becomes a blessing conferred on a cradle built under the remembered place; the blessing may be only that people step lighter near it and predators find those steps suspicious. Still, everyone breathes rounder there for a season. There is a leaf-slit that opened on a noon when a boot came down and made a bright blade in the dark; there is nothing they can erect to avoid randomness except skill, so they remember that day with the practice of not working under drips.

They do not think of themselves as exceptional. They are only as much of the pocket as the rotifers are, as the tardigrades are, as the liverwort is. But other beings pass and do not leave laws behind. The Threaders pass and leave thread. That is difference enough for a charter.

One of them, you can follow for a night measured in fifty drops. She is old by their counting: three freezes, one blue. Her foreleg hairs are abraded into brushes. She wakes before the others pretend to sleep and presses her thorax to the main root. The moan in it says thaw that pretends to be more; she sends a two-pulse word down the straw and hears back a trill from a sleep-keeper: the shutters on nursery three have sagged. She takes the whisker—her whisker, plucked from a vole’s mouth the year the vole died two hands away—and sets its glassy sweep into the chert flake so that the chert becomes a mouth that loves only bark. She is careful to brace with her rear hooks not into living root but into the trained web that rides along it, because law has trained her as much as she trained the hyphae. She lifts phloem in a curl the size of a comma. The curl smells like bread. She lays it aside to dry on a tile of beetle-wing and goes to check the bead that is her family; three eggs stipple it like stars, and a fungus thread passes across it, and she touches the thread on the far side of the bead so that the bead will not break and the thread will not bruise her children. She moves. Her world is two blades of moss long.

Later she will cross the ladder whose rungs are not there until she arrives. She will push—give; she will pull—hold. She will find a drip thickened to indecision over the snail-shell cistern and talk it into falling with a lick of sap on her mouth. She will say the word for freeze again because the root hum deepens, and all along the city other forelegs will lay themselves along other cords and send that word on. She will pass under the harvestman’s silent church of legs and not die. She will drag a sliver of shale up to the archive and set it there, smooth side out, a warning etched into mineral: do not sleep under that shine. She will stop beside a blue light and practice not touching it because her antennae ache to. She will sit, if sitting is what you can call a pause in a system like this, and comb her front legs through her mouth until all the grit that should not hold water is removed from the parts of her that must.

When the boot-light comes—once a season, never twice the same—her body will be a shadow inside a lens of water that is itself a window within a weather within a crack in a marsh that has forgotten that it once wanted to be a lake. She will not look up. Up is for those who need a moral. She will think only: do not be under a drip when luck arrives.

They will be there, when the crack rounds its edges and the lichen finally overgrows the leaf-cuticle screens and the liverwort tiles become velvet instead of scale. They will be there when the spruce that feeds them dies and its pitch dries and they must learn a new varnish from somebody else’s hurt. They will be there until the root masses up with a slow, royal shove and denies them their night. Then they will be somewhere else. Or nowhere you can name. They are the set of behaviors that makes edges into addresses. They are what you get when you give water intention and give intention a place to stand, or to tremble without falling in.
