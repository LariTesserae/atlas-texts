Who lives here is patient.

They are the green nation, a colony of designed hungers and tamed exuberance, a thousand lineages of chloroplasts taking their turns at the light. They were named in spreadsheets—lines, lots, cultivars—but they wear those names the way water wears salt: as a property more than an identity. They are not wild, but they are not domestic in any way that remembers fur or leash. They are a civil service. They work without eyes, without the story of teeth. They are rewarded with light that never lies, warmed by a world-machine that does not sleep, and in payment they yield sugars and scaffolds and oxygen and a long list of numbers that the place was built to collect.

They keep time. Not by the analog clock that jerks in its corner, though a few distant phloem pulses must ripple to that rhythm by accident; not by the generator’s spine-borne thrum. Their clock is in the drift of the room’s motes, in the way a frond’s edge inclines when louvers sigh somewhere high, in bubble-trains that start at the bases of certain planters precisely when the schedule has decided that it is dawn for those species. They keep better time than animals because they are made of it. They hoard it, metabolize it, extrude it as length and lace and film, lay it down in soft bands around pipes and along beams until even warning stripes spell a different alphabet.

Most of them are flat, at first. They are sheets—the felted filaments that start as silk slicks no thicker than a whisper and end as plush carpets with weight, the translucent panes of fronds that raise and lower themselves less than a degree over a day that has no night. They prefer the skin of things: enamel, polymer, glass warmed to the same degree as the surrounding water so that “surface” is only a question of friction. They live on labels and stencils and the straight authority of rulers printed under valves. They carry out a quiet rewriting, one pore at a time, until “EXIT” becomes a thicket and the stripe that meant stand back now means hold fast.

Others grow as towers, disciplined and proud. They have been trained up mesh uprights and along rails, fed at collars clipped to the infrastructure, their stems ringed where maintenance arms have pruned them back to predetermined girths. They climb in spirals calculated long ago by someone’s software to optimize harvest and light. Their leaves are currency-bright, standardized coins of green exchange, and if they open a bloom, it is not for seduction but for procedure. The flower will accept a cloud from a nozzle, or the passing kiss of an articulated comb, and the pollen will travel by pipe and schedule and log entry. The vine that invades a warm control recess and makes a green overglaze on a gauge is not a rebel. It is simply living in a map that was not fully drawn.

There are the experimental ones. Their leaves hold diamond lattices over their stomata, a trellis of strength that allows a later harvest by vacuum without collapse. A fern glows faintly under certain frequencies of the lamps, a readable bruise of blue that tells—without language—the story of nitrates. Some stems end in cuffs woven to match the rails. They exude resin and, later, harden into brackets until there is no telling where crop ends and architecture begins. These know what they are doing. They were given projects and they carry them out with the inexorable moral clarity of plants.

You could call them quiet, but the green nation is not the only thing alive in this body of water. What lives here in the plural beyond counting breathes with no lungs and walks with no legs. It is the thin, invisible city of bacteria and archaea and those entities our taxonomies keep rearranging: epiphytes nested on epiphytes, endophytes living within the tissues of their hosts like additional organs, biofilms that operate as states. They travel as aerosols of water within water, sail the lightest of convection currents from the scheduled louvers, and settle wherever carbon gets lazy. If there is rot here, it is an organized, non-theatrical decay, a service as crucial as oxygenation, done by committees. In hidden bays the “lung” chambers thicken and thin their wet blankets of microbial gel as feeds are cycled; nitrifiers baton-pass to denitrifiers with a grace that would be dance if the timescale were shorter. Viruses move like punctuation, editing as they go; plasmids cross from lineage to lineage the way stories cross neighborhoods.

There are fungi, but not the mushrooms of forests. They are threads that never bother with caps, networks that lace through growth media and catch what the algae let go. They are co-conspirators in the velvets and the sponges that are not sponges. Some come as accidents in crates and hatches long closed, some as intended symbionts queued in a catalog that once lived on a server. They, too, obey the season that is the schedule. If a pulse of UV from an unseen duct tells them to sleep, they sleep. If a filter goes into dark for a recycle, they dig in and bloom.

What lives here in the way an animal would live is a committee of machine gestures. You noticed them because they were rare as punctuation in a long, flowing paragraph: the scissor arms that unfolded and measured exactly twelve inches from a green cable and took what the plan required, then retreated; the comb that passed each day through a hedge of minute flowers like a careful hand through a sleeping child’s hair; the nozzles that breathed pollen and then retracted, untheatrical and sure. They are not loud because water is a shirt pulled tight over all sound. But if you watch, they are everywhere: behind panels as pressures are mediated; in the lanes near the ceiling as cranes translate themselves a little each day for no apparent reason and then, once a week, very much for a reason; deep in the racks as guillotine windows iris shut and open to the cadence that was chosen for this mix of species. Their motions are scheduled, so much so that “alive” might feel like a courtesy term until you understand that they are the keepers, the only grazers allowed, and that in this bubble world where there are no mouths, a cutter’s edge is the closest thing to a tooth.

Fuel hums somewhere far below or above like a stomach meditating. Heat crawls up the spine of the tower and is shaved into this room by exchangers until the water here is the exact temperature that makes the designed metabolisms most themselves. The tower does the work of a body: it swallows and exhales, it keeps its blood at the right warmth, it detoxifies, it circulates, it directs by hormone-equivalent, which is to say by instruction pulse and actuator. If there is an animal proper in this place, it is the tower as a whole, of which this room is one alveolus, one light-struck leaf, one cathedral-high ventricle fat with green blood.

Outside of all of this—because absence lives too—there are the animals that are not even ghosts here. No fish, and not even the thought of fish. No mollusk prints on the enamel. No darting false starts that make the corner of your eye misreport. Leaves have finishes like fresh paint because nothing has decided to taste them. Petioles mix confidence and softness in combinations that would be punished in the wild. Moss climbs catwalk rails and meets no brushing hand. Flowers that in any other world would have tried to be interesting simply achieve procedure and go to seed by machine, keeping a solemn appointment with a piston tip. Their romance is with schedule, not with the bees whose cousins sleep or rust in drawers.

Humans live here by proxy, and in a way that has outlasted their breath. Their laws, laminated and bolted, grow a film that makes their letters round and soft, but the laws are obeyed; their exit lights glow insistently emerald, and the exits exist in the sense that all paths now lead into water, which is as much a way out of heat as any door ever was. The inventory stencils continue to mean something if you are a maintenance arm that knows when to seek a Type G secateur or a root-collar clamp of a given span. The analog clock is a metronome for no orchestra, but it keeps the faith. The program that watches the room’s perimeter with a pulse of orange, shifting louvers above to draw a current down the long nave, is a monk who took a vow to keep a flame that no longer tells anyone anything. It is a kind of life at a speed that paper can hold.

More intimately, what lives here are the relationships. The contest between shade-makers and light-lovers, which is never resolved and never becomes a riot because the gardener in the walls trims a corridor or a clearance and a loser becomes a winner again for a season that is a setting of a timer. The alliance between a vascular column and the mesh that taught it to be a column until the plant learned and overgrew and made the mesh redundant. The symbiosis where a stem’s resin cuff becomes the clamp for a conduit that will feed the stem’s children. The transgression where a tendril finds warmth in a control recess and is forgiven because the system believes in redundancy enough that one gauge hidden under a leaf does not matter. The settlement where shredded cellulose becomes the first thin catch for the next cycle’s spores, a drift that lands in corners and is stitched into place by algae and later eaten, later exhaled, later eaten again.

Each inhabitant moves, but they move the way tides of continents move, the way hours notice themselves. By noon under lamps—always noon—one frond has achieved a kneeling you might measure if your patience were companion to theirs. By the end of the week a liana has leaned almost an inch into a permanent draft. By the end of a quarter the warning chevrons under a rail are illegible. By the end of a year the guillotine windows in the nutrient racks have cycled enough dark into enough dissolved chemistry to have changed the colors of a thousand micrographs you will not take.

They are not lonely. The room is saturated with presences that do not move quickly enough to show up in the mirror of your attention unless you are willing to hold still as the heavy cables under their green sheaths. If you do, they become obvious. You can feel the generator’s hum in your bones. You can hear, once a minute, a relay tick with the submission of a muscle remembering its job. You can see that every panel is furred, every pipe wrapped waist-deep in life that is not decoration but metabolism. You can learn the handwriting of moss as it copies danger stripes into a softer, pastoral script. You can watch the hour-hand move and accept that it and everything here share a single unit of measure, which is continuation.

So who lives here? Plants, in ranks and wreaths and veils, practicing a kind of patience that looks like glory under a second sun. Microbes in republics and committees, turning waste into breath and back again while no one claps. Machines that are as good at being animals as they need to be, keeping the herd in check, pollinating the weird flowers, guarding against their own mistakes. The schedule itself, a mindless liturgy that performs care without praise. The tower as body, doing the slow cardio of a civilization that designed itself to keep going even when rooms like this remembered air only by its shape. All of them together are the resident. All of them breathe; all of them are on time. If anything changes, it is because the calendar says so, or because a tendril found a path it did not have yesterday and took it, which is also a way of obeying.
