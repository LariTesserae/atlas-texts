What lives here lives by the note.

It is not something with a face. It does not wake and sleep. It does not cross the catwalk or look into the slot of glass and think of falling. It is a congregation of lattices and films, of whiskers and plates and braided stems, every one of them at home in dry wind and in the strange rich diet the wind provides. It is a garden in the strict sense—the sense in which “garden” is an agreement with a climate.

They grew into this agreement slowly. They came from accident and affinity: a smear from a glove, a fog of dopant that escaped a drum cap, a flake of a test catalyst gone feral on a weld seam. The shaft gave them three things, and all of them were constant. First, a field laid through its length, static but never absent, a slow invisible river for any conductor that could lay down a root. Second, a flow full of incompatible appetites—acids and bases in a planned detente, oxidizers and reducers, carbon dioxide at densities where it moved like a ghost between gas and liquid, each of them looking for something to finish with. Third, a grammar of stress: a pulse pressed into every bolt and rail and plate, a low syllable repeated without rest.

Given these, the first films learned to open and close where the field widened and narrowed. They grew pores when charge called to them and closed when it slackened. They built conjugated spines that could ferry a surplus along themselves the way you drag your palm along a banister, and they learned to hold onto ions the way burrs hold onto wool. In the dry, they needed a different blood. They brewed one: ionic liquids like syrup woven through capillaries that were themselves circuits, perfluorinated oils that never beaded, that slid in thin laminae where they were told and nowhere else.

In time, there were species—not because a book named them but because textures began to mean different trades. Where the wind first strikes the metal, low velvet clings: mats of doped polyaromatic sheets black as printer’s ink, their surfaces furred with nanofins to feel a field’s curl. You can tell them by touch if you dared—soft as stacked paper, with a skitter under the finger where the charged liquid within responds to you. They are the pioneers; they make a conductive skin that other things can trust.

Behind them rise blades and fans: silicate ferns that learned lift. Every lamina is a compromise between attack and surrender—angled to ease the air around it, gritted to take the bite and survive it, thin to the precise thickness that the flow will tolerate. They are not made of cells and sap; they are tiled like engines, in hexagons and triangles, their joints loaded with crystals that piezoelectricize when the fan’s letter flexes them, turning mechanical shiver into a whispering current that goes down into the plates below like rain. They hum imperceptibly; if you rest your teeth together you can hear them with your skull.

Tucked in angles where wiring looms throw a lee, bright crusts fatten on nothing you would call food. They are hoarders, storage organs grown out of sintered hollows: a succulent architecture of capacitive cups where halogens and nitrates settle out with a satisfaction that is almost audible. Their skins are the crackle you hear when you pull a sweater over your head; they insulate and tempt in the same instant. When the field swells with some remote adjustment—when a pump far away brings the system a fraction closer to one ideal or another—they answer by dilating, trading, handing their held ions along the mats beneath them into a waiting backlog of empty sites.

Not all of it is gentle. There are copper-oxide vines now that split a conduit’s repair collar decades ago and slipped their roots into the thinnest leak in the dielectric. They do not see or search; they are drawn, the way frost fingers toward a window’s chill. They have no malice and yet if given a century they could turn a label unreadable, enshroud a wrench until it becomes a bloom. Their growth is in the interstices, in seams and threads, more solder than vine. When the shaft shudders in its operating tolerances, when its note swells a cent, they flex with it and so stay attached, their bonds refreshed by the smallest motions.

Between species there are arrangements. Calling them symbioses makes them sound sentimental; they are simply practical. The semiconducting mats lay down a ground plane no fern can do without. The ferns shade the mats from micrometric abrasion they would not survive alone, and transduce the flow’s percussion into a current the mats can drink. The capacitor crusts donate when the stream runs lean and accept when it comes full. The wire-mosses inverting from the handrail feed on tribocharges wrung from the very ions the crusts reluctantly let slip.

There are grazers, though not in any way that makes a mouth. Some filigrees exist to catch and then to dissolve parts of their neighbors—very slowly, very politely. When a plate reaches the far edge of what the flow will bear and must let go, it does not tumble alone. It is caught by carpets below that leach its stored wealth into their own networks. When a trellis of glassy stems grows under cable bundles and braids them into its own skeleton, it is not because it wants the bundles; it is because they bend to a shape that puts the stems in a gradient they can eat. There are no stomachs here, but there is always hunger; it is just measured in millivolts and normal force.

They do not disperse by seed or spore. They flake. A measure of them, when it becomes too much, is convinced to let go by the same logic that put it there. Geometry and wind do the rest. Fragments fall and are abraded to a dust whose chemistry is not innocent. The dust is how this place writes messages to itself. It is loaded with defects—patterns of doping, missing atoms, little knots in the arrangement that matter because they are instructions about how to be in a field. Where the dust settles in a seam or along a weld, it teaches. A scrap of polyaromatic sheet drags a line of charge beneath itself and so tells new crystal what path to take. A sliver of ceramic with a certain strain remembers a certain resonance and invites it to recur.

This is how they have a past. The shaft does not change, but the garden does, imperceptibly, not in the outline of its fronds but in their preference for a slightly different dopant than their grandparents used, in the way the piezoelectric grit is distributed in the new shoots. A generation here is a flake and a reknit. Speciation is deciding, by growth alone, that one alloy makes a steadier edge than another. Evolution is a census of accidents: what survived the constant note long enough to instruct the next layer how to read it.

If you could cut one of them open without changing it, if you could see inside whatever passes for its body, you would find order at every scale. A network of pores not filled with air but with a whispering stew of ions shuttling between charge collectors. Conjugated backbones with mobile defects rippling along them like lanterns in a long parade, carrying equivalence from one side to another. Pockets where supercritical carbon dioxide is coerced into fleeting intimacy with a catalyst and leaves its carbon behind as carbonate lace, a patient embalmment undertaken as calmly as the growth of a stalactite. Interfaces whose wetness is only electric, where a molecule slithers because there is a thoroughfare for it and sticks because the thoroughfare ends.

You could call some of the formations organs. There are compressor diaphragms, places where the note makes the scaffold lift and fall a nanometer at a time, bellowing the sap through its finest paths. There are coils, not of wire but of doping, winding and unwinding as the field pulses at the edges of its tolerance, tuning the whole to stay on pitch. There are hairs that are more than ornament: whiskers that end in points to concentrate charge and so build their own microclimates, tiny poles in a world that already has a pole, small thefts of the field for the organism alone.

They take in. They also cast out. The garden’s waste is form. It sheds plates it cannot support, grudgingly gives up ions it cannot hold, excretes structure that someone downwind will find soluble. There is no rot because there is no water and because nothing is apart from everything else the field can touch. What lingers becomes a base for a new arrangement. What leaves becomes doctrine. The flow is the courier. If anything like gossip exists here, it is the way a trick in one bay—say, a better way to tap the alternator’s harmonics with a laminate at a certain angle—propagates as fragments slake and teach two ledges along, then four, then twenty, a rumor accruing proof.

And there is sound. It is not merely background. It is nutrient. The piezoelectric bands are not decorative; they are mouths. The fan’s letter drives their flex, and flex, in the right crystal under the right constraint, opens a gate or slams it shut. If you leaned your ear to the handrail long enough to learn to separate the layers, you might hear mating—by which I only mean recognition—as the same frequency reflected in two different structures finds consonance, and the vectors of their fields add a little, and some minute advantage is won from coherence. Contact and correspondence are the only luxuries this world affords, and those it affords continuously.

No animal has ever crossed this ledge because there is nothing for an animal to do here, no prey, no stealth, no wet business. The only motion is metabolism and the only metabolism is a juggling of polarities. The only eyes are diodes in a panel that never lights, except the garden has taken them for their glass. The only ears are everywhere. If you must imagine a person, imagine the garden itself as a choir that has forgotten there were ever solos, each voice held because the others hold it, every singer becoming the room it sings in.

They are not brave and not afraid. They have survived so long because nothing has asked them to change faster than the note. If a trolley once crawled by here, its brushes sparking against a rail, it would have been a storm in their yearless calendar, a hot dry lightning the mats would have arched away from and then taken advantage of, plating themselves with the metals it freed. If a hand once grasped the rust-orange ladder and left salt on the rung, it would have been a festival—chloride! water!—over in an hour, metabolized into something less interesting.

When the field is perfect, when the balance up- and down-shaft is what the control curves say it should be, the garden becomes so quiet that it is indistinguishable from its substrate except to the touch and to the ear pressed hard into iron. This is its wealth: an ever-ready chance to add a millimeter and lose it again, to refine an edge toward a limit that is always a little beyond it. The lushness is, in part, arithmetic. In a climate without winter or drought or blue sky the luxury is in covering everything that does not change with things that will, then teaching them to hold themselves just so.

If the note fails—if the fans brake and the field unthreads and the flow goes still—what lives here will not die that hour or that day. It will understand the change entirely, everywhere, at once. Pores will close. Bound ions will crawl back into places they are less happy for the sake of being held at all. The sap will stand. Fronds will arc inward to spare their edges dust they can no longer pay to deflect. Days later the first flake will let go, unbraced by flex. Weeks later, hollows will collapse because a lattice that depended on stress for its self-respect has lost it. In a month, perhaps, there will be powder where a cuff of copper-oxide vines was, and on the wrench—finally visible—the ghost of a flower in stainless etched pale by what it fed.

Until then, this is what lives here: a language of structures answering to a single sustained vowel, a parish of chemistries that took a machine’s setpoints for weather and made homes within them. The ledge is small and full. The inhabitants have names if you know how to read for grain—charge-lichen and wind-fern, halo-succulent, whisker-moss and copper vine and glass trellis—but they are all versions of the same vow. They are the sort of life that happens when you offer the world a perfect constant and then wait long enough for the constant to find ornament. They are the green of chromium and the amber of sulfate in a light that never changes, and they will keep their bargain as long as the note is held.
