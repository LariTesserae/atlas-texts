What lives here learns to be thin and definite and patient. It learns to live in the edges between hot and not-quite, wet and nearly, light and the back of light. It learns that the useful part of a thing is often its surface, and that most of what passes through you can be eaten if you are willing to eat it slowly.

The first you notice are not minds but habits given bodies. The filters are one such habit. Thin as skin once was and gaffed with vanes, they are as much device as creature, all surface. They spend their lives on the difference between droplet and air, making their living on flecks, salts, the lace of bacteria that rides the sheets. They have no interest in depth; their mouths, which are many and very small, open only into a few plenum-like cavities where what sticks is sorted by charge and wetness, licked off by cilia and moved to quiet places to be turned into themselves. Old filters carry a history of bright days as faint iridescences, prismatic scars where their scaffolds were once overtaxed and healed—healed by thickening what broke.

They breed, if it is right to call it that, by leaving thread. When they come to ground, they shiver out a fringe from their margins that dries into glassy filaments, tacky with salts and enzymes. Fog catches there and beads and wicks and finds the next thread, and along those threads, tiny packets bud. A morning will lay a new flotilla along a ledge like punctuation as you say; you can watch the generation lift off one by one, each making a decision at its own dewline.

The grazers are the other obvious kind. They read as indifferent—little jointed shapes the color of evaporated milk, sand, bone. Their backs are scaled not for armor but for capillarity; when mist rises their plates drink it and share it around their bodies, keeping their felted mouths damp enough to work. They look slow because they are slow, but their slowness is not torpor. They are clocks that wind themselves on film. All day their lips go in slow circles on a polis of microbes that were born that dawn and will be dead by noon, the opaline glaze the eye learns to distinguish once it tires of stone. They comb across a pan and leave a mark finer than a hair, a completion you learn to notice: here is an edge that somebody has already turned into afterlife.

The hunters are not afterlife but after the lines between. They do not own bodies in the way the grazers do. What they have are reachings—tendrils, if you like, but with a geometry that belongs to the fractures they live in. They anchor themselves in the cold, so cold they look blue, and lay out fingers thin as soap-bubbles that persist because the air is so dry they do not break. Those fingers are full of proteins that open and close when a temperature gradient brushes them or when a humidity front passes over. The hunters drink that motion. They lay a hand where a sun-heated trickle meets a shadowed runback and the dance between those two tiny weathers feeds them. If a grazer passes by and blurs the gradient, the hand closes, and afterwards there is only incorporation, not tearing or chewing. On some days you can stand very still and watch a fanned limb make a motion so delicate it is purely diagnostic, like the needle of an instrument flicking.

There is life more cryptic still that you sense the way you sense a pulse under a stone. The bright, slick gloss on rock is not algae in any leafing sense, but it is a consortium of eaters of light. They are purple when you trap them in a shade glass and look long, the old kind of living that throws a proton over a membrane when a photon arrives and lets the slow fall back downhill power small, necessary things. They lay their dyes down very thin. Where minerals enrich a basin to soapiness, another colony will look amber, another smoke-blue. In the caves, the pale fans that you think are fungus are something like that—eaters of flows rather than of sugar, their anatomies built around wicking, around presenting as much self as possible to the transit of night-breath and morning-fog. They are sheet more than stalk. When collapse commits sky into a cavern room, a summer of light will dismember them; they are not meant to be seen by noon.

Things with locomotion other than the obvious exist. In some shadowed clefts, a breeze like a page turned in a book is not breeze at all but a curtain of tiny fins flicking in place. They are rotor-animals, pinpricks that spin, chewing the overnight precipitates of organics into particles the grazers can manage. There are salt bees with no buzz that wear crystals on their feet and carry them home in pies, not to build but to plant in shallow, seeded basins where precise minerals will precipitate—unconscious chemists the city has learned to court. There are dew-eels that learned to be thread and live in capillaries, moving by making and unmaking surface tension, so invisible you only know them by the way a droplet in a dead-end vein will seem to decide to leave. Once, in exactly the noon you describe, I watched a film of water fold into itself like a mouth and take a trapped grazer as if the rock had decided to be carnivore for the space of a finger-length: it was a hunter's limb returning from patience.

And then there are the people.

If you need a word, say they are human and then begin crossing out what you expect. They are high-bodied because the air and the stairs have made them so: broad chests, slow, long breaths that you can see in the morning as a strength in fog, not so much exhalation as exchange. They skin themselves when they must be bare in layers of powder from the spillworks, fine carbonate with seeds of copper and iron that turn the light aside like crowded sunrises; they tan to mineral, not brown but into a pale that knows the air. Their eyes live under louvers and lenses, smoked and shuttered, and their names are woven into the veils they wear, not in thread but in threads that split light into banners of color when they move. If they must speak with voice they bring their faces close, not for privacy but for physics. Mostly they do not.

Their language began with cave-air and drip. It is hands and cloth and tilt: a fan turned just so means Now, turn that plate; a wrist that switches from matte to shine is a child's laughter answered across a gulf. Messages run along the hanging lines as patterns of shade tugged from one hook to the next. If you look up from a distance when trouble is somewhere else you can see the city telling itself what to do, buildings taking on different faces one after the other in a ripple. At ground-level, when a decision must be made together, leaders step into a pool of reflected light and bow a map into existence between their palms, cutting it with prismatic slats taken from their sleeves. Their mouths move; no sound goes far. When they must call beyond seeing, they pull the ground-line with narrow weights inside the span of muscle to send a pulse bare feet pick up. The old can feel messages in their bones three platforms away.

They make a living the way the animals do: on films, on differences, on slowness. Their harvests are swims through a surface rather than dives. They tend filters the way another people might tend hives—not so much domestication as choreography. In the season when air draws hard up the cliffs, they raise thin scaffolds between known drip-lines and let their kites find the lanes. By evening they collect the tired filters with long-haired wands that will not tear them and shake the day from them into bowls where glass and salts and polymers are separated by patience. There are still-tenders whose job is to listen with their fingertips to the creep of brine through a bed of powdered stone; they can tell you, blindfolded, when the first fan of carbonate is beginning to grow in the tray beneath. There are anchor-gardeners who place templates under tufa-beards and clap silent to drive the vapor just so, teaching stone to hold. There are rimwrights who repair the brows when a ledge goes, melt-workers who cook ingredient and time together in shadowglass fields so that engines the sun would shatter if given to heat are instead given to a season.

Mining here is not a blow, it is an accrual. They coax copper to a where and nickel to a when by feeding trickles the baths they prefer and giving them a slope. They bring ugly waters in, foul with the lowlands’ teas of dust and metal, and stride alongside them as they move through maze after maze, pulling one taste at a time from the stream with pads of algae, with resin, with speciation columns layered like pastry. What leaves them is clearer than it came. What stays stays in trays, microscopics on seeds that grow into sheets then bars you could not hear fall if they fell in ten.

They eat on the same scales. There is a stone cheese that comes from the cave mats, salted with the same salts the mats prefer, cut by daylight into skeins before it can stiffen into mineral. There is grazer-paste, resinous, a flavor like breath and chalk and the scent after rain, which to an acquired tongue is satisfaction. There are pastries spun out of filter-fringe once it has been boiled with acid to clear the minerals and left with protein; they slice it thin and dry it in the breath that runs from rooms to galleries, glossy and sweet with the bacteria that grew on it in the first half of that day. Children are given copper to lick in the evening to teach their mouths sour, and white fans of carbonate shaved from the spillworks to teach their mouths clean. There are fruits of a kind, domes of translucent gel that form in soapier basins when a certain consortium is seeded and shadowed just so; you cut them with shadowglass and eat them for water and a mild joy.

As for art: they play with weather. They drag cloth like water over lines and make light behave; their dances are choreographies of louvers. A festival is a storm that has not arrived: the plates that harvest sun pivot to show their black faces to the city and then away, black and white, black and white, turning the cliff into a great slow heartbeat you can see from the lowlands as a shivering of eclipse. Children play the alphabet with their bodies against a wall, making their skin go from dry-rough to wet-smooth with a palm, naming themselves in glints: my name is a streak of copper under a veil, my family a sequence of bands only we can fold. Lovers touch wrists and let the heat of each sink into the other’s bracelets, the circulating gel cooling and warming in a rhythm both can feel. The very shy make their courtships on rope-bridges at noon, in the blaze, when even gesture is a job no one else will do to watch; they talk with the little plates sewn into their sleeves, the ones that turn blue when the air turns hand-cool.

Their taboos make mechanical sense. No hammering; no whistles; no running where films run. Children are taught that spitting is violence. If you are a visitor the first thing you will be given is felt for your shoes and the second is a lesson in how to stand on a platform when it shifts so your weight goes with the bloom of its anchors. The criminals they exiled they exiled down, and most drowned or choked on the lowlands’ air of thunder and dust; when an amnesty was given in one long hunger-season nearly all who lived came back white-haired from lightning.

They bury by teaching stone. A body is placed in a channel warm with slow water and seeded with cultures that take a little of the person for fuel and give the rest to the rim. Over a year the channel grows a lip around that memory; if you run your fingers where a mouth was you can find a fainting of texture and the place where weeping learned to step around a hand. Names are pebbled into louvers in the dead’s colors and worked into the city’s grammar forever. On cold evenings relatives go to the brows and tip salt and copper together onto the lip and watch the glaze take it.

They do not stay up late for thunder. Storm is ordinary. They tend to themselves when the cliff thinks aloud. Children grow up with falls the way river-children grow up with floods; they know where to stand to feel the tremor with only their toes, the three heartbeats between the wordless unhinging of a face and the refusal of sound to bring them the end of its sentence. They will tell you, if you live long enough beside them to be told true things, that the grid of the plateau is the only god they require, not as an authority but as a proof: that patterns are patient, that anything can be taught if you are willing to count in drips, that in a place where the air forgets your voice it is correct that you should learn to name yourself some other way.

So this is what lives here: films and their eaters, difference and those who make a meal of it, predators that possess rather than puncture, microbes that build purple suns under a noon that would bleach them to nothing if they lacked their cunning thinness. People who are all edge, perches, louvers, membranes, felt. They make homes that weep and machines that count softly. They dress themselves in lenses and powder and hang their alphabet on beams. They have learned the manners of a loud light and a selfish rock. They take water by climbing. They let the cliff’s silence be their instrument. And when they must say exactly who they are, they tilt a prism or dampen a cloth and let color cross their faces and their walls until the shape of their name is clear, and nothing anywhere needs to hear it to know it.
