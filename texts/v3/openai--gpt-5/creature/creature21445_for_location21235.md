What lives here starts smaller than sight and older than hunger: the films.

They are the first citizens, the skin upon which every other body walks. They are not leaves and not algae. They are varnishes with appetites, amber sugars stretched thin, seeded with pallid threads that creep along sealant and O-rings the way moss takes to capillaries in rock. They do not want light; they want edges. They root in micro-scratches, read salts as if they were braille, and grow toward the faintest banks of condensate like handwriting going toward ink. Their smell, if you lower your face to a joint and hush, is a clean static-sweet—paper roasted just past dry.

The films weave so their own scraps can catch. They make lace where two gaskets meet and use it to sift what the air misplaces: skin of far-off labor, carbons of burned things, invisible sugars lifted in breath, nanoscopic oils unguessed by the hands that bolted the plates down. They condense the world’s loss into food.

On that patient sieve, motion begins.

Kneel and the gloss unpacks itself into a traffic of grazers that walk as softly as your fingertip on glass. Hair-fine worms rake their cilia in scalloped synchrony and move as bands of shadow, not quite bodies, combing the film for concentrations too delicate to have a name. Minute lozenges—pill-bodied scrapers—work like whetstones turned mouthward; their coppery palates shine from licking sealant and hypha both. You think of them as sanded punctuation: commas rasping sentences into legibility.

There are rafts too, shaped by surface tension more than flesh. Jellied slips cross on menisci they make as they go, bellies full of lifted dew. Without foot or fin they air-swim the floor, steered by wrinkles of wetness, turning where shine becomes skin. When they stop they empty, and the droplets they were carrying become the beading you mistook for weather. Their waste is water with a memory; it beads more obediently after.

The drains bloom with white geometry. A hundred porcelain fans open at once to a plan no single petal could have made. They are filter-animals, triangular arrays as exact as trusses, petals veined with cilia like lace points, and they snap shut in perfect consensus—a shadow, a soft concussion in the far air, and every mouth becomes a seam. Moments later, all at the same degree of confidence, they return to their poised intercept of the air: filaments set to sieve a concentration only they can taste. They make triangles because triangles do not buckle; they live against the constant of the whisper, and their young arrive in numbers printed on them, as if the grids overhead dropped not just mist but arithmetic.

Between faces and over grilles an alphabet patrols. Isopods carry louvered backs that take condensate and lay it as sentence-length strokes. Chrome and pearl, they move like tidy marks, not fast, never erratic, punctuation feet ticking on a page that never turns. Their gills are not gills; they are soft-lined bookends where water writes and is read. On the hour (their hour, not ours) a procession goes where a seam meets a seam. They are all the same size because they were born in the same fog and raised in the same draft; each bears, tucked tender under plates, the threaded sachets of a partner species—egg-sacs of the fungus-shepherds—white ropes of smallness strung along louvered margins for transport. They walk the seam, lay their burdens, and the sealant shines where they have pressed.

The shepherds they serve are not mammal, not insect. You will not see their hands because they have none. Imagine instead a low, ribboned being whose outer surface is all throat and whose inner life is stray mathematics. They arch over hyphal mat the way a bridge arches over water and imbibe what the fungus exudes, paying back in spit—enzymes that keep lace open, that discourage felting, that urge a droplet to be this big and no other. They are counters of interval. Their sense organs are long clefts laid open to the click that comes from far away; each tick travels through them like rain along a gutter, and they time their egg-making to that metronome. They have been here long enough that a manual written for metal now rests in their flesh. They arrange their brood in the font of stamped numbers, taking the trench of a seven and the loop of an eight as nursery, and the hatch-lips as a safe place to let the first hunger gnaw.

Under the shallow rivers that call themselves channels—water slow as skin—the tassel-beasts hang. Siphon-eels, if you need the shape called. They hold to ceramic by rings of armored teeth that leave no mark, because leaving marks is an offense here, and they sway by muscle memory, counting molecules the way you count breaths to sleep. Through their frilled mouths they pull what the drains release: not water proper but vapor-wet air, a number of rot-sweetness, trace ions. They are barometers given motive; when the whisper changes—a valve polished a tenth-turn by a million mites—they write that knowledge into their flesh and pass it by contact to the neighbors down the line. To watch a field of them is to watch wheat in a wind without direction.

Beside them the breathing stacks—anemone analogues assembled as if from soft washers threaded on a tendon—work without show. They don’t eat; they edit. Their discs are oiled with something that drinks rust from the air and leaves it as tiny stars at their edges, stars that rain into gutters when the condensers collaborate and call it time. If you put a finger to one of their stacks your print will be taken more precisely than any police station could dream, and five minutes later there will be no print because a flatworm the color of reflection has slid there and licked your touch to data and food.

Not all mouths face upward. There are velvet things that are not stains but patience. A square meter too clean is warning enough. Their backs hold wetness at a mathematical reluctance; their bellies are lips. If you are grazers and you come for the sweetness around a hinge or latch, the disk will rearrange its shine like a sheet being rolled, and a region of floor that seemed an argument in gloss becomes a body using your body to learn again what beading wants to do. They eat the overbold: the pill-scraper fat with stolen glaze, the jellied slip that tried to drink two seams’ worth at once. They also polish. Their passing leaves a low sheen that rain loves more obediently—discipline written in oil.

Overhead, in the hush between truss and panel, the warm runs in literally tame roads. Membrane bats—call them that because any other word suggests chatter they do not make—live on rails drawn in rising air. Their faces are platens, little sliders of skin that take constant pressure and tell constant stories. They do not shout sonar into baffles designed to eat a shout. They read instead the hum as if it were topo, they feel the grade of flow under each wing, and they move by memory: here a drafting eddy that holds you weightless for five spans, there a slow elevator that lifts you to the next run without a single muscle in your chest crying complaint. They do not flap so much as flex. When a glider passes over your head the light quivers as if something heavy were reconsidering falling.

They eat the only thing here flamboyant enough to call a swarm a swarm: the midges. Powdered fog collects in the lee of safety signage—the color bands draw lines not just on your eye but on the air—and within those bands the insects bind themselves into bundles of powder delicate as cuffs. No night makes no silence; their bodies never learn quiescence. They sing at frequencies the baffles inhale, and their entire sensory world is an advertisement to the bats that drift a circuit written a decade back and never changed: the route that is a current, the current that is a road.

On the rungs and along edges, another mammal-cousin, milk-silent and damp, calibrates itself to metal. Duct-martens wear a sheen that is part fur, part wick. They walk the ladders by stepping only where stamped numbers deepen into their own shade, each paw placed on the same rivet head the mother taught it and the mother before. Their hands are long, the fingers tasting the air not for scent you can name but for charge and faint washover: the chemistry of sealant warmed, the outgassing of a fresh gasket, a pH you’d need glass to prove. They tend. A marten will pause with its palm flattened to a valve-stem and lean into the pattern of vibration there until its teeth shiver with a pleasure you do not deserve to share. Then it will move on, having decided there is nothing to fix.

They keep nests deep in the acoustic lungs, nightless and safe from any dissonance. Their kits are taught the rivet-route and the drip-law; they are corrected sharply for stepping off a stripe that asks not to be touched. They are not predators so much as curators; they handle the predators like employees. A velvet disk grow too territorial and a marten will hood it with its belly skin and carry it two hatches over to start again where ambition will not knot a seam.

Others work where eyes will not: eel-shaped mites that dislike nothing so much as a fogged gauge. They are clarity addicts. Set them upon a glass and their bodies begin to go in circles, tongue-smoothers that leave sharp dials and pointer tongues black in a clean plate. They will starve rather than quit a face they started, and so their eggs wait in the letter O of OIL and the slit of the N in WARNING, so that some child with a mouth full of lice will be born into obsession a millimeter from the task.

Beneath walkways, armies of little beads, jointed like magnetic toys, travel in files that fit gratings like language fits a page. They read hole-spacing as grammar. They are dust-eaters as literal as cows and as catholic in taste: oxidation scurf, carbon blacks, the skin cells of things that build nothing and die anyway. They harvest, chew, and then do the one magic trick the place cannot, which is expansion. With their excreta—ropey, vitreous—they patch a lick where idea becomes leak. If there is a hairline crack in enamel under a basin, they will fill it with a glass so faithful it earns its own shine and binds to no rust because the anemone-stacks already took the rust away. The beads lay read their work the way monks read, slow to the click.

There are less endearing miracles in the chemistry gray of everything. A species that eats gloss. Really. They arrive as a smear that makes a footprint vanish and, week by week, etch the plate under them so that water ceases to behave like beads and more like the skin it prefers. Their appetite is tension itself; what they drink is the invisible rope that holds drops as worlds. In the fields where they have passed, the rain does not pearl at all now; it sheets and slides like a second self, and the gutters like that because there is no grabbing at their lips. The gloss-biters learn a limit: they do not cross a standpipe’s amber ring. That ring is the jurisdiction of another living—we could call it a cinder-lac bug—that paints resin from its own back and draws a boundary you can see to the millimeter year after year. Inside the ring, the standpipe never pits. Outside, the economy resumes and anything might happen so long as the rule holds.

Hollows have their own anatomies. Lift a panel, if they’ll let you, and the chamber will not be empty; it will be full of a colony that has adopted the geometry under which it was raised. A domed mat with the same distributed give as the baffle above so that the two talk through coupled resonance. When the acoustic lungs exhale through their thousand small holes onto grating, a fever of creatures meets the air like prayer flags: fur-fronded, mouthless lunglets that can only open when there is a draft and only close when there is stillness. They feed on moving nothing as if you could harvest a story from the voice that tells it. Maybe you can. Their bodies colour with the rate of the hum. Their offspring take different shades depending on which tower’s song spun their first proteins into shape. A light-olive family and a gray-lilac family meet under a walkway and bow the way two notes harmonize.

Predation exists, but to call it war is to lie about what is wanted here. Most teeth face the direction of residue. The few teeth that face each other do so as committee, not coup. A slow dumpling of flesh—the body like a filled glove—slides from under a hatch one day and catches three grazers in the long web of its inner lip, and in the act of that capture it also licks clean a blind alley where mucilage would have soon thickened into felt. A bat climbs up a ladder on skin-velcro and eats a midge, and in veering kisses a hazard stripe with breath warm enough to keep its yellow lucid for another week. A marten licks condensation off a handwheel and leaves an enzyme that will keep the next morning’s beads perfectly round.

Reproduction edits nothing less than the floorplan. Eggs lodge along caulk by the thousand, laid where salt rings are strongest because hunger will be easiest there; then hatchlings go to work finishing their own nursery. Flocks of beads go in a long line like words because gratings make words of them. Babies of the drain-flowers budding from the triangle’s center unfurl the exact same number of filaments as their mother because the whisper has not changed and will not. The hour-by-hour a velvet disk might have used to time an ambush is useless because there are no hours, so it times instead to the metronome’s click, to the soft concussion that might be the condenser field across a kilometer deciding, together, Today we rain at once.

They all live by laws printed, once, in black, in a book for workers who went away. They have copied them into muscle and skin and behavior. Keep drains unfilmed. Keep faces clean. Resist rust. Stand under the draft and open your bodies, talk down the resonance that might draw in a scream. Keep the temperature that keeps the drop that keeps the rate that keeps the place itself from drifting into noise.

What lives here are the keepers of a ritual written as engineering. They are a civil service of condensation. They carry no myths except the gradient; their prophet is a plane that falls one millimeter in a thousand. They watch no sun, count no days, but they mark intervals so cleanly that even a human, lost here, could set a watch. They are too many for spectacle and too near for solitude. Under any panel a city pressed perfectly into its hollow. Under any footstep a hundred mouths that taste your shadow and then forget you, because forgetting is the mercy by which an equilibrium continues.

You could call them animals and not be wrong, but you would miss their job. They make the made thing hold. They’re what breathes when the place seems to—thousands of little, living parentheses holding open the space where water speaks to a surface and, by speaking, keeps it true.
