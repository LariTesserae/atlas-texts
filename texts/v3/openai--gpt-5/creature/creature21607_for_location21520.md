At human height you look for figures and find none. At stone height you start seeing neighbors.

The first are thread-lives, strung along edges the way frost is. They have no faces, so you meet them by feel. Beard lichens take the wind as a livelihood: Alectoria and Bryoria like unbrushed hair, Usnea with its pale science of elasticity. Their economy is touch—every long filament a comb for mist—and sound is both byproduct and gossip. When a gust pulls through, their hissing is a market’s murmur; when a raven lands, they go quiet in a circle, edges bending under its weight, then resume their breath-work the instant it leaves. Their bodies are partnerships—fungus sheltering algae, algae feeding fungus—marriages tuned to thin air. They grow an eyelash’s width in a year if the air is kind. Break a strand and you have just made a propagule: a seed without a seed. Some of the orange flares on those favored perches—where a bird has made nitrates a luxury—are the bright riotous cousins, the flamboyant lichens with sunblock built in, pigments shouting their small joy into a light that takes as much as it gives.

Underfoot, the rooms that look like bare courtyards have tenants who learned long ago that all roofs are temporary. Mosses and tiny sedges run the ground-level utilities. They are not personalities so much as neighborhoods, thick with trade. Their leaves are narrow, their bodies spongy and capable of more storage than you would think a green could hold. They move by swelling and shrinking. In the morning they unfurl into cups and fingers. At night they curl inward and harden into nothing. Their clocks are inside the water, and the water keeps good time.

Inside those mattresses the smaller citizens keep their own calendars. Rotifers like translucent commas, and nematodes like pale hairs with an opinion, and springtails the color of dust with a pinhead for a face—they rush when water ties the rooms together, they wait when the air forgets to be generous. Their gift is suspension. A day of dry becomes a week, becomes a winter, becomes the joke of a life folded into a seed crack so deep inside it that the body has to remind itself how to be soft when the first drop finds it again. Tardigrades, bear-like under the microscope, have perfected the art: a city’s worth of them tucked between two moss stems, indifferent to nearly everything until the first warm photon hits a bead and their legs decide that a stone at arm’s length might as well be a continent.

The stone has a skin and that skin is alive. Where the black sheen darkens on faces the drips visit every day, there is more than mineral: manganese-eating bacteria laying down varnish microns at a time, thin as a conviction and just as durable. In the quartz, a different society: endolithic algae and color-stained microbes that choose to live inside rock where light is shunted and sifted and everything is slower; they burn so faintly you can miss them with your eyes open and only notice their presence by the taste of damp and iron when you press your tongue to a clean facet. Lichens that are more etcher than flag chew chemistry into stone, exuding acids that make the honeycombs happen faster than frost alone would ever contrive. Tafoni look like sculpture, and they are, but the sculptors are water and time with a colony of patient mouths.

There are animals that remember legs in a way you recognize. Bristletails—those antique insects, motion like a spasming thought—work the grout lines at night. Their bodies hold the same graphite glint as mica; when they run you think you have just seen a piece of the wall decide to be alive. They eat books the city writes on its own surfaces: algal films, lichen crumbs, the falloff of a raven’s foot. Minute snails, the color of old snow, abrade their narrow paths with tongues of teeth and dream, if snails dream, of humidity. Oribatid mites wear geology: their shells look cut from slate, and they move like turning pages. Pseudoscorpions sit in signs of a world too small for you to read, arms cocked, liking the chafed near-warmth at the backs of cavities, ready to grab a springtail by the scripture of its antenna.

There are web-makers, polite ones, tucking their craft into leeward mouths of galleries where the airflow hums but does not tear. Linyphiid spiders string low sheets like ice that won’t melt, their anchor points set in the shadows of micro-arches; wind writes on their threads, and the vibration of a midge footfall is a sentence the spider reads as calmly as any scholar. Their prey are born from dark seams and wet mornings: chironomid midges that will live the entire ambition of their adult lives before noon, having drunk nothing, eaten nothing, yet fed half the plateau through their larval work in invisible films. Nothing here wastes the hours. There are carnivorous beetles built like gossip—thin, fast, always somewhere else when you look again—dining not on plants but on those who harvest the plants, running by their own ocular maps from one cool court to another when the sun makes a stove of the connecting lane.

Some of the architects are not bodies but proteins. On this stone, in this thin air, ice needs help to decide when to begin. Certain bacteria wear on their sleeves the rare gift of telling water how to line up; they make ice faster, or not at all, depending on the day. They are the underestimated masons; rime feathers feather because a microbe whispered, here. Whatever builds the gutters also staffs them: lichens arranging hyphae to slow a drop long enough that a moss can drink; films of cyanobacteria slicking the way so routine becomes easier, so the evening commute of water can arrive on schedule even when the wind intends mischief.

Plants taller than your hand try the place anyway, and some find a way to be animals of patience. The prostrate willow is a snake by necessity: one long body with many suns for heads, hugging good stone, moving a centimeter of trunk over a decade of life if you take a very small view of motion. Its catkins are bait for bees that rarely arrive; more often they are a wind’s business, pollen spending itself into altitudes that return nothing. Still, the willows make seed, and some of that seed becomes willows again. You can look across a polygon and see ages proceeding in parallel, one branch still with winter on it while the other is full of summer’s miniature. When they die, they die like snow, quietly and in pieces that feed everyone.

The medium-sized beasts the geometry discourages visit anyway and make the place briefly about themselves. A ptarmigan couple trade white and brown as if clothing changes the weather; in among the sedges they blow the room out when they leave it, and for an hour after there is still a certain drag of feather in the corners. A stoat makes a loop no one predicted, ghosting along the ring road with the brittle mirth of someone who can turn into a rope if chased. The raven presides. Its intelligence is a movable microclimate: where it perches, stones get stories; where it digs at a lichen with its bill and smears the scatter with droppings, orange flags erupt the next year, imperial and loud. They are partners more than residents. They bring news in pellets and the small economies swell on the interest.

The sound-lives are not spirits; they are locals with an odd address. Moss and sedge, trembled by passing gusts, use the wiggle to pump water along stems. Drips beat on stone and nothing here seems to mind being an instrument. Collembola—springtails—keep to the beat; they abandon the court just before the rime’s hour breaks by a change in pitch you cannot hear but they can. When a stone finally separates into two stones, beetles and mites answer as if a wall in the neighborhood has just opened onto new real estate. In the galleries each concavity has a note, and there are species that nest by preference according to that note. The ones that like the higher pitches tend to be the bodies that desiccate more easily, as if a bright sound could counter the dryness. The ones with a taste for sub-bass have thick skins and a slow walk.

If there is a guild old enough to call proprietors, it is the frost-workers. They do not have fingers, but they do have edges, and they place those edges where they need to. They lift stones out of place over and over until those stones become a curb, and to stand in such a lane is to stand in the project of something that knows only one verb and conjugates it across centuries. They are faithful to schedule, not to plan. They are how the city maintains itself with no one to write a budget.

What feeds this population are tricks of nitrogen and the debt of thin air. Dust finds its way here, in it the remnants of distant fires and farmlands, pollen that imagined it would spend itself in fields. Cloud-river damp loads itself with minute gifts as it scrapes along the plateau’s windward lip. Bird droppings are jackpots. Dead insects are legacies. Every drop is a courier; when it leaves, it has left behind what small salt and sugar it could afford. That is the grocery.

Time here is not a straight road, it is a switchback. Lives stack inside each other: lichen beneath microalga beneath bacterium beneath frost’s habit beneath sun’s hunger. The true inhabitants are coalitions. When the fog comes up, a thousand green and gray and black partnerships wake like a neighborhood throwing open shutters. When the sun leans away, the same partnerships enter the disciplines of cold, close their membranes, fold their arms, tuck their faces into the crooks of their own elbows. They move by inches and get everywhere.

You could say, no one lives there, if by someone you mean a thing like you with an appetite that makes warmth out of food and food out of soil. But if by someone you mean any will that answers to rules, there are many. Some are called moss, lichen, mite, rotifer, beetle and spider. Some are called protein, habit, route. The oldest resident is a pattern to which water and air belong: a way that edges and openings use weather to write structure. It is not animal and not plant. It is a laid-down grammar that other lives decline themselves through, and it persists regardless of who comes to read it. The city is lived in by what can submit to that grammar without losing itself—lives that find the scale of an inch the right size for destiny, and which, if they think at all, think in days with mornings like glass and nights like the snap of a book shut hard.
