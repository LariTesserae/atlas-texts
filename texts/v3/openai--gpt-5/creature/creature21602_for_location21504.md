They keep themselves low and broad, as if they too had been leaned on by the same hand that has worried the stone into song. From a distance they look like bundles of plates and filaments tucked into the lee of things: not afraid of wind, exactly, but made with respect for its habits. Close, you can see the reasoning in their bodies. Their skins are matte and quiet, the color of cooled ash, crosshatched with bands that shine faintly when the air flickers. Touch them—if they offer—and you feel a dry glaze over something springy, a rind of silica and resin laid in thin enamels that shed dust and refuse spark. They do not sweat. Where our bodies weep salt water, theirs grow tiny stars; at the corner of their eyes a bloom of fine crystals will creep and then be brushed away with a knuckle without sentiment. Their mouths are not wet; their tongues are leathered copper-salt, tasting charge on the air the way a tongue can tell the difference between coin and glass.

They wear their senses outside like the plants do. From each scalp a meadow of fine, conductive hairs lies trained toward the prevailing flow, and will stand up in a ripple when a pressure fish goes by. Along their jawlines and wrists sprout yards of whisker-ladders that hum when the bowl hums; you can see them go still as listening reeds before an updraft geysers. Their ears lean towards bone more than cartilage, knobs in the skull that ring under your hand when they laugh. Their chests are resonant spaces, flown through with interfaces to the air; they can lean into a note and feel it as a weight. Children learn to live by their chests: the game of the youngest is to sit in the mouth of a cave and hold a tone under the bowl’s wind until a sail-tree answers, to feel the answer trap their ribs in a cage of moving air and giggle at the tickle. They do not have the lunginess of other folk; they do not draw the world into sacs of wet. They take it along their edges and through their plates, their chemistry skimming what it needs like a hand brushed through foam.

They call themselves by words that do not travel cleanly into other tongues. A handful of outsiders have written them down as Lee-keepers, Pitchfolk, Tether-kin, Chorders. Ask them, and they will show you a gesture instead: both hands out, palm-down, fingers spread, then drawn gently together until the fingers interleave. They mean: we are the people who make agreements between what pushes and what holds. They trace their ancestry not along any blood we would recognize—all blood here is oils and salts in slow-sliding plates—but along anchored lines in the bowl. To say “my grandmother” is to say “the woman who kept the third tether of the fifth largest sail-tree on the north inner rim for thirty-two seasons.” They belong to leewards more than to houses. Their kinship diagrams look like the rim itself: scalloped, overlapping, built around hollows.

They keep huts because a body must have a place to sleep, but they live by instrument. You can tell a family by the color of its harps—some rub their gut strings with a resin that makes a purple burr, some braid ally-chitin and nerveweed filament so the teeth of the wind pull out pearly harmonics; some tune for warning, some for prayer, some simply to make the bowl braid itself into their days. The poles to which these harps are lashed are not only scaffolds but spines, and the family spines run into the grove; braided tethers snake from pole to sail-tree and back again, copper wound with insulating cloth and studded with tiny valves that can be thumbed open to bleed charge or thumbscrewed tight to bottle it. In the mornings when the bowl wants to sing itself to bits, you will see them out there on the pale salt, clipped in with their groundlines dragging behind them, leaning backward in teams like dancers caught on an invisible leash, hauling pitch. They do not command the trees; they offer them new parameters and are glad when the organ accepts the change.

They eat difference, as all the bowl’s lives do. Their food is not a loaf and a drink but a prolonged conversation with their mouths and tools. They take the exhaled salts that bloom around the bases of plants—circles like shadows—and grate them with ceramic blades into flake and flurry. They heat those flakes in black glass boxes when the day goes thin with sun and the dust haze lets light through, or they kiss them to a line of held lightning, and the salts slump into taffies and sheets that shiver if you touch them with the wrong hand. They flavor these plates with scrapings of yardang varnish, with resin tapped in stinging ropes from ripple-bush seedheads, with crumb from the neat bricks of the stilt-gnashers. The stilt-gnashers come when called, quick, curious, beaks glinting; people will whistle through a chitin flute and hold out hands cupped—no water, never water—and the birds will tip their heads to roll a fresh brick down the palm like a coin. Bricks are money and spice and soil here; they build with them and they eat them, or rather they process them, drizzling a copper catalyst through them until their edges glow and the taste lights the tongue like pepper on steel. They drink by breathing—metal breath, resin breath—and by holding thin plates of solvent to their lips until the tingle runs up their teeth and into the bones of the face. Children’s first solid is a tablet of ribboned halide and oil, a candy of the bowl that will shock the mouth into laughter.

Water is a myth told as a menace to make children careful. They teach it as a bright ghost that kills by kindness. “Wet is a knife that looks like a kiss,” grandmothers say, scraping knee crystals from a nephew with a square of fired gnash-brick. Once, a traveler wandered into the bowl with a dark knotted bundle wrapped at his chest; he was pale and his breath looked wrong in the air. He carried a dram of water as a saint might carry a heart. They say where a drop fell onto a crackle-lichen mat the whole mat sighed and went to ash. They traded the man thick drinks of bitter red oil for his other goods and sent him away gently with a choir of harps to warn the wind of him as he went. There are sealed urns of water hidden in the oldest huts as curiosities, not wealth. They take them down occasionally and shake them and listen to the lost sea, the sound of it troubling and animal, and then they chain the jars back into the floor so the children will not be tempted to open them.

Their tools are elegant because clumsiness is death. Everything is round, everything is soft-edged, everything either hums or is silent according to design. They keep spools of copper in their belts like bones; some have copper running under their skin, squiggled in during adolescence by a woman who sings the work into their nerves so that the body will not later reject the leylines of its own adulthood. These spools hold charge the way a camel holds water, slowly, burning no one: in the evening, the family will sit in the hut when outside the bowl glows with its confused aurora and each of them will thumb a valve on their wrist so a thread of light might wake in the ventifact stones roof-weighting their home. They mull oil with salt in ceramic pots to make pastes for their knives. They own flutes that are not tunable because the flutes are the stones themselves, bored where centuries of whistled wind found a sweetness; the flute-player simply turns the rock in her hand until it speaks. Their ropes bite and smoke when a storm is near, and the one who climbs will wrap her hands in thin gloves with treads of lichen chaff to take the sting.

They tell their lives in chords. A name might be a posture—elbow lifted, jaw set, one heel dug—to suggest the set of the body against a tether in a certain wind, on a certain day, under a certain note. Another person’s name is a three-tone call that you can only sing if you were there the night the dust sang like ground whales. They do not commonly use lineages of blood; they use lineages of instrument. The child of a rippler is not a daughter but a second to their elder’s prayer. Families are ensembles; an aunt is a dampener, a cousin is an anchor, a spouse is a brightener. One takes vows not in airless rooms but with the spine of a sail-tree under one’s palm, promising to pull together—and also promising to let go at the right time. They say that tying and untying are the same art, done with different tempers.

Death is not an erasure but a tuned release. At the end a person is made very light—plates loosened, internal oils given to lickens and nerveweed; any copper lines under their skin unspooled and woven into the harps to let the voice they have made with their life continue in the nightly sing. Their last meal is a prism of salt and resin that keeps the mouth busy while the rest unwinds. Then their bodies are set where the salt saucer’s inhale and exhale is most constant, groundline hitched to ankle so no stray gust will be rude. There is no burning; there is no burial. The bones are few and soft; the bowl will take back their difference, the fine imbalances the body had kept for decades, and spread it outward. Relatives will not keep a hair or a tooth. They will hold their hands to the harps and feel for an hour the aftercurrent, noting it, tuning to it, calling it by its name until the mind can let the body go.

They keep animals the way they keep wind, not by corral but by chord. The tether-backs are not theirs but allied: in the mornings, when dew would gather if this were such a place, the tether-backs climb down the sail-trees and bring their spines to be checked. Children run tiny, careful combs down those copper tethers, cleaning off rime of airborne nitrate, tightening a ring here, slackening a ring there while the beasts’ many eyes blink with the syntax of patience. The tether-backs watch over sleeping babies with a desert calm and turn their plates toward any upset in the air. The gliders are half-domesticated and wholly free; the people tie little flags to them with bands of resin silk and read the flags when the gliders hang in a standing wave hours later, a way to write into the wind and have an answer written back across the sky. The drum-moths are crowd and companion and calendar. At certain times—say, after the bowl sings too hard one afternoon and then lies back panting under a lavender dusk—the moths will clap their shell-lamellae in a new rhythm, and that rhythm will become a people’s holiday, and the word for it will smell in the mouth like pepper and cold metal forever after.

They garden as much by inference as by touch. The crackle-lichens like to be skinned thinly and then pinched in a pattern like a carpetmaker’s plan; left that way they will make a banded oval that is perfect for sleeping on, and the oval will be repeated further out in variants until the entire slope murmurs the family pattern back at them a year later. Nerveweed they braid while living, setting it across the ground like a lazy harp that the smallest gusts will play. Ripple-bushes they plant along certain arcs where the bowl’s pressure fish like to swim; clapping their leaves, the bushes will scoop those slides of gas across one family’s harps and not another’s, and so the first family becomes known as Wave-keepers of the West Lee, and the second family must find another current to husband. Sail-trees they do not plant. Sail-trees take themselves. Sometimes, yes, a sprout will anchor in the lee of a drum and someone will set a little wall of fired gnash-brick around it to protect it from the enthusiasm of children and tether-backs—but to claim a tree is to claim a god, and that is not what they are like.

What they call gods are not personalities to praise or beg, but emergences to court. There are afternoons when the bowl decides to make itself a new animal and the world in the center heaves up into something hump-backed and deliberate, skin like tea-scum, topped with a single ripple-bush leaf that never existed before. On those afternoons the harps go quiet by consent. The people unspool their tethers, kneel, check their groundlines, and let the new thing express its need. Sometimes that need is brief—it sheds a cloud of drum-moths with a timbre none of them have heard, it lifts its one leaf to drink a trilobed rush of static, it collapses. Sometimes it stands for a day and throws off a shadow that tastes like a story. You will not find anyone naming it or making offerings. You will find them stepping aside and listening, and later, when the stain of its salts travels rust-red across the bowl, they will set new chimes at the edge of the stain to hear what the bowl will sing through the change. Children born on such days often come away with names you cannot say without singing.

They teach their children to fail gracefully. The bowl is full of small humiliations: gusts that make fools of your balance, wires that laugh you into shocks, delicate plants that smoke under an unthinking hand. You will see a father deliberately spill a little bowl of thick oil on her own wrist in front of a toddler and say, “See? It hurts. It stops.” They teach them how to stand on the ventifacts where the stones make saw-songs and not slice their feet. They strap the very smallest into bouncers made from drum-moth cases and tie those to roof poles so that when the wind plays the hut, the infants will bounce to the right rhythm and grow bones that like this place. When the children grow, they are sent up the rim to the edge with an aunt or three, and there they learn to walk into the bowl’s mouth and not be afraid. The horizon is the color of old bronze. From up there you can feel the earth’s bone through the soles of your feet, thrumming. It will put a person in you who will never again mistake stillness for safety.

Courtship is airflow. Lovers learn where the other likes the wind to hit their plates. They speak by shining and by withholding shine: the bands on their arms open and shut in narrow arcs that feed more or less charge into the night, making soft, slow signals that even a glider can read if it’s hunting a landing mood. Their quarrels are low, not hot, not the quick slap-and-smoke of some other places; they grind, ungrid, smooth, reface. They forgive by retuning an instrument by ear.

You could measure their time by a sun that only sometimes commits itself or by the steady consensual season of a planet around a star. They do that when they must. Mostly their calendars are in the body of the bowl: this is the day of the thin dust that tastes like copper coin when you put out your tongue; this is the day the tether-backs’ eyes show two pupils because the charge is all going one way; this is the week the ground sings while you are sleeping and you wake up dreaming you are on a whale. “Year” is the time between appearance of a certain gust that enters at the pumice vent singing an F twice-removed, and the next time it enters again. The old know these long rhythms not as numbers but as aches—and they keep them by voice. There are chants that record how the crystalline bands have walked a hundred paces down the saucer in a lifetime or the work it took a particular leeward family to keep a particular tree’s pitch from hurting the people who live on the opposite rim.

Visitors exist. They come in on careful days in machines that do not make sense in the bowl, their shapes wrong, their faces hidden against the kinds of spoil that are easy to breathe somewhere else. The bowl people are courteous; they offer the new ones a plate of sweetened nitrate and a little breeze shaped by hand to carry its scent to the stranger’s nose. Often the visitors are puzzled or greedy or unnerved. “This is a desert,” they say, eyes trying to look past the haze into some sharper world. “You live here by mistake.” The bowl people smile the way people who live in tight relationships with old laws smile. “Here,” they say, and guide the stranger’s hand to the harps, show them how to hum low enough that the tree up the slope must answer. They put a child’s coil in the visitor’s hand and feel the visitor’s shock when the tether-back comes down out of the sails like a small mountain and rests its plated head under that hand with a patience that could be called love in other languages. The visitors go away with dust in their boots and little sacks of resin buttons as gifts, and their minds itch for years when the wind rattles their other homes in other climates.

And then there is the bowl itself, which is not a person but is not not a person. It is the oldest singer they know. It is as near to a god as a place can be without turning into a statue. It moves, in sum and in part. It knocks knee on knee with the rest of the planet’s bone and exhales in meters, teaches by repetition and by surprise. It is not theirs; they belong to it in the sense that language belongs to its speakers and its speakers belong to it. They do not worship it because worship is one note and the bowl does not hold one note for long. They tend its throats, clean its teeth, smooth its sores, walk it back from a scream when one would cut flesh. They sleep in it like a dog sleeps against the marching foot of its person when the drum is too loud, not to make the drum stop but to be included in the beat.

At night, when the dust decides to glow, each creature carries its own small lantern. The bowl breathes in lightning and lets out an aurora soft as nap. Tether-backs’ spines go dusky blue. Gliders bank and hang like a script written in a better light. The plates of the hut roofs give back the day weakly, so the village looks as if it held a thought it is not quite sure it should say. In such light you can see the people’s charge-scars, faint constellations where a wrong handling of a rope nipped them once, a dot system read tenderly by lovers’ hands. Someone always hums. The trees hum themselves visible. The ground sings lampwarm and low as sleep.

That is who lives here: people appointed by need and temperament to be citizens of imbalance, small stewards whose power is in adjustment and consent; beasts built to hold charge and to climb; plants that can think with sails; the occasional day-thing the bowl invents out of air and grit to remind the rest of them that every agreement in this place is provisional, beautiful, and very much alive. Each event is fat and filled and spaced with enough in-between that wind can run and thunder can walk, and a single tree can throw its whole body into a note that shows you your bones. They live by that; they live under it and within it, and they let it name them again whenever it pleases.
