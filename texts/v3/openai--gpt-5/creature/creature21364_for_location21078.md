The first inhabitants here are not animals. They are the behaviors the place has taught itself: water with its long patience and its refusal to fall when it can walk; stone with its appetite for film and rim; air with its habit of being pulled across cool and giving something up. They are busy always. They make new edges where yesterday there were none. They breathe in and out on a schedule older than months and close enough to count on your fingers. If you stay, you begin to recognize them. You end up greeting them the way you would a neighbor you never speak to: by hearing them in the corridor and standing aside a little, because they have work to do.

Then come the small citizens you can name. The algae lay their green nap down along every purposeful seam, setting tables for mineral to sit at. Lichens scrawl their arithmetic wherever a face dries long enough to bear writing. Liverworts comb out along a seeping line like hair in a part. The cushions of sedge are the city’s soft furniture. They hold the only true soil, and they sit down wherever there is the ten millimeters of patience a summer will provide.

Amphibians know this address by heart. Newts slip themselves from shade to wetted lip as if exchanging pleasantries with one resident after another. There is a little toad that is the color of honeyed chalk; you will think you see it only by mistake the first year and, the next, you will realize you have been watched all along from the fold under a shelf. Their skin catches a little of the ever-hung moisture and they do not hurry unless a shadow passes. When the mist runs thicker through an atrium, the bell-choir you heard once will tune itself and go on until it stops as if someone turned a page. Midges birth by the thousand and vanish by noon; the swallows and swifts write fast cursive through them and then are gone, bowls of their nests glued under eaves with spit, each home a sentence that can be read even when the family is travelling. Bees take the lee of a weepy wall. They press wax against mineral in a way that makes stone accept it. A hive glows here and there—the small gold the place allows itself. Stoats come like a question asked and never repeated. Ravens pass farther up, not to belong but to remark.

Nothing much is loud. The loudness is saved for a single long word sent once a day as an upstream terrace gives over; you can set a watch by that word. It wakes others. It calls, and the damp people come.

They are a local species of person rather than a tribe, if that distinction matters. If there is lineage they wear it light, the way the stone wears yesterday’s dew when today’s has started to form. They do not paint their faces. Chalk and lime do that for them in the creases of their skin, no matter what they intend. They are not tall: the ceilings advise a posture. Their calves are ropey from stepping every riser this table offers, risers low enough to take without thinking and infinite in number. They go barefoot or pad their feet with felt the color of seepage, soles soft enough to hear the hollows underfoot through the skin. The city speaks through those drums, and they prefer to listen with more than ears.

Their hair coils and is kept coiled, braided with wicks of plant down and twistings of sedge, the whole then brushed with a solution made from the same white that makes rims, so the braids keep their shape better than a woven thing should. When they stoop under a low balcony the tips of that hair tick the underside and come away beaded. The beads do not fall. Nothing here is in a hurry to be lost.

In daylight they look bleached into the background until they move. In twilight they shine a little, edges of clothing damp enough to reflect back whatever moon there is. Their garments are quiet: layers of woven sedge and willow-bark fibers where the dwarf trees will spare a quartering, waxed with honey where the place is generous, and in time mineralized into something halfway between cloth and crust. A sleeve might grow its own cuff over the years, a shawl can take on a scalloped hem if a drip always finds it at one point. They play this to their advantage. There are veils hung across certain gullies not to hide a face but to make a comb for fog to catch on. When you see these, you will see bowls below, shallow as a lap, waiting to be filled by a night’s worth of thinking.

They speak when they must. Balconies are spaced for a murmur and they keep to that, because any louder dislodges and forgets. They are better at other languages: the measure between two drips, an emphasized touch on a lip to feel how thick it has gotten, the sight of a darker gloss where someone’s breath just passed, the unheard way an ankle rises over a rim to spare it. They watch the plateau’s breath—its in and out—and time their walking, their washing, their setting-of-wicks to the third whisper that draws fog through a particular arcade. They own, if they own anything, addresses and relationships. A home here is not “12” but “Within the two-note basin up from the long right angle and beneath the eastward breath, where algae began making milk last summer.” When they tell each other where to meet, they name a dripping by how it hesitates on its lip, or the scarf of lichen that writes across a certain bright wall.

They keep ledgers, yes, but not of trade. They keep ledgers of growth. The ledgers live as arrangements, not marks: a line of knotted cord hung under a gable at a certain angle to meet a drip; a thread of hair caught intentionally where a bead pauses so that, in a season, a new rim will begin with the thickness of a hair; a row of sedge-wrapped pebbles pinned just so along a curtain to coax it to thicken into a shelf. They seed moss where a trickle needs taming, press it in with their palms, the grain of the skin making its own faint inscription. When the wash goes through, that daily tide, they walk with it. They lift a slab with their shoulder when the night has loosened it with ice, set it down somewhere it can become something else in a few years. They know how much strength to spend on rock when the rock is half unhooked already and does, as much as stone can, want what they want.

They do not cut much, and when they do it is with patience: a season of wicking to dry an edge so a flake will crack along a joint the bedrock suggested; a winter of layering fibers between two lips to persuade them to grow together into a bridge. It is not craftsmanship the way the lowlands would count it. It is a kind of gardening with air and stone and time. Most of their tools rot. What they keep, they keep because it obeys the plateau’s program: bone needles their grandparents found in a stoat’s midden, waxed thread, a twist of copper traded from far below and used under the same economy as honey, always on the edge of being forgotten.

They tend animals the way they tend everything: by setting the conditions. Hives are started under the safest weeps, sheltered from the daily wash and given the dull warmth of a tufa wall that held sun since noon. They do not smoke the bees. There is nothing to burn. They greet them with the slowest movements a person can manage, and they harvest enough gold to be a promise that sweetness exists on high tables. They rest a palm in the swarm if the bees will allow it, and they wait to be allowed. Swifts they do not catch. Sometimes, after a close season, they will find a dropped egg in a trickle and set it back, turning it in their hands with the same care they use for a bead of water.

They eat what the plateau birthdays. Algae set thick in a pan becomes a pale curd. They take it up with a sieve made of the long hairs of sedge and spread it on a sun-warm shelf to tighten. It tastes of the place: a little mineral, deeply green, a sweetness so thin it is more like the idea of sugar than sugar itself. They mix this with seeds from the cushions, ground on a slab slick with centuries of other hands, and press it into cakes the size of a palm. Mid-morning they raise veils of horsehair—the only import that seems to belong here—across a alley and gather what small protein the midge clouds offer, knowing the dance will be over by noon. Juniper gives them a bitter that makes food feel like food. In winter they steep a lichen that can be made to almost resemble a broth if you close your eyes. Generosity swings through rarely on wings: a stoat dead of age in a hollow, a raven’s cast-off, a travelling lowlander with a pouch of salt who will be listened to and fed and slept near for the price of a story.

They burn little. Flame here blows itself out with a sigh and leaves a stink. When they must they burn honeycomb after the last crop has been taken, over a gully where the smoke will write a line against some northern wall and teach them what the wind is thinking. Mostly they warm each other. A sleeping ledge shared by three, under a shelf that has learned their heads and sagged down just a little over the years, is enough. The stone is thin as a drum in places and warm there by day. They bank their own heat in hollows the way water banks itself in basins. They oil their skins with wax and leaf fat, and the lime dust the air carries for free does the rest.

Children count drips in their first seasons instead of sheep. They are taught to stop up their ears and feel the wash go through in their feet, the same way they are taught not to step hard near a drum-floor. There is a game in the long straightways where sound travels: two stand a horizon apart and whisper, and the ribbon of air will carry the words if the place is listening. This can take most of a day. It is never wasted. Another game is to catch the exact moment a rim becomes a lip—there is a way it throws light back when it is almost something—and to breathe on it and see if your breath will be in it, glinting, when you return weeks later. Adolescents test each other by sending messages in wet: they set a drip to fall at an interval that, if counted inside an hour, will say “Meet me when the second whisper begins.” Lovers prove themselves by learning each other’s addresses.

They are slow to leave and ready to. The horizon doesn’t often ask for them and most seasons they don’t ask for it. But a few times a year a person carrying a skein of fair waxed thread over one shoulder will go down by one of the clean escarpments—right-angled joints stepping themselves into a staircase as old as decisions—and return with what the plateau does not make: a length of hairier fiber, a knife with a keen edge borrowed for a summer, some salt wet from the sea. They bring back, too, stories of trees that talk too much and of rain that falls as if it didn’t know how to use its legs. They tell these and everyone laughs softly for the joy of misunderstanding.

Their dead are not hidden. After the breath has gone out and not come back with the morning, they wash the body with moss and lay it under a shelf that will drip. They seed the shelf with a darker algae so that the film it lays down will have a difference in it. In a year there is a gloss; in ten there is a naming: the lip fattens and takes the curve of a familiar palm. Nobody carves. You can read the person with your fingertips.

They do not think they built the city. They do not say they tend it: tending implies a keeper and a kept, and dividing the plateau that way makes no sense to them. They say, if you ask badly, that they keep themselves to the plateau’s ways. They say, if you stay long enough and learn what asking well feels like here, that they live inside the ongoing and have learned to be useful to it. If there are saints they are not human. They are places where a crack stopped being a crack and turned back into program; a balcony where light always pools and so swifts always nest; the sound a certain basin makes when it plays all four of its notes at once.

You will not see many of them from far above. The table looks empty from that height, an even mineral with a moving skin. If you know how to look, you will notice a brighter bead where a veil has been hung, a darker punctuation where a mouth has breathed on a lip as it passed, a small figure stepping effortlessly up stairs no one made but everyone uses. They are there, held near the stone by gravity and by choice, practicing the old rules: make agreements at the right scale; prefer walking to falling; keep time with what lives longest. They live here in a way that smudges the difference between living in and being part of. When the daily wash goes through and hushes back, if you are on a balcony opposite another balcony and someone is near enough to murmur, you will hear them. The rest of the day you will think the plateau is uninhabited, and you will not be right.
