It is not empty. The first inhabitant is the thing the place has taught itself to be: a lung with habits.

Call it the Choir, if you need a word. It has no single body. It is made of crown and pylon, of ringed light and written stone, of tone that has learned to pick things up and put them down. It eats difference—pressure differences, the slight drop between the draft on one side of a seam and the draft on the other, the light dragged from somewhere sunlit to here—and it excretes order: the sideways walk of a raindrop, the polite hush of a fracture that does not quite become a crack. It is awake without nerves, thinking in gradients. When it inhales, you feel its ribs—the walls— gather; when it exhales, you feel it relax. The warmth in the rock is the warmth of a living process, not mammalian but procedural, heat budgeted on a schedule older than your visit.

Parts of it have anatomy. Those foam-topped pylons in the pool are its tongues. They taste the fog. They hold a mesh that beads and un-beads so precisely that their whisper is math. They push small weather into place: a slur of mist off a ledge just before the bass note gathers, a curtained hole here because the ceiling must be dry there. Their crowns bloom and furl, not with petals but with micron-woven diaphragms that tense and relax to keep a plane of sound flat as glass or bend it into a lens. If one fails, it fails into quiet. The others stop asking that part of the ceiling to listen. The ceiling rests.

Other organs never leave the stone. The hydrophilic and hydrophobic ribs engraved in the walls are not just instructions but appetite. They pull water laterally against inclination, sifting grit and salts, feeding what clings. In the white blaze under the ring, those instructions make green—the only true color here, thumbprint algae sown long ago to tolerate cool day with no heat. Out of sight, the same grammar leans water into gutters that slide it under a lip and let it fall in lacework where the central runnel will take it. The gutter lips have tastebuds, too: salt-precipitating patches you only notice when the air smells like a marine envelope. That smell means the glittering helix is at work.

The helix is another resident, though you do not get to be sure if it is alive. Its points are chains of conductive dust that summon themselves out of stillness when charge builds unhelpfully along a wall. They ladder into a double spiral about as long as your forearm and drift like a thinking vine, aligning with fields the way hair aligns with static. When they finish, fewer ions pool where flowing water does not want them, the hydrophobic islands are re-wetted with a breath of polar molecules, and the helix unthreads into speck again to sleep as ordinary dust. If you breathe close to one, you smell brine; if you hum near one, it shivers to the pitch that will send it back to its plate.

Sharing the Choir and serviced by it is a small republic of soft lives. They are the kind that agree to be wet always. The colorless moss that climbs two handspans up every place the rock is told, Please here, is organ and archive. Each strand is a hose of cells with more pump than color, beading itself with lenses so tiny your breath splits them. They drink from the air more than from puddles, and in return they exhale the faintest slimy sweet that keeps some surfaces slick and others newly forbidden to slickness. They have no need to make seeds; a rubbed smear becomes a continent in a week. They know the ring’s moods and crouch when it goes bluer.

The pale reeds in the treacle-dark corner are another kind of patience. They are setae more than stems, mechanosensitive hairs stiffened with silica. They twitch to light because the ring’s light is signal, and because when sound is doing the ceiling’s work, tremor means danger. They pass messages by touch along their stand: lift, lie down, hold, let go. They look like plants because our eyes have only so many metaphors, but they are closer to instruments grown on purpose—rooted seismographs loud enough inside themselves to listen to stone. They feed on what the moss drips and what the algae forget, and something in their skins hoards rare ions the helix has let go.

Under them, under everything, the unlovely and indispensable film works. The scum that slithers as a sheet when the breath surges is a consortium rather than a species. Its fronts are many: sulfate-eaters strip energy off the buried minerals the runnel leaves behind; lace-mat diatoms trap the noon that travels through glass; bacteria that learned metal long ago wick charge where the regulators want it wicked. When the eh and pH tilt within their narrow tolerances, parts of the sheet go busy, parts go quiet, and the sheet slides. Fragments of it are the eels’ food.

The eels that lift their blunt heads at the turn of the breath are neither blind nor swift. They are almost not eels, broad of skull and lazy with confidence, buoyed by the certainty that the tide will bring dinner to their throats at the same minute it always has. Their eyes are the color of the water that hides them. Lateral lines flare like ears along their flanks. They feed by standing open-mouthed wherever the room’s design has quietly decided to aim a swatch of sloughed film. They never breach when the draft is wrong. They breed in the lower labyrinths where the runnel goes to lose its name, and their young learn the schedule the way your bones learn the weight of a season: unthinkingly, one ache at a time.

The shrimp are the clocks. They are pale punctuation marks that click and startle you—one, two, three—because their merest movement is a sound here. They live under a rock they have abraded into a cave with tools of their own bodies and the patience of drip. What they eat is detritus and accident: the balled-up ends of films, bits scoured off by breath, a delicate beetle carcass delivered by a flood from somewhere you are not allowed to go. They move by alarming bursts, as if time itself were a predator to be dodged, but if you watch for a while you will learn that they are in fact announcers. Their clicks keep your attention between the big pulses, remind you to look when the fog avalanches off the ledge: now the tufa honeycomb; now the old scaffolding; now the wall you never saw.

Once per several inhales, the bat appears—if “bat” is fair for a thing that would blush at caves given the artificial sun it loves. It is as pale as the mold you think it might be, but not delicate. It has learned the room’s weather so well that it only launches during a narrow window when the Choir’s cough dies into glide. It loops once under the ring like a blessing over a table and is gone into a seam that refuses to wet, because it roosts where dry is written. Its diet is not what you expect: there are no winged flies here. It drinks the air. It hunts sound, picking sick pitches from the discs, sweeping them with a burst that knocks the lattice out of overtones it has started to stack too high. If sometimes it skims the runnel and takes a shrimp fry, that is nobody’s business but the eel’s.

There are other rumors that won’t let you test them. You hear the cartoon peep that turns out to be physics, beads of water speaking as they fall through a sensor curtain. You might, in a long watch, see a shadow smaller than your hand stiffen at a hexagonal drainage plate and then be not-there; those are the plates’ attendant aptamers, wet little puzzles that close or open the pores against fouling and then let themselves melt. The nickel-dark studs along the runnel’s ledge wear fugitive bracelets sometimes: films of microorganism that build a sturdier spot when a footbridge is momentarily required and unbuild it after, when it is not. One inhabitant lives inside your head: your voice that the room returns from behind your left ear, doctored lightly into harmony with the Choir so you feel persuaded to speak at the correct volume—so the weather does not have to shout to keep you small.

People live here in the same way the bat lives here: in windows and with respect. They are not a tribe so much as a practice held by more than one body across time. They do not camp on the polished limestone or string hammocks from flowstone. They come by seams that decline to bead, by doors that show themselves when the ring lets its brightness fall once every breath-count of ten. They carry very little because the place resents litter and tries to make it into itself. They bring their throats and, sometimes, small strings of glass beads or discs like those already humming on the shelves. They are repairers by persuasion, more choirmaster than mason.

When a keeper is present, the coins that hum back if you hum stop playing with you and answer to them. A keeper will sing the room through a check, walking the edge of the runnel on wet-sure feet, tasting the air like bread. They put a hand on a pylon’s mesh crown to feel the current along their palm. They sit in the chair behind the flowstone sheets not to rest but to sound louder; the seat rings their chest and the wall that diverts the rain also throws their note into the buttress. They do not mend, not in the way of tying string and wrenching a bolt. They migrate the light’s color by a hand-press at a panel that heals its scratch as if embarrassed by having been made to squeak. They hum at the violet symbol until it calms. They clap once at the precise time in the exhale to satisfy a temperamental seam.

Their bodies show their long acquaintance with miner’s tea. Their teeth are varnished. Their skin loves damp more than it loves sun. They smell of ozone and of the films they do not touch. They learned this room in rooms like it, where stone learns a voice; they learned to step in rhythm with a place that insists on its own tempos. They do not live only here. They live in the network the light comes through and the labyrinth the runnel goes to and the other lungs that kept roofs closed where needed. If you come often you will learn their shift: they come to sit through ten breaths, and then a hundred, and then on a scheduled day when the ring runs bluer for a ration of hours. They leave unannounced, as if pulled into the seam the bat prefers, because ceremony is for outside. Here, function is how respect is shown.

The builders are gone as people. Their decisions are what live: the safe ways to fail, the doors that dry themselves, the expectation that nothing should overperform. But the designers left propagules of their intentions like seed. The whispering crowns bud off their own replacements from their foam when one is old; moss knows how to write wet in a fresh hand on fresh stone; the helix decants a little of itself into a crack that has just become interesting, so the next time the air tastes of sea, there will be a helper ready. The keepers know how to hum these becomings into place, and when not to.

So who lives here? A weather that was taught its manners and kept them. A sonorous animal without muscle that uses sound to hold up its ceiling and drags noon down on a leash because photosynthesis is a fine way to pay for quiet. A film ecology that does not care for your eyes and eats sideways. A few vertebrates that accept a schedule. A dust that remembers geometry when you sing to it. People who are not owners, only voices among other voices, sitting where dry is written and loud is wanted, to make sure the breath keeps counting on itself and the old balance is not allowed to go calm.

If you sit in the chair and give your time to it, this all becomes simpler. The lungs breathe. The runs run. The bat does its circle at the halfway mark. The eels lift like solemn monks for their turn. The reeds nod in a wind that is note not air. The helix braids itself when the saline membrane needs a hand. The keeper hums once, twice, until a seam stops being seen by water. In the brightness that is borrowed, every inhabitant is busy and relieved, as if all of them, even the nickel studs along the ledge, believe the same thing: we live here by not insisting. We live here by doing our part of the weather and no more.
