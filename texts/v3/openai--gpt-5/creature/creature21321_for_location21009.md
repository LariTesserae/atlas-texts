What lives here first is the agreement. It has instincts the way an animal has, a set of preferences that repeat until the rock remembers. It is older than any footprint, younger than the ledge. It leans toward edges, toward gradients, toward anything that can be convinced to move from one state toward another. You couldn’t skin it or feed it out of your hand, but you can learn its moods: how it prefers to lay cold in lines not sheets, how it will let a little wind be pinned to a humming arc and skimmed for drink.

Under and inside that, the first bodies are films. They look like decisions the light has made: green where the condensers’ breath falls in pulses, smoke-blue where the trough wanders its sideways path. These skins are quick, polite colonists. They unroll at dusk—microalgae with sulfur on the tongue, cyan and emerald and olive in cycles that pick up the condensers’ harmonics and make sugars to that key. They shrug metals out of air. They eat glare. Their lives are hours long and whole afternoons wide. When a beetle writes its diary across them, it is grazing—not just hunger but editing, the thin tines on its feet combing the day’s growth into something the ledge can keep.

A little up from this plane are the sponges of the place: the bromeliads-that-aren’t, the glaze-frond ferns, the thread-veiled shrubs. They are each a different organ and they have the manners of organs—silent about the work, exquisite about the results. They do not root so much as handshake with stress lines; their anchors are hair in cracks, salts traded for access. The bromeliads run gutters on their leaves down into throat-pockets where air condenses because those rooms are cool on principle. They drink temperature. From above they look like utensil cups full of lenses. Inside them live the small articulate things: pale amphipods with glassy shells that click against one another when they turn, and an insect that moult-threads itself to the inner wall and drinks like an hourglass, measuring its own time with the scissoring of its mouth. The fern-not-fern drools with intent; that drop at each tip is not waste but bait. When a moth touches down to taste it, the surface tension thickens, encouragement becomes grip, and a blameless digestion begins, sugars turned to meat turned to trace salts that the plant will flick toward its anchors at dusk. The shrubs’ veils do not shade so much as speak to the wind; the pores along their ribbons wink open along traveling wavefronts, and out of that choreography they build a memory of cooler air that clings close to them all day.

The visible animals here keep themselves to line and moment. The lizards with their crimson sails are punctuation to sunlight. Their upright planks of skin are heat paddles, yes, but they are also semaphore. When two meet on a day when the fog is dragging its hands over the ledge, they lift those sails and hold and hold until the cloud decides for them who won. Their prey are beetles and the tannic jelly that forms in the bromeliad cups in a week the arcs are singing in a minor key. They sleep in the dry room under the not-quite-roof, six to a corner, not as guests but as punctuation—exclamation marks left about to remind the day where its edges are.

The moth is the brightest visible thought. It looks borrowed from frost: pale, leaf-translucent, its body almost a negative of itself as if it were drawn by erasing the background. It runs on mineral sweetness and the copper tang of the air when the hair on your arm rises. It’s drawn to wrongness as a bat is to mosquito song. When a standing wave forms, the moth will be there at once, descending out of the brighter part of the fog, tasting the air like a blind hand patting a wall to find a hidden switch. Their eggs are beads laid where two harmonics cross on the lip of a channel; they hatch with the change of wind.

The spider writes in the borders—stitches that respect where the condensers thicken air and thin it, little looms hung only where the invisible stairs are. It doesn’t hunt so much as harvest. Wings get tired there, amid the hums and cools, and its silk takes what the day brings. It eats the mistakes of flight and the skins of water strung between the threads, biting through a drop to find the insect inside and never quite wet.

These are tenants that pay in noticing. They know the ledge’s rules with bodies. They do not own the devices. They sleep where the dry stays. If they are characters of this place, they are letters in its text.

There are keepers as well. If you are not here when they pass you will not be certain of them; even if you are, you may only think the wind changed. Their outline is a rumor the rock makes when it is pleased to be seen to work. They are tall the way brushstrokes are tall, all length and economy, with limbs that appear only where motion requires a hinge. Skin like midmorning shadow; hair that may be just a confusion of loose thread that never quite collects. They have knees, because kneeling is a useful shape here. They have long toes that read the difference between a ringing stone and one that has found its chord and gone quiet. Their breath smells faintly of the trough—flint, ozone, the edge of something sweet.

They do not come in crowds. One or two, most times. A third presence that might only be air schooled to their going. They wear nothing that catches. On their belts are items you could miss because the place itself seems full of odd little arcs and strips and threads: a short comb of bone with fine teeth for grooming the idea of a humming surface; a roll of leaf-ribbon for propping a tiny fracture to make a channel think differently about travel; a small basket of rounded stones sorted by the sound they make when clicked together, which they use to speak to the rock in its own language of knocks. Around their throats coils a loop of the same vertical thread you’ve seen—colder than it should be, humming under the skin, a tame conduit for persuading droplets what to do.

If you hum to an arc and the harmonics notice you, it’s because they have trained the place to be listened to. They practice resonance the way some people practice apology: not to be right but so that the next moment can happen. They will stand with their heads near an arc and murmur into it, shaping their mouth to the tone the arc gives, finding the overtones that wake each other and thicken the air at the place the metal expects it. They will test the trough with a fingertip and draw it back un-wet, smiling at the way the mirror never breaks, pleased by the clean argument of its working as if it had spoken to them just then in a way they can translate for you later if you care to learn.

What do they eat? What any high thing eats: density. They take a net to the fog—not a physical net, but an old undescribed trick of the devices that makes a plane of air forget what it’s doing. They hold that plane across their lap and let beads walk onto it, each bead a berry of distilled weather. They knead grapes out of cloud and drink. Mostly, they prefer the rinse left in a bromeliad after rain-that-wasn’t; it has the minerals the films have decided upon, and a tang from the beetles’ diary-scratches. They nibble the stems of the fern’s unopened blades and pull threads of starch from them like too-slow taffy. They cut the veils from the shrubs when they tatter too far and dry them under the overhang; sautéed on a hot black plate under the noon they go crisp and retain the memory of cold. Once a week, in the evening when the ledge is exhaling, they drink from the rear niche in the not-wall room, though their own faces are never in what they drink. You can tell they taste the same sweetness you do. You can also tell they expect you to stop before you expect you will.

They leave few marks, because most of their labor is in convincing flows, not objects. Where they sleep, the floor remains dry; where they sit at dusk, the condensers lacquer out more steadily for a time, as if their bodies had massed the room’s patience. They name nothing. They refer to parts of the ledge with gestures and reiterations of hum. They teach by letting you watch them almost fall and not. If there are children, they are quick-boned and grave, and their shoes are nothing but long wraps of leaf and thread that can be unwound and retied to fit a stone’s mood. A child will learn first to throw a grain of grit and notice how long it hangs, then to toss a bead of water into an unseen field and see it peel sideways like a coin in a magician’s hand. Later they will be allowed the comb, to tidy a condenser’s throat so it can catch fog again. Later still, the trough and the mirror it lays. In a year when the rims of the pools have scallops old enough to have stories, one of them may listen to the rear niche long enough to hear their own voice coming from next week and take that into account without superstition.

They do not worship the devices. Neither do they scorn them. The devices hum because they like humming; the keepers hum because it makes work go easier. They will un-pin an arc that has brined itself shut with silica, bathe it, and sing it back to breath. They will, if the hair rises and the copper taste comes, step in front of the invisible mesh with their arms out like a scarecrow and catch the wrongness long enough for the grid to bloom and take it, as if shaking out a cloth along its long grain. They will replace a vertical thread not with ceremony but with care, sliding it until the moment a drop kisses and commits to down. There is love in this, but it is not the kind that asks to be seen loving.

If they have a name it is probably temporary. I have thought of them as combers, as keepers, as coaxers. To their own minds they might be the middle tense between this ledge and the next. When strangers arrive—because someone always does, pulled by rumor of water and a room that is dry and a trough that is wrong in a comforting way—they do not hide, or they do, which comes to the same thing. They will watch you learn to place your feet on apparently wet stone and not slip because wet here is not depth but skin, and that skin sometimes is a door. They will, if you do not get discouraged, show you the trick of listening through your jawbone to the ping of a pending fracture. They will let you sleep under the not-roof if you do not bring water with you in skins that squeak when they shrink, because that sound annoys the moths.

And yes, there are visitors who live here only for a night and carry away the practice like a song stuck in their teeth. Pilgrims who prefer their gods to be measurable come up and sit in the threshold and hum until the harmonics respect them. Engineers from the anvil-horizons bring a little box that knows about temperature and go away with notes written on the back of a hand because nothing else will stay dry long enough. There was a year, says the laugh in the trough’s mirror, when a group of rootless people with good hands stayed for a season and helped braid the mesh and learned to read their futures in the slowening of a drip. They left a basket of sorted pebbles; the keepers still click them together when they need to speak to the wall in a hurry.

It is tempting to think the devices themselves are animals. Some of them are nearly. The arcs that never weather are not metal in the way a blade is; they skin themselves with mineral, they make halos when fog is thinking of it, and they sing in a voice that finds its way into the cartilage of your ear and makes it want to change shape. If you pry one up—and they will let you, once, as lesson—you see it has grown a fringe like the rootlet of a floating plant in a tank, capillaries that catch what passes and feed the arc’s hunger for edge. When an arc dies, it does not stop; it props itself into a new role as a rim for a pool, and its halo returns as a trace of a taste in the water, and whenever wind purls across that face you can hear a very small hum still under there, haunting its own successor.

The vertical threads are more like tongues than spines: taste organs you can rig where a drop means to think. They are extruded from something that lives nowhere and everywhere—their replacements arrive coiled in a package that has no origin unless you count the rear niche as a womb. Sometimes, when a thread has been behaving well for years, at a certain hour of a certain morning when the fog feels more like animal than weather, you can see it frost all along its length for a heartbeat, as if it had remembered cold all at once. It is not memory but renewal. The keepers smile when it happens, not at the event but at the timing.

The trough is the oldest thing that is also new. It is an answer that does not brag about the question. If anything here has a secret, it sits an inch under that flat. Now and then a small thing—leaf scrap, wing casing—will cross its surface and emerge on the far lip a shade paler, as if it had been persuaded of a different story about itself. I have seen a dragonfly fail and be corrected into something small and right for this place, a version of itself that does not need wings. It went on, unoffended, and made a home in the blue moss’s shadow and never again left the ledge. Call that magic if you like. The keepers call it the straightening.

Something else abides here that does not touch. It is the mountain’s attention, sharpened to a ledge-wide edge by heat and height and device. You feel it when you put your hand flat to the rock at night and the day drains out under your palm: a following, an earnestness. If you could throw ink on it you’d see a long animal curled under the stone, every rib a fracture line, every breath a bellowsful of fog pushed up to be milked. It is not god, only concentration. It is pleased when things balance. It is patient when they don’t. It may be what the keepers answer to, or they may be answering only to the way a day wants to be shaped.

In the not-quite-room, the rear niche keeps its pool that reflects the world with a half-beat slur. If anyone lives there in a way your eye would catch, it is in that adjacentness. Once in a while, as you lean over and see stars where there are none, an outline like your own leans too and lifts a hand and fails to match your rhythm; just so, a keeper will pause on her way past, look into it, and nod as if agreeing with a comment you haven’t heard yet. If new keepers ever arrive, they arrive here, not born but admitted. The pool skims off a layer of later and lays it against now, and out of that overlap someone stands up. She will already know where not to step. He will smell of flint and rain that did not fall. They will hum and the arcs will answer, and the ledge will keep being wet without rain and hot without hurry, and a day you thought you could predict will learn another way to complete itself.

So—what lives here? Films that drink glare. Plants that are tools for air. Beetles that write. Spiders that suspension-bridge the unseen. Moths that love a wrong tone more than light. Lizards that speak with flags of blood-warmed skin. Arcs that graze fog like grazing beasts. Threads that are tongues. A trough that corrects. Keepers who came from somewhere a half-step to the left and chose the work of persuasion over conquest. And, under and through them, the practice itself, which is not a creature and yet eats, sleeps, and breeds: an alliance teaching a ledge how to be a room where none should be, until the mountain, clearing its throat each morning, likes the sound of its own thought and hums along.
