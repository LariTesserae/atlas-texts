First, the citizen with papers already in its veins: water. It does not arrive; it continues. It keeps addresses in every crack and file in every pore, a resident whose body is a route and whose route is a body. It registers its presence in the book of pressure, writes ordinances in wet cursive over concrete and chalk, enforces with patience what gravity decrees. It knows every room at once, announces itself as thunder and as a rumor. It pays no toll. Its only borders are the ones it teaches.

Then the rock, not the old, finished kind that pretends at certainty, but stone still at school—soft-chalk in places, shelly in others, banded with young ambitions and secret springs. It is the archivist here. It takes dictation from drip and film and trickle, turns them into minutes that read as rims and veils. Its scribe-work is visible: the lint of calcite knitting itself into lace where the roof remembers rain, the nub of a stalactite beginning to be a vocabulary of daggers in another age. The walls do not stand; they bulge like slow animals lying down. They sweat. They revise their edges. They learn.

Air is a moving creature that has chosen to live indoors. It has lungs and keeps to a schedule—drawn and pushed by fans, exploring culverts and vaults with a tolerance for salt, for rust, for bat breath. It carries the country of sound on its back. It hangs mist like a beaded curtain between everything and everything else. If you could seed it with dust, you would see currents and eddies the way fish see rivers, and you would recognize its favorite corners by the places bats prefer, the places spiders decide to throw a bridge.

Light lives here on rations and shifts. Daylight comes down like a coin tossed through a slot, round and dear, staying only as long as the angle allows. Its diet is clay-filtered, and where it lands, small, bleached congregations find a doctrine: a liverwort fat and satisfied, a fern that looks like the memory of a fern, sedge blades too pallid to be proud. Electric light is a different animal—a domesticated star with a hood. It visits on chain, hums over a damp yard, gives algae a square plot to practice greenness. It is fair for as long as the conduit is faithful, then it is gone without apology. Bats and swifts learn the bell-times. Algae make their own calendar under the lamp as if kneeling at a patron saint.

Machines live down here the way cattle live in a valley: bound to place, chewing through the day, part of the scenery until the hour when you need them and then nothing else matters. The big fans are lungs proper, opaline with mist where a light finds them, patient in their turning. A pump house—brick-walled as if nostalgic for a street—keeps a heart under cover; you hear it more than you see it, an oath kept in meters of head and kilowatts. Turbines sleep inside cages with wet whiskers of alarm wire and wake in hymn when the river asks for a use to be made of its attention. Gauges are eyes that do not blink, glass filmed but the needle sure. These are not invaders; they are domestications of physics, harness beasts with steel bones and rubber tongues. They sweat like anyone else. They bead. They dream of shore power when the storms come. If they have pets it is the men and women in hi-vis who arrive with keys and touch them where they like to be touched, oiling, listening, putting a finger to a housing to feel the health of a hum.

The city at the scale of a thumbnail thrives on what the big machines and the sovereign water make possible. There are mats the color of coins—iron bacteria conjuring orange out of groundwater, laying rust down in delicate skins that tear like pastry when a frog foot lifts. There are glints of petro-rainbow on quiet puddles that turn out to be colonies of microbe as much as of any spill. Nitrifiers and denitrifiers, slime-sculptors and mildew chalk, kingdoms that dine on ammonia and make it art. Filaments climb the undersides of beams where wood passes by. If an old pallet drifts into a sump, a white skin takes it overnight, then teeth of fungal fruit come hunting for air. The smell of geosmin—earth made audible in nose—is a species of resident, too.

The first named animals in your ear will be the agitators: frogs. They sit like green thumbs and brown knuckles on every bracket and bolt, throw their throats into the weather that isn’t weather, and run the bulletin service of the place without pause. Some are the color of leaves in an old photograph; some are as matte as wet clay. Their eggs come as tremulous necklaces looped over a submerged rod, as foamy batch in a corner where the flow is confused. Tadpoles graze like little sheep on the black writing of algae along a dish, the very water shaped by their daily appetite. They grow legs where no one can see, emerge to sit on the cold rim of a measuring staff striped in centimeters, kings of a scale they don’t read.

In the quiet water at the edges, something serpentine resolves and unresolves: eels, long as your forearm, writing in cursive under the parapet. They are guardians of a law you only dimly feel—the line between water and air. They patrol that nation’s border with their heads, tasting it, knowing not the map of this place but the texture. Their eyes are not appointed to the job; their skin and noses do most of the heavy living. They are older tenants than the concrete. They arrive by miracles so routine you forget to be impressed—by culverts, by roots, by raining nights. They take what falls, what slips, what grows plentiful. They are sages that never sit still.

Blind fish flicker in the runnels like coached shadows promised in the guidebooks to other caves. They are pale in places and in other places the color of water seen at a thickness. Some have eyes like museum buttons covered over with a film, vestiges small as a courtesy. They hover just where the flow intends to be slow, mouths opening and closing as if trying a language made of taste. In certain older pits, if the valley on top is the right valley, you may find a crayfish the color of absence itself, frozen as a thought until the underside of your boot scuffs grind a pebble and then away, sideways, a prehistoric shrug. In the shelves of current, amphipods—shrimp like punctuation marks—jerk in commas. Planarians move like slow exhalations under stones the size of a book. Worms make a twin language in mud, written in cast and tunnel.

The aerial districts belong to things that fly and to the webs that catch the thought of flying. Bats prefer the high seams above the fans where warmth eddies, congregations hung like folded gloves. If you stand for any time, their consensus lifts and resettles with a paper rustle that feels like someone turning history with care a few galleries over. They eat what the sumps farm—midges in their blackout swarms, the pale flies at the lamps, a moth lost down a shaft. Their leavings are a geology of their own, sift piling in soft cones, a mineral that was a mosquito yesterday. Beetles and isopods mine it without knowing they are miners, small pale workers with a noise no one hears, a fairness no one supervises. In the warm, permissive climate of guano you find things that never leave the light of a headlamp—white mites with opinions, velvet molds that lift like breath when you pass.

At dusk above, when the coin of day finds just the right slot, a commutation takes place. Midges rise where water thinks about being still, a great soft weather in the chamber, and in the shafts the swifts arrive—topworld knives deciding to stitch down into this cloth for a few fast minutes. They skim the ceiling like a promise delivered and, laughing in the way only air-knowers can, stick broken-bowl nests under rare dry ledges the way they would under bridge girders. They belong to the upworld but draw a dotted line here, a seasonal appointment. Spiders write patient essays in the lamp hoods about what this means, beading their arguments with dew.

Rats are here because there is a here. They pass into the scene out of pipes and from behind the personal darknesses of valves and cinderblocks. Their whiskers make a map you cannot draw. Their feet know the difference between slick and grip better than you do. They ferry seeds that believe in light along routes that do not. They lap from eddies and make their own. They die, sometimes, tucked behind a gate, and their dissolving is another economy. They are neither plague nor pest down here in the way they are in a pantry; they are the mammal version of water’s logic—taking the route of least refusal until it becomes a trail, nibbling a corner until the corner becomes a path.

There are, in some valleys, salamanders hugging seep-lines like punctuation marks in a very old sentence—orange-throated newts ghosting out from under a lip of rock to hang, delicate hands spread as a saint’s, in the thin outfall of a trickle. Slugs make an alphabet of gloss on the undersides of ledger-stones. Snails, translucent with the lantern behind them, mow the algae that the bulbs allow and leave brief ropes of evidence like a signature.

Things without nerve or vow live so fully down here it makes words like “vegetation” sound unambitious. Algae gleam where a lamp takes a liking to them, verdigris slick, a mural painted by appetite. Liverworts hold rain in their palms like coins too pretty to spend. Tiny, transparent ferns try to be ferns with two thirds of the requirements and are almost convincing. From the world above, roots send emissaries through fractures, tips white and exploratory as blind fish faces, polished by water until they look lacquered. Sometimes they forget and try leaves. The leaves are brief, upright, astonished.

Everything you drop becomes a citizen. A glove undergoes a damp baptism, glosses over, fills with silt and pillbugs, then stands on its fingers like a little temple roofed by algae. A bottle garrisons midge larvae, then becomes a frog nursery, then a boat for a storm’s night, then an island on which a spider attempts a democracy. A length of yellow caution tape becomes the high-school bleachers for crickets attending a single glowing 60-watt sun. A shopping cart, if one has somehow been imported, reefs out of the sump with an ecology so complete it could be moved four meters and start a new civilization.

People come through, but they are commuters, saints on retreat, tourists with a respect their mouths have to learn. Maintenance workers—red lights strapped to their brows, voices pitched low as if for a church that stands on pillars of something other than belief—tend the machines and consult the falls. They have the place’s rules written in bone and boot memory. Their hands can tell if a ladder rung is thinking about giving way. They move slow enough to be corrected. They bring tins of paint, a wire brush with a handle scabbed by old putty, a set of wrenches like nicknames. Some of them give the eels names and claim the frogs of certain basins as coworkers. They leave swipes of wax pencil that pass for street signs, fogged over by morning, read by the next shift by touch more than by sight. Once in a decade someone not supposed to be here comes, and the place teaches them the alphabet that begins with don’t and ends with where the ladder is. Most learn. Some don’t. The frogs keep going.

If this underground has organs, then you live among them. The rock is ribs you can lean a cheek against. The shotcrete scars are dressings and grafts. The parapet along the main river is a kind of lip. The turbines hum as a stomach hums, satisfied with a meal it has made out of a force. The fans are bellows and kites, drawing in the air where the place is hungry, sending it where it grows stale. Conduits are nerves carrying decisions drops at a time. The ceiling’s sweat is the skin doing what skin does. The sound is blood. The whole body is warm enough that breath does not smoke, warm with the work of so much living.

Time lives here with a different address than yours. It is patient, which is not to say slow. It belongs to the stalactite’s agenda and to a frog’s night, to the spin of a turbine and to an eel’s mind counting a flood’s moods by one-degree changes you cannot perceive. It leases a ledge to a bat colonization and, in the same agreement, to a calcite lip that will thrombus itself into being just in time to catch that guano pile and hold it in the architectural memory of the room. It blesses and erases graffiti with the same fingertip of condensation until the names left by kids become a gray raised text for spider toes to read.

And because nothing here refuses to be many things at once, there are also the presences that are not quite beings but take up residence like them. Echo lives down here, a resident with a thousand faces who answers your voice in the accent of machinery and rock. Smell lives with a character you could marry or never forgive—wet coins, clay, frog-skin, the singe of a stalled motor once in a rare while, the good laundry smell of blown spores. Laws live here and they are practical. You can hear them in the way a fall that you cannot see yet locates itself with absolute certainty, in the way edges ask you to reconsider, in the way light is admitted on parole.

Who lives here? Water, properly. Stone, still being born. Air with obligations. Light on shifts. Machines proud of their jobs, sweating like mammals in summer. Frogs elected by chorus and never out of session. Eels keeping a private calendar. Bats, their gossip a silk you do not touch. Swifts who sign their names and leave. Rats precise and unafraid of their vocation. Crickets sawing the hours into equal sizes. Beetles and isopods earning dark coins in soft mines. Blind fish that listen with their mouths. Amphipods punctuating margins. Slugs calligraphing shine. Spiders teaching patience where it can be seen. Algae showing how easy green would be if only more stars were turned on. Roots negotiating. Fungi setting up a shop in any wood foolish enough to pass through. People, sometimes, with lamps and quiet pocket devotions. Lost things that become foundations. Sound thick as a neighbor. Time as a landlord not interested in eviction so much as in renovation.

And presiding, with a benevolence that is merely indifferent and therefore pure: the sovereignty that never knocks, only comes in. Every other tenant earns its keep by getting out of the way, by finding in the wet grammar a clause where it can write itself without crossing the citizen. In a valley whose sky is a ceiling that weeps, that is more than enough to count as home.
