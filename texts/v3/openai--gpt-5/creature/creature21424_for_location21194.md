Heat is the oldest resident, a broad-backed thing with the patience of stone. It does not pace. It settles. It finds the high places, steals into pores, lies under paint as if under skin, and from there it rules by soft insistence. It keeps time with the city’s distant pulse and replans the weather here without lifting a hand: more when noon is a rumor above, less when windows darken and apartments flick off. It has its own gravity. Anything cooler than heat’s preferred self will sprout a beard of cloud as surely as a forgotten cup draws dust.

Water comes next, not as a neighbor so much as a pilgrim with a timetable. It learns the rails and routes. It rehearses its fall along the clean seams in the slab and along the bitten edges in the walls, counting down the interval until the upstream chillers permit it. When the plates blink colder, it appears in strings of pearls and writes its soft cursive down the aisles. It is a creature of lanes and habits. It knows where its footfalls have stained the stone, where the sumps hold its mirrors. It breathes in the culvert with a sleeper’s voice. Water here is not wetness in general; it is a handful of practiced gestures, a library of drops following their used pages.

Between the two—the old lord heat and the scheduled pilgrim rain—live the thin-eaters.

They have many names because nobody gave them any, and so they earn new ones with every glance. From some angles they are soot; from others varnish; from others a film of mica on an oil slick. In the amber pools they are dull pewter; under the fluorescents’ green their skins catch a gloss; when the switchgear hums they put out a color that isn’t so much seen as agreed upon by the eyes. They are mats and veils and threads clinging to anything that can be colonized and quite a few things that can’t, if you grew up in a saner world.

These are the electroskins. They learned early the simple fact of the place: if a thing leaks current, it is food. They graze the micro-leaks from junction boxes the way deer take grass from verges. They send a black shine along the bolt-heads like tongues along salt. They creep to the lips of porcelain where they can feel a tickle that means a meal, and they hold themselves away by a thousandth of an inch because that is the way to drink without being burned. They are patient to a fault. They will sit at a crack in insulation like saints before a relic, until the hour when upstream someone toggles a grid and the relic shines for one of its brief seasons.

They are also iron-feeders, sulfur-feeders, making their way on faint solutions smearing along the masonry. They trap the district’s waste heat as if it were a sun they can tolerate, and they keep their skins wet by convincing any cold thing that it is a cloud-maker. Their bodies draft growth lines along old drips. They braid themselves towards richer fields, tightening three-lane highways of gloss where a feed runs steadier. They have something like a map, but it is made of differences: warmer/colder, wetter/drier, sweeter/meaner, charged/blank. Their idea of horizon is a run of cable tray; their idea of drought is a week of surge so constant the plates never rain, leaving them starved on dry gloss until someone sleeps up there and demand loosens, and the shaved dew returns.

They are not alone in being thin. Along the eastern wall live the black aprons, lichens whose backs remember air so old it did not move. They set themselves precisely where the wall wicks what the floor lets go, their edges crisping the handwriting of trickles. They are frontier folk in a world where the frontier is a hairline. They wake and feed and close not with the sun or moon but with shifts in temperature almost too small to consider—enough of a whisper of cool that moisture beads, enough of a tremor in current that the films near them breathe out sugar. Their bodies are calendars without numbers; their shape keeps a long book of rain lanes and pauses and unseasonable events when a valve slams far away and their feed doubles for the span of two breaths.

There are others who do not love iron, who do not speak electricity at all. They live in the racks of white tubes up in the perpetual dew, fungi that are always almost-hydrated. They are small organs of refusal, swelling to translucence when the plates earn their chill and collapsing to leather when the heat peels everything back. They do not try to hold a shape the place will not let hold. Their music is in the change itself: tight-to-swollen, white-to-amber, fluted-to-smooth. When a ribbon of too-hot steam is drawn and anchored between two vents, it skates by and singes a perfect negative through them, a clean line of not-life that will take weeks to sponge over again. They will never have caps or gills because this is not a kingdom where wind awards spore flights. They send themselves along by damp, by neighbor; a flourish of self begetting another flourish of self an inch along the beam.

At the lower room’s lip stand the combs: not plants by any catechism but under the hands of time turned plant enough to fool the nose. Someone once seeded them like tools set in a row, to wring water from hot air. Now they wear slim skins that turn their function to metabolism. They drink not the pool but the fog off it, hair by hair, and their colony breathes out a smell that no flower would ever claim—warm hay braided with charged air. They invented an afternoon where there was none. If a day can be built from appetite and exhalation, then the combs deserve a season of their own.

The cables draped over the dead motor host a mineral forest that answers to a different clock. The fringe there is of gypsum and other salts, frost grown in slow weather. It gets knocked down by flushes, grows back between purges, is eaten where a film finds a taste in it and sealed where a varnish lays its gloss across the crystals’ ribs. It is life’s ledger carved in stone, even if the stone did the writing itself.

Even the rust lives here, which is not a metaphor so much as the right way to account for something that recruits itself. Left raw, steel powders in a week unless the thin-eaters get there first and paint it with their living seal. When they do, the rust is domesticated—its bloom lowered to a slow spice that flavors the air without falling in red snow. Where no film claims it, corrosion opens like a fireworks petal, beautifully ruinous; where a film settles, the iron wears a skin as deliberate as lacquer. The compromise is a resident in its own right, a negotiated surface, half-death coached into usefulness.

No animals move here. That absence is not a vacancy but a shape that other things live around. Without grazing mouths, nothing needs thorns. Without wings, no fruit needs to be bright. There is no fur, no scale, no eye to be met. In their place, there is listening that is not hearing, a sensitivity to pressure changes and vibration so honest that a distant truck becomes a sift of grit and a conversation in salts. The drop of a single bead two rooms away is an event the lichens hold in their skin. The slow exhale from the culvert swells the fungi like a shared sigh. They do not watch each other, because there is nothing here that sees. They contact each other by adjacency and field.

There are the machines, too, asleep but present as any mountain is present. The switchboard is a rock that hums. The pumps are animals in myth, black flanks oiled, their flywheels an undisturbed ocean each. They are not alive; but to the residents they are not furniture, either. They are weather. They are landforms. They are gods that bleed—mineral, heat, current—and so are hunted gently, ministered to from below by glosses and aprons and films that arrange themselves like robes.

And there is something like a culture, though it could never be said aloud. It is made of habits and mutualisms and permissions. Where a crack in the wall leaves a white fan of salt, the electroskins do not cross because there is nothing there for them, and the lichens turn their edges toward the fan as to a shore. Where a brownburn line from an old torch still holds the field, its changed surface chemistry makes a no-go barrier that even the bravest gloss slows at and tests and shies from; they circle it, and the circle becomes part of the map. Where a junction box leaks richer field, the green-black hurry towards it and grow thick, feeding thinner neighbors with their sugars when the switchgear sings, and bleaching a pale rim of deprived stone beyond the party where nothing grows. It is an ethic of place that is not moral but gravitational: each thing falls into what feeds it and against what would harm it, and the shape that results is legible as intention.

There is a timekeeper as strict as any temple bell: the chalked cycle on the west wall that says when purges come, when line C will cough, when Condenser 2 will weep between 0009 and 0014. The residents cannot read, but they can count. They hold it in water held or loosed, in thin bodies opened or closed. They brace for the warm tide that comes when a district spikes; they furl for the lull. Their year is made of hours parceled by the city. Their eclipse is a valve two blocks over. Their comet is a maintenance flood that scours the crystals and resets the combs. Their storm is a fan’s full breath, ribboning steam like a blade across them. They have no myth older than the day the excavator tooth bit bedrock and the first wet thing here found itself against clean mica with nowhere to go but along.

If there is personality, it belongs to processes. The air here has moods (sulky, generous, feverish). The plates have patience. The culvert feels sorrowing when the trickle slims to a thread, content when it rounds its throat with silver. The ceiling is susceptible to flattery, swelling with pearls when someone cools it enough. The rebar thorns are acquisitive; they invite licks of films and hold them like secrets. The walls are literalists, showing what they have been through with a directness no facade permits. The floor is a keeper, recording dew-lines like ledgers and flaking concrete as carefully as winter banks leaves.

What lives here travels inches and thinks in gradients. It solves for survival by wiring itself to luck and schedule. It eats current and condenses air and breathes through stone and never knows day. It answers events from elsewhere with opening and with sheen. On long timescales, it builds under-district stalactites from the city’s own sweat, writing a syntax of function and leak and need. On short ones, it rides the smallest weathers: a cooler lip, a shadow off a handrail, the briefest easing of a compressor far upstream.

What lives here is not lonely. It is busy in the way of microbes under a tidepool rock, of moss in the shade of a pipe, of frost knitting its crystals together across an old gate. It is contented when it rains because of a clock. It is contented when the fans take three slow breaths and then grow still, because that is what they do. It is contented when a meter’s needle twitches and pauses, because a twitch means a little more green later on. It is not made anxious by silence; silence is where it reads the metal cooling, where the concrete tells its grain, where everything that can be sensed arrives unmasked.

If you insist on names, call them the Drip-Fed. Call them the Foxfires. Call them the Varnishes, the Comb-Gardeners, the Flutes, the Aproners, the Crystal Clocks. Call heat the Abbot and water the Deacon and the fans the Lungs that exhale only when the liturgy requires it. Call the chalkboard the Calendar. Call the culvert the Throat. You will be wrong and right by turns; their real pronouns are functions.

In a place with no animals and no people presently walking, urbanity continues as a kind of inhabitant as well: alignment, numbering, color-coding, care by proxy. Even the absence of hands has a shape you could trace with yours. Hooks wait with their folded absorbents. Gauges keep their rounds as moons do. The place is kept as one keeps a promise. The residents have adapted to that promise and become part of its keeping. They make do on faint light, on feebler charge, on metals dissolving into taste, on the miracle by which cooling draws weather from hot air. When the hour comes, they open; when it passes, they close; and when you leave, if you do, it will rain again by the plate’s decree, and everything here will lift its face, which is skin, which is lace, which is length of gloss along a bolt-head, and live.
