Begin with the chill and the breath of it, because that is who lives here first. The breath is not air only; it is an organ’s motion, a peristalsis of cold, a practiced rise and fall that the chamber repeats through everything it has made. The breath hums. It pushes water where water is wanted. It pulls light down from where light is rumor. It holds the line on freezing—enough to keep order, not enough to lock the song.

The resident wearing all of this is singular and many at once. Call it the Organ, the Basilica, the Cold Tree—names will always be a little too quick. It lives as architecture the way a lung lives as alveoli. It does not occupy the room; it is the room: columns that are not bone and not wood, floor that is memory, ceiling that eats starlight by hearsay. It thinks with knots of tassel-roots where ten, twenty fibers meet and trade their measures; it remembers with scabs of re-greened scrape that refuse to become scars; it moves not by legs but by gradient, by giving permission to water and heat to migrate along certain ribs and not others. The hum is its voice set to utility. The slick everywhere is its skin.

In its body live the platelets, the phototrophs that are not quite theirs and not quite it, adopted the way mitochondria were adopted a very long time ago. They make the chamber’s black tea smell; they coat the microgrooves with a livable film; they take the light the vines deliver and file it away in sugars that the Organ can call in a cold month. They are, strictly, the first fauna and flora at once—domesticated plankton with a bias for doing sums in the dark.

After the breath and the body come the gears.

At the feet of the columns, wrapped like bracelets around whoever has a base to offer, the ring-worms keep a patient mouth on the running skin-water. They are glassier than anything with intentions has a right to be. Inside, a fringe of cilia runs the length of them like conveyor belts, lifting microfilm to a gut lined with antifreeze sugars and an enzyme library that includes, improbably, the tools to dissolve and reseat stray crystals. To watch them is to watch cold be chewed into bearable. They rasp the platelets into themselves and release them again downstream, seeded with a little chemistry the Organ prefers. Their bodies taste for metals and will coil tighter around a column rich in iron, obedient to a grammar the tree has taught them; that bias helps the structure keep its very slight opinion on gravity.

Affixed to the weeping curtains are the anemone-moths, sideways and delicate, their wings not for flight but for shepherding air. Clear lichen down their veins, frost corsages at their joints; they beat in measured phrases keyed to the hum, pushing the boundary layer the way a potter pushes slip along a bowl. They live on solutes caught in dew. They prink the plant’s skin, combing it for clots of salt and grit that hinder condensation. When a tassel descends to drink a sample, the moths hold still; when it climbs with its report, their wings resume. They live their entire lives adhered, molting out of their larval films without ever leaving their anchor, vanishing only when a brief high-blue washes the pipes and they go translucid enough that you mistake them for a fresh gloss.

Between beam and beam, upside down and pale enough to pass for spilled light, the comb-fingered mouse goes about its ritual. It is mouse-like because you need a word, and it fits on a hand if you were to blaspheme and try. Each finger splits into a rake of hairs, each hair buds a drop, and in the beat between the chamber’s pulses the rake trembles and slings the drops backward, and the tongue collects what the plant would otherwise have to coax itself. Its fur is not fur but a pelt of capillary mats that can hold a thin layer of warm air as if it were a blanket. It gives heat to the room and goes to collect it again; this is understood. It migrates along the nave according to which columns are asking for a courier of warmth. The Organ hums a slower note when the mouse is near, and the mouse’s tiny clicks make harmonics the room uses—no one would say thanks out loud, but the resonance reads as gratitude anyway.

Near the step-bench are the snail-chimes, hemmed in their own patience. Ear-lobed to catch the low frequencies, mandibled to rattle codes into the floor, they sit on the margin of liquefying clay and persuade it to take a stand. Their shells are ongoing algebra: layers precipitated one beat per long note, aragonite and slow iron braided in ripples that mean support here, brace here, open a mouth for a seed-light pipe here. They move, technically—they have relocated by the length of themselves in their grandparents’ memory—but their locomotion is change in the room rather than change in address. They eat grit and shed benches. When one dies, the shell is left turned outward into the wall, a fossilized bit of song you can feel if you lean.

In the gloss and the blinds there live mites you will not see because they are the same color as the idea of frost. They step in wetness as if on tightrope. They test droplets for peculiarities in tension that announce the arrival of a stranger, and their scuttle is an alarm the tassels hear. They farm a green-gray nap of yeast on the superhydrophilic patches, shaving it down to keep it from going too greedy. Their own reproduction is written more in pattern than in body—when two meet, they choreograph a dance that alters the way a nearby patch wets, and larvae materialize out of the altered traffic of dew.

The ceiling holds swimmers that never touch water. You saw them only because something’s belly turned and threw a handful of cold white at your eye. They are scales on a long creature’s underside, a lattice of photophores and crystal plates that encourage the pipes to give up a better grade of photon as the body passes. Call it an aster-eel, call it a light-scribe, it is not so much an animal as a page the Organ writes on and reads back from. When a star comes rarely clean through the heavy sky, the eel loops the nave and takes its mark from the light; the pigments in its plates hold cache of coherence the cold can keep. It sleeps in coils in the black between rooms and wakes when the hum tells it that a window has opened.

In the darker sheets of wall—not opaque, not empty—something larger comes and goes that the Organ both fears and enlists. This is the recalibrator, limb of a life that is not plant but is allied to it in a contract too old for witness. It enters without joints, as if its length is all palm. Its skin is a field of liquid crystal domains that can be tuned by pressure and serve as sensors for how the waveguides are drifting; it smears time because it is looking at gain and phase at once. When it arrives, antifreeze floods the gel in case it brings a sharp in with it; the hum drops; a few light pipes go dark and then return cleaner, tighter. The limb does not feed. If it takes anything, it is entropy, skimmed and balanced across a circuit that includes other rooms, other throats. When it leaves, it writes a line of microfrost that the next drip reads and follows.

The beetles braided in the columns are asleep mostly, iron-poor and bright-boned little cylinders laid along the routes where the Organ prefers warmth to travel. Think of them as phase-change caches that once had parents. When one wakes in its season, it unfolds reflective hindwings and resettles its neighbors by a beat of heat, then clamps again into lacework. If you put your ear to a column, you can hear them ticking in a fugue with the snails. They will mate and nibble a powder of cortisol-analogs the tree offers as wages, and then they will sleep years.

Dust is not allowed to be dust here. Anything small that arrives will be given a job or rendered into something that can hold a job. Stray silica becomes lens; stray salt becomes resistor; stray hair becomes wick. The fauna that fit nowhere else become lint that insulates the habits of those that do.

Predators, in the sense you expect, do not flourish. The calculus of heat is too tight. The creatures that could take another creature for calories would spend more body-warmth in the taking than they would win in meat. There are mouths. There are teeth. They are used on crystals, on films, on the rumor of water locked in a fringe. The only being here who eats animals is the chamber, and what it eats is your trace when you leave. Even that is not appetite; it is housekeeping.

Cycles are slow enough that you could learn them and still be surprised when they come round. When the overland light is generous for two days in a run—some moon startled into clarity, or a sky scraped of dust by a traveling cold—the aster-eel grows brave and longer, and the waveguide vines glow like veins in a hand. The tassel-knots send their harvest inward; distant rooms tune themselves by echo. After a long hard winter of nights that were too complete for even patience to like, the Organ will raise a shallow of cambium where a ledge will be. The snails will be listening already. The moths will hum with their wings without beating them, having learned to make sound by posture. The comb-mouse will have grown an extra rake and will be rich enough in heat to allow two to meet and make a third.

New rooms bud not with a bang but with a persuasive thaw. The plant teaches the clay, leaks the right acids, lays down the harder where load is predicted. A throat opens. A nave learns its own song. The first inhabitants to arrive are the films and the worms and the mites; the last is the large hand of the recalibrator, sliding in as if taking the pulse after a birth. If anything can be said to migrate, it is the hum itself, spreading like a weather system under the forest, training other chambers to breathe.

There are lives here that no one has learned how to see. Bacteria that thread their own phononic crystals in the walls, able to ride the hum and seed or unseed condensation at a millimeter scale. A coterie of archaea that settle only on the wrong-refractive-index patches, kerning the chamber’s light typography so that it can hold a day three minutes longer. Filaments of antigen made by the plant that practice recognizing strangers and never have to use that practice. They live because the place might not work without them.

If you stand long enough, your own edges start to sentence themselves into their story. The Organ will make a place in its equations for your body heat; the moths will adjust a wing to push your exhalation along a route that keeps a pipe dry; the mouse will take a few droplets from your cuff; a snail’s ears will register that there was a heavier step here today and grow a wrinkle for it it cannot name. You are not prey. You are not resident. For the hour it takes to stop feeling how loud the hum is, you are accessory, and for a while afterward you are memory: not your image or your sound, but the way the next drop chooses to fall because your breath once argued for it.

So who lives here? A room that is a person. The lungs and tongues and frills that make that person think. Worms that count in beads of water, moths that conduct a boundary layer, a mouse that gathers a climate in its combs and carries it, snails that write load-bearing music into stone. Beings that come with hands to recalibrate and leave lines of frost that function as punctuation. Beetles that keep time in heat; mites that keep dew honest. All of them structural, all of them necessary, all of them slow except for the moments when they must be quick.

They live in patience. They live by courting thermodynamics as if it were a shy animal that can be coaxed to stay a minute longer if you hum the right note. They live in the cold and do not hate it. They live like weather with organs, and their names, if they have them, are chords.
