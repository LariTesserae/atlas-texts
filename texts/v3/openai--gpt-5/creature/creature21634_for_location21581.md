The first inhabitant is the plain itself—this organism with a city-mode. It is many and it is one: a braided mat with a civic mind, a choir of wet wood and sugar intelligence distributed along pleats and hollows. It does not move the way an animal does; it steers by offering easier ways. It is all ovals and patience and pressure gradients, an elder that learned, over seasons like centuries, to think with humidity. It keeps its own hours, breathes on its own count. Its attention is most awake where the routing organs hum and the cathedrals of leaf-skin angle open and closed, but its slower thoughts live everywhere water can choose.

It hosts what would be called people by anyone from away. The plant calls them guests-with-seeds because that is the first agreement between them. They are light-steppers, wrist-singers, skinners of membranes, coaxers of flows. Their bodies keep the climate’s honesty: warm without sweating, hair always a little dark with the air’s embrace. Their soles learn pulse the way a sailor learns swell. They dress in work that grows—jackets and wraps woven of fibers that were fed the cultivar’s DNA when they were thread. When they arrive in the morning, the mat knows them by their jacket’s pattern and rises a shade to meet their weight, signaling trust. On days when the breath is fast and the air tightens, the guardrails lift a little higher along the catwalks when a seed-wearer comes, and tilt a touch for visitors to make the safe speed.

They carry instruments more often than tools. Reed-flutes, pleated like a sprig; wrist-cuffs whose inner lamellae vibrate at intervals the plant prefers; a speaking comb for coaxing fog-fish out of thicker bands of mist. Their cutlery looks grown because it is: stiffened sap edges and folded cellulose that flex rather than slice, tools that separate rather than sever. They hum to open a door the way you would knock: high chords for a thin film that needs a shiver, a soft drone for a heavy leaf that sleeps, a doubled note that calls a white heron-shape to pry the seam if a neighbor is too tired. Those who are hoarse use the bees, vertebrate pollinators with fluted jaws made for answer-notes. Encounter them in market hours and you see small bracelets singing around handlers’ wrists, and the bees pressing their many-fingered mouths to membranous locks to release feeds of nectar and exchangewater. The bargaining is literal sound: frequency as currency, amplitude as favor, the way honey might be at a field’s edge in a drier world.

They time their work to the breath. Every seventeen minutes, give or take the drifts of the day, the city exhales and they step with it, a smooth forwarding as if the floor itself carried them an inch. They do not talk of minutes, mostly. They say a step is two leaf-blinks, a task is five exhale-lengths, a promised return is one fog-shear and a cough. When they are in a hurry, they do not run; they arrange sympathy. A path-tender goes ahead and salts a braid with a fine resin that promotes the right flex in certain proteins; an older child sings a wavering triad to open the long vents; someone with a head for chance leans down and smooths a shimmer-thread until it catches within another, and the labor of walking arrives a handful of footfalls sooner.

Their faces are not all alike, any more than leaves are alike on a single bough. The plain has gathered them from more than one provenance, and then kept them with its small acts of kindness: a cool pocket held just long enough in the heat of a chore; a fruit that splits when you are walking past the moment you thought you could not think of food. Their pigmentation trends toward leaf-shadow and laterite—brown, umber, green-gold undertones that read the light as if skin had learned, a little, to be a lens. They wear their hair with purpose: braided into wicks that draw off dew to be sipped later; combed loose to make a larger edge when a membrane needs gentle drying; threaded with white filaments that catch and display certain wavelengths so a door recognizes a friend by halo as well as by song.

They do not divide labor the way a metal city would demand. What they do divides itself:

- There are the braiders, whose work is to persuade roots. They lay routes by whispering chemistry into soil-thin, mapping gradients that roots will taste and choose. The braiders stoop for long hours, fingers stained with iron and tannin, listening through their palms to the faint tug as a young rootlet decides to be boulevard instead of burrow.

- There are pleat-keepers, who read the civic organs. They stand with their ears to the corrugated, humming columns and tap out what the plant has prioritized for the day: open these five mouthways because fish-traffic will be heavy; tighten this membrane because a storm rehearsal is on its way; close that long gallery for a half-breath so a tired crane-lizard can cross below. They are not bosses. They are bilinguals: plant-into-people and people-back-into-plant.

- There are thread-tenders, who spin and nurse the shimmering filaments that boil the edge off walking. They wear gloves of chitin felted with a fungus that enjoys improbable adjacency, and they murmur to their coils as if they were infants. It is not magic; it is a way of making many small maybes line up. But the tenders treat it with reverence, because a day that lands as intended is a good day, and nothing ruins a market like an out-of-phase footfall.

- There are breathwrights, choirmasters of climate. They listen for the long drone that tells of sap and root-pressure, and add their own layer: horn made from a stiffened petiole, a drum the size of a large turtle’s back, voices bright as a reed or a throat-basement rumble. Their work coordinates the city’s lunging—a closing here, an opening there—so that everyone sweats less and no one’s mushrooms drown. On festival evenings, they throw chords up a ribbed nave and watch words come back wearing halos.

- There are laterite-tasters who kneel at the rust seams and drink a fingertip, then decide which creepers to offer, which brush-tongues need feeding. They use their sense of iron like a compass, mapping slow rivers underground that the plant will drink with no bank to speak of.

- There are the keepers of the white heron-shapes, which are neither heron nor quite tame. They are trouble-solvers with hinge-skill, and they need bribery of flicker and tone. Their keepers practice quiet: two-handed, fingertips out, a pose that would be prayer if the city allowed prayer to be still.

- There are fisher-children of air, who tend the proto-fish that live in the droplets braided through canopy. You hear their laughter at night when the fog is thickest, their footfalls careful along the petiole-catwalks. Their fish-school with bursts of silver in a lantern’s spill, and the children hold out thin frames of vibro-silk that give the mist a little more edge so the fish can change direction like a thought. They harvest nothing, mostly. They are practicing what everyone practices here: how to apply a small pressure to move a large soft thing.

- There are seed-weavers who make the jackets and are also, colloquially, midwives. They oversee entrustments between plant and person. When a child is ready, they press the jacket to their shoulders, and the plant tastes a friend’s friend. The jacket thickens when it storms. It directs dew behind the knee. It carries, in woven code, a handful of permissions that keep the clumsy angers of the mat from being visited on that child’s small mistakes.

- There are silo-stewards who step in and out of the hardened hollows where saps are concentrated. If you stand with them, you smell a dozen moods of sugar: bright freeness for tongues; sticky gratitude for wasps; stern slow sweetness for the fungus that holds a beam together like an oath. The stewards are hands that decide what goes where and how much—accountants who count in liters and likelihood.

They sleep in hollows that do not separate life into rooms and not-rooms so much as into densities of touch. A sleeping nook feels like the inside of a dimple. A family’s breath softens the surrounding film until it dimples deeper. The polite thing in the morning is to leave the dent less than you found it: smooth with a warmed palm, hum a small closing note, hang your cup where a sun-pan will slant at midday.

Children run their first races to the breath’s beat. They learn etiquette in sound. Politeness is hearing: to know a wall is listening to someone else’s sentence and wait one exhale. Name is a chord, not a word. The older people have longer names because they can carry notes across more than one breath. Lovers often share a bridge-note for months, so that when one hums in a stairwell the other’s name opens the pantry door without either of them thinking; sometimes there is laughter when pantry doors open on accident and a heron-shape looks in with a lightless eye, wondering if it was summoned.

They do not fear animals because there are so many exits and very few teeth mean harm. But they respect the busy rules of the small world. They raise the vole-things not in cages but as households: a leaf-patch here, some scatter of petals there, a spider they introduce to a back and teach to pluck parasites rather than nerves. Amphibians sit like punctuation marks on the edges of pans, chirruping chords that hold a corridor’s acoustics in good shape until a breathwright can attend. Bark-lizards footchurn along horizontal water-films and keep the membranes clean at ankle-height; the city itself pays them in gnats when it opens a blossom out of phase. The dog-sized grazers, glossy as bread, are allowed to tongue the outermost skins of certain galleries; their keepers carry soft hammers that give a hint of percussion when a grazer’s mouth goes greedy and threatens to pierce instead of smooth.

Predation exists, but it has been co-authored into ritual rather than spectacle. A fruit falls like a meteor and a dozen fog-fish die and feed a dozen ferns, and after a while a child touches one of the fish and feels the keen muscle in it go quiet. A mantled snake waits where a stair narrows and takes a vole without drama, then slides away in deference to a pleat-keeper’s slow step. Danger speaks before it bites here: a change in smell, a pressure difference against the cheek, the faintest prickle along a jacket’s woven weft. The city gives whispers, and its residents are, above all, listeners.

Guests come, and the place treats them with good manners—no more, no less. Locals can step onto a leaf-skin right after a downpour and never skid. A stranger is given a shade more friction, a suggestion to choose the easier arch, a hum they won’t know how to answer. It is not unkind. It is the conservatism of a living thing that remembers salt in a tree’s wound. Some guests choose to stay, and the longer they do, the more their walks add to the city’s habit of them. You can follow someone for nine minutes by watching where the dew avoids being dew. You can tell, by the way a guardrail flinches up a little late, that you are a week new and the plant is not yet writing you into its reflexes.

Authority, if that word fits anywhere, belongs to age-of-pattern rather than years-of-breath. The elders everyone takes counsel from are not the ones with the heaviest hair or the longest hum, but those whose bodies the place has memorized. When such a person stands at a routing organ and says, low-voiced, “This pleat will be better if it runs to the old swale,” the plant flattens a ridge and raises another with the softest crackle, as if to say yes, yes, that is a shape we recognize; the work goes quicker, the day lands a minute longer.

On feast-days the market hum becomes music, more than bargaining. The vertebrate bees hum together in freighted, perfect intervals, jaws gleaming with nectar-sugar. White heron-shapes open a hundred little zippers in polite frenzy, the way one might peel a hundred lychees for guests. The fog-fish schools are shepherded into arches where they glitter like a river pinned in air; children hold still and are washed in a skin of droplets that makes their eyelashes heavy and their laughter thick. Someone strings a web of probability-thread above the main way, and people walk under it feeling the minute forgiveness it grants to their steps, thinking—because they are sentimental like anyone else—that love feels like this: the world editing itself a little to accommodate you, being carried a little wherever you are going.

Some few who live here speak of gods, but most speak of tendencies. They honor the long learning that tamed this plain from erosion into repose. They keep a shrine to old stones—the last laterite clinkers polished by hands who think to remember what once would have cut a foot. And they tell their children stories that begin, “In the years when the plant was a plant and not a town,” as if the change were a moral choice the greenery made, which in a way it was: it picked a shape that let it be visited by new kinds of sweetness.

If you stay late and walk home along a ribbed catwalk, a guardrail grown a palm higher than your hip because the leaf decided you were nervous, you may see them—not people, exactly, but also residents—the small nothing-things that live along the not-quite-in-space threads. They are hints more than bodies, a glimmer of refraction where a droplet should not have condensed. The elders say you should nod when you slip and do not fall. There is a tiny citizenship in your gratitude. The city hears it. In the morning, the path that took you home will take you home again, and slightly less of you will be lost to stumbles and indecision. The day will, again, arrive a minute longer than anyone thought it could. And the inhabitants—leaf, lizard, bee, fish, frog, heron, spider, vole, seed-wearer, thread-glimmer—will have made that minute together.
