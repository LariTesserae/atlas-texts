They are not a tall people, because nothing here rewards height. What grows is reach and exactness instead. You notice wrists before you notice faces; you notice the way every hand goes out ahead of the body, testing the next handhold, the next cord. Ankles are bundles of small, clever muscles; toes are independent and used. The calves of elders are like the roots of the vines they tend—ropey, patient, tuned to a ladder’s narrow rhythm. The young have the kind of stillness that elsewhere has to be taught; here it is a reflex. You learn to be a counterweight before you learn to run.

Skin doesn’t dry out and crack, it drinks. A day outside the cleft would make it thirst; here it glosses without oil. Hair is braided wet and threaded with little slivers of polished stone that direct drip into small catch-flasks at the collar. It is a decoration and a habit—a thousand personal aqueducts. Shoulders and forearms are always beaded. The eyelashes that the mist grows on are a local inheritance: thick enough that when you blink you can feel each drop shift in place like a pearl.

Eyes are adjusted with practice more than with anatomy. They don’t bother much with distance, because distance is a rumor; they are connoisseurs of near-dimness, of shadow within shadow. If you were to meet someone from the high flats, they would say your pupils stay open too long. Your hearing is better in the bones than in the eardrum. Put a heel down and you know if the path has shifted under its flavored moss, if a hairline crack is fresh. Put a palm to a fin of rock and you can tell by the faint answering tick what the temperature did an hour ago and what it is doing now and whether somewhere above you a slab is unlatching itself from the idea of being a wall.

They dress to catch and to shed. The garments are layered, light, never dry. Outer wraps are lattices of fibrous stems and leaflets that condense fog and let it go in designed rivulets, down to a little clay belly gourd. Beneath, there are close wraps that hold heat but breathe like moss does, spongy with treated plant fiber and the very occasional skin; skins are gifts, never taken in force, of whatever the place offers up dead. Belts are not for cinching so much as for hanging the quiet little tools: the bone awl polished by your grandmother’s hand, the thin stone blade that hiccups light at its edge, a couple of meteoric rings given like trust and used like a song.

Because sound is a pressure, a thing with bulk, you move as if it might bruise. You do not clap. You have ways of showing delight that don’t ask the air to carry them: a fastening of eyes, a set of fingers, two hands held around a cord at the same time so the message runs through both of you at once. Speech exists. To say there is no speech would be wrong. There are names—sharp, short ones for sudden instruction; longer, breath-lapped ones for nobody in a hurry to hear. But the language that was born here listens as much as it speaks. It enlists the cords that vein the town, so that thought can leave the mouth and enter the fingers. It has a grammar of pulls and relaxations, a lexicon of weights. There are tones no ear hears that travel just fine along a stretched gut line to the ledge next over. There are old women who can tell you who is sick by the slack in their pluck.

If you want to understand property, you do not look for fences. You look up. Strung between roofs and rock, hung from the underside of bridges, flumed across rooms and over cradles, the finest capillaries of the settlement draw fog into drink, turn pause into resource. A line of drip that runs regular is a dowry. Rights are drawn not on ground but in air and time: an arc from this lip to that stone, between this hour and that. When two families join, their names are knotted into the cords that handle their shared water. There are arguments—softly—but they do not happen in the plaza. They happen with both parties holding the same line, feeling each other feeling the pull.

Work is what your hands do while your feet remember not to die. Every craft leaves a different texture at the fingertips. The netters smell faintly of the gut silk they harvest from the caterpillar molts; there is a way to comb the caddis cases from the cups without drowning the tiny engineers that built them. The gutter-carvers have the thin, hard films and micro-cuts that come of holding a blade against a stone that wants to skip. The tetherers have scars you do not see until they go shirtless: a map of where cords have slid with speed against damp skin when something heavy took its leap and did not fall all the way. There are plate-listeners, who watch the talus and can tell a new ring in rock from an echo; there are spore-keepers, who manage their crops like weather and can rattle off a hundred names for mushrooms that never show themselves except as lighter, warmer dark. There are people whose whole worth is knowing the breaklines, and people who can plait a load-bearing vine bridge from two living plants that would rather not touch.

There is trade, at the scale of a pocket, a handful, a cord. A runner goes down once a month, fog-cloaked, with a pack that has one sealed lamp and a pocket of stones that answer to heat. She brings back salt, a pin of true iron small enough to be carried like a talisman, a story of a bright day told in low voice with hands on a cord because it will be someone’s only window for another year. In exchange she takes puckered stacks of dried driftlings like wafers, a roll of net fine as hair, a plate of rock that knows the hour without a shadow.

Children are taught to be friction before they are taught to be selves. They learn how not to kick loose the dew before they learn to run lines of numbers. They are tied to their mothers, to their aunties, to the aunties who are not kin but live in the next platform and know the cords you live on. There is a ceremony at three years when the first personal cord is strung, from a child’s sleeping place to the nearest message line; you get your first code on that day, two taps and a slide that mean "I am here. I wake." There is another at the first fall, the first time a foot betrays and nothing but the tether all children wear saves you. There are no shouted scoldings. The entire ledge letting go of breath at once is enough.

Death is not performed with flames, whose brightness is a call to things with useless teeth and useful noses. Bodies go into the cool bells of rock that hold the town’s richest mosses, into a series of chambers that have been seasoned by generations. You do not let rot run to loudness, to stink. You curate it like a plant, like a quiet process. Some bones come back polished and useful: you can tell an awl that once belonged to a mother by the way she held it in the work. Some stay, and feed. Names are not put on stones. They are put on lines; the knobs and knots you pass each morning on your way to the cistern are the people who came before. You read them with your hands.

As for what else lives here, it is a catalogue of things that have chosen smallness and exactness over spectacle. You already know the fish of the fog, the driftlings you net and honor, but you may not know what watches them. The owls with weather-faces do not hunt them. The owls take the larvae that cluster at the mouths of moss—the velvet worms that are not worms, that throw a net of glue on their food from tiny organs that look like kisses. The owls come without edges and go without consequence, and the only trace is that the drips restart faster where their feathers brushed the air.

The big spiders are architects you do not slight. That avenue of silver that runs from the cistern’s rim to the gusset in the roof is a road you leave whole; it belongs to the spider with a pale chevron on her back who mends it each dusk. Morning is when the town is furred in white, not with snow but with what the spiders dreamed. The adults pick a path with the practiced eye you use to step over a sleeping infant; they know which sheet is new and must be let alone for an hour, so it collects what it needs; and which one can be powdered between fingers to flavor porridge. Children pass with little brushes, clearing the cords that carry news without breaking them.

There are salamanders curled translucent in the upturned cups of bromeliads, ribbons with eyes that will never leave the water that is four fingers deep. There are centipedes you do not harm because they are the only things that will willingly eat the white mold that blooms under the lower bridges. There are snails that live in the rocks, not on them; you see them when a fresh fall opens a seam and there is a moving moon of pale flesh tasting the young mineral. There are slugs with feathered tails that look like fancy handwriting learning how to be a creature, which the children feed like pets with hair-thin strands of fermented fern until they are big enough to go away.

And there are the hunters that are elsewhere only ever metaphor: the rays made of cartilage and a will to turn. They come when the pressure is wrong—wrong for you, right for them. They read heat as a blind person reads the face of a friend. A bright fire writes a letter in their alphabet. The oldest story of the cleft—the one that is told by fingers on cords, because even the word for it is a bell in the wrong air—is of the first people who brought a brazier into the notch and saw the ceiling come alive with moving shadow. These hunters are not many. A year may pass without one. But each child has felt the way the school of airlings in a breathwidth ahead of her parted like watered silk for something she did not see. Each child has learned that if you feel your teeth go strange, you pull the lamps and lie still.

Food is not something you go and take; it is something you coax. The gardens are not beds but skins: you grow mats of flavor, sheets of sour, thumb-depth plots of crisp. An entire meal will bite only with acid and salt, and what sweetness exists is an accident of unseen fungi. You raise a crop of sporocarps that is never seen except as a powder, you put it on everything and think nothing of the way it makes your skin feel good in a drizzle. The main protein is air you have taught to be flesh. You lift it on mornings when the hovers are lazy and do not sense your nets. There are days you take only a handful and days, rare, when your net is so heavy you have to tie yourself to the stone to haul it. The elders say: if you cannot see the net, it is enough. If it shines, it is almost too much.

When the weather pulls thin, there is color. Then there are feasts. The children go running up and down the ladders—slowly running, which is this place’s contradiction—and back with fern that caught sunlight like a dare. Someone will always have found a fruit wedged where a wind-shift shoved a flier from the high trees into your slot of the mountain; you crack it like a skull, you hollow it with spoons made for the purpose, and you feed the pulp to the whole string of platforms, person by person, a taste at a time. That bright sugar is the loudest thing you eat.

The town has holidays and none of them is a riot. The White Fall is reverence and sweeping. You put out basins to catch the silk and the bodies and you do not trap. Do not trap is a law that is only a law because someone once needed to write it. The spider will be hungrier than usual after that; the town feeds her for a while. The Lift is the opposite kind of plenty. You untie your lamps so their inwards not their outwards face the night, you wrap your hearths, and you stand with your backs to the walls with your heads tilted and it is daylight, except you can put your hand up and move the light around. Some people rub their fingers and paint their faces with that light. The old say it picks you and it stays in your skin a week, and you glow faintly inside if you lie awake and look at the inside of your arm.

There is governance. Nobody who lives pressed into a rock that will drop you if you pretend gravity is a suggestion gets to be a complete anarchist. There are guilds: Ladders, Nets, Gutters, Plates, Ropes. There is a council called on the cord that has the right to carry three messages at once; when those three come in—ping, ping, slide—everyone with authority stops what they are doing and meets in an emptiness the size of a breath and makes decisions with pieces of string they lay on the floor, moving them, knotting them, until a shape is agreed. Law is written by blade into stone, not because words are sacred but because blades are slow, and if it isn’t worth the cut, you don’t make it a law.

There are also those who fall in love with the mountain itself, who will never marry a person. They tend the spiders, or they tend the basalt plates, or they spend whole afternoons lying on their backs on the terraces that have nothing growing on them on purpose, counting the breaths of the cleft. They are the ones who can tell you, with a look at the way the moss is holding its water, whether it’s going to be the rockfall season three days from now or whether the mountain is too happy with its shape to let go of any. They are the ones who will hold your fingers to the rock and teach you to know the difference between the mountain inhaling and something enormous making up its mind.

Strangers sometimes come, not in caravans—you cannot caravan where a wrong footstep is a day’s falling—but in singles and pairs, led by the ones who trade. They are so noisy they might as well be bells. You teach them by example. You show them the cord that means Stop and the cord that means You Are About to Take My Water and the one that means Do You Hear That Under Your Soles. Some of them leave changed forever, and never go home except to fetch salt and small metal and to return with a need in their bones to be the size the cleft requires. Some of them leave because they cannot sleep where the lamps do not throw shadows, only disclosures. You do not sell them much. You have very little to spare in the way that such people mean, and plenty in the way that you do.

Names are taken from the place because the place is the only thing that does not leave. A child might be named Fifth Shelf North or Fog-Wheel or Plate That Sings At Dusk. Lovers give each other the kind of names that would be immodest if they were said in the air; they are written instead into a tiny knot and tied to the inside of the other’s wrap. You can always cut a name free. Everybody knows how. You cut only if you must. If a ledge loses its right to water—if a dripline dies, if a fin collapses and erases a room—then the name goes with the broken rock, and that family is taken in by the next ledge over. They string their knots to a new cord. They do not say orphan; they say portable.

Even the way you tell a joke is tuned. Words are fossils of sound and they carry too much freight. So you play with drop patterns. You tilt a gutter when nobody is looking and send a rival’s careful arrangement one heartbeat off; she looks up when she hears the wrong pace, sees the grin, resets her rack, and counters by rerouting the micro-rill above your doorway so your morning wash drips precisely into the collar of your wrap. There is laughter here, but it happens in the throat, in the shoulders, and the air forgives it because it arrives clothed.

There are stories, of course, about how the ribs of the giant came to part, about the first people who read a seam and a fog and said Here. There is the tale of the meteor—the true one, because the ring is still around the wrist of the grandson of the one who caught it. There is the story of the child who shouted because she was afraid and woke a hunter shadow and was saved only because the spiders pulled a sheet across the cleft with a speed nobody had seen before. The spider in that story has a name. You do not say it. You hold the cord that runs above her home and tap-tap-slide.

You leave, sometimes—some of you. You go to the flats where the sky is not a ceiling, where light is not filtered into patient gradients. It is too much and you cannot drink it, but there are things to learn out there that the mountain will not teach you. You build a little frame you can carry that makes its own drip with a leaf lattice and sleep under it and then come home with the idea of a new knot that will let a cord carry a second message without confusing the first. You come home with a word for a color you can only use twice a year. On the way back you stand in the high wind and let it scrub you of everything loud.

What lives here is a community practiced at being exactly the size of its cleft. It has made an ethics out of not disturbing. It has made wealth out of intervals. It has made a syntax out of cords and a diet out of breath. The animals around it are not tamed; they are noticed, and that notice is a kind of pact. The place is new, which is to say it is honest about not being finished. The people are new the way children are new: full of assays, repeating, recursive, building a civilization on platforms of etiquette and condensation. They know what a single drop is worth. They know what a sound can cost. They have put their city into the space between stones and taught it to hum without humming. If you stay a while, your own body learns the rule: that everything that can be done slowly should be, and that even a moth, if you are careful enough, will not change its mind.
