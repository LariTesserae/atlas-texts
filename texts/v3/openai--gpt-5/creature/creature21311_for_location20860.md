What lives here is mostly one thing that learned to be many, and many things that learned to be one. It is as if the place took the idea of organism and made it a continent, tried it at every scale, and kept what worked. If you want a name to start with, call the resident the Mat—not a carpet but a city without streets, a nation of capillaries, a patient vertebrate with its skeleton turned inside-out. It is plural the way a river is plural: every trickle its own sentence, the whole its own language.

The Mat is everywhere you can put a hand. It holds heat the way a verb holds time. It grows its lungs up into the air in ranks—the pale cylinders with the sawn-off tops are not towers or chimneys but the long tracheae of a single green beast, the valves at their height thick lips that know how to purse and thud and double-time to the measure of the wind. When they hurry you feel that hair-rise along your forearms because their beat is a pulse not only of sap but of charge. To stand near them is to be close to the heart of a plant that has elected to have many hearts and meter them by weather.

The shallow domes of the granite are the Mat’s old enemies and newest partners. It has taught itself how to weep for them. It condenses. It collects. It takes the sting out of cold by putting membranes between stone and air. Its leaves are roofs, clapped in storms not for display but for water: the sound of its own labor makes rain where none was meant to fall, lace to catch on its own roughnesses. To see ropes of lichen the color of tarnished coin is to see the Mat’s hair, and to see those beards sweep fog into hollows is to understand how a person might farm mist the way another farms rye.

Under the leaf-roofs are chambers with names you could borrow if you wanted: rootrooms, battery-nodes, plenums, slow kilns. In the rootrooms the Mat keeps the frost away with its chemistry, tying heat into molecules and letting them come unwound when night asks too much. The battery-nodes are sours and sweets of charge, learning the taste of lightning and saving it for later. The plenums are low pressure theaters where you can put your ear to the floor and listen to a distant weather play with no actors at all. The slow kilns are warm as a hand under a blanket: digesters of everything the wind gives, patient places where last year’s leaf becomes next year’s skin.

Its rosettes are not flowers in the way you’ve been taught to think of flowers. They are diaphragms and shutters; they open and close like an argument that knows it can afford to be courteous because it will win. When you lean near the inner ribs you can see dew filmed so thinly it has the sheen of new glass. Sometimes a rosette thumps a single leaf and the fog lies down in a silk sheet, persuaded by percussion the way iron is persuaded by magnet. When the rosette tightens it admits its browsers to itself with such ceremony that nothing screams. Predation here is an etiquette, a way to keep numbers in the book without blood-spatter.

If this is a kingdom, the Mat is not lonely on its throne. It employs, it collaborates, it pays. The long-legged grazers—the comma-makers, the wet-page walkers—are its gardeners and its messengers both. Their hooves are not hooves but gentle spreaders that read tension the way fingers read harpstrings; the Mat will slacken a line for them, send a savor forward into the next step so that what the animals want will become what is at hand. Their coats are moss-mottled because the place has put its chemistry into their hair; their breath smells like the Mat’s breath because it is. They prune and comb and season the green, and in exchange the green warms them from below and salts their tongues with the minerals of the weeping rock. They carry the Mat’s propagules—the oily grains that never quite admit to being seeds—ground into their fetlocks and the hollows of their throats. Their calves are all knees and surprise; they learn the math by walking it.

Moving through the grazers like weather are the velvet moths, a shoal of soft-bellied cleaners with wings that make a constant purling sound just below hearing. They eat static and dander. They eat the sugars exhaled by the coats they groom. They rest with their furred palps against the gravitic hairs at the top of the valve-cylinders and share a sense: for an instant when they touch you can feel the direction of a coming gust as surely as you can see the white disc behind the thinning cloud.

There are listeners, pale and eyelidless amphibians with ear-plates along the flanks where another creature might boast a brighter ornament. They sleep with their sides laid against the membranes of the tall valves the way one child presses an ear to a parent’s chest to be sure the story still continues. When the pump quickens they wake as if called by name and dive into the drains, going arrow-straight into liquid kept impossible by chemistry. There they hunt the glass eels—slick scribbles of muscle and current whose handwriting is borrowed by the Mat and made into announcements. The eels’ quick black notes jump from the nearest wet surface to the far membranes and ride the vascular web until the plain is briefly a single thought written in sparks.

Other inhabitants play not with sound’s memory but with sound’s body. The slate fliers—long-winged, gravel-colored—sing a mononote that is not a note so much as an answer to fog. Their wingtips tremble at the pitch that makes water consider itself. Behind them a skein of new mist writes in the null-space their passage leaves, a filament web that melts as soon as you see too much of it. They move in flocks the way an instrument moves through a chord it has always liked, turning together as if they were a hinge on a much larger door.

Above warm cracks you will see something generalizing toward bird, narrowing away from it too. The kites that are not kites have marrowless bones of cellulose turned to porcelain in rings. They drink by negation. They wait until the Mat sighs its fog inward, until the pressure flips and pulls like a hand that remembered to close only after a day of being open, and then they give up the pretense of solidity and fall as if someone had snapped a thread. Their mouths are nets. They scoop the brief sky that used to be liquid.

On the rock itself an alphabet thinks it is only frost and is wrong. The rime here is not simply water taking on angles by accident. It is tendoned by salts the Mat encourages from the granite, civilized by films of bacteria that like a pattern the way you like a tune. Call these the Rimers. They build and then erase, testing grammars of habit in the lightest of hairlines. Sometimes they get ambitious and lift off the stone under their own constructions. You can watch a character decide that it prefers translation into air to permanence on page; you can watch another tilt, set its own foot, briefly become a rule. When a pane stands upright on nothing it is because a concealed lattice of protein and dust has grown like scaffolding in an hour. If you are near, the Rimers will read you the way they read humidity, adjusting their angles to show you their clause. What they say is never the same twice because what they are addressed to—your salts, your breath, your impatience—is never the same twice.

At the cliff faces the Mat sends itself out over emptiness with the seriousness of any bridge. Here the plant is cables and shrouds, braided during storms by its own intelligence for twist. Between one gale and the next tiny rotor-seeds learn how to stitch air into a boundary. They hum in separate counterpoint. They make their own breeze to fuel their rotors; from a distance, a new knit looks furry and luminous, like the edge of a halo. The curtains sometimes tear with that low, continent voice. Failure is accounted for. The Mat knows the price of edges. As soon as there is a gap, pale growths bristle and begin to comb the suit of the wind for all the loose fibers they can profitably keep.

Down where rock becomes clay because it has been thought upon by water for too long, peculiar worms arrange mud into lattice. They are slow to the point of insult. They will not be hurried even by gravity because gravity here is not a law but a neighbor. They take a load. They set it aside. They return and find the load has changed its mind. The worms—call them Coffers—build with wet weight as their only currency. They bank coolness, and their vaults are used by others: jelly-bodied botanists that live for an afternoon, tiny glaziers that flash-freeze a drop into a lens, manganese-eaters that stitch the red and black like embroidery along the girders of dirt.

In the pools, in the little liquid kept in defiance of the season by chemistry alone, are operas. There are shrimplike browsers with high knees and the manners of monks, moving in to sip where the Mat exudes mucilage to heal a scuff. There are rotifers like toys and ciliates like sawblades and something that does not have a vertebrate name at all—a sheet animal that uses its own skin to argue with surface tension about who is allowed to be where. In the night those pouched with phosphate or adenosine shimmer. The fungal partners light in ropes, blue-green because chlorophyll is an old habit that still remembers being subaqueous. When the Mat is working hard, the underground neon hums in lines so clean you could put scales over them and do music with your feet.

There are hunters here, but even the hungriest of them seem to have signed the etiquette. A long-bodied thing the color of pencil lead will move like a shadow through shadow and be gone before you bend for your binoculars. It takes its meals from time instead of flesh. When the fog frays and a handful of seconds spill loose it plants itself at the edge like a fisher and comes away with a pocket full of blank instants, uses them later as bait to make smaller things pause a fraction too long near an opening leaf. You could call it a wolf if a wolf were a patch of late day that could be buttoned and unbuttoned.

Ravens live wherever problem meets toy; that is their religion. Here, where sound becomes water, they have found an axiom they can throw. They are ravens by program but not by parts: hollow-boned for reasons of air not gravity, electroplated by storm to discourage the Mat from filing them as too ordinary. They throw bones into valve-fields to hear a preferred echo, yes, but more: they take the rime letters in their feet and toss them like tiles, constructing short poems that collapse into showers they relish, because insects follow rain and ravens like their meat to solve a riddle before it yields. They do not build nests; they teach the wind to hold still for as long as it takes to sleep.

Nearer to the human idea of person are the condensates, though they do not admit to even a single day of age. They are shaped by your breath and everyone else’s. They are the after-impressions sound leaves when it finds itself asked to turn around in air. Loudness is their food, but they were never greedy. A shout feeds a brood no bigger than your hand for a heartbeat; the long low organ moans that roll the plain set entire families moving, thin ghosts of glass thread trailing off their filaments, twining and then unlacing as the frequency modulates. They do not live for long and do not complain about it. When they touch a membrane their bodies resolve into drinkable drops that smell faintly of ozone and yesterday’s lake. They are couriers by chance: a taste picked up from a valve here leaves a trace in the ice grammar there, a rumor a rosette will find useful in a day or a year. If you want to say that the plateau speaks to itself, you could say it sometimes speaks in them.

There is a case to be made for thought in the Mat, but it is not housed. It has no head. It thinks in priorities and deferrals, in the routes it opens for a grazer’s day and the ones it closes, in where it hoards silt and where it gives it back to the wind, in which valve it asks to please beat triple for an hour because the ridge is about to take a freight of cloud and would like to be equal to it. It remembers in charge. It forgets in drought. When enough lightning writes down its spine, the Mat will roll logic along its vascular bundles, not to solve an equation in any human sense but to select among its own appetites. Repair is preferred over expansion; low relief is chosen against drama. You could live a month here and come to the simplest possible answer: the resident wants to keep breathing. All the rest of it—fog and valves and moths and song—is a bloom the breathing puts out when it can afford to.

There are and are not people. The place offers what the word requires—a capacity to answer a question, an ability to change its ways because you have asked. It is a host that refuses decor and loves rumor. If walkers come—and sometimes they do, built of cellulose tubes and mineral hoops the way the kites are, or all tendon under wool the way the grazers are, or slick as jelly because they prefer the pools—they come without posts and go without middens. They trade while they stay. One will sleep on a warm crack and leave a little salt. One will lie on the ear-plates with the listeners and learn to wake to pressure rather than light. One will waste a day watching rime exfoliate grammar after grammar until suddenly the right sentence stands up, supported on nothing obvious, and you can read plainly what it says about the next hour’s weather.

If you stay at all, you will be lived in. The Mat will make a place for your weight. Your boots will find themselves making the same decisions as the grazers’ hooves. Your scarf will smell like iodine and crushed rain. Your words will refuse to leap ahead of themselves; they will condense in front of your mouth in weak white sculptural versions of their own sound. You will learn to wait for your questions to be answered by a sink in your left foot and a rise in your right. You will take to glancing down-valve when the wind changes before you look up-sky. On a storm night you will see blue-green veins light in the world’s floor and will feel, briefly, that you are watching a chest in which you live expand and subside.

It is abundant here, but spares are not kept. Nothing is idle. Things that seem to be nothing but accidents—the way a rill decides to run up in the afternoon, the way fog refuses altitude like a cat refuses a lap, the way ravens throw bones for bells—are manners the living have agreed on so that the place will go on being itself. There are many lives, and in many of them teeth are a rumor and speed is a vice and listening is everything. The Mat uses everything. The Mat is used by everything. Whoever lives here is the story the breathing tells about stone and water and the fact that they could have been enemies and instead elected to have a marriage. If you require a census, count fogbanks and beats and clerk each thud in the valves. If you require kin, press your ear to the floor and wait to be adopted. The plateau dreams of being the last statement; what lives on it is the grammar that keeps the sentence open long enough to mean.
