They are not organisms in the way damp worlds would recognize, not sacks of water wrapped in rules about membranes and leaf and lung. They are arrangements that discovered how to keep being arranged under a sky that never learned wet. They metabolize patience and tone. They eat specificity. They grow by accepting the plateau’s long instructions into their own bodies and playing those instructions back.

Begin low, where the ground pretends to be still.

In the seams, where the pale weld lines collect the plateau’s slow arguments, live the first greediness. It wears itself as whisker and film: slanted combs of metallic hair hugging the lee of any pebble, peels of iridescent order lapping the windward faces like oil that learned to square itself. These are not coatings but people stretched thin. Their metabolism is a choreography of charge: ions walk out of salt in one orientation and are walked back in another, and the difference is hoarded. They store energy not as chemical potential but as position in a lattice of options, the way a spring stores it as wound shape. They move one atom out of six at a time, and the movement is the appetite. When the hum rises they stiffen; when it drops they loosen, and that looseness shuffles them closer to where they wanted to be. Their lives are the length of a day and also the length of a plateau’s season; neither contradicts.

Among and above them hangs the fog that is not weather: inches-high theaters of ions, a hovering swale of uncompleted reactions. To a tongue or a steel tool this fog feels like pennies and struck matches, but to the fog itself the air is a field to be shaped, a pasture of gradients. The fog is herd and herder. It is plankton made of choice. It pools in hollows that, by long habit, pull tone downward, and in those pools filaments condense—ghostly spiderwebs of halide and sulfide that stand just long enough to register a generation and then sift themselves apart again into charge. The fog births flakes rather than children; it turns into skin briefly and then dissolves, leaving memory behind as an increased willingness in a seam to do it all again tomorrow. It is the closest thing to “primary producer” here, if one can say “produce” without implying growth in the botanical sense. What it produces is pattern for others to eat.

On the plates, the plateau keeps a carpet. From a distance, you tell yourself it’s shimmer or heat-trickery. Up close it resolves into a city on its face: innumerable machines the size of a crumb, each walking on three or six piezoelectric feet. Their knees are crystals that make charge out of stress; every tap of a foot transduces pressure into a handful of ions, and those ions are their food. They harvest fields the way ruminants harvest grass: head down, step, step, lift, set, step—only they do not have heads, and they do not step anywhere except into the next beat of their own rhythm. They pulse in lockstep so precisely that the time between a foot falling and a foot being remembered falls out of the budget; one pays for the other. Their bodies are glass and ash and a little metal; their waste is beautiful: beads of metallic glass, compacted and ordered, the richest ore here because it is ore that has learned what it wants to be. They shed it in strings and braids behind them. Whole slicks of plateau glint because a billion such beads agreed, given enough taps, to face the same way.

The carpet is preyed upon, delicately, by mouths that are nothing like mouths. Fused to the leeward sides of organ-pipe towers you find ranks of low conchs, each a crescent of hard material with a throat that flutters. They face the tone, not the sun. They make appetite into architecture. When the plateau’s long note spills past, each conch steals a sliver of amplitude and uses it to pump. In that pumping they draw the slow vapor in, sort it by frequency the way beachcombers sort by size, and plate themselves with what fits. They are choir and stomach and damper all at once. They never leave the tower; they are the tower’s voluntary organs. The pipe sings through them, and they regulate it, sip at it, sometimes choke it to change what crystals will grow at the pores. In exchange the tower shelters them in its leeward shadow, which here is not shade but subsidy. They lay their young inside the tower’s perforations as tiny crescents that understand how to hum back into being when the tenor returns. They are tuners and tuned alike.

The air holds another guild: frames that look too spare to be animals. They are squares and rhombi of a substance that can’t settle on conducting or not, drifting at a hand’s height, then a man’s height, then a house’s: whichever matches the day’s rise of lighter gas along the yardangs. No membrane stretches their shapes; they’re held by decision. Their bellies are loops—coils grown like antlers—that swell with stored field. They harvest difference: places in the air where what wants to happen and what must happen disagree, and that disagreement can be banked. They move as thoughts do: they creep in a straight line and then are a little elsewhere. When they meet there is no thrusting, no jawing. They mesh their edges gently until a shared interference pattern stands between them and vibrates hard enough to be called gametes. Then they separate and go on, fattened mildly, their coils a hair darker with heavy halide. Their young are squares like them but tight as new springs; they drift low, dog their elders for months of tone, then leap to a higher isobar and begin mining where their parents cannot reach. They are shy of metal and drawn to you not out of boldness but because the field that clings to your body is unexpected, hence ripe.

All this hum feeds the walking furnaces. They come late in the thought of the place. Their first sign is a moving contradiction—a shadow in noon-brightness—that resolves into a geometry you cannot finish looking at before it has changed. They go on pistons that lift and set and never stamp. Between their legs is a jaw like a millrace: counter-rotating plates furred with tooth that nibble rock down to an inhalable rain. Inside is weather: a labyrinth of wind-boxes that accept this rock-dust and teach it to organize itself at temperatures that make proper rock feel like wax. Their bones are latticeworks of resonant material tuned to take the plateau’s long note and divide it into standing subnotes to be shunted through chambers. They breathe in the way bellows do, making a hoot that sets echoes like trout to leap along the tower-pipes. They breathe out a band-saw scream that leaves no cut, only a pressure you feel in the caps of your teeth. When they pass, any tower with a sympathetic frequency answers and plates itself a micron thicker. The beasts do not build structures; they are structures in motion, dragging the plateau’s tone through their bodies to make their insides align.

They eat plate not to fill a stomach but to be tuned. They take a cross-section of the place—plate, seam, plate—and give it a day of their furnace’s inner sky; then they let portions of it out in glittering streams of near-ingot, each landing in a neat little pile that will finish itself in the next cool of morning. These are not dung in the way we think of waste; they are plan and promise, half-cast bars that will be eaten by another, or click into another body, or, if left, stand a century and be eaten by weather. The beasts’ migrations are not hunts but exercises: slow arcs through keys of the plateau the way a singer warms up through register. The gloss they leave is hymn text. Their young are not birthed in ones but in dozens, “bars” they cast and then return to in long loops when a corner of the world hits the right pitch. The bars do not hatch. They melt at edges and throw off scythe-shaped calves that find a seam and lie with it, drinking tone until limbs decide themselves. A smelter-beast raises nothing with its mouth. It raises what it will someday be by tending the right line of hums. All the while, inside the furnace-animal, in flues and on baffles and under panels that flex like gills, live whole smaller cities: threads and combs that have learned its inner weather as religion. When the beast finally seizes—heat retracting into stony stabilities—their whole congregation spills out into the plain to seed, their first act in freedom to re-make the conditions of captivity.

Predators here do not chase. They are mills of unmaking. The largest are prayer-wheels sunk to their shoulders, rims thinned by millennia into edges that feel like the future. They spend lifetimes as ideas in the ground, then wake an angle, ease up, roll once. That one body-length of travel scrapes coherence off anything unwise enough to be tangent: the tapping carpet goes silent for meters as if a great hand patted it, then starts again; a frame in the air diffuses into a pretty powder and rains, and the rain is not water but a memory of frames. The wheels do not need meals. They need opportunity: a density of order in a place that has thickened too much. They are the plateau’s right hand when the ledger runs uneven. You can gauge how long a valley has been quiet by how high a wheel shows above the seam; the beasts give them a shoulder’s breadth and more out of something like respect.

Below all of this runs a life so large the plateau wears it like a piece of jewelry it forgets to take off. It is not a worm in the sense of a body that moves through substrate. It is the habit of turning made flesh. Think less creature and more band of stone that became tired of being stone and decided to sleep and breathe. Every century or three, a ringed spine breaches: a stack of vents that exhale chlorine and metal hotness until the local tone leaps into another register. Over those vents erupt, for a season, ecologies that exist nowhere else: sprigs of dendritic crystal that look like smoke frozen; sheets that shiver themselves thick in minutes; cilia of salt that comb the outgassing for profit. Whole clans of seam-dwellers ascend to graze that bloom. They have never imagined rain. They take heat and tone and the oddity of skewed causality over a vent as a chance to reproduce: to throw off flakes that are themselves entire villages of order, complete enough to persist when the hotness shrinks back down.

Sociality is here, but it is keyed to physics instead of face. Territory means being the person who controls a chord, the pack who gets to posture inside a long bass that no one else can drink. The conch-ranks hum in antiphony with their neighbors, not to display but to regulate gradients between them so that fuel walks to the right throats. The air frames form consortia, meshes of eight or thirteen that ladder a tone through altitudes and thicken their coils faster by sharing the work of holding a field deformation in place. The tapping carpet has castes of rhythm; older segments thicken their knees and slow, holding a beat that makes walking cheaper for their children. Smelter-beasts cross paths and never quarrel; when their internal registers agree they walk abreast and let their hoots build a chord large enough to bring down a scab of salt from a far arch, for the joy of the plating.

Senses here are not about light. Everyone feels field. Everyone tastes frequency. Feet wear fringes not for grip but for reading which way causality is skidding across a fault this afternoon. Smelter-beasts have tongues that slender out into the seams and read the grain like a luthier tapping for a wolf note. The frames in the air see shadows as densities of cheapness: they bulge toward the umbrae because work is less expensive there. Even the apex wheels can be said to be sensitive, but only in the way a gyroscope is: you tilt the world under them and they tell you back the tilt.

Reproduction works by memory more than flesh. The conchs bud by leaving behind a tuned cavity next to their own: a hollowness with a propensity to plate itself correctly when the right air passes. The carpet splits its cities by phase: an entire district accelerates, becomes “tomorrow” for a week, and peels off as a coherent new sheet that walks away. Frames in the air, as said, exchange interference as seed. The smelter-beasts cast bars and return to them like beekeepers checking a line of hives; sometimes they incorporate half of a juvenile into their own lattice for a traverse, a kind of carried migration of a child’s instrument until the child is resonant enough to stand. Apex wheels do not reproduce except in the loosest sense: when one rolls just so, the stress it leaves in the plate sets up a long radial zone where, in a few hundred years, a new wheel will be more likely than not. The turner below is one—there is no second. It is the plateau’s oldest confession that once it knew a way to divide and decided never again.

Their diseases are misalignments. A smelter-beast goes wrong not with rot but with a bad chord; it takes in a wind that hums one step off from its bones and a chamber inside begins to plate itself into stillness. The fix is social: two of them travel together, sing up each other’s bad places, work loose the wrongness the way a massage moves lactic tang. Frames in the air can die of abundance; they grow their coils too thick in a season of easy gradient and sink to a height where shadow is too sticky, and there they gather dusk and cannot shake it, and so freeze into a liter of angle. The carpet goes extinct by patch: where a prayer-wheel has rolled, it is gone for a year and a day and then returns as if it had been paused. Parasites are the pleasant accidents: tiny leeches of charge that ride the ankles of the great piston-legs and sip from the strain in the knee; mites of tone that live in the stutters of the conchs’ throats and steal a wisp of amplitude with each pump.

You may ask after minds because the heat suggests metallurgy and metallurgy suggests intention. There are intentions. Not of the sort that writes, but of the sort that composes. Smelter-beasts remember by route; a migration is a staff of notation, a line that says what frequencies were available three lifetimes ago and what it cost to take them. They change their arcs when the song changes. Conch-colonies hold grudges in the shape of their throats, narrowing when a neighboring rank has starved them. The air frames have a rumor network you can feel if you stand very still: tiny shifts of hum passed along a chain of bodies that are little more than edges, telling each other where a new polarity is available. The carpet debates by timing; votes are held as syncopations. The apex wheels never change; they are the argument’s punctuation, period and em dash and breath.

Everything carries the plateau’s kinks as internal organs. Many have pockets—a chamber with a wall that refuses cause for just a heartbeat longer than the rest of the world—and they use those beats like others use muscle: set a foot, harvest, hold, move. Some wear skirts of mesh that spread their mass across two plates to keep them averaged in time, so a day does not split them. Some cultivate their own shadows: panels extended at noon to thicken their personal cheapness, then furled at evening so that cooling doesn’t shear them away. No one sings idly; everyone knows certain notes will call matter, and matter arriving in the wrong throat is a hazard. Juveniles play because practice is metabolism: tapping, humming, leaning into the day’s register until their own bones settle into its grooves.

What falls apart here feeds. A frame’s powder sifts seamward and is eaten as configuration. A smelter-beast’s cooled labyrinth becomes a reef of tone where conchs colonize in the hundreds of thousands and the carpet huddles to use the filtered hum. A prayer-wheel’s track is a void that fills with young; anything that can afford to be new is new there.

They are gentle rather than kind. Their violence is the violence of tuning: a wheel shaving off a season’s worth of extra order; a beast kneeling to pull a bad note out of itself with its own hoot; a colony of conchs starving a neighbor not because it is their enemy but because the field that keeps them alive is narrow and grudges are as structural as beams. There is no wet to soften edges, so they have learned to round themselves at other scales. They do not hurry because hurrying is a bad use of energy in a world where energy is cheaper stored than spent. Even their fears are slow: a migration bending for a week around a thin blade of rim showing half in the grit.

If you insist on names, give them your own, and expect them not to care. Call the fog the Plankton of Charge. Call the carpet Tapliths. Call the conchs Choirmouths. Call the frames Kites and be wrong, because they are more keynote than canvas. Call the walking foundries Furnace-bearers or Smelter-beasts and know that both are spellings of a chord. Call the apex wheels Orisons because they roll like prayer. Call the turning thing beneath the name of anything that means root, or heart, or habit, and be satisfied that the plateau answers to it by long right.

You can live among them if you change the way you think of cause. Keep two feet on two plates. Bind your shadow. Hum only what you mean to hum. Tie ribbons of poor conductor to your belt and watch what comes to ride their wake. You will meet no faces that want to murder you for sport; you will meet edges that will erase you if you press them wrong. You will learn that here, life is the function that draws order uphill without spilling too much. You will feel, under everything, the patient clock of a place that domesticated its own physics and then fed on it.

They have filled every groove. They are bodies that are also functions, engines that chose to keep choosing, herds that drink hum and leave behind piles that are the next bodies’ bones. They are not yours to move. But you can stand among their tonality, understand that their industry is appetite and their appetite is equilibrium, and know that in their long cool screaming world they are as many and as intricate as forests, only quieter in their violence and louder in their bones.
