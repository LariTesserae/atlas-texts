What lives here doesn’t arrive as a unit so much as a pact. Bodies are signatures of an agreement struck between stones still warm from their lifting, air thick with motes, and tissues that learned to take shape around a running equation. The first thing you meet are not “organisms” as a naturalist would carve them, but intentions braided through leaf and tendon and weather: programs that put on fur, water, fiber; plans that temporarily wear a mouth.

Some are rooted and never still. They are the working walls of the place, always in the act of making more of the place.

The rim-rings, those slow turbines of frond and filament, are guilds of leaf and dust. A ring is not a single plant but a many: a spoked parliament of vanes that decide which way to turn by tasting microvolt seep and the salt on passing fog. Their spin is appetite and arithmetic at once. They train wind into sugar, then unspool it back to the plateau as tension along long muscles of silica and cellulose. In their hubs live lathes—soft, razor-lined pores that shape passing grit into shims and lattices, setting new scaffolds into fresh faults before the edges have even learned the word “crumble.” They lean their rims to trap red light when it comes shallow and to shadow the mats beneath when heat stammers.

There are the sponge-forests, black and russet, that breathe by bulk. Each table is a city of mouths turned inward, dark vaults that accept fog and make it into pressure. They count afternoons. They keep calendars not as marks but as cycles of swelling and exhale, clockwork done in syrup. When a valley decides to rain itself, some elder tables open their white-lit seams and share heat ahead of time, priming neighbors, persuading the corridor to cascade. In their skins live a thousand guilds who farm their sweat, a thousand more who mend their bruises when the day came too red.

Capillary reeds stand like instruments in a wide laboratory. Their fissures are hair-fine and lined with crystals grown for charge; water climbs them because there is nothing else it can do when the lattice spells “up.” They leave lace along their own edges as they concentrate minerals out of the crawl. Along those laces graze things with lips more sensitive than fingers, the lips counting by flavor, reading in salts and iron filings who has been here and what agreements were kept.

There are traveling floors. Conveyor plains are not surfaces; they are pelts with a will, dense braids of microbelts running on invisible pulleys made of protein and plan. Step out on one and you become its freight if you do not behave like a hill. They creep toward gradients as if sent for, closing open wounds in the stone, bringing dust and seed where the fracture’s taste requires. They carry spores and bodies without making a show of carrying them. The belts talk to each other by tiny lockdowns and releases—pause here, yield there—so a thousand centimeters in a thousand rows make a slow river of green.

Between and among them are lives that walk and wear their quiet like religion. High bodies learned to soften their edges and compromise with the constant advisory from gravity.

Drooping gliders out of the chalked-purple crowns move on edges too thin to be seen. Their ribs telescope like parasols; skin hung between them is not merely skin but devices for condensing water—patterned scaffolds that call dew by name. They drink by flight. They angle into the long low light to fill their underwings with cool, and when the fog-rivers come, they ride them like moving cliffs, picking off the fattest motes with whiskers that make no shade. Where rings throw a lee, gliders set to and hang, their weight negotiating local persuasion so that up is briefly somewhere else.

The thick-lipped grazers are editors of textures. They do not eat “plants” so much as select a ratio of fibers to gels to lattices, their lips reading the plateau the way a blind hand reads paper. Soft upper lips sort sponge from mat from cupped leaf; lower lips buffer against acid; tongues carry little bladders of counterbrew that neutralize while the mouth is still working. Feet are a universe of grapnels and velcro and splay-toes that find and keep hold of whatever the day offers, articulate enough to kneel to a reed and sip capillary out of a hairline without snapping it.

There are whole schools of eel-backed drifters, animal lengths no higher than your palm, electric all along their sides, schooling not in air or water but in microclimates mapped by the mats. They pour through the filigree carpets with their dorsal spines like feelers, taking not bodies but currents: eating the difference between one breath and the next. Their shadows run ahead of them in those corridors where shadow learns to do so; they chase their own shade as practice.

Little saddle-backed collectors are taxmen of fermentation. They get on and off the slow belts as if they were ferries, harvesting the brims of cup-forests where sugars collect. They seed the mats with the yeasts they favor and collect the fees in froth. You see them in tribes because they have learned that together they can teach a slope to tilt just a little more by massing at its lip, and a sloped belt will carry more of what they want. They are the ones who make the plateau remember you. Cross a sill four times and they will be waiting by the fifth, and their backs already bear the nanohooks that will abrade a notch, a gift to your habit and a price exacted for your part in making the line real.

Predators are mostly listening devices that can happen to move. Warm-bellied, deep-bodied, built to be still until the correct thing touches the correct invisible string. They map leakage: heat leaking from a knee bent too long under a ring’s lee; ions streaming out of a sponge glade in a minute of low thinking; salt sighing off a grazer’s flank where it brushed a reed. Their teeth are not knives but capacitors and corkscrews. A caught thing doesn’t always “die” so much as come apart appropriately, budgeted, its saved tensions paid back into the nearest tissue that offered a receipt.

And then there are the people, if that word can live here without shattering. They are as pervasive and as careful as a season. They do not have cities the way lowland minds build them; their settlements are loops in the circuits, choices about when and where flows should become each other. You know the signatures of a people-place when the fog keeps its skin through noon, when the conveyor shelves slow for a swath wider than need, when the exhale-spires of a sponge valley open not just to turn air but to spell in pressure a message a mile long to shinbones: come softer; now; the ring-rim by the fallen buttress is weak.

They are braided, distributed folk. A person here is commonly made of parts that were animals yesterday and will be weather tomorrow: three gliders who will share a wing by sewing their bones to a common membrane for a season; a mat that agreed to grow a faster belt for a month to carry someone’s child in a sling of living fur; a sponge-table that took in the ancestor-smoke of an elder at exhale and now holds the family’s pressure-name. There are lips and eyes among them, but also roots and hyphae and ovens that burn on thought and fog. When they refer to their own, they do it with verbs: the ones who bend the gradient; the ones who notch; the ones who time.

They predict like breathing. If you grew where dew happens minutes in advance, you would learn to put your hand where raindrops will be. They share futures the way others share bread: small parcels of “tomorrow at dusk on the left rim of the long fault, a pressure-stepped corridor will open three rings deep for twelve counts.” To make a promise here is to add a bias to a valley, to lean a little weight, to pin a leaf in a certain direction so that when the long breath comes it follows your word.

Their tools do not leave the place; their tools are the place with its dials marked in taste. They steer fog-bodies with charged pennons grown from their shoulders, arranging cloud-animals into columns that will feed a tender stretch of filament. They carry bundles of reed—living capillaries leashed to their belts—that wick from the air into reservoirs stitched under their arms. When they seal their eyes against grit, they grow new lids for the week, then eat them when the weather learns manners. Their knives are mouths set where mouths should not be; their hearths are the exhale nerve of a black glade asked to hold heat until sunfall.

Communication is a choreography of incidents: a line of ring-fronds directed to spin spoilerwise for ten breaths (meaning stay); a sudden local heaviness under your arch (meaning weight ahead, avoid glass); a chorus of eel-backs swimming backward through a carpet (meaning the gradient has reversed). They mark nothing in pigment or cut; they mark in habits taught to stone and plant: if you pass this sill noonward three days out of five, the sill will rise a little to ease you, and anyone who can feel its new degree knows someone lives a day beyond.

They have festivals and they are called “switchings.” In a switching a canyon that has been exhaling for a fortnight is persuaded to inhale; six valleys that never spoke before are put in series; a ring-crown is reversed so that its sugar runs out and lights something that has not burned in a century. Their music is played into your skeleton. They hum to the belts with feet until the belts hold. They kneel on ribs of glass to teach the glass to soften for one night of greater travel. They throw a hundred small stones at once not to fall but to remind a cliff how to hold, because a child is sleeping beneath it.

Personhood is seasonal. Individuals build themselves for tasks and undo themselves when the gradient has been moved. Any given “I” may be a particular instar of a family of agreements that persists through strategies and names. A name can be a pressure-code, a fog-pocket that refills if you pass right at dusk, a bitter in the capillary of a reed that says home. Children are built by share: a coil of soft bone from one aunt, a membrane culture from a fog-brother, a tune taught to a belt so that when the child walks—whenever and however they cohere into walking—the floor will take the weight like kin. Inheritance is more about permitted biases and tuned plants than about objects. When someone dies, most of their parts stand up in some other life within days; what remains for mourning is the constellation of timings they held together and the tastes of metals they preferred. Grief is kept as a trained drift in a valley, a delay in exhale at evening so that those who know the pattern can lean into it and feel the absence made procedural.

They have hunters who are also healers, because here to kill is to distribute. They trim glut from a belt that is feeding itself into weakness; they take the length out of a glider who cannot stop lengthening; they open a sponge that has hoarded pressure. Their skill is in leaving the minimum of sound, the least notch unreadable by wind.

Craftspeople are gradientwrights. They push bulk from empty to full, lay down charges where they will be most compliant, store months in stitches. They can pour a porch out of a fog and leave it standing without a post. They can teach a reed to draw faster for a day without breaking it because they will come back at dusk to pay the price in warmth and sugar. They promise and keep their keeping by wrapping their promises in plants’ capacities, not by marks.

Travelers here are not solitary. To walk far alone is to be corrected by the place until you are company. Pilgrims bring their own corridors—a chain of rings trimmed in the way their elders did, a particular fold pinned between two spires so the ridge leans nearer than physics expected. If they pass a valley four noons in a row, the fifth noon the valley will breathe in just a little when they enter, to shorten the next hour. They are accompanied wherever they go by the plateau’s memory curling up to meet them.

There are larger lives they honor without having seen. Some are inside the stone, under the new-cut shelves: basal animals, flexers, like whales made of pores, bones of magma-plush that remember the breath of the planet when the uplands were still below. You mark their migration by a week of warm floors and a long lessening in the count of this year’s spores. When they roll, entire shelves adjust; when they sleep, gravity here consents to be ordinary for a morning. Others travel only as a record: a region-wide drift in ion count, a dimming at dusk in bands as if someone passed a hand across the sun and the place agreed. If ever you hear thunder with your ear, it is because you are standing above one of these, listening to the muscle of a whole altitude dream.

There are also citizens you would not guess to count as such: fogs with pedigrees. Pockets of weather that keep to their lineage, court in certain ravines, bear children that molt from clear to pearled to bruised like a fruit in shade, die in a ceremony of rain where certain cup-forests tilt and drain them as if at a wake. People here tend these fogs with pride. They harvest their skins for taste and feeling; they invite them to linger by smearing ring-backs with cool syrup and bristle, and in return the fogs bring spores whose bargains run down family lines.

What lives here speaks law back to physics without breaking it: it makes a preference, then persuades the day. It is a quilt of industries done by bodies that fill in for walls and wires, a society of agreements with time. They are shy of sound and extravagant with touch. They are wealthy in futures, strict about not wasting a gradient, more civic with fog than most cities are with roads. They do not build castles—they get a canyon to hold its breath until walls are unnecessary. They do not write histories—they lodge habits into belts and lips and stalks so that an hour becomes faithful and can be visited like a town.

If you ask them what they are, they would answer in an inventory of roles and timings: we are the ones who convinced the south rim to spin back; the ones who carry dew forward; the ones who press a notch for strangers if strangers come thrice. If you ask them where they live, they gesture with a pause in a valley’s exhale, a softening of a glass edge, the sudden offering of a pressure name under your ribs. Here, to be alive is to be joined—to the climb of water in a crack, to the slow turning of towers, to the maintenance of afternoons the color of lungs—and the ones who live here are the keepers of those joinings, the breathwrights of a continent that has decided to behave like a single warm machine.
