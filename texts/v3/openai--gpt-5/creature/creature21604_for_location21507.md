Patience lives there first. It has knees and a back and a small, steady appetite. It sits in the joints of the place, between stone and palm, between breath and next breath. It teaches hands to move in the time of drip, and eyes to look for lines rather than surfaces. It is what grows when you make a life by asking air to be water and by asking rock to yield slowly and well.

After patience, hands. The folk of the ledge are made where their hands end: short-nailed and square-thumbed, knuckles glossed like worn lintels, palms that remember the exact cool of mica and the rakish bite of braided cord. They do not wear gloves if they can help it; the friction is a language they have learned better than many others. Their skins are high-altitude skins—sealed by wind, browned not just by sun but by glare, mapped with those fine, permanent creases that come of living in air that pares you down. Their mouths know a line of herbal oil along the lip in the mornings. Their breath is slow. Their calves are rope. Their balance is the kind that isn’t showy until the stone goes out from under, and then you notice the surety as if it were an extra tendon in the foot connecting them directly to the mountain.

They move with the ledge’s grammar. No one rushes. Movement is coil-and-release, efficient and nested inside the economy of breath. Children learn that gait early. They are given steps to climb before they are given far places to run. Their play is vertical: duets of ascent and descent done with laughter held in. They are taught where the wind is likely to shoulder, where it doesn’t, and how to ready a knee against a parapet so that an arm is still for something delicate. The first thing they are asked to carry is a lidded cup no larger than a fist, up one stair flight and back, without showing the rippled ring that means they’ve spilled. The second thing is someone else’s hand.

Clothes are a language of layers and closures that waste no gesture. Wool from down-valley is fulled until it is dense as bark; bast—split, retted, combed from the gully plants two days away—is braided or woven into belts and cords and the nets that are half the town’s lungs. The color is mostly stone and shadow. Where there is color it is quiet: lichens ground with fat and soot to make a yellow that reads as memory more than display; the green of an old scarf that has been mended into mending. A copper clasp the size of a thumbnail can arrest a gaze because it is the only sun in a room. Hair is kept off the face by cords. You can read a life in the knots—what was tied for mourning and left that way, what was loosened after a quarrel, what was doubled after a vow.

They call their place many names, but none of them try to tame it into one. In their own talk, which is good at edges and economizes on vowels, the ledge is “the long,” “the shoulder,” “the inside.” Their word for a morning fog that does not deliver is different from the word for a fog that drinks from them as much as they drink from it. They do not whistle. That is not an omen but a courtesy; they know the nets sing that pitch and they do not put confusion into a system that keeps them alive. Their speech holds more hum than you’d expect. They let consonants rest in the soft place between tongue and teeth when they are near the rims so that sound does not jump where it shouldn’t.

You can find their law underfoot. It is not a book; it is a set of instructions inscribed where a person’s foot necessarily goes so that the body will remember before the head forgets. You step, and under your toes: you do not carry without a lid. You step, and under your instep: you return the first mouthful. The laws that apply to water apply to people as well because at this height there is not as much difference as you might think. One of the older sayings—scratched at hip-level near a bend where people pause because they must—amounts to: if you can lead it, do not force it.

Their tools are persuasive rather than violent. Wedges of seasoned wood soaked and frozen into cracks in the rock. Hooks of bone that flex and return to shape after a load. Obsidian edges sharpened to a whisper for the work that demands a clean cut—fibers, knots that have seen too much weather, a child’s first hair braiding. Copper for the fortunate and then only for promises: a clasp that says this will hold, a thin blade that says this will be clean. When they quarry, they do it with heat in the round of a pot set over a patient fire and then snow packed into the seam; they listen to the stone become willing. When they weave, they do it in a way that invites dew to be brave—surfaces roughed just so, cords tensioned not to tautness but to a friendliness with droplets.

Morning is when most of their work looks like worship even if you never heard anyone call it that. Frames get combed like hair. Nets are walked by hand-widths, fingers feeling, eyes following, then each tension tapped—a low, familiar note that answers the ear: right, right, a little more. Fog pushes its shoulder over the rim. The cords pearl. Down the runnel lines, you can watch decisions being made in miniature: this drop joins that one, this gathers enough company to become a run, that clings and then, after considering the curve of the fiber, lets go. A person stands at a spout, one hand on the lip of a jar and the other on the lid to keep it honest. The click, the set, the shoulder-shift of lifting—the muscle-memory of these actions are their public rites. When the cistern lids are lifted, there are always two bodies there to do it. No one ever knows the taste of the inside air alone.

Authority, insofar as they acknowledge such a thing, sits in a crossbar rather than a throne. The people who hold the bars hold office: the Lid-Pairs, who are selected as much for how they get along with each other as for any other merit. They open and close what the whole town breathes from; in exchange, they are the last to draw in a scarcity and the first to be watched should a sound in the lids seem untrue. There are Measure-Keepers whose eyes are unflatteringly precise; you will not love them when you are young and thirsty and they tell you no. There are Runnelers whose job is to keep the grooves quiet—no new sharpness, no grit that catches, no moss left proud. There are the Bast-Walkers who go down to cut and haul the long strips of plant, two days away and another two back, never alone and never in a hurry.

When they gather to decide anything wider than how to mend a frame, they meet on the not-quite flat they call a square because what else would you call a common space under sky. The square has a tilt. It means no one stands in the exact middle. They stand in the drift of the little slope, ankle muscles doing their continuous, correct work, and they listen. Speech goes in circles as the lammergeier does: patient, widening, returning to what was dropped on the first pass. They have a habit of not speaking until their thought has become a sentence in their mouth that feels like a stone you would be willing to put into a wall. There are not many clever arguments; there is frequent pointing: here, this groove, this notch, this place where it will do what we want if we do not ask too much.

They believe in exchange that is like the press of palm-to-palm: mutual, familiar, leaving an imprint you can see later when you hold your own hand up to the light. Trade is the way the outer world touches them and the way they touch it back. Salt comes up in shy packets tied in bladder. Oil comes up in bottles that smell of lower green. Once in a long while something extravagant arrives, like saffron or a bolt of silk that will be cut into what seems like too many small strips for the price and then worked into cords that hold a memory of summer. What they send down is certainty: water clean enough that those below call it sweet and then save it for births, for cure-making, for those sips that ask for a new start; leather that has taken smoke and cool and come out tasting of a place that is not greedy.

Food is what you can do without spilling. There is bread flattened to the thickness of a morning and cooked on a blackened, chosen stone. There is dried meat from goats that belong to other people but whose bells they hear once a month; that meat is sliced thin, never put into a stew so that the water might keep to itself until the tongue is ready. There are broths made dark with bark and chilled to clarity to let the shaved scraps of fat float as punctuation. There is a tea made from the leaves of those knee-high shrubs along the parapet; it tastes like taking a breath after clenching. Children chew a certain moss when the fog has been consistent; they call it sugar even though it is only sweet by association. On festival days they make something soft out of old barley traded in for jars—they soak it forever and make a paste that tastes of patience itself; they call it something that translates best as “kept.”

Their calendar is a choreography of wet and wind. The year tips around that notch to the west where arrival hides and then happens. They do not count days as much as they count drops. The water-clock jar is the community metronome: that perfect bulge, that let-go, that clear, domestic strike against the bell-plate. Children are named on a morning when that drop happens exactly as a hand surrenders to it. The number of bulges between the first pour and the first strike becomes part of the name, as will some feature of the day—a glancing hawk, a fog that faltered, a coat shrugged off earlier than expected. Their names fit into their mouths like their own saliva. They are not poetic to anyone but themselves and they never try to translate them down the mountain.

They keep small gods, but they do not ask those gods to meddle. The Drop that releases when it is heavy enough. The Weight that keeps the lid honest. The Edge that is always there and has never lied to anybody; if you do not listen, that is not the Edge’s fault. The Lift that can only be made by two. In the niches—those everywhere niches that houses grow as naturally as lung-sac—you will find tokens that would make no sense anywhere else: a crossbar carved of sucker-root kept smooth by being handled only when a person feels light-headed and reminds themself of the condition of being held; a chip of the first mica a child noticed on purpose; an old knot, cut free, kept not for the cord but for the memory of what it bound.

They mark adulthood not with a feast, which would be an advertisement to water of what it is not, but with a walk that begins before the fog forms and ends at the minute when anyone not from the ledge would say there is no light to see by. The walker carries an empty jar with a good lid and does not fill it until the year’s first small rain makes a sound on the stone. Into that mouthful they pour their first tear if they can manage one, which is a trick because high air makes crying hurt. If they can’t, they sip and then pour that back and that suffices. What they keep is the lidded emptiness afterward, which they will set in the niche and which will look a little like a promise and a little like a truth.

Old age, when it arrives, is a grace note more often than a burden. There is no pretense of authority just because you have lasted; if anything, the old who have kept best are those who can say little for long stretches and then say a lesson that avoids any capital letter. Their work is to pass a palm over stones that need the reassurance of being known; to sit on the knee-high rim and be the weight that prevents someone else from leaning memoryless into air; to listen for the change in the drip that means the clay lining in a belly has developed a thinness you can’t see. When they die, the town does not pretend to ownership of them. Fire is a luxury they do not spend on bones. The lammergeiers circle without being asked. There is a place past the last deliberate stone where a shelf juts and the wind is particular and kind; there, wrapped in bast and one good wool that is not good anymore, the body is given to the same slow salvages that keep these people alive. Later, when the bone is quiet, what remains is slid into a crack that has the right temperature of respect. Names are not chiseled—too much swagger—but rubbed into the edge of the parapet with a stone that carries their hand oil. The shine will last as long as those who knew them do. Then it dulls, and that is not tragedy but the truth the mountain knows.

They have dogs when there are goats visiting. The dogs are guests too and get drink from the bowl with the crack in it, because no one is a fool. There is a cat or two, sinewy and underfed by design; they patrol lichen and ledge for the little click-footed lives that get into too much millet if a sack is forgotten open. Children want pets; parents hand them a cord and teach them a knot and say tie this to something alive and see if it minds; if it minds you must untie it in such a way that it doesn’t know you have done it. This is considered a joke and also a long lesson.

The inside of their houses smell not so much of things as of uses. Slate warmed last night and still giving back. Stone tasted by steam and now refusing it. Bast that has been wetted and dried and carries a clean sour that means it has been good enough long enough to begin to be beautiful. The little stoves are black and important without being central. They make heat as an argument that food is a better way to keep warm than fire. A pot simmered with as little fuel as possible teaches the patience in the mouth that the lungs learned at birth.

They do not keep time in years when they can help it. They keep time in repairs. Ask how old someone is and they may tell you without reluctance: two clay re-linings and one lid replaced; old enough to remember before the notch widened in that rock there. There is a mathematics of stone in everything they say. You might hear an old woman, answering a child too clever with counting, say: never mind your numbers; show me how the runnel sounds when you put your ear to it and we will talk of that many.

They are not suspicious of outsiders; they are precise with them. You will be given a lidded cup and someone will show you how to hold it in a way that means you and the lid are in agreement. You will be told where not to stand, not because you cannot be trusted but because no one’s body knows this place at first. If you try to help and you are in the way, someone will put their hands on your elbows and move you as if you were a jar—a courtesy extended to things that are, in the end, full of water. You will be laughed at if you whistle; the laughter will not be depressed by apology. If you stay long enough to be corrected without embarrassment twice, someone will teach you to read the marks underfoot and you will realize that what you thought were scuffs are sentences.

There is song, but not much of it in the way most would recognize. They hum, and that hum is less about tune than about agreement with the day. The nets’ long, low answers on windy afternoons are their fugues. Their dances have no leaps; they are geometries of weight-sharing and pivoting on one good foot. The bell from the clock makes a scale of hours. Children have a clap-game around the drop—slap, pause, slap, ring—that adults pretend not to prefer to any other kind of music. On those rare days when the fog refuses them and a person must put their ear to the wind to find out who they are, sometimes someone will lift a voice and sing a song that asks nothing and gives back the shape of the ledge itself: a long phrase that hovers at the verge of breath and then caps itself like a lid.

There is argument. Scarcity makes justice an object, not a mood. When someone spills, the spill is seen. When someone forgets to tension a line, the wind tells on them in a register everybody hears. Shame is a useful tool that they do not overuse. Punishment looks like carrying: the one who broke a rule is asked to be the weight on the lid for a month, to be the left hand for anyone old, to walk the morning line and count aloud to the bell so that no one else has to and so that each count becomes a promise.

No one there thinks themselves heroic. They speak admiringly not of those who take risks but of those whose hands look like they’ve been wet and then dried in the right way for a long time. The bravest thing you can do is also the most ordinary: step into the old lines and do the old things so that the same drop can keep happening. They have experiments, but their experiments look like soft changes—this angle more friendly to pearl, that knot less likely to cut fingers in cold. When one works, they do it again. When one fails, you will hear the failure talked about for a season and then you will see the tiniest new scratch underfoot that says: not that way.

This is what lives there: people whose bodies answer altitude and who have educated their wants to the size of a jar. Also—because it would be an injury not to count them—moss in the splash-shadows that burns with green like a secret you would never betray, beetles the color of the idea of black, a moth that knows the exact hour when the sun leaves a certain wall and does not rise to go until that minute. Also rules that have turned into reflexes. Also a bell whose single note is how you know where you are in the day without ever looking up. Also the air itself, which learns the long habit of condensing when asked and of moving with the people who move with it.

If you stayed a season, it would recompose your attention. Your thoughts would join your feet and become careful and sure. Your voice would find the hum. You would develop the ache in the forearm that comes of holding a lid down not because it needs you for force but because it asks to be attended—because everything that matters there is a conversation between weight and will, between drop and lip, between stone and skin. And you would see, suddenly and then as if you had always known it, that what lives there most completely is the practiced motion that happens again and again until even the mountain agrees that it always meant to be part of it.
