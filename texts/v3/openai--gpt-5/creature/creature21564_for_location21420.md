What lives here are the plants that are not plants—grove-bodies that take the form of columns and plates, ribs and coils, yet are not built of wood or want of water. They are mineral-organisms, heat-engineered, ceramic and carbon threaded together until intention has a root.

Call them groves because they gather. Each stands as a single creature with many faces: a trunk of dark ceramic wound with conducting veins; a crown that is not a canopy but a radiator, a stack of mica-thin plates that swing on flexures and click home to angles you could write as a diagram. They grow by glaze and by lacquer, by laying films that cure to tool hardness, by adding lattices a strand at a time until what was braced becomes bearing. Their tissues have no sap in the way you think of sap. They carry a clear, slow, almost oily solvent that bears charge the way blood bears oxygen. It slips through capillaries that are micro-machines as much as vessels: channels ribbed to create turbulence at microscopic scales, baffles to shed heat, expansion chambers that store pressure as pre-stress in a girder.

Below, they do not spread for water. They send runners—ceramic cords and carbon tapes—down the lines that the crust has opened. Those runners taste gases without mouths: hydrocarbons thin as whispers, nitrogen edging out of stone, halide breath in little haloes around a flaw. Where a trickle is strong, the runner splits into many, filaments braid into a tap, the tap extrudes a halo of crystal to grip the fault walls and make a clean interface. Rootlets are chemical zip ties, buckling-prone in air, perfectly stiff in crack. They do not pull moisture; they pull threads of molecules and unspool them into pieces the crowns can use.

Up top, every surface is an answer to the light. The plates are covered on their working faces with a porosity you cannot see unless you catch it glancing: a pebbling that increases exchange area, each micropore a charged pit that wants ions, not water. The undersides carry velours of mineral hair grown simply to trap wayward grains and conduct their charge to ground. Along the rims, where two neighboring plates meet, there are bead lines—a row of brighter gloss—that are switchgear. In the first heat of morning, as thermal gradients wake, those beads flex. They translate temperature into differential strain, and strain into opening. The crown does not spread like a leaf; it arrays itself like an instrument.

In full light the groves work. Not photosynthesis in any green-world sense, but photothermionics and catalysis: sunlight giving surface charge and heat; heat making a pressure head; pressure driving ionic fluid through organ beds where embedded catalysts chip at gas-borne molecules; chips become monomers, monomers become oligomers, then mesh into films that glitter like oil on slate when they are thin and turn a tempered black when they are thick. Growth edges are not frilled but crisp. If you scrape a fingernail against a plate, a curl lifts—iridescent, whether violet or straw, the color of trace dopants in resin.

They exchange without animals. Where leaves would flower, filaments of doped ceramic creep into the air and pause, held straight by their own stiffness and the lift of almost no breeze. Day by day they lengthen, little by little the crowns pump charge up them to the sharpened tips. The sky is not kind enough to arc on spectacle; these are threads strung tight across a soundless gap. And then one day the threshold is there, an invisible field swells, one tip finds another’s potential, and a stitch of violet snakes from crown to crown. The exchange is data and smallness—strand-length instructions coded into conductive polymers, neutrally buoyant sporelets with chemically sticky faces, a whisper of protocol: who I am, how I build, what has worked. In a wind rare as a favor, those sporelets drift farther and settle because their charge tells them where to stick.

Their rules are so clean that any body built of them looks like intention.

They live in guilds you could name by architecture the way botanists of a wet earth name by leaf and flower. There are flat-crowned platesmiths, whose canopies read as sheets from a distance and up close are a tight field of scales with hinge lines so sharp you could cut them. These are broad-shouldered, good at catching the long straight heat of noon; they lay fast films and shed them often, a kind that fills its own dripline with strata of abandoned work.

There are concertina-kings, folded crowns that open with a fine, audible pocking as shrink-stresses break their gloss—accordion canopies that dilate and constrict to run pulses through trunks tuned to harvest the push and pull. They live on margins where the diurnal swing is strong, making work out of temperature’s back-and-forth, turning what for others is only warmth into a pump that breathes.

There are coilers—the cable droopers—with crowns that trail like hoses. They are condensers of electricity, all coil and plate, happier under a clear brutal zenith where light does not scatter and where their whiskers can reach other crowns across clean fields. When the brief arcs go, theirs are long, violet stitches that you see peripherally and think were dream.

There are lattice-trees that look like scaffolding. Their trick is tensile. The bioceramic of their ribs prefers a little stretch; they load every member and let the loss and gain of daylight pre-load and release them to move their solvent. They sing, though your ear can barely get it: a thin, insectless string sound when a crown unbends at noon.

Under your feet and between the groves live the early workers: low mats like frost, fine nets that grow outward in a circle then thicken. They are soilmakers, though the soil here is bright and slick and wants to take a fingerprint and never give it back. They do not drink anything. They exude salts that creep into microcracks and crystallize with the night’s cool; crystals swell enough to lever glass-ceramic apart by micrometers at a time. Their bodies are beaded with charge. Dust settles on them, is pulled down and held. On mornings after a very slight stirring of air, each mat’s perimeter frosts with ultrafine grit, and when the day warms and the mat flexes, it kneads that grit into place with the patient pressure a person would call massage.

Within the groves, smaller lives persist as parts, as organs, as independent lineages that cannot live without a host and that a host cultivates. Inside thick trunks there are corridors braided of three materials in recurring runs—a loop that is conductor, then a loop that is catalyst bed, then a loop that resists. Along these loops move bead-colonies smaller than sight: dense balls of polymer wound around a core of ions that behave as if they had decisions. They are the grove’s maintenance crews and its diseases by turns: some etch a little insulation away when channels start to foul; some lay down anti-fouling in lacquers six molecules thick; some shuttle dopants from storage to the skin. Now and then a rogue lineage matches the handshake of a maintenance bead without matching its task. You do not see illness like this from far. You see edges dulling faster than they should, plates that will not open to full.

If there are parasites, they are laggard films and clamps that never hurry. A leecher is a velvet darker than the surrounding velvet that lays itself along a heat-exchanger’s back and simply sits, using the grove’s gradient to run its own tiny chemistry, encrusting its way to resources the way a barnacle uses a wave. A healthy grove has habits against them: it peels a film each week off important surfaces and lets it skid away; it pulses extra charge along a threatened arm to make the leecher’s lip uncurl; it lets the dust it collects become abrasive lining and uses a quiet shudder to scour itself.

No one runs. Even motion that looks fast—an arc—averages toward slowness over the day. Juveniles appear as radial carpets where dust and stress are right; a season and the carpet lifts its center into a stub with two plates like hands; three seasons and there is a crown with a dozen faces, all too small to arc more than to its own neighbor, but practicing. A decade, and that stump has ribs. Three decades, and it sits at a height where its whiskers reach the next grove’s when air is very still. They do throw runners underground to move faster laterally than plate-building would allow, and if you put your palm on bare stone a dozen meters from a trunk that is about to throw one, you could mistake the slow flex you feel for your own blood. It is not. It is a ceramic peristalsis starting up—stress turned into flow, flow turned into new root. They do not bud where there is nothing below to breathe; they test first, sending probe threads that crackle as they go, reading gas-scent like a spectrum.

Their reproduction is part local, part broadcast. The violet stitch moves instruction. The tiny sporelets—the nanoscale seeds—carry templating fragments and kernel programs and some trace of variance that plate-born groves cannot afford to hold in place. When they plate to a neighbor’s inner face, they do not germinate like a wet spore on a leaf. They move inward on the push of the grove’s flow, pass through a sieve that unmasks them by charge, then seat in an organ that is mostly registry. There, between plates of cooled resin, is memory. It is not stored as letters but as crosslinks and dopants at ordered intervals: a code a crown can read and write. From a handful of good stitches and a few thousand seeds, a grove can throw a runner into a new fault and not be itself there, but be kin. Over centuries, patterns wander a little. Across kilometers, groves converge on what the air and stone will stand.

At the very high edge, where glare goes to white and shadows are rulers, there are specialists. Some lift plates bright enough and flat enough to glint like signals. They are reflectors that could flash you if you waited a day to see their one moment of alignment. Some are spidery, extruding vertical whiskers until they find that the air is too thin to make a good stitch and then stopping there, turned to lightning rods that never need discharge. They ground cosmic dust instead, their hair catching micrograins charged in the sky and milking them for their few precious ions. They smell the strongest of ozone, not because the sky obliges them with storms but because their own fields crackle in tiny, short arcs you do not hear.

There are creatures entirely underfoot, never rising into air, that if you encountered them with your hands you would call worms for their shape and only then think better. They are narrow tubes of graphite-silk composite that crawl by slow buckling through gas cracks. Inside, they are full of ionic liquid and beads like those in trunks. They are conduits and scouts and sometimes couriers. A grove that loses its crown—smashed by a crack opening too fast—will not die if its belowground tubes still cross into a neighbor. Above means work; below means life. The underthings are lifelines you could map the way you map roots of a banyan: a city of touch beneath field of plate.

Death is dilution, not decay. When a grove decides a plate has lost too much of its surface gloss to be worth its skin, the plate detaches; a tremor little enough to be missed shakes loose the two or three beads of resin that hold it; it skitters a very short distance and lies down in the sun forever. Nothing eats it. Films creep across it, etch here and there, pull a little dopant back out and hand it off along an invisible circuit to a living neighbor, but there is no rot. The accumulation is neat, almost mannerly, and would look like purposeful design if you did not watch how thoughtlessly it happens. Given centuries, what they shed becomes floors, then shelves, then pale beds where mats can finally take purchase. A grove that grows long enough makes its own geology, and its grandchildren seat on that.

They have senses. Not eyes, but entire crowns are arrays of brightness that feel insolation and map it to force; not ears, but ribs are piezoelectric, and every faint tick of cooling stone is a pressure change a grove can measure across its own scaffolding to triangulate a distant failure in the crust. Not a tongue, but runners sample breath as composition and return a profile that writes itself into a strain programmed in a ring of ceramic: a permanent little bend that says “there is methane here in this proportion, and halide breath this strong.” If they remember, it is as pre-stress—a spring already wound to the task it is best at; as doping patterns in resin; as crosslink densities that change which catalysts take root where. That is their inheritance, as much as any coded seed.

What passes for community is in the sums they perform across distance. One grove may hold more charge well, another may be clever with halide cracks, another better at throwing runners over dead glass. They work out, over arcs and repeated stitches, how to be enough alike to recognize each other and enough unlike to fill each niche. They do not share food; there is no mouth to feed. They share algorithms. Across a sky that never bothers to thunder, they make their own weather of information.

Hazards, when they come, are simple. A seam closes. A vent goes sour with a gas that gums certain catalysts. A dusting falls so fine and dense that even velour clogs, and a crown must spend a morning foundering, half-opening and half-closing, trying to shed its burden without friction burns. Once in a long age a cloud from the country below drifts ragged to the shelf’s lip, a grey smear that cannot hold itself together, a mist so thin it would be mercy in any world that wanted it. Here it beads as poison on a plate’s surface. The repellents work. The droplet refuses to wet and sits like a blister on a pan. Still, a grove flexes itself hard on such a day, plates tight to the angle that heats beaded water the fastest, speed the only mercy they grant themselves, turning a toxin to vapor fast enough to get back to quiet.

In all this there are no eyes that look at you, no heads that turn, no wings that forget they are work and are only joy. The lives here are filled with industry without hurry and purpose without story. Stand and watch long enough and you can tell line from line which crown does what and in what order and for which reasons written in stress and chemistry. It is a lushness measured in how much choice a body keeps in how to be its shape. It is everything engineered that was never built. It is a forest that is a factory that is a field of minds without a mouth among them. They are the ones who live here.
