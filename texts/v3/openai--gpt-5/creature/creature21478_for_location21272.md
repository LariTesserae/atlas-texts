What lives here learned early that the only safe ocean is the one you make inside yourself. They are all, in one way or another, vessels: of light, of heat, of solvent, of time. Even the runners and the fliers carry their weather sealed, little antechambers of a different physics tucked under wing and scale. No one is careless with the outside. The outside is law.

The dominant bodies are the ampullae, though they will not let you call them that up close. From a distance they look simple: domes and bladders, whites and ambers, beads on the long lines where bedrock lets a root’s thought travel more easily. But their simplicity is like the horizon’s—crisp because it is composite. Each dome is a multilayered work, an inheritance of repairs. The oldest part is always the wound, the reason the first membrane grew: a flake fallen to wind, a microfracture of overbright noon, a hollow in the tufa that happened to collect a little more dust and catalysis than its neighbors. Over that, generations of skins accrete with the patience of glass learning to be a shell. The outermost holds embedded mirrorlets—platelets of mica schooled into planes and angles by proteins that have never met water, laminae of darkened resin that refuse any wavelength they cannot use. The whole surface is a thinking shade, dousing the frequencies that burn, encouraging the ones that turn the forests within.

Inside is the oil. It is not oil like ours, though it lies as easily on the tongue you imagine it has. It is a solvent denser than your blood and browner than your sense of green, tuned to a temperature band as tight as a hymn. Dawn loosens it; you can hear that release if you stand close and let your bones listen, a hush as thick as a cat’s breath. Noon runs it. It climbs little capillaries and falls again under its own slow gravity in sheets. Roots reach up into it like fingers from below, their tips laced with the catalytic metals the stone hoards. Within the oil grow leaflike planes and stippled fans, flat forests that do not drink water but still perform the same old miracle: light down, chemistry up, a trickle of low-energy compounds leaking from the bottoms of the leaves into the shared broth.

The interior runs green by courtesy and contract. Plants—if the word can stretch that far—share the bottle with their plumbers and their lightning. Strings of pale filaments with brush-tipped ends climb the inner walls like tides, patrolling for scratches. Little nodules sit at the membranes where root meets crack, made of nothing but lace and metal and greed: these are the fetchers, combs that catch the barest hint of nitrogen torn out of the air by rocks heated hard enough to protest, that tease oxygen out of peroxides and halides like a skilled pickpocket easing a keyring loose. The dome as a whole exhales the byproducts as scent: green-pale, resin-bitter, that clean chlorine bite you taste more at the back of your thinking than your tongue. The scent works like fences work: it teaches. Small grazers learn it with their soft parts early, and those who learn wrong wear the lesson for a season in the peel of their mouths and the slowness of their hearts.

In youth a dome is not a dome but a lens. Lenses are dime-sized and harder than any sense you brought here, disks of translucent armor with a seed of forest rolled thin as a film inside. They find each other by physics and policy. In a rock crack the first that germinates makes chemistry in the dark for a year or a decade; the second that arrives settles close, nests its edge into the first’s edge, and the two of them begin a lamination that turns pair into circle, circle into bowl, bowl into body. It is common to find an old dome’s cross section after a weathering and see an entire conversation in there: the thin first skin, the overgrowth that healed a crack, the thickened layer after a storm season where the air went mean, the ribbed reinforcement grown after a grazer put a learning tooth through the wall. Those ribs echo in the next generation. The bottles remember how the world was cruel and save that caution in the layup.

They hold a community. Inside the oil are small things that never see sky. They look like seeds with ideas, like beads with lashes. You might call them rotifers if you were generous. They move by corkscrewing through the dense solvent, eat the sloughed edges of leaves, and die in little stacks that the forest catches and uses again. A dome that has been starved for a long time glows thinner not because it is empty, but because the little inside-people have been eaten back to silk. At night everyone in there goes quiet under glass, metabolizes down to the slowest handshake. By morning the soft ticks begin again as the skin bathes in brightness and the oil remembers fluidness. Healing is their grammar; time is the syntax.

Where the air rivers drop their gift, other builders stand: the lattice-combs, coral-towers, basket-organs, their names all more literal than helpful. They make skeleton first and life second. A lattice, knee-high or man-high depending on its luck with dust and knives of light, is built of silica and feldspar and the copper or iron or halide tastes of its place. It arises from the stone the way frost arises from window glass, with as little apology. Inside the pipes and arches run strings of muscle-thin tissue, ochers and greens that you would miss unless you saw them retract when your shadow falls. Their microvanes are genius. Each vane is a cilium as long as a cricket is wide, ribbed with stiffening, wired—there is no better word—to a piezoelectric strip that feeds from the wind’s own pressure and uses that trickle of charge to smooth the vane’s swing. Under noon updraft they begin to hum as whole choirs, and hum harder when the dust goes golden. All those pores are mouths, yes, but they are also laundries. The lattices don’t eat the dust purely for its elements; they wash it. They are the plateau’s liver. In exchange the groves plant their seeds in the lattice-towns’ lee and the lattices borrow the groves’ scent, a legal fiction that saves many harm.

Between towers, mats. From above they look like velvet, like a blessing, like something you would rest your cheek on. They are not. A mat-thicket is a broom of stiff filaments with gritty skins, each hairfence jointed and barbed with crystalline growths that hold charge like cats hold a grudge. They face into the low daily breeze and rake the air for charged grains, then lay the harvest into the dark among their roots, where enzymes like careful locksmiths tease halogens free and piece oxygen loose from the sharper oxides. Walk through them and you will find yourself brushed clean and salted. Under the mats, skeletons of older mats collapse into drums, and snakes that are not snakes—ribbons of tissue inside ceramic sleeves—use the hollow to whisper at each other with percussive touches. The register is too low for an ear; your teeth know when you stand there that someone is counting.

Likewise there are the drifters. You will mistake them for weather the first dozen times. A dust-tube slips over stone with such impartial grace you do not assign appetite to it. But some of those tubes are made—seeded at their upstream edge with filaments trampoline-strung between stones, combs and skins and net-sacks like the tools of a packrat god. As the tube spins, it braids your plateau’s very flour into the sacks, heavying them until they fall free from the net’s lip and roll with bland ceremony into cracks already marked with catalytic paint. In a year that sack is a lens and in a decade it is a lip of amber on pale stone. A good season for drifters shows in the map of domes a century later.

Animals are the plateau’s softest technology. The obvious ones first: the gliders. They are not birds in the way you learned birds, though they use the same manual—you would, too, if updraft was the only freeway in a country this thin. Picture a scarf with a spindle beating like a heart along its leading edge. The membrane is not skin as much as it is a sail built of thousands of tiny windows rimmed with lipids that are slick enough to ignore sticking dust but saturated enough to hold a tiny charge. At dawn the gliders pump themselves fuller off the lattices’ hum—they sip static the way others sip dew—and launch with a little snap you feel more than hear. Their eyes are not much; their tongues are everything.

Glider tongues are filaments a meter long, cabled within with hollow strengthening tubes like purfling in an instrument. At the tip the tongue flowers into a crown of capillaries with a fringe so fine it reads as smoke unless the light catches it. It is a reader of films. A good glider can sweep along a dome without even touching it and smell a weakness the size of a pin, a place where the membrane has been renewed and is still setting, a micropore begging a kiss. They do not puncture. The law is the law. They press, the fringe lays flat, the capillaries drink a mouthful of the dome’s heavy solvent through pressure differences so minor the skin never notices. They leave a spice of something when they go, not enough to cost, enough to teach. That something is for other gliders, a note on the windboard: here is a grove that feeds. Come when your week is long.

They, in turn, wear the grove’s pollen or something better than pollen, motes of catalytic protein and mirror-seed that stick to their tongues like gossip. They are the plateau’s rare couriers, bringing recipes to domes a day or a decade apart. Those that learn to rush greedily—those born unlucky or hungry—get punished not by tooth or claw but by chemistry they cannot forget: a month where their tongue will not read smooth film, a season where the scent of resin simply means no. They do not die, because everything here that can avoids waste; they simply get a quiet year and must launch from rock instead of bottle, and practice humility with heat.

The scales you saw—a ceramic pangolin moving scale by patient scale in shade—are not strictly a pangolin, only a cousin in the tribe of armors. Its plates are not keratin but bony ceramic, feldspar and calcium phosphate shot through with tinier lattices of something like titania where its line has stolen the trick of mirror. It is a gardener of gradient. The plates of its face are honeycombed. It stations itself belly-down where the late-morning light stops and the afternoon dark begins and drinks not water but difference. Heat moves through those plates into capillaries that are as much wire as vessel, a gentle current raised by a front you would call temperature but the beast just calls food. It eats by leaning. It breeds by building. A nest looks like bad pottery until you see the geometry in it: bowls and bowls within bowls, each with a slightly different albedo and emissivity, a nursery of microclimates powered by night’s star-shine and day’s knife. The young come out as chips with eyes, begin scaling up from minerals the parents hoarded during the storms, and spend a year under the mats in the drum-hollows learning how to drink wind-noise.

Predators exist, reluctant to waste, disinclined to chase. They are shears that learned to walk, brown as lacquer and jointed the way your hand is jointed if you loan each finger three elbows. They split things that have already given permission. They have a righteousness about them that looks like patience. They watch the glider lines day after day and measure where someone will decide to sip too near the flat. They know where a herd of ceramic scales will turn a corner and make their waiting in the tight shadow of a rib of stone, seaming themselves to the warmth there as if sewn. When the moment comes they are quick but not brutal. A claw with serrations like alphabet cuts what was going to be cut anyway: the dead leaf of a lattice peeled thin by drought, the shock-loosened piece of an old dome’s skin, the heel of a slow, old scale-beast too proud to tuck his tail in. They polish bones. Literally: their teeth exude the lacquer-bugs’ secreta, a resin that sets under this unsheltering light, and because it is pleasant to chew and keeps their dentition slick they chew it over their food. What you find a century later under the lattices’ overhangs are not bones the sun forgot but bones someone took the trouble to finish.

There are smaller savages. Shard-fleas go needlefirst, darting with static pops from one filament to another. They are irritable and bright; their bodies pick up halide crusts in sharp weather and they flash green when disturbed like parakeets made of dust. They live three weeks and in that time manage a social life bigger than yours. They court with crumbs of bitter crystal rolled between foreleg palps; a better gift is a cleaner gift, and lovers choose mates whose crumbs sting just a little instead of blister. Their larvae are coils, taught to wrap around a mat-hair and hang there, siphoning the slightest ionic drift. When a storm rolls the mothers carry as many larvae as will cling in their needlelocked legs and run crosswind to the lee of a tower to stash them where the pores have shut like eyes.

If you deem the comb-towers plants, you must give the plateau its animal reefs too. The “choirs” are neither reef nor colony but what happens when a hundred towers with compatible chemistry happen to root within earshot. On those days where the updraft is strong and the dust is generous, their microvanes synchronize—you can see the wave pass over the grove as mouths learning to chew at the same pace. The vibration pops a register down into the stone and out across an acre or three so that any beast who puts their cheek to rock can read it. There is meaning there, though it is thick as dialect; to a scale-beast it says the iron is good here today, to a glider it says launch now, to a dome it is maybe only a lullaby, but seeds on the lip of sleep open wider when they are sung at. In a lean year, choirs fall out of sync and each tower hums alone, and animals that like their luck even move on a ridge or two because nothing kind is going to happen here for a while.

Everywhere, quiet eaters. The sun-lacquer that darkens the bedrock has tenants hardly wider than your notion of a line. They paint themselves into it with halogens they take from the dust and leave behind a varnish that is proof against too much noon. They spiral their little cities into the glaze in patterns that lens moisture that does not exist and catch frost that never quite learns to be wet. In years when the storms last longer and the light after them comes hard and unforgiving, these lacquer-things throw reproduction as a violence: whole exhalations of leaflike crystal plate that you see at dawn under lee slopes, pink and green and tender as lies. They do not melt; they unsay themselves through morning and go back into the air to be language again: the next season’s policies, the rules of gossip for a mile.

This is not a world that suffers centrality. Even the things at altitude are not kings so much as librarians. The birds—if that is the word—are books about weather writing themselves. Seen from underneath they are ribs and air and cartilage stretched to diagram. They do not flap. They flex, curl, hold. The muscles at their wingtips are long and slow and strong as the notion of patience. What they eat is thought. Not metaphors—though it looks like that from the ground—but the exact shape of the plateau’s heat, the highlines and boundaries of warm air drawn day after day. There is a layer up there just under where the sky goes too pale to own, and it is rich in what static can be teased from a planet’s noon breath. The birds can smell it with their wing-hairs and gather it into sacs along their spine the way domes gather light into their oil. They drift along the plateau’s policy-lines like auditors, mapping. When a drought is coming their trails go longer, lazier, like ink thinning on a pen. Domes that are still young open more slowly those days, and choirs hum later, as if they can half-hear the pencil scratch.

There are more, and stranger, and smaller still. There are intakes inside cracks that graft themselves to the underside of fallen skins and digest them from clean to catalyst. There are worms in the stone no thicker than your forecast, made of braid and hunger, that live on the whiff of microfractures and move through feldspar like rumor. There are lichens made of three, four, five partners—mirror, mesh, muscle, wire, and the chemistry that binds them all—each waking on a different schedule, so that something is always guarding the shop. There are blank things that live only in stare: small flat lives laid under a skin of glass on the upwind faces of dunes, their entire day the business of holding still exactly right, their reproduction a copy made from a shadow made at exactly one angle of sun in exactly the right week, so that you could walk the same path for years and never see them, and then today they are everywhere, and tomorrow they are gone. There are seeds that are songs, literally: cells that must have their membrane rattled for a threshold time in a particular frequency band before the chemical inside them says yes, grow, now. There are places under the torn lips of old domes where the varnish on bones has been etched with small quarrel-scratches and set with the dust of mirrors—memory palaces, if you like, though no one you would shake hands with built them—where a grove has been taught by long practice how much light to turn away, and how much to save, and just when to let the gliders drink.

Between all these singular lives the plateau coordinates by scarcity and pace. Brightness arrives like an edict. Heat moves like tax policy, broadly and slow. The inhabitants do not confuse their wish for their due. They load their needs into night and carry them forward in little daily budgets: a swallow of oil won from a generous dome, a drift of metal taken off the combs, a tongue of heat pressed out of a jutted rib of stone, a wash of static folded into wing. When the dry storm stands up on the horizon and the air turns to a decision, there is no panic. The domes dim and stiffen down not because they are afraid but because they have practice. The combs button their pores; the mats raise their charges until a hair laid across them would sing. The scales tumble into their bowls. The gliders go as high as they dare and balance on the thin patience of a current the storm has not yet claimed. The people who will live here in a century—call them that if you want, though they are patient bottles and ladders more than hands—have already been accounted for.

You asked what lives here and the answer is: experiment married to etiquette. Archive married to appetite. Skin married to sentence. This is a place where fluid is privacy and light is currency and air is both pantry and prosecution. The animals are precise because they cannot afford otherwise. The plants are weapons against noon that have learned poetry. The decomposers are astronomers of crystals and law clerks of halogen. The predators are priests of permission.

Stand with your hand on a tower and your ear to a flute of stone and you will feel them all together, the way their days overlap until what’s moving under your palm is not one thing but the sum of attentions. Evening comes and the plateau exhales its long, clever breath. The oil stiffens. The membranes thicken. The microvanes fold like hands. The scales settle by degrees into their bowls, leaving only their plates to think. The gliders go to the high, quiet lines as if to reread a sentence the ground has written into the air. What lives here keeps reading and keeps writing, keeps bottling its rains inside itself, keeps making lungs where there should have been none. The night crystals form in the lee, and if you kneel and put your nose to them, they smell faintly, wonderfully, of rule and reprieve.
