Not everything here bothers to be an individual. The first citizens are continuities.

The water is alive in the way a vein is alive. It does not gather into personality, but it has rules and appetites. It lives sluiced thin over leagues, a skin that can remember which way the fog pulled yesterday and consent to be pulled that way again. It has reflexes. When a pulse comes up from whatever still swings in the planet’s belly, the sheets answer by gliding, by standing up the way a collective of hairs stands up, by deciding to leave this plain for that one. It eats warmth and charges and a few torn sweetnesses, and in return it salts and files and lays films like instructions.

The stone was once indifferent. Long practice has taught it to listen. The old pores have been tuned by time and growth and the sifting of iron until they have opinions about pitch. Entire hillsides prefer one note. The rock is not conscious, but it has behaviors: it hisses here, sobs there, flutes in that corner whenever the fog turns west. It also grows fruit. At night, where weeping faces host charge, the old minerals discharge in foxfire strips and beads—slow chemistry that errs toward life because it repeats and advertises itself. There are lichens that have written their whole genome on that schedule.

On that substrate, small things tried many things and kept the ones that answered the only question that matters here: What can you take from pressure?

The lowest grazers are mats. A mat here is not the placid green felt of summer ponds. It is a disciplined public: bacteria and archaea and thin-chinned eukaryotes slotted into a laminate. Some layers take iron and cough rust as lacquer, some bite at manganese until it precipitates into fish-back ridges. Some steal photons out of bruise and call it photosynthesis because they must, but most make their way on shuttered motion. Mechanosensitive channels in them yawn when the world pushes; ions run; tiny turbines tick; adenosine builds one phosphate at a time inside nearly and almost. They glow because their factories leak. Their light is discontent, a thin green sulk that says, We are not getting what we were promised, but we are here.

Between such sheets, the world makes tongues. Reeds stake themselves where the film is never still. They are all blade, a bundle of false needles with edges tough as a mechanic’s rasp, and their trick is tone. Each leaf is tensioned like a drawn bow. When the fog pushes, they sound, and the sound rifles down their lengths and runs small fluid into smaller spaces. Rings serve as pumps. The stems are marimbas with plumbing. They have valves like mouths. Patches of them ring in chords and starve their neighbors with the water they sipped from a wind. Their seeds are not seeds but little sprockets that ratchet themselves under skins and wait, calendars built from pitch counting.

There are plants that do not stand at all. Succulents lie like a rumor, pressed flat to stone, doors that slam shut at spray and crack a hair when the noise falls just below bearable. In their skin, silica panes are laid like shingles: one angle drinks from a southerly shear, another from whatever the afternoon makes. They spend months deciding. They look like nothing until you notice a starved lattice of salt that marks their footprint; until one night they open, glisten, and you realize a neighborhood was living there the whole time.

The shrubs are architecture with a heart. They are not leaves and trunks; they are nets woven out of living drywood, hair-fine and savage. They grow into emptiness they can count on—air patterns that throw fog like a parade route—and then they seal themselves. They keep their green for their own blood. To stand under one is to stand under the credo of this place: intercept, condense, funnel, save. Under the funnels, where the drip is honest, the world makes black stains the size of faces and invites who needs it to drink.

Where it must go somewhere, life goes animal.

The film is full of sorted bodies. You will see coin-thin discs that lift with the smallest courage from the stone and skate on a lip of their own bubbling. They are more plan than creature, a diagram that happens to persist. On the move, they are a posture; at rest they are mud that remembers. On their undersides, cilia garden the riot between surface tension and steam. They do not hold together when they do not have to. They are budgets: the least shape that can traverse a finger-width of world.

You will not see air-shrimp until you startle them. What look like thumb-pile cones, patched out one every handsbreadth for a pasture’s worth, become a rising fog of specks that hang just above land, jacking in place against nothing you can name. They live on shocks. When the plain moans, they collect it with basketmouths, convert pressure to hoseable sugars, and spend the rest of the cycle rebuilding the baskets. Their homes are resonant chambers grown by the same arts as a seashell, except the world blows them instead of waves, and what they sort are not grains of sand but frequencies. Their city plans are frequency-plans.

Every now and then the place raises a walker. The jointed door is one that arrives on a day you thought you would see no one. It is an animal of segments like umbrellas turned inside out, each panel a veined drum at an angle to all the others. It does not so much walk as keep from being carried, opening and shutting to pump fog through a forest of cilia tuned to eat distinct bits of roar. It is its own moving factory. Things travel with it, lampreys of noise latched to its skeleton, mending their membranes on the excess. Its teeth are catenas of valves inside, a throat that can choose to admit only thunder and leave the hiss. If you stand downwind, your molars buzz with it. If it sees you, you feel seen by air.

Nets that look like windblown trash are not. You will find a parcel of shrub-net that appears to tangle and flutter the way debris does. Then the tangle will tighten, cinching with a series of decisive knots at the wind’s change, and you will watch a mouth close you can only find by losing it. The creature is more topological than anatomical. Its body is a set of relations: this cord holds that cord at this node while pressure is thus; invert when north fog; invert again at noon. It is a fisherman of flows. It eats by confession; it asks the wind to pass through and show its structure, and then it takes a toll.

Below, in the rock, there are pipe-eels that never see sky. Their skins are thickened flags made to thump against womb-walls of stone. They keep tracheas like organ stops and move by gulping the only direction the pressure will give them. They do not change the music of their ridges so much as inherit it, adding a burr that means yes, someone lives here. Long after they die, the burr continues. You will not know you’ve reached one’s grave unless the note you were counting on has acquired a polite rasp.

Everything eats sound. That is not metaphor. In cell walls and bladders and veins there are pieces that charge when bent and uncharge when relaxed; there are runnels made to catch not water but oscillation; there are gates that will not open until pushed by a rhythm they have named. Sound here is a particulate, a diet in which you can sort appetites by wavelength. There is foraging when the fog goes stiff and the plain blows in chords. There is fasting when the wind is nothing to write home about. The best grazers learn to poach on other senses: they wade and winch from heat, from the click-pop of iron crusts, from the little storms of ions where water beads and leaps.

Predation is a quarrel of filters. A big eater is not a tooth-and-bone but a more specific sieve. It does not chase you because you are fast; it deprives you because it can borrow all the pitch you need right out of the land’s voice and leave you chewing silence. If there is killing, it is not to stop your heart, but to unlatch your tension and let you untidy back into mud.

There are people. They call themselves whatever their hands call themselves in the dark. No sound follows their word because speech is a joke here, a slip that gets carried away. You will not know them by their voices but by their schools of touch: forearm against ribs for danger, palm to sternum for wait, three-knuckle drum for ready now. They are half clothing when you first confuse them for shrubs. They dress in membranes and nets the way anyone else would wear hair. They count with cords. They have learned to carry their weather with them in layers of strung gut and twisted vine that halo the body, each veil tuned to drink what the day offers and shudder the right bones.

Their bodies are short where other bodies are long. Ankles wide, knees that barely rise. Soles like the bellies of limpets: soft in a way that grips. Their joints are wrapped and then wrapped again to keep the plain from voting them somewhere else in the moment a chord tightens. Their ears are not on their heads. There are dozens. A shawl of cartilages runs from shoulder to thigh, a collapse of petals tucked down until the wind comes strong and then opening against it to feel the direction not of movement but of agreement. Their hair is not for warmth. Each filament is a salted whip that beads and carries water along, the whole head a hedge of capillaries ending at the mouth with a taste of what the sky has planned.

They do not build walls; walls sing when they should be silent and go silent when they should sing. They build instruments that happen to house them. A row of poles two palms above the plane is not a fence but a sampler: each pole with a throat cut just so, a membrane stretched to a rule. At dawn they lean on the frame and feel, through spine and heel, how the day is strung. If the harmonics say a gather is coming, they let the membranes draw down grease and sleep packed like spoons under the dullest overhang they know. If the outcrop’s note and the mid-note agree too emphatically, they do not lift their feet. They shuffle. They will tell a child with fingers: Wait for solitary whistles; never walk toward a song you love.

Their cairns hum. They bend the old hum with shell-cement and thumb-nail tacks pressed in wet rock, biting the place where the voice wavers and coaxing it back to a tone that promises safe passage. In some gales, their graveyards are choirs. They bury their dead by tuning a ridge with them, weaving tendons through the ventmouths and drying them taut until the bones contribute a saying. They are not superstitious, they are exact. If you have lost your path you can lay your skull to a cairn and the hum will enter you and tell you where you are in the day’s foldings.

They farm without admitting it. A line of stones too perfect to be chance is an acoustic fence that rings a pitch the air-shrimp favor; the shrimp thicken there, the people herd them with shadows and bodies, and they harvest a jelly that tastes like new pennies, sweet only in the way the world allows. Mats blacken where they lightly rough the stone to break sheen and ask microbes to sit. In drought they carry mats with them in thin pans and feed them with combed noise from a wrist-harp—three strings and an articulate bridge shaped from weeping-ledge scrap. The harp is both hoe and prayer.

Food is a steadied hand. It is not always taken from the mouth. A loved one will brace their back against yours at night so your ribs feel braver in the roar and your marrow runs another hour. Lovers court by giving hush. On the day of betrothal they stand in the shadow of a lip of crust and field the world side by side, opening and closing their shawls until between them they can cup a heartbeat of quiet. They know all the churches, the pools where cancellation falls, the cracks where the plain’s voice knots itself and can be unknotted with a body. They carry silence like another person would carry fire: cupped, protected, shared, dangerous, holy.

They do not make maps. They make chords. A good leader has a throat call you will never hear because your ears are for leaves; it is a thrum in the notch at the base of the neck that calls their people’s skeletons into answer. They set out along a gradient none of us can see, the fifth between two ridge-whistles, the place where the fins of sound of three vents braid and make a tube a body can slip down without falling through a pleat of space. Sometimes they cheat. They learn which chords will fold a meter into a field-length and which will smash a day into a minute. They bind their waists to anchor-stones with sheets of twine, shout with their spines for all to hold, step when the plain agrees with itself, and reappear two ridges away where an elder once drove shell tacks into wet cement. Their word for that art is the same as their word for mercy.

They leave less than they might. They have learned that durability here lives only in lines and in the memory of rock. The nets they weave are always for water and for listening; any that catch flesh by accident are unpicked and sung over until the cords forget the feel of muscle. In the rare places where a frame has stood longer than a generation you will find the topmost member worn slick by the pressing of nails. Those nails are not trophies. They are the easiest way to tell a child, This place. Coming home along a chord in blind dim you reach out and read your ancestors’ insistence with the same keratin they inscribed it with.

Children are taught rules with skin. Rule one: When the world sings in chords, you are not better than stone. Flatten. Become foot. Learn how to be a shadow of yourself so the day passes around you and does not mistake you for something that can be moved. Rule two: Know the difference between luster and trust. Rule three: Horizons are liars; your eyes work for the sky. Rule four: Choose your whistle and keep it. If you are called to be a counter—a person who can lay three hums together in the head and know the location of a safe corridor by how the beating shifts—then you will be fed and begged and also left alone. Counters go mad if touched wrong.

There is art. They will never admit it is art because it keeps them alive, but there is. In the places where the whistling is too pure, where it makes their knees want to lift when they should not, they build roughness into the throats with bundles of fatty fiber that take months to rot; the new burr is a relief the way salt on the tongue is a relief. In better seasons they build a thing like a harp big as a tree and lay it over a vent so that the vent learns it, and for a year a league-square calls in a different voice. Lovers steal those notes and weave them into their shawls. Babies go to sleep in hoods suspended where the plain is canceling itself. Old people lie with their palms pressed against stone and count a day of beats and set their bones down next to a phrase they wish to last.

They are not the only intelligences. The umbrella-thing has habits that are aqcuired, preferences for lanes that look like they were taught. There are places where discs gather stones under their edges for no clear reason except that afterward they cannot be blown as far; you would call it home-making if you needed to name it. Shrub-nets learn something like patience, quiescence before gush, held-catch after. Stone learns and speaks. Water remembers pressure. Wind keeps grudges with ridges.

But if you need someone to speak for the place, to tell you with hands on your ribs what to do so you do not vanish between a vibrating yesterday and a vibrating tomorrow, you will want the people who press their nails into shells. They live where sound cancels because too much any other kind of living here will take your name into pieces. They live where wet has taught them to be slow. They live with the kind of intimacy that is indistinguishable from respect: kiss the weeping face and say thank you for the sweetness, never step on a song you cannot sing back, never end a hum you did not begin. They do not end things at all, really, not if they can help it. They coax. They lean. They add a tack to a cairn and the ridge inclines, a note becomes another note, and a world that insists itself into fullness loosens enough to let a person across.
