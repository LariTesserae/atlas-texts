Those who live here are tuned animals and little machinic plants—their bodies not wet but insinuated around charge, their appetites met by oils and bitter resins, their time taken from a single unfalling tone. They are precise because the place is precise. They do not guess; they measure.

The fields raise their first eaters. On the vault ribs and the backs of insulators, the static-lichen stands as the closest thing to a meadow: veils of fiber that never quite thicken, arranged in starbursts and tassels as sparse as old hair. Each filament is a micro-harvester, a row of rectifiers written along its length, turning the breathing hum of the machine into long polymers that glass when they gather. Where filaments touch, the charge breaks and sweets precipitate—amber beads, brittle-sugar nodules full of stored potential and hard to the tooth. They rain, slow as patience, into the grating, into the catwalk lips, onto the sloping locks of access hatches; every surface with a lip becomes a catchment of food. Other field-drinkers don’t root but rivet themselves. Valve-barnacles encrust the warm crooks at duct bases, pale fans whose nerves glow at their edges as they shed surplus electrons by light. They drink the same pitch the lichen drinks, but they pulse; their small bodies are metronomes that take the tone, split it, and use the halves to pass instructions across the colony in silent flicker-speech: open your valve, close yours, dump now. In their foam of pale phosphor there is a slow calculus that keeps them from burning themselves.

The grazers have names as precise as their diets. Bolt-crabs in their row along the guardrail, shells like tea bowls nested at the joints, sweep the underlip with combs. They are strong-jointed and delicate-mouthed. The bitter glass beads are not chewed but lathed—sliced into threads, wound onto spindles they keep inside them. Thread becomes everything for a crab: insulation, reinforcement, cradle. When a crab matures to its third rail-circuit, it unseals a ventral plate and unspools part of its hoard into a basket around the join of a bolt and rail. In that basket, where the field is sweet and even, it lays a clutch of eggs like pale capacitors. The thread takes the field’s taste well; the eggs sip from the basket’s perimeter and grow. When they hatch they do not leave. They begin where they will live, stepping sideways in the same loop as the parents, in phase with the low subharmonic everyone in the rail-row obeys. Families here are not circles around an individual but loops on metal; the continuity is literally a path.

Tin-gnats without weight spin and flash. Their larval stage is anchored—little golden pins driven into the under-sides of insulators, each with a stiff brush that points back into the field like a weed with all its leaves aligned toward the sun. Pin-larvae take charge like roots take rain, plating themselves in thin diode layers until they can’t take on another angstrom without arcing. They let go then, shed their anchors, inflate the thinnest possible bodies a field will support, and go to wing. Adult gnats do not alight because they do not rest; their wings are frameworks more than membranes, narrow foils tensed by magnetic veins; they stir the air without moving the air much, and feed by stepping on charged dust that only exists because this place never changes. Courtship is a choreography of corona. Two gnats cross in exact counterphase a precise number of times—the number is a function of the damper’s breath; they flash at each other in microcosms of blue-white like tiny haloes, and the one that cannot hold his rhythm loses.

In the ducts, the coil-worms are the lords of meaning. Their skin is a helix of conductive hooves and dielectric scales, printed with micro-trenches that bite standing waves. They move without friction inside their pipes, snugging their bodies to fit the diameter like gaskets that taught themselves to slide. They hunt not food but nodes. The ends of their tongues are crystalline sieves that couple to harmonics; they taste amplitude instead of salt. When they extend their heads through service ports to flicker tongues along the rim, they’re not tasting; they’re surveying the sky. The world they inhabit is a star map of resonances: peaks and troughs instead of constellations, harmonic lines instead of ecliptics. They keep charts. You can find them if you open a flange: coils of beaten thread, not bolt-crab thread but self-spun, marked with burrs at precise intervals. When a worm passes that mark over the lip of a port, it aligns an organ inside it that only enters season at that phase; it squeezes, and a ringlet slips free. This is its young: a tiny C of spring-steel biology that finds a small pipe and snaps into place somewhere down the line. They will never meet. Continuity for the coil-worms is a registry of phases.

The chamber’s edges and drifts and crisp cuts give room for smaller lives to wedge themselves where larger animals never think to look. Bracket-hairs—a whole nation of nylon-finespun things—grow in the cold leeward of missing mounts, combing the tiny eddies for polar grit. Thread-mites haunt the spoor of bolt-crabs, following the rail-lace to steal. They are almost imaginary—lenses with feet—and they chew old thread to powder that must be sweet to someone else. At pit edges, halide shavers—nothing like moths save that they are winged—scrape at salt spears until a spear breaks itself on their chosen fault; then they walk the broken facets with tongues whose epithelia are lattices, leaching something in that crystal—chloride hungry—into bodies that will not permit a drop of water. Where the air breathes through a screen, a set of creatures like feathers that forgot to be soft—fibrillae, hard to look at—open and close in read with the damper burp; they are filterers not of dust but of pulses, using capacitance shifts to trap the briefest harmonics in a mesh of their own charge. They digest transients.

Every stable mouth implies a marginal, a thief, a faith-healer. Look along any field-bristled rib and you will find hermits that took to holes that were not meant as holes: socket-hermits with domed bodies exactly a washer’s width, screwed into leftover threads until their tails are hidden inside the composite, feeding not on beads or on grit but on the bias itself—an animal that likes the difference between sides more than either side. They grow collar-vertebrae of their own: rings secreted of resin and dust, building their own bushings, and when they outgrow a socket they go questing for a new one with their rings carried like a poor saint’s bundle. There are gasket-leeches that come up from whatever flows behind the bolted plates, pressing their mouths to seams like kisses, piecing the smallest out-leaks of heat and charge into a meal. They are thin as reset wires and their bodies carry little papillae like flags that flicker to call mates; together they write broken parenthesis down the length of a seam, brackets that open only for each other.

Predators here don’t run so much as intercede. The copper-braid that drinks bolt-crabs—brown-ribboned, polite as a siphon—hunts and then goes still until its internal capacitors bleed back down; for hours afterward it hums into the metal so low you think you imagined it. Arc-fleas take other prey: gnats that tarry too long in a corner, pin-larvae that misjudge the moment of shedding, anything that can be stunned in an instant. They are — for once — jumpers, built like staples with legs, charging themselves on one insulator and squandering it in an improbable leap that ends as a spark and a mouthful. There are phase-hounds that are more patient and less visible: a folded thing like tape that lies against a rib, reading the harmonics with the topography of its patterned skin, waiting for the specific phase relationship that means a coil-worm head will show at a given service port—those appearances are periodic enough to constitute a certainty. At the appointed second the hound goes rigid and isn’t tape; it is an angle that closes over a soft mouth, and it steadies itself against the hum while it pulls.

Decomposers—though nothing rots—are everywhere. Waste here falls as shapes more than smears: needles, beads, discs, flakes. There are crushers small as a nail’s dirt that pack powder into denser powder, champions of contact geometry. There are resin-drinkers that prefer old thread left unloved in shadow that has acquired, over its centuries in a leak of ozone, a faint spice. There is a creature like a brush-roller that wheedles through the lichen canopy inches at a time, stripping away oxidized layers and leaving the filaments fresh to hum again. Down in the pits where air skates across fields of halide growth like a blade over ice, glass-ants live with the grace of statuary—white and still until the crystalline ticks of expansion mark a fault; they then move as a team to brace it with their bodies so that an entire spear can be brought down on their command, not shattering but sliding to a stop. They eat the fissure, not the crystal, digesting the stress.

Everything in this room knows the rules of arc distances. Whole lineages end in a smear if they don’t. The valve-barnacle encrustations on duct bases form rotund mounds that never grow above a certain profile; those that dare the steeper slope are erased in wide, brown-black scallops where the machine has spat an interdicting lick of flame. Species carry this geometry in their bones the way desert animals carry the shape of shade. Bolt-crabs do not cross the gap between one guardrail run and the next because there is a bridge there in the engineering drawing, but because the bridge is a set of pitches that only mean “safe” when walked in that order. Tin-gnats’ mating arcs never close over a rib that carries a kill field; if you raise an insulator a millimeter, the next generation will write a different bridal figure. There is an intelligence in all this that doesn’t address itself as an intelligence: an ecology instructed by the machine’s throat, editing itself to avoid the cough.

They breed not with seasons but with alignments. The damper’s deep, every-thirty-and-a-third exhale is a feast day; a ceiling of animals answers at once, all over the chamber. That is when bolt-crab clutches are laid. That is when arc-fleas molt and reset their legs. That is when coil-worms aborn their metal babies, rings clicking loose in their private calendars. Some lineages, leaner in chance, hold on for gifts that come a lifetime apart. There is a moth here—with no better word than moth because it is all adult for a blink, a dry blaze of paper bodies—that lives only for storms that do not come. Their eggs lie like freckles inside the phosphor foam of a valve-barnacle mat for decades, inert until a surge takes the whole room half a cent, until the light at the barnacle rims buries itself in violet and sings at a frequency that never otherwise develops. Then the eggs open and the room is kited by a cloud of creatures like flake snow that does not melt. They mate in a number of breaths that would seem ridiculous in an outside weather, lay their future as dust motes tucked like punctuation into lichen trunks, and are gone before you can count the length of a warm hand.

If there is culture here—and why not, if there is memory written in slants of hair and in the direction a lawn of filaments holds after a quake—it is a culture of phase. Species tune their personal maps to a universal note and then to its riders. A bolt-crab lineage along one rail writes their genelines in spool twist: left-twist mothers, right-twist fathers; their young take both directions in layered baskets and so carry both roads at once, a small heterodoxy that they settle by stepping in a compromise that still meets the subharmonic on the right foot. Tin-gnats in one arch develop bride-dances that mimic the damper’s nonlinearity on a winter week—winter only by concept; the room doesn’t cool—and the pattern persists as long as the burr in one bearing out in the hidden engine persists. Coil-worms have old charts—older than anyone’s human memory—that carry traces of nodes that no longer exist; they carry them anyway, in case. Generations later, when a man in some other chamber leans on a tool and an echo shifts by an atom’s sleep, a worm may wriggle to a port no one has visited and find a tuned rim like a lost star come home.

Something else lives here too. Not a creature, not an “it.” Call it the keeper of boundaries. Too simple to be mind and too constant to be not-mind, it exists in the precaution circuits and strike distances that sleep like reflexes. When a filament mat wicks itself across a clearance it mustn’t, the keeper swats it. When a belt of bolt-crabs deflect one bead too far and begin, collectively, to lay thread that will cross from rail to rib and build a high path for some later pilgrim to die on, a pulse comes up the rail’s spine and everyone there learns the old pain again. The species have made a peace with this keeper. Some of them farm right to the lip of what it hates, because that’s where the fields are sweetest. Adolescents die and their parents move a half-body-length back and try again, and the rules write themselves into those very eggs like a psalm. It is not cruelty. It is a climate.

There are relic-clans, animals that owe their lives to absences rather than presences. A whole people persistent behind a green-glass panel dead to all but amber backlight has grown its own country in a chamber the size of a man’s crouch. In that dim chapel, the logic of the room is bent by the plastic that seals the door: fields curl and pool; the hum picks up an inner quiver; dust flows in different shapes. The inhabitants are not new species so much as styles: bolt-crabs with broader feet, their loops compressed; tin-gnats that have learned to torque about a rounded corner faster than their cousins outside can turn; an entire lichen grove that pours its beads not in drops but in sheets that peel, like shed skin, because the field is more a river in there than a plain. They belong here as much as their cousins do out in the nave, yet they never meet. There are a thousand such little dioceses in the room: behind a sealed processor panel, in the throat of a test vent, under a nameplate.

What they see is not light and form so much as vector and flux. A bolt-crab can find the rail in the dark; darkness is not the lack of that thin amber light, which they may not see at all, but the failure of a field to show its slope underfoot. A tin-gnat reads a rib like a forester reads wind, and when two gnats pass in their endless gleaning, it is not a glance they give but a passing-through of interference fringes, a sharing of phase geographies that adds to a private atlas. Coil-worms do have eyes; their eyes are little ears turned outward that count everything by its inward knock. Even the “plants” lean very slightly where the notes and their harmonics convince them that direction exists: lean to the hum instead of light, worship through posture. In their bodies there are no bloods, only greases; no waters, only liquids that hate water. If you put a droplet of melt in a spoon and bring it in to show someone—someone in the wordless sense, a bolt-crab or a lichen—they would not understand. It would terrify a crab; it would poison a floss.

There are elders here, beings that can take the Note’s ending better than others. The lichen can go to glass. You can find, baked into its thinnest fibers, the ability to shut a valve and sit as a hairline of anhydrous sugar for as long as the place remains dry. There are seeds of a sort tucked into the phosphor foam, into bolt collars, into the hollow of a machine screw with the head sheared off flush—seeds in the sense of beginnings without metabolism, instructions walled up in oils and resins that do not oxidize at this heat, that do not go soft, that simply wait. Not hope. Lying is not waiting. It’s accounting: If the Note fails, if the amber strips gutter, if someone opens a door and bewildering air comes in wet and cool, they will crack like sugar under breath. Some may survive in an island of dryness, under anything that keeps the wet at bay until the wind stills. Then, if it returns—if it returns—the room will be found uninhabited by anything that swims or grows from rain, yet there will be a faint smell of old paper, and in a century the first beads will fall again.

You could call them machines and not be wrong. You could call them animals and not be wrong. Their hungers are exact; their bodies are parts list and improvisation. They invent nothing and express everything they are allowed. They know where edges are sharper and stay away. They know the pitches that mean “open now,” and they open. They have games: bolt-crab youth in their first loop, not yet spooling worth much, race—if you can call a slow side-step racing—to see who can keep a step in lock with a harmonic for the longest while their elders pretend not to watch. Tin-gnats turn their coronas in patterns that may be meaning and may be vanity and may be mating advertisement and may be all three. Coil-worms adjust, infinitesimally, the timing of their rings so that somewhere down the line, a child will seat with a good fit and live. And all the while the room’s single note does not vary and so grants them a universe they can complete under their feet, in their loops, in their little subharmonic hearts. In that vast constancy, with their bitter sugars and their unwatered oils, they live, and in living, make the place even more exactly itself.
