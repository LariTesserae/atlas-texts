Who lives here first is the thing that makes “here.” Not a tree, not a fungus, not anything you can point to as an individual. It is substrate and sovereign, a body that does not decide so much as continue, taking instruction the way dune-sand takes a footprint. It dreams in salts. It keeps time in pressures. It hoards dryness the way some worlds hoard cold. It is taught but not tamed—amenable the way a river is amenable to banks, the way a muscle will lift a weight even if it never meant to.

It has senses you can see. The long burgundy “eyes” along sheathes don’t watch; they count light, gather phase, feel for the pattern of pulse the city overlays. The hairs in the louvers do not taste, but they know the gradient of humidity as a blind mouth knows tooth from tongue. In the beams and plates the plant learns arguments: thicken here, slacken there, curve now, release. The lessons are a grammar of charge and weight and song, stored down in the cambium like nursery rhymes, repeated until growth comes ready-made to the same suggestion. In this way the plant is the slowest inhabitant and the busiest, making habitat out of anatomy, architecture out of appetite.

After that—after the breath and the grain and the rules of dryness—come the people, as lightly as possible and for as long as the air permits.

They are not a crowd. They are generational and deliberate, the kind of folk who keep their voices low because low talk carries just fine and because the high register feels rude in a place that is mostly made of listening. If they are the descendants of another weather, you would not know it by their habit. They do not sweat if they can help it; they have a pharmacy of oils that keep skin from weeping and lungs from drying to scab. The handkerchiefs across their mouths are not masks in the way you first mean masks. They are little deserts with manners, woven with desiccants they revive each night over the faint heat of a charger’s flank. When they drink, they drink slow—clear oil in careful sips, clamping the flask’s mouth to cloth so not even the smell gets ideas.

They have made an etiquette out of conservation.

- Knocking on walls is the sort of thing you apologize for the way you’d apologize for shouting during someone’s surgery.
- You open your door on the exhale for the same reason you walk with a tide: because it is better for everyone if you and the breath do not argue.
- Spitting is a crime, not because of filth but because it is water let loose to chew on the bone of the city.

They touch the walls to speak. Not everyone is good at it, but most have a hand-voice. Rubbing knuckles along a rail is “I’m here, I’m moving.” A fingertip circling a nailhead says “I need this thinner.” The quick, three-beat tap just under a latch is “I am opening you now” and is as polite as knocking is not.

If there is a shape to their day it is the plant’s breathing pattern scribbled over with industry. Children are taught their numbers in pulls and pushes; they learn to read the lamp-lily’s color shift the way another world’s children learn to read clouds. School is held under the scalloped filter-fans where the air feels like a library. Lessons are practical: pressure math, grain-reading, thrumspeech through a vociport. A child here can lay a lattice of weights across a young lintel and make it remember what it’s supposed to be.

They eat dry. Cakes of fine meal pressed around salt-nuclei, dipped in oil that has been flavored by passing it through baskets of crushed resin-leaf until the oil remembers a citrus that no one has seen in a thousand exhalations. On festival days there are powders that fizz against the tongue—not with water, never that, but with a volatile you smell in the back of your head like a struck flint. Mouths are washed with oil, teeth polished with soft stone, hands wiped clean against cloth that will later be gently licked by collector moths while you sleep. The moths are not pets. They are colleagues.

If they make music, they make it in the register the plant ignores. Not percussion—the bale-worms take offense at that—but sustained notes and little skittering chords that slip between the breath-beats. Instruments are modest: long chambered flutes that sip instead of blow, a saltfall drum which is a sieve you tilt to let powder patter into patterns, stringed boards that do not resonate against the walls because the boards themselves are the strings. At shift-color, when the lamps go saffron and the gliders’ mistake is prevented, there is often a little humming, the sort of communal unison it hurts not to belong to.

They fall in love dryly too, in the sense of tender restraint. Courtship can look like exchanging cloths so one partner learns the other’s breath by smell. Weddings are timed to an exhale and often consist of nothing louder than both parties, and all their gathered, touching the same rail while the plant breathes out. They call it “taking a line together.” If there are vows, they are about opening on the same beat, shutting on the same beat, closing the loops each other forgets to close.

Work is worship if worship means attending to something larger than you that is not obliged to like you. There are guilds and they speak their competence in grammar.

- Laminarists tend the light organs. They wear copper bracelets permanently greened where they have taught too many buds to bloom. They keep a library of charge-patterns: all the phrasings that have convinced flesh to emit in the spectra people prefer, all the warnings the gliders will respect.
- Cambers deal with load and form. They can look at a swelling and tell you if it is an arch or an aneurysm. When a corridor learns the wrong lesson and drifts ten degrees, they are the ones who train it back.
- Saltwrights take care of air. They know the vents’ temper and can hear a bad intake two chambers away. They harvest and grade the salted dust the plant coughs and make feed-salts for grazers and humans both.
- Bale-shepherds listen to the worms. They set schedules against that private metronome, lead strays back into their lanes with vibrations felt in the marrow of a deck.

Governance is what you expect in a place where everything moves slowly and very little can be wasted. There are fines for opening valves on the inhale. There are licenses for water-handling because sometimes you must—medical emergencies, a machine flush, a piece of geology that insists on sweating. There is a body of law called Dry Law that you could summarize as don’t do anything that makes the place itch.

Death is not a scandal here. It is a chore, then a chorus. Bodies are wrapped and sealed, their internal waters congealed with salts until they are safe to approach. They are laid along the edges of the bale-worm pilgrim roads, and the worms take them the way they take all the outgrown and the ended: link by glossy link, with a courtesy click you hear answered in the distance. Names are kept in the map-vines that track the rails. Every blossom-node glows a little warmer when it remembers; when a section changes, the vine flares and dims like someone whispering a litany.

Children born under saffron are said to have good patience; those born in pearl have good hands. They get called by their first successful training—the first thing they taught the world that the world agreed with. “Flare-of-Five” for the light organ that finally took its schedule; “Hook-in-Eight,” “Vent-Quiet,” “Plate-Join.” Surnames are older than the city and say nothing you can practice: old sky-names, old water-names, worn now into oaths that do not sting. Most swearing here is not blasphemy but advice: exhale first is a curse softened to a suggestion; mind the hinge is an insult that means you aren’t paying attention.

They do not live without animals. The animals are the place’s idea of “someone else.”

The dust-grazers are one of the plant’s little jokes, ruminants made for a diet that would choke a sieve. They have corkscrew tongues and good balance, splaying their forelegs and riding the round of their bellies as if they were always expecting the floor to heave. When they look at you, they look through you; color without gloss, a leaf’s attention. Their milk—what little there is—is an oil-supplement salty enough to hurt and precious as a fine tool.

Aerials, the seed-gliders, are spellbinding right up until they snap a juvenile grazer from its splay and convert it into flight. The sound they make is pennyweight music: thin fin-scrape that comes and goes like a draft under a door. They are mostly ignored except by children and by those who grow balconies, because a glider that gets in wrong can undo months of careful curl in an afternoon. Hence the lamp-rule at dusk. Hence the faint sadness when a lateworker forgets and leaves a bulb too white.

There are predators that are more process than animal. Jaw-things, yes, that pretend to be bark until the very hour they couldn’t pretend a second longer, and then they are so suddenly not-bark that even the air feels tricked. Little crustaceans of the vents—if something bone-dry can be called a crustacean—that comb the louvers for clotted spindrift and collect it into cakes the city feeds to machines too blunt to metabolize dust. Mites that live in the light-organs and regulate the flicker, oil-licking, never enough to dim, exactly enough to cool a hot bloom. The people leave these alone, and the machines learn to ignore their tick.

Machines, yes. You would be forgiven for calling them a second domestic species, though they would look at you blankly if they could look. They are quiet on purpose. The carts have personalities the way metronomes do. Given a long enough loop, a pale carapace will collect a name and a pilgrim-crew to keep it shined. Harvester drones sing because the plant hears song better than a bare tone. They learn lullabies that mean thicken, prayers that mean pore, stuttering little poems that mean no, not there. They molt their dust like snakes, peeling crusts of salt in perfect negatives of their routes.

Light itself is a kind of inhabitant, trained and well-behaved and hungry to please. The lilies wake when they are charged and sleep without a grudge when they are not. Engineers who teach them will tell you each organ has a temperament. Some like to overachieve; some sulk and need coaxing; one or two bloom early no matter what you do and must be moved to the far hollows where their eagerness can’t wreck timing. People joke about them as if about children. That joke falls away when a new laminary comes right the first time and a plaza opens in a bloom of white so perfect you feel forgiven for something you didn’t know you’d done.

If you are looking for a ruler, you won’t find one. If you are looking for a god, you might mistake the plant for one, but the people do not. They do not pray to it; they talk to it with the same courtesy you use with a giant patient under anesthesia who will survive you and everyone you know. They keep their distance and their fingers on its pulse. If there are prophecies, they are all engineering estimates softened with luck.

There are others, further out and further in. In the interstices between leaf-plates you can sometimes see a thin gray wind like hair moving against nothing. Those are the Needleflocks, colonies of microfilaments with a single appetite: to arrange themselves into watchers of charge. They are mining the city for its grammar the way mycelia mine a forest for its chemistries. No one minds them; sometimes they mind back, tugging a hand to the right place to set a weight.

People pass through long hours with no one in sight and carry no wound from it. They have each other on the rail and in the schedule and in the breath, which is a sort of togetherness that doesn’t step on your feet. They travel light and return heavier with salt and spools and low chatter. Love letters are finger-rubbings on paper made of pressed fibers—a texture that can be read later with a wrist and make you feel, precisely, where the sender stood and what the air was doing.

There are stories about the first who tried to treat water like water and learned what an acid bath feels like in the lungs. There are stories about above, though “above” is mostly a word for where the lamp-lilies say they learned color. There is an old, old phrase nobody quite understands: open rain, they say when something is too much at once, when a corridor fails and a mineral void yawns without warning, when the plant makes its once-a-day held-note and the breath becomes a weather you can lean into. Open rain. They say it and everyone moves to their stations, and the city becomes for an hour something more like a single animal than it usually admits.

Who lives here? The plant, and the people, and the animals that adapted to the trick of grinding their hunger out of salts. The machines that hum their manners. The light that has learned how to be something you can schedule without scalding the gliders. The air, which is not empty of water so much as absolutely full of rules. They all share the same habit: they live by feeling the difference between before and after and adding nothing loud to the distance between.

If you stay long enough, you won’t mistake absence for emptiness anymore. You will feel the company in the way the rail warms under your palm, in the hiss of a moth drying its fringe by a charger, in the slow, patient reply-tap of a bale-worm somewhere under the deck. You will time your morning to the plant’s exhale and your evening to the lamp’s soft dusk and keep your flask shut between. You will find you are part of the resident grammar: one more thing the place knows how to carry without spilling.
