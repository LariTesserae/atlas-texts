What lives here has taken the city as its body plan. It is a people of pressure and patience. They are animals and bacterial nations and mineral habits recruited to the work of staying alive in a world where the weather is water and the law is shape.

Begin very small. Along the gray seams and in the mortar’s pores live the edge-makers: films of chemosynthetic bacteria that glaze the places the stone still leaks its faint meals. They lay down metal bright at their fringes like coin-edges—a thing you can see from arm’s length when the skylight passes—copper-gold, iron-blue, a green so thin it is mostly dare. Their colonies are tight braids of cells in clear jelly, and the jelly is whole arithmetic: it swells when the tide slackens and lets grazers in, then tightens against a faster sheet, beads bubbles and sends them up in drapes. They know seasons as changes in squeeze and furnish the city with breath; wherever you see curtains of fine bubbles sighing out of hairline cracks, there is a slow industry beneath, exhaling a chemical history.

The grazers come. Stubble-brows of blind isopods bunker against the lips of sluiceways like a row of plugged stones and lick the flow with fringed tongues. Their armor is mouse-gray and polished with life, not shiny but rubbed—even the manganese sheen on their backs is something they farm, a patina that sheds silt and rejects ice crust. They crop the metallic edge as carefully as a line of hedgers and leave the film intact. When frightened, they do not flee; they go limpet-still and become landscape. In their brood pouches the young are carried like a phrase held in a mouth, released only when the plaza hum says the draft will take them down the safer courses.

What looks like moss from a distance is animal: colonial fabrics of mouths so densely knit they are almost cloth. They cover buttress shoulders and the undercuts of long, low stairs, pale as palm skin, dimpled with apertures that furl and unfurl like thousands of coins flipping to the same beat. These moss-animals move. They do not walk; they loosen and flow an inch in a week, a hand’s width in a season, leaving a peeled-clean lattice behind them like the negative of themselves. They know walls as students of posture, reading by fingertip pressure which arrangement of their openings will set the water’s grammar most profitably for them. When the city leans under a slow internal tide, they roll their field by degrees to keep harvesting from the same long syllable.

Siphon-shelled snails raise palisades where courses bend. They are social engineers by necessity; a lone duct is a deathtrap. They spit grit and glue until their pastel chimneys ring a corner like a miniature colonnade, each tube with a mouth and a door they can shut to the width of a thought. They click. At first you think this sound is geologic—some polite clatter of shifting load—but it is them, signaling pressure and timing by knocking their lids on the exact pitches their angle to the wall makes. When the hourly braid-run of needlefish comes down the main spillway, the palisades close in a ripple before the living arrow gets there, not to defend themselves but to let the street do its intended combing, for the snails profit on what the fish lift.

Doorways have their own citizens. Thin-boned doorfish own the thresholds; they are made of louvers and silicone sails rather than of donkey flesh. Their fins are films in vanes, their ribs are hinges. They sit side-on in an arch and put their whole bodies into sieve; when the pressure train arrives, they flex in sympathetic bows so that the water forks into the number of streams they prefer. They can drink a corridor dry of silt in an afternoon without moving a meter from their chosen lintel. Color is not their business. Texture is. You can only tell one school from another by the cut of their fins, like seeing the city’s window-frames in miniature.

Under barrel vaults live the arch-urchins, small moons crouched under old stone skies. Their spines are instruments. If you pluck one, it sings a note you almost cannot hear—felt more than registered—and the urchin listens to its own chord with an organ we would have to call a body. They will not breed under flat. Larval clouds are released only when the vault hums with the right pressure note, a consonance of mass and tide that arrives perhaps once a season. You can predict it if you watch the rollers of bell-jellies along the parapet; when their lace-bells loosen and set the smallest of their tentacles adrift, it means the same pitch has reached them a few minutes earlier on the slope outside, and the vaults are about to sing.

Bell-jellies are the parapet’s choir stalls. They hang from the lower lips of slumped towers like chandeliers gone to ruin, pages frayed to lace. To call them musical is indulgent—water and stone do most of the phrasing—but the jellies have biased themselves to answer certain intervals of swirl. Their bells have tears in them, and each rent makes an extra beat in the pulse that passes through. The colony is thus a carder of current; it separates, it delays, it slightly modifies. If there is purpose, it is the same purpose as the builders’: to help the weather of water be legible. In their tissues live the only light here that is not theft from a skylight—faintness like grief, not to see by, but to ask a question of a mate in the dark of a rift.

There are gardeners of physics who do not know they are gardeners. Ladder-lobsters patrol the steps, one per edge for a boulevard-length, antennae full as feather dusters. They brush eddies. They scold swirls. They chase pebbles back down to the level where they will no longer insult the gradient that the stair means to enforce. Put a single extra stone on their run and you will discover a whole personality organized around putting it back, and then a day later you will discover the cousin-personality on the next edge that has learned the lesson from watching with the sense lobsters use for watching, a long attention by tail-fan to another’s quiver. Their houses are holes under rails where the metal-sheen gathers; their courtship is a duet of tidying, a test in which two will take a minute to make a flood behave as if it had hours.

Big things do not hurry. A gray sheet larger than a room rises out of a colonnade and floats down an avenue almost indifferent to fluid time. You can call it a ray but you will have not named it. It breathes with its whole underside. Its trailing fringe is an organ of literacy; by letting small bundles of filaments press the wall for a split second against each gloss, it reads the ledger of stone—cracks, charger marks, shivers—and builds a geologic weather in its head. Scars lace the leading edge where years of misjudgment met edges that would not yield. It has survived by coming to agreement with the city’s sentences. There are only a handful of these great readers in the plateau at a time, and they are older than any two-generation romance among the quick.

Others are needle-bodied schools that keep their appointments like bells. Call them swifts-of-water for what they do. They arrive with the hour, not because they carry a clock but because the culverts do, because the whole place was made to lunge and swallow and then rest in a rhythm they have taken as their tradition. The boulevard that ends at a comb gently braids them; living ribbons pour through hand-width apertures like cloth drawn through a carding frame, and for the brief bright minutes while the skylight happens to show, they are a silver script writing itself down the center of the city and then unwriting just as quickly into spareness.

Between plates and beneath stairs, anemone-libraries hold. Do not look for pages; look for filaments arranged like shelving to stack microbubbles. These colonies grow into the mouths of seeping cracks and file bubbles by size, using vanishingly small differences in buoyancy to sort their treasury. They farm gases the way we tuck away food. Later in the cold-long season the grazers will come to pluck them—bead by bead—with tongues like paintbrushes. This is commerce, and it is faithful; nothing here crosses open without a plan, but everyone knows which corridor keeps which pantry at which hour.

In square rooms remade round by flow live the round-logics, animals whose bodies are diagrams. They map the canceling of angles. Their skins are ringed with microridges that set the local shear into prayer beads they can count. It makes them mathematicians of their own pressure. They are not clever. They are exact, and this exactness lets them stay in rooms where the stone and the water are still negotiating what the room will be. You will see them suspend themselves at a place where nothing suspends and be as certain as a plumb-bob, and then the internal tide will come in sideways and turn them into tumbleweed without malign intent. They have adapted to be wrong and survive.

Along the seam where city drops to deep, a different set keeps border. Edge-sentinels coil in the last repetitions of carved edge, lean into the pitch of the wrong hum, and face outward with sensory surfaces turned like satellites. They do not feed; not there and not then. Their job is to listen with their skins and, when the black velvet underworld reaches a fingertip up the escarpment, to unroll and make a dampening film over the parapet—a soft curtain; not a dam, a suggestion. They do not cross. Nothing of the plateau crosses down if it can help it. On bad centuries, something from below comes up anyway, a matte snake like a length of night, and the places along the parapet are very empty for a time. The silence the city keeps then is a different silence, as if there were a third party in the room.

In winter-terms, when frazil falls like ash and new frictions sleep on every surface, frost-keepers bloom. They are kin to the fabric-mosses but grow long hairs stiff as eyelashes and seethe them whenever crystals come down, holding the ice just long enough for microbial dependents to scrape calorie from it before the tide peels it all off. The frost-keepers talk with their hairs, but not as talk; as choreography. From above, a plaza of them in soft skylight looks like a wheat-field in a gust, except the gust is remembered rule: one wave is “food,” one is “storm,” one is “mate now if you can afford it.” Those who speak out of turn die of pressure they did not call.

Eggs lie like frost, anchoring to pores, stacked by surface tension into patterns the builders never expected but would have admired. Larvae are splinters that take the course they’re born into without opinion until the city teaches them another. A culvert that thins a flood into invisible combed threads becomes, to a generation’s young, a test; only those that are the right length are slowed enough to hear a certain resonance and change. It makes them citizens of one hall and not another for the rest of their lives. Whole species here belong to single profiles—an arch, a stair, a weir—and are mysteries outside them, as awkward as a land animal in air. There is nothing wrong with this. It is their address.

Communications do not rise much above etiquette. The place is not a talkative commons. Conversation is made of posture, of stillness, of when a doorfish chooses not to choose for a minute. If there is a council, it is in the once-hourly decisions written underfoot—a citywide turn of bodies like a school that contains everyone, triggered by moments you can hear in great low knocks in the stone, by a foreign flex in the ice ceiling, by a note that moves through the vaults as if they had one long throat. When urgency comes, it comes for all: the arch-urchins hunker, the palisades close, the lobsters sweep, the gliders lift, and the streets empty in a way that would look like fear if it were not also grace.

The builders’ tools without moving parts are maintained by moving parts that learned them. Valleys of posts that taught tide to spend itself before a threshold are combed and kept by shrimp with harp-antennae that stroke the little turbulence-buds off their tips so the field does not trip too soon. Culverts that made wild water docile are occupied by eels that have flattened like belts; they press themselves flat to rim or belly as needed and so become adjustable baffles, not for kindness but because thin water brings them more food and thick water drowns their eggs. A splay of stair grooved by everyone becomes the garden where ladder-lobsters court. None of them worship the city. They use it. And in using it they tend it.

What of minds? There is a mind made between them as there is a harmony in a bell that no single atom performs alone. No one here has hands or words. But there is memory—layered in shells that accrete different under different hums, in spines that lengthen when winter-terms are short and blunt themselves when the cold remembers too long, in great gliders’ scars read by their own young with the pressure-organs along their lips. There are rituals that are not named as such but occur again and again: the hour, the season, the rare snowglobe of a slush-berg falling from the high ceiling, through which everything lifts and orients like flowers turning to a sun that is not a sun but a rule. There are feasters and fasters and specialists who die across open because their corridor was blocked for a day. There are opportunists who learn a new rhythm when a column becomes a mound overnight and open all their mouths to catch the sudden change.

Even the emptiness has tenants—the idea of echo. Deep vowels your body hears when you did not know it could are answered by small things in their portion of silence, a tremolo of gill-membranes aligning so a draft sings through them the way a bottle sings when you blow across it. Shells knocked once in months against balustrades ring a note the builders must have meant; there is a three-second pause afterward in which many living things hold what passes for a breath. If you wait in a corner with any patience at all, the bead-chatter of crustaceans on grilles will begin again at the very edge of hearing, and the courtyard will populate with the low doing that keeps the place working.

Beyond the parapet edge, in the black rent where rules alter, other things live that belong to the other air. They are matte as soot and feel warm by comparison, not in temperature but in appetite. They do not like laminar. They do not like hums that go where the galleries point. When they come up, jellies dim and polyps fold and the whole plateau seems to withdraw behind its teeth. The below-livers write in noise and chew edges. They are a minority presence here only because the made height makes them unpleasantly high. Where their world reaches a hand into this one—the rifts polished clean—the plateau makes no law. The citizens draw themselves long and thin and do not look.

Mostly it is citizens of water who stay. The old architecture gives them pages. They turn them with fins, with cilia, with hair. They obey rules so strict they seem like worship until you see the opportunism that lives in the pores of rules. They survive by being quiet together, by keeping the weather legible, by cleaning a flow the way a choir clears a throat. The city had intentions; the water has its own; the living that have come to occupy their argument have made a culture without our words, a culture of pressure-notes and rooms that are also currents, of being where they must be when a certain hum arrives.

If you want to name what lives here, call them keepers. Not of relics—time takes those—but of use. Everything is slightly wrong now and keeps working anyway. The plateau sets its jaw against being unmade; the citizens are the tongue and teeth that adjust the bite so the jaw does not crack. They are big and small, armored and soft, sessile and swift, but all of them are aligned to the same book. When the skylight slides over and, for an hour, makes the plazas legible as themselves, you can see them for what they are: inhabitants of a patient instrument, not passengers. A world tuned for water has made a living in tune with it. And in the little slow hour when pressure drops and dust lies down on steps and the hum is a room’s breath, they live their calm like a held thought. Then the next pulse comes, and they go to their places, and the long silk of the city’s grammar writes again.
