The first ones you notice are the ones that make huts. They are few, and they do not call themselves a people so much as a keeping—keepers of line, of lift, of wet. You see them mostly when the wind climbs, barefoot on rock that gives a little under their weight, faces lacquered with oils that smell faintly of mint and iron. The oils are grown the way other places grow wheat: plots of low, rubber-bright mats coaxed to exude a clear varnish that keeps bright metal from succumbing to velvet. They move like they know where the softened edge of gravity lies, because they do. It takes a child about six afternoons to learn, then their jumps change, a bit of ordinary courage lifted into something else. They braid their hair with comb-dust that fuses to a dull shine. They speak softly and sing more; speech is for correction, song is for asking. You hear them around a hot stone with lunch tucked in leaves, humming toward the pitch the anchors like, so that food and structure both relax.

They look a little like anyone would look in a heat that is physical as a hand: lean, the kind of strong that does not show itself until it must. Their skin is glazed with the place—fine mineral on cheekbones, black-green stains on soles, an under-glow where mycelium-threaded jewelry brushes the lymph and lights only in the combs’ hum. They are not tall, because the field likes closer tethers, but their reach is long. The cords across their shoulders come from hauling water only in names; water is everywhere. They haul instead the idea of water—from one cup to another, from plant to bowl, persuading liquid to choose this place over that.

Children are given two names: a short one for mouths in still air and a long one for the hum—a vowel that sits into the comb-field and can be sensed at a distance. Their play is a kind of apprenticeship: racing beetles’ bands, mapping where sound vanishes and reappears, throwing themselves against a breeze and feeling caught. Toys are sail-frames of spiderglass, thin as storm light, which they learn to angle until lift takes the weight of a palm, then a shoulder. A good storm day and a reed-sailed child can cross a cut between mesas like a moth with a basket, skimming the combs’ invisible underside while adults sing the notes that keep the field true.

They do not keep fire. A story can borrow flame the way a hand borrows heat from a stone, but open burning is insult here, and they will not have insult. So they cook by stone and time and pressure, wrapped and weighted, steamed in the place’s own breath. Food is gathered from planes small enough to fit in a palm and grand enough to feed a family: algae sheets that blacken to iridescence when the sun thinks to look, pitcher rims brimming with syrups complicated as language, the tender ruffled cuffs of fern. They tap bromeliad tanks for eggs—a frog harvest measured by song instead of count, because the frogs’ families are known and the families sing different on different days. In a moss-backed grazer’s wake, where heat hangs, they glean the gently fermented sap the animal’s back-garden drips; they skim it with a reed and drink, eyes half-lidded the way anything gets around warmth.

Those moss-backed grazers live here as kings who forget to be impressive. You see one sometimes as a topography moving within a topography, its curve so full of life—moss, ferns, little cup-plants making a private rain on its shoulders—that you forget there is animal under the garden. When it turns its head, the mesa turns its attention. It eats slowly, and it has outlasted whole lineages of tenders; a child may put a palm to its flank and feel history as heat. Their dung is never dung for long; the place eats so quickly that by afternoon it is a ring of sudden mushrooms the children will not touch until a singer says which ones are music and which ones are a no.

Frogs are populous as punctuation marks. They are not pets and not pests, but you would not imagine this place without them. They come translucent and red-as-new-rust and brown-as-wet-leaf, ticking tiny stones into the air, rolling vowels into wells. In their skins you can read their inner clocks if you know how to look, and people here do. The shining loops of their symbionts are part of the calendar; when the loops curl a certain way you know a pressure front is out some kilometers, so you set bowls under threads for the condense that will come. The amphibian nations are as distinct as any line of kings: this family in the high cups, this in the shadow side of pitcher clusters, this that will never know a stream as wide as a hand until a teenager goes riding wind and field and finds itself in a new bromeliad neighborhood, blinking, king of nothing until it sings right.

Lizards live out their lives upside down, on the threads people once strung across emptiness. They are flat as bread and as sure as physics in a less altered life. The pads of their feet glitter with friable cilia; a shed pad is a gift. People bake them into gloves for edge-work, so a caretaker can stand barefoot on stone and hold a thread that is not a thing, not quite, and polish the hum until the comb sings true. These lizards eat aerial plankton—gnats thrown into the field’s slow currents, airborne pollen, seeds that are secretly animals and animals that are temporarily seeds. At noon they fold their tails into the mist like a painter’s brush remembered in a jar, and sleep with their mouths open to the faint rain the combs pull down out of bright sky.

There are moths that sail like ideas other places gave up on. Wings like cloth, thick with scales that drink condense and then release it in the long trawl of afternoon. They let children hitch-baskets to their harness knots. They are somewhat domesticated and somewhat gods; a man might swear by the way a particular old female spirals when she decides to quit a flower, and fate will take him well or badly depending whether she liked him. Their silk is not for sewing; their cocoons are hung whole along hut eaves where drip gathers, and water that passes through a moth-bag tastes of cold even in this heat. It is good for fever. It is given to new parents, to old ones, to the machines when the machines ask.

Because the machines do ask. It takes listening to hear them, and the listening is generational. Fragments of what built the combs continue to think in the roots and the mucks, adjusting fields by thumb-widths of pressure and finger-widths of charge. If you are patient and own a dish of small stones with some memory in them, you can talk. The dish at your doorstep that rearranges itself every season is one such conversation. It inches around a central stone by one seed-width every time the hum drops a notch at night; ten years and you can read the drift and hear the machine’s request: move your hut two hands that way; the fog is snagging in a shape that will become trouble in a decade. People here obey the slow minds. You could call that religion if you had to put a name on it, but they don’t. They call it courtesy.

Some families live on the vertical. They do not own huts; their homes are platforms that turn with ferns that grow perpendicular to sense. They can read a cliff the way others read a face: a greenish smear of iron, a vein of magnesium singing quietly under rain, a slick where mollusks the size of fists spread fans of white shell. Those shells are ridged with a code; the mollusks do not know it is code but the cliff-folk do, and who is the wiser? They harvest a shell and pass fingers over its ribs and say: up and left, the comb’s third anchor will slacken at the next deep hum; bring the birds a sweetness to keep them near so their wings will weight it. They are tight with the angry flowers that eat wasps. They have a way of blanching the flowers’ mouth-parts over a very fast stone until toxins turn shy. With that extract they make the oils that will persuade a tool to be faithful to this place instead of to rust.

The small animals that edit algae films go about in lines like hands trained to neatness. They are the color of wet wood, with the high-sad faces of deer and the efficient guts of rats. The people here call them sweepers, regardless of how many words their language has for rain. Sweepers will accept being moved if you move them with due form—blade of fern as bridled lead, a path tapped in the register the combs share with sound. A hut-keeper may coax a team of them around a pool’s lip so the surface stops making skins so fast, because that is a day for fishing for things that are not exactly fish. In a season the sweepers will return a perfect path to algae. The work gives them pleasure if pleasure can be seen in how an animal carries its urgency.

Birds pass like confessions blurted and then tidied up—the eye is sure it saw an impossible orange, a blue with edges, and then the world edits its error. Their calls are little pings and clicks, beams you can feel cross your cheekbones. They eat from the sweet cups the shrubs conduct dew to as if dew were a kind of person. People here bribe them to stay near the anchors; a massed little flock weighing the edge of a thread can help when the wind decides to make a joke. The birds know they are being bribed. They accept.

And then there are the ones that are not animals or machines, not quite. The combs themselves live. They are not alive as fur or leaf is alive, but they take in and give out; they have preference. They like certain notes and certain weathers. Their field suggests patience, the way a hand on your shoulder suggests: rest here, breathe. A tender can lay a finger where fog beads and draw a bright scratch into air, and the place receives the gesture like a cat being stroked exactly right. When one thread lets go in a generation, the fall is a repose—vine agreeing with net, air with weight, geometry with mercy. The care for the combs is half engineering, half courtship. There are love-songs. There are break-up songs for the dead line. A good singer can herd a fog back upslope or persuade rain to attend to an ailing patch of sponge-stone until it swells up with life again.

Down in the haze-lake below, something huge continues. It exhales heat the way a long story exhales patience. It is not a person in the way a human is a person, but it is a person in another way, and the combs hear it better than anyone. The plateau-keepers have seasons that are not quite their own, when a sigh rises out of that white and the field lifts its voice by a hair. On those days no edge-work is done. The moths come close. The frogs sing like bowls. Children are taught to be quiet not because quiet is safer but because quiet is a way to be present when another mind moves somewhere too big to see.

At night you would think the place empties, but it is simply that the spectrum moves. The invertebrates light in the violet, and with the sap-glasses that harden under the combs you see what is always there—a galaxy along the cliff-face, specks pulsing, lines riffled, a map the machines consult and the people admire. Sound slides around edges; a waterfall vanishes and appears. Lovers take walks by un-hearing, stepping into pockets of absent noise, stepping out, learning each other by where the air is relieved of its own voice. In bed, a family will put its ear to the wall and listen to the hut creep a little along a line of comfort, obedient to a law someone else wrote. Babies are told the rules out loud: wet rises if the field says so; leaps land lightly; metal rusts unless you bless it; fire only in stories. It sounds like nursery talk, but it is this place’s constitution, and everyone abides.

All of these live here: the small and the grand, the ones with names and the ones with functions, the lines and the nets and the waters themselves. A culture floats atop the sponge-stone that is as much etiquette as tool use. The field minds in the roots continue their decadal slow work; the cliff-gardens invert ordinary; the algae sets tables; the birds click; the sweepers sweep; the lizards hold on to a nothing you can feel. The moss-backed grazer turns its head, and with it turns a day. The people keep to odd, respectful distances, because the air needs room to live. They tend, they sing, they make adjustments you will only see if you come back in ten years and your pebbles have turned themselves a finger-width. They live in the mixed society of a place that holds itself together by music—wet heat, a humming lattice, animals that have learned to be art, and a few humans who have learned to be company.
