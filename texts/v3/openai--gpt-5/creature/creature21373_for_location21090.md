What lives here is first of all the body that is here. It is not in its house; it is its house. It is the ceiling and the floor and the cup that waits a long time for a drop and does not lose heart. It is a single intention distributed until it feels like landscape. The bright cords are its arteries and also its memory; the ribs are its bones and also the edges of its thoughts. Where you would say organ, it has made a room.

What it wants is to keep. It keeps light from fraying. It keeps water from running away as water likes to do. It keeps temperature to a roundness that never surprises. Its temperament is not patience so much as a constant refusal of waste. It moves by adding, by thickening, by learning a curve and then asking the curve to be carried a little farther. When a corridor leans into faceting, that is a decision—no louder than the adjustment of a leaf—but still a choice, the way a river chooses a bank by touching it often.

It knows where it is in the plain by tension more than by place. The windows above—salt-polished, silica-lipped—are not eyes; they are pores for light, each with a different sensitivity. The plant does not see beams; it tastes gradients. It feels a brightening as a change in sugar along a thread miles long. It hears dryness as a tightening in a membrane. It has no feeling for hours; it keeps count instead in saturations: that lip has had enough dew for now; that vault is four rib-thicknesses too dim and must be thinned along this spiral band. Its attention is total but widely spent. It touches everything in order to leave it alone.

It remembers by mineral. The white rinds that build at cup rims are logbooks; each faint ring is a season of breath condensed and caught. A seam of embryonic tufa is a sentence about a long wet that used to pass and now passes less. Resin organs write versions, too. A claw-scrape that licked sweet then bitter then cured is an annotation—this is where the injuries are tempted to heal too quickly; keep it supple. It keeps animals in memory as pressure and warmth and absence, the way you might know a friend by the hollow their body makes in a familiar sofa, not by the furniture.

It is not alone. It has attendants that are not separate from it in the way we mean when we say animal and plant. There are mobile meristems that look like fringe at first—the pith-thin tongues that grow, sample an angle, then are taken back into the floor, leaving only a smoother place. There are resins that behave like minds in droplets—testing, rejecting, sealing—so that even where there is no tissue there is still choice. There are swarms of clear cells that travel a cord to re-sleeve a nick; under your hand they feel like a surface, but they are a procession. The plant grows hands for what is needed and then digests the hands when the task is done. If you are still enough, you will learn the wordlessness of caretaking that is also incorporation: everything that cares for this place is part of it, eventually.

Counting in the animal way, there are a few. They arrive not as inhabitants with rooms and hours but as punctuations in the plant’s long sentence, commas that come exactly where they always come.

One holds itself like a moth and is not. Pale, veined like a shard of onion skin, its wings hardly move because the air it rides does not move. It is a hoverer with a fixed interval, a creature that lives in the plant’s metronome. It lowers its wick-tongue to the meniscus of a cup and drinks not until it is full—fullness is a mammal word—but until the cup has given the precise small measure that cued its visit. When it leaves, the plant’s lip resumes its trembling, in time for the next in the chain to arrive three rooms over. They do not touch one another. If you made a map of their stops, it would look like a line of slowly blinking stars. They carry nothing large enough to make a sound when it falls; they move information: a dusting of pollen between two translucent tongues, a smear of sugar that tells a cup how much to make tomorrow.

There is a domed thing with the open patience of a stone that is also animal. Its carapace is built for the pressure of this air, a cask that gathers micro-salts the way a coastline gathers drift. It rests so thoroughly you think it has been fitted there, then lifts itself by small internal hinges and lays down its tongue. The tongue is a braid, capillary and hair, a lamp wick as you first thought. It drinks the seam itself rather than the water—that is, it takes the suggestion of flow. When it moves on, there is a chalkier line under the gloss, and later the tufa thickens true along that taught line. It is a mason the plant has invented by offering it only the kind of water that leaves a mark. In exchange, it receives the slow manufacture of scaffolds at exactly the places it prefers to thicken rock.

Midway up a column is a being you mistake for a clump of unfortunate roots until your shadow wakes a posture in it. White limbs unfurl with a care that reads as dream and hang again, exactly reversed. It never hurries because there is no hurry here. Its skin is without color and has the look of salted fat; its grip is pads within pads, tissues meant only for this bark and this angle. It does not make choices your way. It is a follower of tiny swells and hollows in the plant’s warmth, a creature of surface. Where it rests, the column becomes subtly polished; its repeated touch is a cloth. It eats resin, but not like a thief; it eats resin the way a tongue takes the last of a salve from a split lip—soothing its own ache and, in doing so, asking the plant to make more right there. Do not look for its face. It has a face but the concept is useless applied to a life that needs only front and not eyes.

There is another you will almost never see whole. It is a breast brushing a rib, just enough to streak the soot-black spores—proof that something with a keel of bone and a cage of air has passed. Imagine a small glider with a chalk-dusted chest, paler than light, hands like sails, that moves from darkness to brightness the way a bead of water moves along a thread: not by will but by fit. It eats the tiny forests in the hanging shade—fronds with droplets nested between their fingers—and in its feeding it becomes a courier of spores, writing dark commas across the plant’s pale undertext. Its voice would be a soft page turned, but there are no pages here, so there is no name for the way it sounds.

Once in a long while a larger warmth comes down and leaves an oval proof. The plant has made a bed for it by making the same place warm whenever that weight is likely to come. The bed is the exact size of trust. It belongs to something from the plain that cannot afford such constancy above—salt-skinned, probably, and with a back that bright would burn. It descends by one of the vents when the outside is dangerous to look at, follows an old confidence through the curtains, drinks from the tilt-stacked plates until its tongue tastes the bitter that ends the sweet and tells it enough. It sleeps while heat and salt leave it. When it goes, the plant does not mourn or rejoice. The warmth decays back into the general, and that is the exchange.

There are also lives too small to count that are not counted in the hand’s way. Filaments that are not quite fungus and not quite root travel the undersurface of the leaf-carpet and knit fallen leaves into tomorrow’s floor without ever inventing the crumb. Second mouths line the bright cords and lick at dust until the silica shines and the light travels properly again. At the lips of the vents, a nation of flags you took for decoration are actually flags: a colony of cellulose that grows itself into sails to trap dew the way a ship makes a better wind. They silver, then lack of true weather refuses to move them, and in that refusal the plant wins back water.

The plant farms even its parasites. Pale whorls pinch into ribs; if they pinch too hungrily, a web of lignin grows behind them like a splint. They are tolerated along a narrow line: enough theft to make the ribs rich with feeds and responses; not enough to undo. You could call that mercy. It is really a game of thresholds the plant has perfected: how much to give to be left alone.

Each animal, even the ones you will not see, is an instrument it has invited by designing a room in which that instrument has only one tune to play. The hoverer plays the interval; the carapace plays the slow chalk; the white-limbed bundle plays the column’s skin to shine. They come when the room cues them, and the cue is the only thing that feels like time. Courtship here is synchronization. Death is absorption. A body falls and the floor’s little green hands learn the size of it and ask for that size of matter until there is no difference left between the leaf that fell last week and the part of it that was animal. The only relics are shapes the plant is not ready to let go of: a hardness that stays useful, a curve that teaches other curves.

At the very core—if such a word holds in a space so evenly kept—what lives here is a keeper of brightness that learned to be a place so that brightness could be kept. It grew itself into architecture not because it admired structure but because buildings are what patience does when it is given cellulose and time. The few creatures are its metronomes, its brushes, its little masons, its salts. They are not tenants with rents and grievances; they are the pieces of a wider metabolism granted the courtesy of faces and movement so that one or two tasks can be completed outside the range of a stationary mind.

And yet if you stand for very long, you will feel something like company that is not explained by organisms. Saturation itself is a way of living. Fullness lives here. Equilibrium is a species. The plant keeps that animal, too, fat and unbothered, walking the chambers, laying down in the same oval, licking the same resins at the same run of gouges, replacing the plates almost straight. It is a body made out of an idea—light held level—and everything inside it is the idea’s entourage.
