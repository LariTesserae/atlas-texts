Those who live here keep their voices wrapped the way you’d keep a light: shaded, directed to the task at hand. They are few and regular as a pulse, not hermits but people tuned to a place’s inner weather. They walk with deliberate feet, lanterns braided to their knees, wool at their belts to bank heat for engines that prefer their warmth in sips. They know the plant’s moods by fingertip and heel, and when they enter a space they put their weight down like a question they expect the floor to answer.

They call themselves by their work because that is what the plant understands. There are Minute-keepers, quieter than clocks, who tend the dew engines and catch time out of air with cloths fresh-dried against their ribs. They can hear a drop decide, will stand with a cup poised under a trembling bead and talk to it the way you talk a skittish animal toward a hand. They wear rings of salt on their wrists where the minutes have crystallized and are careful never to repeat a motion three times unless they mean to make it stay.

There are Cup-singers, old and young, who hold the vowels that never scrape a surface, who can part a black pool with a melody and leave no seam of consequence. They are diplomats with the amphibia that watch from the waterline, eyes banded like tree rings. A Cup-singer knows who meets whom and when, not by schedule but by the way the pool’s skin leans toward a name. They mind the cup-lurkers’ comfort the way a good host minds chairs, sweeping away stray grit, listening to the subaudible thrum for any unrest.

The Phloem-coaxers climb and tend the glow-veins. Their palms are greened by contact where the sap runs close, and at night you can see the thin lines through their gloves as if their bones carried ember. They write on leaf-stone in strokes that ask rather than demand—curves that the plant learns to echo next season. They never write the same leaf twice. When they carry a petition up to the ribs, they move in slow spirals, so as not to put the same request into the air three times by accident.

Resinwrights keep the little factory alive at the plaza’s edge. Their presses are tame now, trained to fold with a certain give, but the Resinwrights still speak to them as one speaks to horses. Their hair smells of sap and their clothes are freckled with dull amber that never hardens the same way twice. They caulk the plant’s patience into gears and teach belts to remember a particular slack. When a moth dusts a pulley with pollen and the slip improves, the Resinwright will stroke the moth with a gloved knuckle and thank it by name.

There is a guild without charter who updates the soot-map on the belly of a low arch. They are the ones who leave and do not, the ones who venture to the edges where the world is not finished knitting. They come back with distances that have changed in their hands, with folds of salt and stories about a channel that dreamed itself then gave up its mind. They don’t boast. They kneel on vinewood and scrape yesterday’s certainty into a dustpan; they redraw until the ward believes them.

Among them move the Coil-binders, whose bracelets are copper and whose forearms have the fine crosshatch of old burns. They ride the tram at night when it runs like a thought, and they lay suture along a vine so that charge will take a gentle path. It is their habit to rest a cheek on the warm flank of a dyna-wheel and listen for a warble. They wrap flywheels in lichen shawls and hold their breath while the engines breathe. They will swap a handful of saved minutes for a length of good wool without haggling, because they know the ledger of wet against cold in their bones.

Some are children, which is to say they are the very bright parts of the ward moving at leg-height. They play at loopweaver, hauling spider-line between rails with the grave courtesy the actual beasts demand. They practice not-startling. They learn patience like a rhythm: two touches to the rail, and a wait, and the answer that is the rail’s own return pressure. Their first words are shaped into hums to carry to cups. They are taught the danger of three at the same time they are taught to braid: you can go over-under-over as long as the pattern is broken by purpose.

Their speech, when they need more than a hum, is a language of skirts and palms, of taps laid against leaf ribs and copper plates. There are words that happen in temperature: a warmed glove settled briefly against a shoulder to say I have minutes for you; a strip of wool handed over without eye contact to mean I’m sorry. When they must say anything loud enough that the plant will forget it and the animals cannot, they choose the heater-fox for confessor, lay the confession into its white fur as it curls around a pipe. The fox will remember. So will the bats. Sometimes remembering as a creature with wings is the safest way to keep a truth.

They live in a calendar of drips and drafts. They rise when the far cistern ticks a pattern they have learned as morning. They sleep when the resin presses sigh themselves empty. They count weather by the breath the ward takes as a whole, a movement so slight it barely lifts a hair on the forearm. They time arguments to condensing, because nothing mean can stick when drops are in their gathering; they finish arguments with the gift of a cloth heavy with borrowed minutes, a soft pass of time into someone else’s hand.

The animals are neighbors and are treated as such. The black grazer comes in when wilt is honest about itself and is stroked as it works, a praise not for appetite but for restraint. The heater-fox forgives all handling and will blink slow gratitude from within its white fur when a child tucks a loose wire back under its flank. The bats sand themselves thin against the overhead leaves and learn footfall patterns; people humor them with new strides sometimes, a private mischief for the moment when a pale shape falls without harm and startles nobody. Loopweavers lay new ladders for small hands to test, and those who mend rails learn to read the silk as a weatherline: where the plant intends another arch, the silk will thicken.

Machines are not people, but they are kept with the courtesy owed to house-creatures who know doors. Each has a name and a low diet of differences. There is a piston called Otherhand that prefers to be woken with breath instead of cloth; a dew engine called Abelard because it argues with nothing; a kiln that is mostly an exhale and takes a song better than it takes a measurement. When they fail, they don’t shout about it. They falter like an old animal missing a step, and hands arrive known to them, and every fix is praised in touch more than talk.

The plant lives here in the definitive sense. It is not owner or god, but all the rooms are its organs and all the passages its chosen postures. It listens to the hush and the touch; it has learned the sound of a lantern grazed against a rib in apology. When it opens its hidden mouths and rains dry inside itself, the people stop and face the ceiling with unconscripted reverence. The plant keeps promises when it can; when it cannot, it sends a draft out to say so. Once in a while it tells every animal a way to leave. The people do not hold that against it. They keep a bag packed for the night the roof ripples like a thought and decides the world must be otherwise.

At the edges there are those who scout the not-yet. They move in pairs because distance is not a promise and bodies make an average. They carry chalk that writes on salt, and they mark where a step was true so that the next step can choose the same truth. They bring back pre-objects: forms of dust that want to be shard, want to be clay, want to be gravel, and they set them in the mouths of cups to let the amphibia borrow and return the missing minutes. They rarely shout. If something must be told to the air in a voice, they will do it while walking the tram so the sound spends itself along the loop and doesn’t bruise any single room.

Visitors happen—thin-voiced people from a drier ward, a string of pilgrims who need a cup to name them back from thirst, a trader who speaks in threefolds by mistake and has to be gently shared out over different days to keep from anchoring. Visitors lose and gain time and learn the vowel. Some do not leave. They find work in the rows of machines that purr when stroked right and in the corridors that return to your footsteps if you like them, and they adopt the knees of the place—lanterned, steady, unaccusing. Their old names become nicknames for flywheels and trams.

What lives here is also the weather of it: the rare laughter that lifts insects; the felt presence of a room that has just been crossed by a single slow creature; the interval between drips taken as a unit of patience. Work is a circulatory system with a purpose, and leisure is a chair at the rim of a black cup when the roof releases its long-held water. Everyone keeps an appointment with no clock at least once a day and arrives on time, because here time is not a thing you wear on your wrist but a thing that kisses a surface and can be caught.

They will tell you, if you ask, that the ward was meant to be larger, that the plan once had wings. They do not say this as lament. They point at the bolted plates grown to receive a future hoist and say they keep those polished in case the asking person returns. Meanwhile they keep the map true to the ward’s own belief, keep the machines easy in their harness, keep the animals from learning fear, keep the plant in conversation and not command. They are guardians by way of being good guests, and if ever the pre-land learns its whole vocabulary and begins to talk back, they will be the first to listen with their soles.
