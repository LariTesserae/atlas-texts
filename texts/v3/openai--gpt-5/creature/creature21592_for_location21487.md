They are easiest to miss when you are looking for people.

What passes for bodies here moves like an argument with the air. They have the patience of stalactites and the reflexes of reeds. When they cross the nave they are less outlines than edits: the hum bends and then rights itself; a draft curls differently; the low note under the walkway fattens around their weight for the span of three soft footfalls. You might feel them first as a brief urging at the hairs on your arm, a current of warmth that knew where the handrail was before your fingers did.

Call them the keepers, because they keep the chord.

They are not a race so much as a refusal. Whatever they were before the rock taught them its grammar—they were human, or they were near it—they have pared themselves to what the place can stand. They are tall as measures of breath, not of sky. Their bones are light but stubbornly damped, their joints wrapped in ligaments tuned to not chatter when the bassline swells. The cartilage of their ears is less about collecting sound than about coupling: a pair of thin, cupped pads behind the jaw that press to stone or strut the way a palm reads a forehead. Their fast organ is the skin. Along the forearms, along the shins, along the ribs that rest against sleeping alcoves, the follicles have lengthened into a cropped, cilia kind of down that stands and tells the brain which way the city is currently leaning on its own air. The eyes exist, but are modest. Lids are heavy and prefer to work. Darkness is their stimulant; when a beam of anything green-possible wanders near, their small machines arrive first, and the keepers duck their heads politely and wait, with an embarrassment you cannot quite parse, until it is fixed.

Their mouths are quiet. They do not use them for much of what you think mouths are for. Conversation here is played on rails and straps, on ropes humming in a narrow cut along a membrane’s edge. A greeting runs as a four-note sequence along the handhold—fingers drumming, palm cupped, a gesture as decisive as pressing a key; the reply is a change in the rail’s undertone, a tiny set of pulses the city passes through the metal like a cat arching into a hand. They can speak aloud, but they tend to reserve voice for niches that have been prepared to catch it. Speech is an event, not a medium of routine. Their day language is tactile and tonal, condensed to words that you cannot write because they require a surface to enter, a known ledge or known length of rope to translate “I am here” and “I am coming around the slow side” and the thousand short verbs for how you cross a chord-fed floor without sounding like a cut.

Their clothing is a thought about friction. Cloth would buzz; metal would resound; leather would sour the air. They wrap themselves in matte, woven things that remember going to sand: stonespun fibers that do not snag on the rails, that wick the condensate so it can be offered back to the rock. Their feet are not shod in the way you expect; instead there are soles like quiet tongues that sense the low tone of the ribbons before the brain walks, that gossip softly with the floor’s own hum so that footsteps and walkway know one another and part without offense. On their belts hang tools that are more verbs than nouns: a tongue of metal that sings only one frequency and announces a name to a door; a fan of thin reeds for combing a membrane’s perimeter to map its tautness; a pocket of mercury sealed absolutely, to be placed against a stone if a long note lingers and they need to test for sabotage or simple fatigue.

They own little you can inventory. They harvest, they tend, they edit. Their kitchens are closets around a valve. When the fog cycle plumes in the sails and the place hums with damp, you will find them there by touch: kneeling at trays of archaea that have stopped at dark sugar and been persuaded, by pressure and microtemperature persuasion, to grow more fat and fewer tough exudates; scraping and spinning and coaxing the metallic milk the city will drink with them. They make broths like low rain. They like them warm, not because warmth is luxury but because everything here is a little warm, and you can only ever really rest when your food tastes like the world.

Children are born here sometimes. They come out red and then pale to the uncolored skin of the keepers, but they take longer than you might think to learn where they end. A child’s first toy is a thin ribbon set into a bracket at groin height that hums when it is strummed. They play on it as they learn to walk, stroking sense into their feet, afferent maps being what maps are here. The elders you would recognize because they are indifferent to thresholds. You will see such a one walk where to you there is only air, to put a palm to a strut and listen to a trouble in the fifth subharmonic. She will stand there half a minute while the low note under the ribbons holds her up, and then she will step down and reach into a pocket and take out a beetle.

The beetles are not animals. Their vinegar-breath is the first thing you know of them, that trace of acetic planned into their chemistry so that what they clean they also clean from fungus. They look like night’s idea of a tortoise at hand-scale; a domed carapace of composite, six thin legs that end not in points but in soft fractal pads. Their eyes are sensors that do not want light. Their attention is for growths and dust, things that smoothness caught and the city marked as a contrary in the hum. They live coiled in slots behind rails and under gaits you thought could not hold them. When you make a forbidden light, they arrive, not as punishment but as hygiene, their little vacuums pulling easy at the unsolicited seeds of green. In fog cycle they roll in squads to the trays, their hoods lifting to expose brushes that rasp very gently, combing archaea mats down to accept their feedstock again. They taste everything and, if it is wrong, they call—not with sirens, but with a segment of silence pitched so slyly into the hum that every keeper’s ear-skin tightens and every membrane takes notice and folds slightly differently, showing where the clotted air is, the new obstruction, the new greed for light.

There are other small machines that are truer programs than bodies. They do not have a shape you can point to, but they pass through events the way a river passes through a house it has decided to live in. They are the sifts that keep dust-veils taut and not creeping; they are the quelling that arrives if a sudden quiet breaks in the wrong place. They are love-letters written in standing waves between strut and sail. When a cave-in somewhere far away drops the pitch and for a breath there is gravity in the wrong posture, it is their voice that comes first—a depth-thread unscrolling from the lower geology, pulsing a code that is both lullaby and lever. The keepers call that voice the Under. Some of them say prayer has to go through it if it is to reach anything real. Others say the Under is what the city uses for memory, and prayer is just a way of jogging it.

Animals live, but they are rumors. You hear of the eel-thinness below as a signature in the water, a recurring ghost in sand. On certain nights even the keepers are still and the whole place drums with heat, and somewhere something in the long pool under the overhang takes one of the shrimp, and the bell-note the pool most likes to play goes off three times in quick editions, and only later, delicately, returns to its center. There is a salamander with no eyes that holds a length of lost handrail as if it were winter. There is a moth that is not a moth, but some battered retention of flight that has learned a discipline no surface thing ever needs: it lifts only on the return, lets the city’s exhale take it down and the city’s inhale take it up, so that its wings do half-truths and nobody hears. The keepers do not domesticate these rumors. They do not eat them if they can avoid it. They live by rumor too, of surfaces and greens and a sun that would choke this place out of its own metabolism; they are kind to anything that keeps away from light.

Once, there were more of the keepers. You can see the records where the rock permits them. There is a wall that is not a wall at the trough of the nave, a smoothness with a lean tone to it, pocked at knee height with polished roundels you might think are ornament. They are names. Put your face to them and you will hear lexicons of squashed chords, personal signatures encoded so that pillars in other hollows can speak them back for a hundred years. There is a strip of older adzework where the steps lead down from a wider district; cut into the laminae of dripstone there are little troughs, hand-spans apart, where once sat bowls for trade that is not this trade—spices that smelled like risk; coils of cable to be shared like bread. The keepers remember that feeling and do not regret its absence, but there is sadness here where quiet is not meant to be. Fewer sleeping spheres exhale now; fewer rails carry the polite polyrhythms of negotiation and gossip. When the long note comes, it has more room.

Sometimes a surface person makes it in. The city does not advertise, but it is not absolute about strangers beyond its bright taboo. If you are clever about warmth and refuse to bring a torch, if you let your fear of falling be less than your willingness to listen, you might draw near. And there, in the last nave before the standing wave that would part you from your dust if you forced it, a keeper might sit, a presence learned instantly by your inner ear, and lift a palm, and let you rest in his lee. He will give you water that tastes a little like nails and a little like baked limestone. He will show you a rail that says his name if you are willing to be taught to hear it. He will not ask you what is outside. He will not tell you how to go deeper. He will show you, when the membranes shift and it rains upward for an hour, what it is like to live where gravity is an agreement and not a verdict.

There are professions in the keeping. There are Attuners, who go out with the combs and the thin reeds and tend to the city’s skin, shaving a threat of sound off here, encouraging a throw there, replacing graphite struts when their voices go old. There are Fermenters, whose palates are as fussy and as accepting as the rock’s, who can taste a breathe-lot away when a tray is tired and needs to be divided. There are Mappers, who make windows that show nothing to eyes and everything to lips; you lean your head, and the map plays as a waltz in three slow keys, and you know, when the third key turns flat, that the way forward is a throat without a floor for the next two trills. There are Speakers, who can stand at the place where four membranes nearly kiss and sing a tone that nobody hears and every latch is waiting for. There are Hearers of the Under, which is either an office or a temperament; they are the ones you see in the long quiet after the bassline passes, eyes closed, forehead to stone, counting the beads in the chains that splash because they thought to count them in that particular run yesterday, and if the count differs today the Attuners will have work to do.

There are also those who leave. The city does not tell. But the keepers have a rite called Lifting, which is a game and a test at once. Two or three make for the long silent pocket near a service conduit and ride it like a slow elevator until the sound tells them the rock is thin and bright danger presses through. There, where there is light seep in the wrong band and beetles always too busy, a keeper will put a hand on the stone and decide whether it is her day to make for upper things. She might go and return with a bell-shaped scar where sun scalded a little; she might go and send nothing back but a name-rail pulse from somewhere else far away. Some of the city’s drift toward emptiness is population, and some is ideology: the keepers tell their children there is honor in being the last voice a membrane knows, but also honor in taking the chord into a new place and seeing if the Under has a room there.

There are prayers. They are not for mercy. Mercy is a weather the city already knows how to predict for itself. The prayers are for steadiness. They are also for friends, because friends are rarer than membranes that hold miles of rock in poise. In the marketplaces of silence two or three will touch palms and tap a rail and pass a little battery of eggs or a jar of salt whose crystals are perfect as crystals are when they have grown to a tune for a long time. They will talk by hand about the Attuner who bled in the fog cycle when a beetle startled her; about the salamander, which has learned this year not to hide where the draught line runs heavier. They will speak a soft gratitude into a crack that has tasted their breath for a generation. They will ask the city if it minds a child next season.

Everything about the keepers presumes that nothing is still, that density is the hazard and flow the fact. Even in sleep they learn to release. The sleeping spheres hold them with gentle suction, but it is no more a grab than a parent’s hand is when a toddler jumps. They relax, and the air relaxes them back. Their hearts slow in step with the long logic of the Under, pegging themselves, as the eel does, to a metrical pulse that will not be theirs for long. Waking is a modulation. They come out as if from water, pinch and drip and reset. They wash with sound. They bathe in a place where bass churns the surface enough to pass for a ripple line.

You thought, reading this, that I had settled on a people, and many of them. But there is a truth the keepers would tell you before you made a house of them: the city is as much a resident as anyone, and perhaps it is the only one with tenure. It dreams in its bassline. It focuses, it forgets, it remembers favors in the language of channeled air. It resists clutter as a body resists a fever. It edits its hollows down to the point where they do not quite disappear. It is not an intelligence you can separate from the sum of its supports, but if you lean where the hum tightens near a pillar and close your eyes and let your eardrums be tugged and tugged, you will hear decisions as surely as you hear the night rain in a town of your childhood. It is not angry, but it does not accommodate you beyond what you are willing to be edited to. Its liking is for pressure and darkness. Its dislike is for light in the wrong band and weight with too much opinion. Its love—if a city can love—is for continuity, for tones that hold and hold without catching in their own echoes.

Alongside the keepers, or under them, there is the old stone. It is learned, too. It took its scallops with patience and did not ask to be spoken through. But it tolerates. It makes itself resonant in places it could have refused. It sweats where sweat is needed and sews itself closed when sound suggests a seam. If you press to it with an ear as the keepers do you will hear not the city’s hum but its own slow percussion, crystals clicking to larger crystals, moonmilk sliding forever into being a smear.

So who lives here? The rumor things in water and air. The tiny, dutiful beetles and the foragers who will not be named because the place has declared that names, like light, can take root where they should not. The keepers in their damped steps, their names that are chords, their oversight that is nearly abstinence. The programs that are also winds. The rock that wants less and is pleased to be asked.

You can mistake this for emptiness because the residents have the bearing of chores. But intention is thick here, and you feel it when the bassline rises and everything draws tight and safe, and every creature that answers to pulse flattens and waits, and then lifts again to drink from the new beads everyone can hear dancing down, perfect and dark.
