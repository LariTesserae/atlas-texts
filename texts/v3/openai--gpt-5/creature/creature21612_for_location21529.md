At first you think there is no one. The place behaves so entirely like itself—intent stacked on intention—that it seems to do its own living. Then your ear sorts human from instrument, and the people reveal as more joins in the machine, the only soft in it, the only warm, trained until their warmth is disciplined and their softness clipped to usefulness. They live here as a polished part lives in a clock: not as passengers, as teeth.

Outside they are called Dripwrights. Inside, names are situational and temporary. The same person is a Rope when their hands are on the winch, a Tonguer when they stamp the ceramic tongues, a Floor when they kneel with a box of sifted ash and dress a plane. The sound someone makes is the handle you grasp them by. “Three-spaced-thock,” you might say, and three heads turn, all of whom have made that spacing their habit. The private names, the mother-given and beloved ones, are spoken under cloth covers with mouths turned away from bowls, or kept in drum, or kept not at all.

They are built by their place. The big pupils and the way they don’t have to look to put a palm flat on a slab without leaving oil—practice gives them that. The skin takes on a fragile fine salt where the air pinches water back, especially at the hairline and the bend of elbow; they rub that salt back into sacks where it is kept for weight and work and law. The hair is kept short or braided to serve as wick and to keep shed from clogging a groove. Eyebrows are shaved to stop the drip that would fall from them, a practical sacrifice, and there is a little gull-wing of clay stuck to each temple during work to catch sweat should it dare.

They smell of lime and hides, of copper that has been touched and put back down, of long-handled tools. The resin they use to seal a joint varnishes their knuckles as if they had their nails dipped; in certain lights the fingertips flash pale glass where they have brushed glass. Their teeth are healthy and look cold, because most of what passes over them is no hotter than breath on stone. Their wrists are wrapped in wet cloths tied with horsehair that hums if they shake them: not fashion, instrument. The cloths are salted to keep the wrap cool and to remind the body to hold some of its own damp back. They learn their own breathing until it is a quiet tide and not a warm draft that would rise and spoil a fall.

They dress in layers you can unstack without setting them somewhere that would drink them. Hide aprons that have been smoked and then washed and then smoked again until they hang like slate. Bone-thimble caps for the first knuckles so the fingertips do not have to touch raw. Ropes looped around the waist like a second belt, each ranked by a twist or garnish that tells its song: oak-leaf stiffeners on one to damp it; a wrapped spiral of horsehair on another to give it a constant small purr. They carry a pole the way a villager might carry a walking stick, but the end of it is complex—a cluster of hooks for lids, a blunt stone striker, an articulated leaf of copper to flip a shutter and catch it and hold it with a spring. From the pole hangs a sachet of sifted ash for roughening a place gone too smooth and a sachet of glass for the same done finer.

They do speak, but voice is rationed. Conversation is a soft knuckle on a rib, an elbow against a diaphragm of hide, a thock forward. A call you could shout your throat hoarse to make is sent instead down the string-lines in a run of taps, or thrown to the roof with a felted mallet so it will come back translated in time and depth. Children here grow up hearing a different grammar: the syntax of delay. A mother will tell a story by touching a bronze disk with different parts of her palm until the roof answers with the right echo, and the child will giggle when the deep ends of the room answer late, because that is where the big cisterns live, like whales who have remembered to be architecture.

There are children. You can hear them in the dry hours moving like concentrated animals, hands tucked away, breath through their noses, learning not to lick. The first game they get is a cord strung between two clay uprights with little bone tags on it; they make it speak with a nail and then walk away and listen to what remains. The second is a row of bowls stretched with skins that do not mind being hit; each bowl has a different depth and a different lesson in it, and they take turns setting the place to pearl in miniature if they can. It is a victory when you can get a bowl to bloom one bead in spite of your heat. It is a rite when your first bowl refuses you. Later, they are given the job of checking the tiny lipped steps down the squared spirals, collecting with an ash paintbrush what a thousand heels have left behind.

They live close. There are sleeping places but no bedrooms in the usual sense; bunks are slung over the driest hollows and curtained with hide that listens as much as it conceals. The curtains are marked with the owner’s wait: the pattern of damp knocked on the copper disk to call for the turning-in-time. Lovers make a sound together and check the echo; if it is clean and round and dies in kindness, they live under the same curtain for a while. Marriage is not a separate ceremony; it is permission to share the same patient work, to call the same opening at the same time and be trusted to hear each other through the makeshift thunder. They have children by agreement or accident, and when a baby is born, a small ceramic tongue is stamped with a new line and strung under the least active reach of roof, where the sound of its name will not push a drop away by mistake.

Their dead are particular. Bone is used here for things that must be strong without being hungry, since bone is never thirsty. When someone goes, their ribs are not numbered in a bowl like a wicked people would do it; they are offered to the roof in thinly shaved plates, if the family wills, sanded until they gather a whisper when the cold runs. A ceremony is the hiding of your person in the place that feeds you, and over time the ceiling is scaled with remembered muscle, quieted into use. The taboo is not the using of bone but the answering of the wrong question about whose. “You-don’t-ask-what rib,” someone says, and you feel the courtesy of it.

There is a hierarchy, but it is not a tower. It arranges by ear and by patience and above all by timing. At the top are the Tellers of Delay, the ones who can drop a mallet on hide and know by how the room throws it back when to open the western vents for a made rain. They are old, or not old at all, because some ears come out of the womb with a built-in memory and others wear it down clean before fifty. Below them are the Tuners, who grade roughness and remember what handful of sifted ash to rub into a plane to make it right, and the Tongue-Stampers, who can read the standing wave in a wet clay leaf and hit it again so it will sing the name that makes the droplets hold. The Rope-keepers watch the cords for fray and keep their songs; the Smoke-Breeders handle all flame in exile, big-lunged and slow-breathing people who can sit in a pit with a cooking fire and not heat the room with their impatience. The Salt-Librarians keep the white law from dissolving off the walls; they refresh it, re-inscribe it, smear it with a wet fingertip and watch to see how long the mark takes to blur, which is an index of how much the air can take and give.

There are rules you could break without harm elsewhere that here would be a sin. You do not laugh open-mouthed under the vault. You do not cry near a fall. If grief comes, you go to a place with a cloth curtain and a hide floor and you let your face do what it wants with a bowl under your chin: this is not because they are cruel, but because the room has a task and so does your body, and the two should not tangle unless the Tellers of Delay ask them to. You do not lick your fingers. You do not spit. You do not name the eels. You do not clap except in the right places. The last is the hardest for someone not from here, because the urge to test a room by sound is universal—but here the test and the answer are work.

What they eat, they coax too. There is culture kept in the silt that makes worms; there are eels in the bigger cisterns that are fed and harvested with a shy gratitude. The pale isopods are not eaten; they are let be, encouraged by dotting the underside of plates with a trace of salted broth after a harvest so the colony will thrum there and keep that place scoured of the certain softnesses that lead to slime. Fungi serve as bread and light both; they grow leather-bloomers in closets where no roof can find and eat it, and they shave it like a citizen’s beard and dry the shavings over cold smoke until they can be stewed in the deep fires that they bury. Dried organics come from outside across a rope of trade layered with promise: bone, hair, hide, copper for water and salt and a turn under the shelter of the lid on nights when the sky is wrong or loud.

They do go up sometimes. A small team, pale in a way that catches like a bite on first light, will fold their aprons away and climb steps cut in the side of a vent well and brace themselves for a bite that is not like any bite down here: the sun, the dust, the way distance becomes a wound by being complete. They go because they must pay the world its exchange, or because a particular rib is needed, or because the cloth on the vents has rotten at its ties and the tie must be replaced from the outside with room to sway. They go with breath held like a weight against the chest and a plug in the mouth to keep the habit from being unlearned; they come back with wind in their hair and a hush in their bones, and for a week after you can hear the vault answer a hair brighter because their ears have been taught a new edge.

They believe in water the way a shepherd believes in sheep—not as a god, as a living that lives better when tended. It is coaxable, not compelled. Words like please and here and brave are certain patterns of beat; apology is a silence held in the right place. There are stories taught to children about the first time somebody shouted in a nave for the joy of it and made a lane go shy and how long it took to win that lane back from its sulk. There are also stories about surrender: when the water chews a new gully into a crisp corner, the Tellers of Delay strike the bell whose note means lose, and the whole crew applauds the edit, and the next day somebody casts a new lip into the amended curve. Their prayer is not to own what they work but to keep time with it.

Not everything here is human. You have to count the eels, though you do not look at them while you count, for courtesy. You have to count the vault-moths, which the Dripwrights assume are also working, drawing a thin drink off the fog where no surface had a right to take it. There are the spider-analogues, legs all bone and intention, felted at the joints where they brush the ropes. They are hunting a taste you cannot name. The Dripwrights give them a digit’s worth of respect and make way when one wants to cross; a superstition says if a spider-widow stops on your pole and licks its own front leg with that little stiff mouth, you are due an easy harvest, and the superstition has held enough times for it to be sworn by the Tuners too. The pale salamanders that come once in a long while and stand still like a small thought where a lip meets a fall are not touched. They are a mirror. You check your pulse by them without looking at your own wrist; you honor their spots that look like dried salt, because the wall also wears that pattern where it is good.

And then there are the rumors of bigger things. Every few seasons a resin-polished lip will show a tooth-scratch that did not come from a mallet and did not come from a dropped ladder, and everybody will look away in the expensive way that means they all saw it. This threatens no one’s sleep. It adds a caution to the edges of the big basins and it adds teeth to the resin, and the teeth are a way of talking back to a thing that may not understand a disc struck or a hide beat. The thing—if it is a thing—learns the language of edges. That is enough of a truce for now.

They keep time by what the roof says. Their day is a fall rate; their night is when the foxfire in the mortar shows a little more; their calendar is the interval between harvest storms and the weeks of coil-silence kept to let the place set. Love lays itself out between beats like lichen patterns on ceramic ribs. Old age is when your bones stop liking the step with the lip that is the second from the top in the north cistern and you stop taking it and nobody asks why. Death is when the roof remembers you in a weep that sounds a shade more like the way your hand used to strike.

There is governance, but it is the kind that dissolves and reforms with the weather. A written law would either flicker away in wet or stay too long and crust a place that should seethe and go on. So laws are salted on a wall and topped up and read with a scraper. They are also kept in sequences of knocks that the old Tellers teach to the new: you can see law if you know how to hear it in the body of the place, a public code built into the standing wave of a cistern when you hit it on purpose. The white script on the wall is less a list of commands than a list of proven courtesies written in a material that makes you tongue-search your teeth as you look. “Do not carry oil with you.” “Clap only where a clap is a tool.” “Do not start a fall, and do not stop one.” “Name nothing that tastes of water.”

They are not austere people, only careful ones. They have jokes that are timing instead of words. They tap them out standing back-to-back across a room, and you can feel a smile get passed around a nave like a ooze does around an obstacle. They have festivals, though you would not mistake them for wide-bellied affairs: the raising of the drums when the big cold moves in from the west and everything in the room lifts a little, the massed patience required for the tuning day when the tongues are all struck by hand and the tongues answer and the roof hums back and someone cooks a stew that has waited for a month and nobody hurries even when they wish to.

If you stay long, you can learn them as an animal that the place makes with itself. You will see why the ankles know the fall better than eyes. You will understand how they recognize each other in the dark by the timbre of a breath diverted through a hand, by the shape of the silence someone holds. You will begin to feel the draw of their taboos as comfort and not constraint, the way not licking your fingers makes the coffee of taste when you do finally wet your tongue. And you will admit with them that other beings share this habitat, though they are not the kind to call it sharing as a humanist might. The eels are citizens who do not pay into the drum; the isopods are guild; the moths are script that writes itself; the fungi a domesticate that hums when the chimes speak a certain way. And the water is the neighbor and the task and the guest who is always expected, always late, and always forgiven.

It is not a large people. Many rooms do not know them. There are more platforms than they can tend all at once, which is why certain lanes begin to draw their own white lines where the tile was mislaid, and why that is not a failure but a dialogue. Everything alive here is rare because everything is accounted for. Even their joy is measured out and placed where it will do good. And yet there is tenderness that does not try to hide. The way a hand will pause over a new lip to feel its cool, a fraction of a second stolen and then given back; the way someone somewhere, after the loudness has gone down and the bowls are feeding only in a quieter way, will scrape a spoon and then tap a little answer with the back of it on a plate: a nick out of the ration of sound, but the plate does not mind. It has been made for nicking. It has been made to be a throat. So have they. They live here like throats, filled with the work of calling and swallowing and singing back. And if they sometimes dream of a sky that is not a lid, they do not say so under the vault. They drum it out in private; they take their wish outside on a rope day and burn it in the sun and come back with their ears full of light, ready to be part of the place again when the first peck starts on stretched skin and the long soft cough of the ceiling goes answering out, mapping distance, welcoming home.
