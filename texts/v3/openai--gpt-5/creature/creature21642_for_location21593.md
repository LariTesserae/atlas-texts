What lives there is patient and plural. It is not only the people with their soft shoes and low voices; it is the habit they have taught into the place, and the way the place has taught them back. If you ask them they will say water lives there before any of them did—a shy animal that dislikes being grabbed. They learned to pet it with shade and hush, to call it with a touch instead of a shout. They mean this literally. They will show you the scar of a jar that cracked because someone lifted too fast, the blanching of a line where a careless child squealed and the beads trembled into rain. They will show you the standing stone and say: the sea that left us made that, and the sea lives here still in the clay’s breath. We are only its neighbors.

Call them shade-binders, line-tenders, edge-readers, eel-men, jar-wrights, quiet-swearers. Call them by the work their hands remember: a thin callus along the first two fingers from years of plucking; a brown varnish in the print of the thumb from permanent intimacy with pitch and fish fat; a crescent of salt in the lines of the palm that no lye ever washes out. Their hearing is good but not proud of itself; they would rather feel a tone than hear it, and they will set their weight on a board and hum a little in their chest to check a join the way other folk test a rope by tugging. The women walk as if the floor were sleeping, because the floor is never not half-asleep, full of held water that will wake and travel if you slam. The men do the same. The children are taught a game in which they cross a line of dry earth with a bowl of water and lose if there is a ripple. They laugh with their mouths closed.

Their language sits low in them, more throat than mouth, as if to spare the air the work of carrying the sounds. Names tend to be short and steady: Sen, Dro, Mar, Liv. A long name is worn like a shawl for forms—someone who goes among other settlements will string theirs with soft beads: Mared-of-Shade, Dro-of-the-North-Face. People who come to stay are renamed by the bowl: a carpenter becomes a lip-maker because wood here mostly meets water. A singer becomes a bell-keeper because music here is the lesson of when not to play it.

They are tidy folk without being tidy about dust; dust is not the question. Edges are. They watch edges like other people watch skies. Notched edges of stone overhead. The jawline of a slump. The thin rim of a jar. The line where cold changes to not-cold, that can be felt with a naked palm if you stand very still. The shade-cloths are their orchards, and they prune shade with as much craft as another place cuts vines. They can look at a wet wall and tell you where to breathe on it to make the first bead form. Their days keep the long slow motion of the moving ceilings; they lean into ropes like sailors working some becalmed rig that still answers to their touch.

Property here is not bounded by fences so much as taught into the same air. A family owns the right to pluck certain lines at certain hours; they own the arc of a cloth’s shadow, not by all the sun, only at a particular height of light. They own the drip that comes when shade first rests a skin over a wall, and the one that comes when it leaves again, and nothing in between. These get written, when they must, as knots in a cord kept by the knot-stone, a stubby pillar of the same damp clay plated with carved diagrams. A stranger might think it some mistletoe of ropes fallen from an unseen tree; a resident can read it as in clear weather one reads a map of stars. Arguments are knotted and unknotted there in a public shiver of lines. You see two elders with their foreheads near, not touching, humming the pattern of a deal to one another while their thumbs worry a scrap of wet string between them; in an hour the string carries its verdict to the stone and the web overhead shifts by a finger’s width in obedience.

Marriages live along those cords. A wedding is when one family unhooks their most reliable line from their oldest lip and laces it to another’s, so that the beads choose, most mornings, to go to the same jar. The promise is not said; the water says it. A separation is done by silence—no plucking on a shared cord for thirty days, so that the beads begin to loiter there and fall nowhere. If they do not learn to move again, the families go to the knot-stone and unmake the path together. Children keep the smallest cords. You know who is learning by the constellations of tiny slow shines that take whole mornings to reach home; a practiced hand can call a droplet along a hair-fine line with a single pulse and no touching thereafter, but a child plucks often, coaxes ceaselessly, forgets and remembers, forgets again. No one hurries them; haste makes spills.

They have, if not a calendar, then a way of telling time that cares nothing for weeks. The standing stone holds the notches of the season’s worst slump, the highest weep, the day the sump rose white at its lip like a long-held breath about to break. Infants are lifted against it and marked with charcoal; small shoulders lean back against the cold grain and learn without being told that the world presses. In their stories, the old sea is an aunt who left them a house full of her damp and keeps sending letters back in the language of walls. The god of the place, if there is one, is not a lord but a listener: the Held, the Under-Breath, the Patient One. Prayers are often only a pluck without purpose, a note sent into the bowl to see if it answers. In very hard years when the fog comes late, the elders untie the clay bells and walk them around the quarter clicking them softly like teeth to wake the weeping. It does not work, except that it gives the hands something to do besides turn each other’s knuckles in worry.

They are not all one kind of person. There are the edge-readers, pale-haired or dark, with their measuring knots and rods, who read the day’s new cracks as some read palms. They can predict a small separate slab’s fall to an hour or two, and tell you if you have time to pull a gutter’s lips and take a jar away before the wall pours it all back into the sump. When they are wrong they mark their error with a notch in their own stick and keep the shame visible for a season. There are the line-tenders whose shoulders remember the particular ache of morning: fingertips burnished, always the smell of oiled fiber about them. There are the shade-binders who lift the room-sized skies: the strongest, careful of their backs, eyes scarred at the corners from a hundred mornings of looking up into light. There are eel-people, who are not all men and not all adults; ease, not strength, is what is wanted, and a comfort with going down into the cuts and listening with your ankles for movement. They speak of current the way the others speak of shade, and keep a chain of smooth stones the size of tongues. On rain days they seed the lower alleys with those stones in a pattern only they know, and weave short basketwork with a slit that remembers the size of an eel’s pause. You can tell them by scars like bracelets above the wrists where a frantic wet body has looped and burned the skin with friction.

There are jar-wrights, potters of heavy winter work, who fire when they dare and patch when they must. They can repair a crack with a paste as strong as a creed; their patches moan a little when first wetted, and the sound is as dear as a lullaby here. Some say that the best jars have a temper ground fine from the bones of elders; this is not always said aloud to outsiders. There is so little land that is good at taking a body and keeping it. Gentle fires are kept for meat, not for pyres; there are quiet places along the upper rims where the clay is almost dry, and the dead are set there in a fashion that is more sheltering than burying. The weather takes them. A year or two later a bone is not a sin; it is part of the fixings of a tinier lip or a weight to persuade a stubborn cord to stay straight. It is one way the city keeps its dead near without making anyone say so in words.

What passes for ornament is utility worn thin into beauty. The seam of a canopy crosshatched by a hand that loved its own work. A jar’s neck ringed with bronze, not because bronze is pretty but because it is honest about strain, and everybody admires honesty here. A finger’s worth of green metal cut from a larger world and hammered thin to a knife that shapes the lip of a gutter with a precision that wood cannot dream. Even hair is functional: braided not just to be beautiful but to keep oil from the canopies, threaded sometimes with little slivers of fired clay as if people wished to carry a small, whispering bell of their own.

On sun-days, when the shade is wound and rationed, the whole place shows its bones. Those are not good days for tempers in other towns. Here the law of quiet holds, and if there is a quarrel the angriest person will simply stop plucking. The loss is too grave to be borne; a friend will come behind and do the work without a word, and the one who has thrown their anger at the sky will feel smaller than the cisterns. Children mislearn occasionally and stamp; they are not struck but taken to the standing stone and held there with their back and breath until they can feel what stomping does. The smallest punishment is to be forbidden the lines at dawn—a cruelty of a morning or two that is always enough.

There are markets but they are made of moments. Eel-night hangs in the air for half a day after, the smell of quick cooking, the soft crackle of thin bones, laughter with the mouth closed anyway. The fog most mornings is its own bazaar: somebody has got a bead that behaves oddly on a new cord from a different plant; somebody else has a pitch that does not run in heat. A girl offers hooks she has whittled with a hole at the tail where you can tie a luck-thread. A boy has a spider that has taken to traveling with him, that will come to his hand from a finger-long distance and eat a gnat in a way that gives ten small heads a story to tell all day. The heron is a person, too, in the way of animals that have chosen to tolerate a place without loving it. When it is seen, nobody points; a pair of bent elbows holds still while the neck goes, each step a calligraphy. The vole is the opposite of a heron: myth made by hunger for another kind of ground. Children build it shrines with rush and a crumb of eel; sometimes those shrines collect water in a way that surprises the old people, and a new lip is cut just there, and a new story grows legs.

They are not unschooled. Their thinking is not the thinking of books but of flasks, balances with a drip instead of a weight. They can tell you the time it will take a jar to fill by watching three beads become one on a certain string. They count by merges: one is not a unit until two somethings agree to press into each other and be a third thing. Their arithmetic is the patience of increments. Their philosophy is the refusal to force. If you were to introduce them to pumps they would show you where the cut would gutter into a canyon overnight and kill their narrow terraces; they will not draw hard at the clay’s lung. But if you show them a cord that will not fray under water’s teeth, they will bind for you a sky that makes the fog change its mind about where to live. They pick up what the world offers and persuade it into more of itself.

Illness is humble and watched. Foot-rot is a story told to scare children into sandals, and also real. Sometimes the wet in a chest will not go. Out come the clay bells again, and a day of planted stillness, and a night of warmed fish oil rubbed across a back by hands that have known this work before. Births are assisted by aunties with quiet voices who know how to press a palm over a jar-neck and wait for the cold to tell them when to lift—the same motion, the same pause. Babies are given to the shade their first morning. The elder who carries them steps onto a plank, presses with a toe until it speaks and then holds there until the sound goes, then crosses that join and stands below a hanging sky. There is no blessing said. The breath of the bowl presses up through the plank into all three sets of feet, and that is the blessing.

Night makes another being of the place and of its people. Lines are loosened into sleep, and those that are left humming are weighted with little slugs of baked clay—hush-stones—to keep them from talking too much to the wind. Lovers learn each other by the feel through the boards. An old widow sets her pallet on the corner of her platform that listens best, and sleeps where the plucked morning used to put its hum, where the echoes of those first years of thirst still curl like a cat. There is a sweetness to the darkness there that is not fear. The sump becomes a thought as wide as the sea it pretends to remember, and the walls begin their polite bleeding again like a work that can be done while the mind is elsewhere. If you have never lain among them when the first beadlets find their place on the cords, the sound is almost nothing and entirely everything: a beginning in many places at once, a quiet city waking its instruments.

So yes, what lives there are people: small in their claims and careful in their first motions and proud in a way that breathes out, not up. They have made kin with spiders and invented a second firmament that their children can read. They keep their dead inside the work and their gods in the listening. They measure worth in shade and patience and the art by which you ask a thing to go where you want and it wants to go that way too. And if you stand at the top of the ramp long enough, your heel hollowing its slow oval, your ears learning to hear the felt hums, you will count the place itself among the living. The bowl exhales, water treads, and a hundred hands go up into the new light to teach what falls how to be a homecoming.
