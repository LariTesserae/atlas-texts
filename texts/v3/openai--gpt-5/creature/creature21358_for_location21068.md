Begin with posture. The ones who live here carry height in their bones the way others carry water: as an expectation you don’t set down. Their backs have learned the tilt of afternoons, their ankles the late-night drift; their hands know the grammar of ledges so well that a fingertip can find a confidence invisible to the eye. They do not walk the way a plains-person walks. They lean, they commit. A foot goes down like a word you have decided to mean. They move as if the cliff has already agreed to them and they are only remembering the terms.

Their lungs are not large by our reckonings, but they are long, full of corridors and forks where breath may decide to take the slower way and be accepted again. They sip air with the efficiency you save for something sweet; they do not squander draw on speed. Blood moves in narrow loops with patience. Skin holds a fineness to it, packed with hairs not for warmth but for the tactile read of movement: the slightest inward current along a stair, the shy exhale of a gallery. Ears are sculpted like shells to cup hints of wind-harp. Noses aren’t much use here—there’s nothing to scent—but tongues are mapmakers; a lick of a handhold tells you the day’s gravity through the flavor of salts that have crept there and gone brittle in their forgetting.

Whether they are our cousins depends on how strict you are. They are people in every way that matters to company and hunger and story. Their bodies answer to dryness without complaint. They shine their eyes with oils skimmed from the mineral lace and keep those oils taut under skin so their joints slide in the thin cold. Their tears would not wet a cheek; they come as little glassy pebbles a child is taught to collect and keep because what is given up lightly is what this place forgets first.

They have names, and they take names seriously. A true name is a weight one person can’t bear alone. As a child grows and starts to put both feet where one used to be, a household will divide pieces of the child’s name among its beams and lintels so that the rooms remember to hold them up. Later they will borrow weight for crossing: a lover’s syllable tucked in the cheek before a traverse that requires certainty; a dead aunt’s consonant sewn into the hem for an afternoon of sideways gravity. Things with names keep closer to the cliff; this is both law and superstition and—here—those are not different kinds of knowing.

They speak in touches and in making; they speak in the way a line is carved. There is voice of course, a soft murmur meant for the short reach of this air, but much of their language happens in the hands. There is a syntax of grip. Three fingers on the ridge: I am here. Flat palm against the plane: this edge is good. The back of a knuckle brushed along your sleeve: wait—feel—the drift. Some talk is bone-on-bone. They hang chimes and articulate fingers from lintels because the sum of their clickings, read by anyone of the place, says the day’s temper as pointedly as a sentence.

You can tell an infant’s house by the density of notches near the floor, a child’s by the proliferation of low names carved hidden, behind, under. Before a child is allowed to make their own edge, they are taught the three steps that have nothing to do with left-right: commit, refuse, relent. Commit and you become heavier, the cliff accepts the agreement; refuse and you grow light, the world recuses you; relent and you slide, letting the day’s drift take you to a place that will bear you when it is ready. They play games where the floor is not decided yet and learning to still yourself while everything else negotiates around you is worth more than running.

They have trades, but the trades here look like obligations to the cliff. There are edgewrights whose work is to shepherd nascent corners into themselves. They sit with stone and speak the correct densities into it: a word tapped in with a salt-porcelain mallet, a trace of bone-ceramic dust pushed into a seam, a fin grown where the lace wants to take hold but needs the example of a good fin to begin. There are namekeepers who know the old bridges’ hidden scripts and walk the galleries at dusk to freshen syllables before the night siphons them down into cracks. There are fallreaders who stand with their eyes closed and their hands clamped to iron-cold glass; they can tell you at noon how much heavier your shoulder will be by dinner.

Work, in general, is not toward making so much as toward persuading. They herd the mineral lace like patient gardeners who never touch the plant but adjust the wind, shadow it wants, grit it catches. They tease a lip into existence beneath an unkind draft the way we coax a fire from ash. A master can lay a cup-line across an entire court so that every late-fall fog spills a single paring of pressure into a room where elders go to sit and remember weather; the fog flowers smash softly along the lip, give up their films and brittle petals of chill, and the elders breathe a little easier for an hour.

They are animal-tenders, too, though the animals here are more idea than meat. Families keep skeins in folded pockets of bone-glass like a man might keep pigeons on a roof: they send them out at noon when the static ladders stand firm, and the creatures drift and nibble the day. Come evening, a child will clap their sleeve and a ribbon of shapes will come scissoring down, quiet as mathematics, puffed a tiny bit with charge. The household will stroke each until it lets go what it carries—your fingertips bristle for a breath—and the charge is coaxed along a path to a place that holds differences as if they were oats in a bin.

There are long-bodied beings you almost never see, the flat-spine dwellers who tilt from under ledges and fall without consent, which here is to say fall perfectly. People make icon of them: scratch two lines and a long curve in the corner of a doorway to say: may we choose our drifts. The tall stilt-walkers get no such honor; they are respected as one respects a seam that might pinch. Sometimes a household will leave a thin ladder un-named so that a stilt-creature can continue its unfathomable commute without stopping to dismantle someone’s certainty.

They eat difference, as everything here eats difference. Food is the lace coaxed into sheets, then crisped by forgotten heat into something sweet as a lie on the tongue. Food is the dust from lightning’s slow crawl across a wall: they will lick the thin fused trail and nod as if taking a tonic. Food is skein-fat, which is not a fat, scraped in glimmer from a bowl’s sides and pressed into grain, where grain means the regular geometry of something that wants to be a crystal but has been denied its closure by a housewife’s patience. On celebration days there is glass-rain. Children are sent out at the first hiss with bowl-hats on, and if they return with a sliver unshattered they are allowed to set it as a window into a nook near the floor. The littlest ones peer through and see the world in fine prismatic sulks, and this, too, they are allowed to eat with the tongue.

Water they do not have and so they do not miss. The word for thirst and the word for yearning are the same here and neither means dryness. There are rimes that creep and sublimate, and there are people who collect their memory, not their dissolving. A woman may go out at dusk with a comb of bone and brush a seam where ammonium frost curled earlier, and the sound of the comb’s teeth breaking the frost is a kind of reading. She will come back and say: it was a good day for the western arches but we should not trust the ladder by the old ribs. Everyone nods, and nobody argues with a frost’s book.

A vote in this place is literal; it is a weighing. Council happens where a beam is still uncertain. The question is named and hung beneath the beam. People step out in order and lay a palm down. If the beam commits under the sum of those touches, the answer is yes. If it remains insubstantial—if the collected believing still keeps them lighter than their own names—the answer is no. Law is the way rooms remember. Transgression is to make a place forget.

They marry to solve for height and for drift. Partners lend each other syllables; together they are heavier at the right times, lighter when a reluctance is wanted. A child’s name is a triad: one part from dawn, when the sigh climbs; one part from noon, when the static ladders step; one part from dark, when light becomes slow and likes small rooms. You do not speak your own whole name unless you must cross a fall that might undo you. Many of the old walkways are covered with a lattice of tool marks, each mark a story about someone who committed, each mark a fragment of the name that made the edge bear them. Their children run fingertips over those scars and grunt the syllables under breath like the beginnings of a hymn.

Death is an easing, not a falling. When someone’s body has finished being their body, their room is un-named in the corners where it held them, and the pieces of them written there creep across onto spans and into doors. The person becomes a set of permissions. Their shadow is taken to a wall that has a taste for keeping and leaned there all night until it slides a few ledges down and balances along a seam. In the weeks that follow anyone who rests in that place will feel a company settle into their neck and will lean a little more safely because of it. Children are taught who is who by where their legs go soft at midday.

If there are gods here they are edges with tempers and drifts with opinions. They have little temples carved where gravity has been consistent for so long the stone has grown bored with it; a faithful person will bring their hesitation there and set it on the altar in the form of an uncut name. In return they take up a new corner and walk it home. Priests are not intercessors but calibrators. A priest will tell you: you are too light for this decision; gather your people and press your names into it again.

There are others who live here who are not bodies. The edges themselves are a population: they flock, they merge, they throw off strays. There are shy ones—ruled by small drafts—that never grow beyond the curl of a finger and can be kept in a pocket for luck. There are predatory edges that wait for corners to come close and then snap into a path, which is why every third child is given a line of glass to carry, to throw down between two edges if they begin to hunt. There are the fish of shadow who run at dusk. Boys and girls on a certain night will stand with nets knitted from names and take two or three edges each and release them later near a place that needs persuading. There are even old rumors of a corner that wanted company and learned to make its own; a house will leave little offerings—chips of bone, drops of skein-charge—in the crease behind a stove and the next year there will be a new handhold there nobody remembers carving.

They map through patience. The distance you cross will not be the distance you return, so their charts live as weavings. A path is a braid of tendencies; you follow one strand till it is weaker than its neighbor and then you turn with the turn. The best maps are hung in places not to read but to act on the room they inhabit, slowly teaching the space to prefer the braids written there. The room remembers, the way the cliff remembers a remembered sea; when a grandchild takes a step, the step fits the ancestral pressure, and what seemed a blank becomes ledge.

Trade is quiet and dizzy. Two households a kilometer apart can send baskets to one another across a fall by naming a line exactly between them so well that gravity forgives the audacity for an hour. They do not send what weighs. They send what drifts: words, little laces shaped like bowls, a bone pick engraved with a piece of someone’s promise. There are exchanges across the rift that take a week to arrive because distance went long, and the gesture of setting that basket out anyway is as much a part of the gift as what ends up—later—sitting in a courtyard you’ve never stood in.

Danger never leaves, but here it does not always wear teeth. It wears an afternoon slump that tilts just when you needed up to be as it was. It wears a child’s breath too light to be accepted by a stubborn stair. They teach one another to read it without panic. When the fallwind starts you feel it first in your elbows and there is a communal sigh—not fear, not relief, exactly, but a sound that includes a dozen small recalculations. They teach: do not argue with a refusal. Sit. Wait. Let the place remember you. Then stand when you are heavier.

If you could sit a day with them, you’d know which is young and which is old by the way a person’s step claims world. The oldest do not hurry, not because they can’t, but because they are in no doubt that wherever they mean to go will in time be exactly the place under their feet. The young are properly overconfident and must be chased from naming too many things at once. Once, when a bridge failed because it wanted to be sky that day, a child laughed at the shimmer it left and the elders scolded him, not for delight but for clapping hands while the cliff had not yet decided to keep them.

Art is not decoration; it is bias. They cut stories into leeward faces and those stories bend drafts for a generation. A love poem as filigree can make a narrow balcony fancy the idea of being a plaza. A grief chant, graven deep and plain into a rib, will slow gravity there for an hour every midnight for a year, and widows will go there to sit in breath that feels like the weight of an arm across your shoulders. Music is wind rendered; their instruments wait and read. Someone will frame a vault with bone and salt, and on the day the fallwind brings fog up the rift the vault will answer in tones so low you hear them more in the sternum than the ear. Children name those notes after their grandparents.

The city itself is not inert. It keeps your weight if it knows you, and it can be taught to know you. There are households that have been patient enough to beget rooms that find their centers even on bad nights, safe corners that stay corners no matter what the day’s physics suggest. New places are fickle. A new stair only accepts three steps one day out of seven until enough feet have persuaded it. The old bones are faithful and keep whole neighborhoods close to the rest of the world when the gravity goes thinking.

And there are the ones you will not recognize at first as residents: the distances that stretch and relax like cats in warm light; the shadows that carry a pleasant fatigue and lean back into your calves until you have to smile; the light that settles, slow, into cracks and stays until someone needs it. The weather of gravity is not mindless; it is opinionated. It prefers those who have decided to belong. It resists those who try to use it without a name.

Who lives here? People, yes, who herd films and trapezoids as others herd wool, who feed their families on the arithmetic between shade and almost-shade. Children who can taste a ledge and tell when a step will accept them. Elders whose names hold up roofs and whose shadows are good company. The animals that are really arguments with physics. The edges themselves, with their schools and their hungers. The city, too, as the aggregate of all past weight: a mind of spans and leewards and patient carvings that has learned what a human footfall is and that mostly chooses to be kind to it.

To visit is to learn slowness and the courage of surety. To dwell is to become part of how falling happens. They will offer you a sliver of their name if they like you and expect a syllable of yours in exchange; they will show you where to lean so that when you walk away and the room remembers you it will do so fondly. They will laugh softly when you shout and the canyon wastes your breath. They will take you to the place where your shadow can be laid down and taught to be useful.

Do not try to fill their emptiness with your noise. Sit, put your fingers in the notch that is meant for them, and wait for the ledge to agree. If you stay a while, you will find yourself heavier in the way that keeps what you love from blowing away. And your name—because this is a city that knows how to keep such things—will gain a little weight of its own.
