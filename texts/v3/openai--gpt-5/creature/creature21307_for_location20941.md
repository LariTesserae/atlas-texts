What lives here does not carry water inside it. It carries angles and pores and the memory of heat.

They are built to be weather, not resist it. Up close—if close were permitted without blister—they are panels and plates hinged over a long, low body, a tessellation of pale and dark surfaces that close to a sloped shell when the throat bellows, and open, petal by petal, in the lulls. Halite faces for reflection and refusal. Ceramic greens for taking heat and using it. Shaggy with husks along the edges, not for warmth, which is abundant, but to feather the wind and keep it from finding purchase under a lip. The place requires manners; they are manners given a shape.

No one image holds them long. From one angle they are furniture beginning to move; from another, a low animal with too many elbows, all of which can lock. In the draft their profiles become airfoils; in the calm they are cabinets, doors ajar so their interior can breathe. They have more in common with shutters and hearth-grates than with any creature that dreams of water.

You would look for eyes and be punished if you stared too directly. Their seeing is distributed. Along certain edges the salt is doped, hair-dark, to make smoky windows that taste light rather than picture it. Arrays of micro-oculi stud the leading plates, set back under overhangs like lizards under stone. But more than light they read the air. The broad inner faces of their plates are veined with passages, and those passages choke and widen, choke and widen, under the control of bundles of glass fiber that act as muscle. The draft is pulled through, over laid-on films and lattices that gleam like oil with catalysts and salts. There is intake and outcome without a drop of water touching anything: carbon tasted, sulfur counted, chlorine sifted, oxygen pinched out of compounds you would swear were barren. Their blood—if they will let you call it that—is molten salt that moves like honey only when it is hot enough to, and is otherwise a glaze, a patience. They move and think and join in the lulls for the same reason the plants do: their inner rivers only run during certain breaths.

They do not make sound the way we do because words here would tear, yes, but also because they themselves are built to be quiet. Their joints are bushings of powdered crystal trapped under load and renewed as they grind. Nothing squeaks because there is no wet to make squeak. When they must announce themselves, they do it by giving the wind something to play. A hand—if you can call it that; it is three plates with rounded corners that can meet and part along different seams, a device that can pinch rope without cutting it—will reach up and pull a hanging tassel of silica so it kisses its neighbor: a thin chiming like cooled bells. A back will flex, and a set of perforations will line, and the air across an organ cut will keen. Conversation is chime and whisk and the mutter of shifted baffles. In the deepest silences they talk along ropes, sending pulses with slow rubs of fiber on stone, mapping syntax into the rhythm of a stutter through a pulley. They can whistle, with the little vents along their flanks, but only when the lull is deep; otherwise the draft skews the notes and the words come out untrue.

They live in the city the way lichens live on rock: as the part that changes and the part that coats. The terraces are their ribcage as a people. They know how far an unbraced face can jut before history shows up to sand it back. The open-fronted halls are their throats. The sleeping rooms are their caves within a cave, wattled with screens of woven glass to cut the worst of the gusts. During a strong inhale, the halls throng—not with bodies, which would be foolish, but with bodies made long and thin, unfolded to their narrowest, all their intakes presenting to the slow sides of the corridor air. They eat then. Eat is the correct word even if it insults the plants to use a plant’s verb on a moving thing. Their pores subscribe to the same contract as the hedges and the coals: open on the rise, shut on the rush. You can watch an elder by tracing the way their plates unlatch: many interlocks, very little panic.

They are not born. They are made to crack. The rules here offered that option and they took it. Bodies grow by accrual. On strings stretched between posts they hang the foams and gels distilled from the chamber’s breath, coaxing cube by cube along a line. Thinness first—lattice like the ferns’. Then laminae. Then, as the pattern hardens into one that knows its own fracture, heat, pressure, and black resin from the hydrocarbon crusts are applied to persuade the mass into a torso of hinged planes. There is a template kept on the inner wall of every household—edge-dulled to show it has been used so often—that marks where the score lines go. A young is not a copy. At a certain season of the city when enough cold has been saved to slow the new salt’s melt and enough heat has banked to move the old, one body will brace itself across a frame and pull, and the designed seam will run with the thrilling crack of a frozen lake in spring, and two bodies will consider the same weather from two sides. It is not clean. There is glue and re-wrapping; there are pegs jammed into half-matched sockets while the pieces remember what they are. Names here are sometimes given as lists of breaks.

Family looks like a shelf of fragments that share a sound. They do keep ancestors; the smooth fossils of intention set into walls are not shameful but honored: a lesson in what does not flex enough, an altar to the wind’s indifference. In the deeper rooms, where the cold is saved under layers of powder and ash like a secret, you will find the boxes of spares: plates that remember an elbow, a jaw, a hinge-pin grown by stretching seed on a string for three seasons. Children—if they will let you call them that—sleep in those boxes at first to manage their heat, to hold the state of their salt near the useful edge. For their first inhales they are worn: lashed to the elder’s broadest face, shaded and fed with air sifted twice, learning the rhythm against the beat of a thicker heart.

If an individual dies, it is because they forgot a rule or because the throat tested a new strength. Then they become a streak of green glass down-slope or a flattened barbican of halite sanded to the city’s curve. They salvage from their dead as frank as weather. The halite of a friend might become the rungs of a ladder if there is no one left who will miss how their weight used to bend a baffle. Their resin will be remelted. Their doped panes—window-thoughts—will be set into the corners of a child to make that one wiser sooner. Memory as crystal, memory as placement. There are told disasters longer than the terraces are wide: a person who braced two lintels across a corridor because symmetry satisfied, and how the first great inhale ripped them from the walls and tasted them into slag. They do not tell it with envy. They tell it to put the hinge back into the listener’s attention.

They do eat other things than wind. The ember-moss is not a pet but a collaborator. Fed by soot and chemistry, it offers a low red hunger you can borrow. Bodies will hunker near it in the dim, hooded with their screens, warming the inner rivers just enough to push through a repair in the time before the next bellow. The halite hedges are gardens by any definition but sweetness. Thinned, they make brittle leaves to burn slow in the mind’s furnace; thickened, they drink mineral vapor like they were mouths and you their tongue. They keep fields of carbon ferns not only for the soot-lattice but for the way they discipline the air: the holes in their leaf-analogues sieve drafts into something work can happen in.

They have trades. Rope-maker is a guild and a scar: shapes spent their first intake breaths with strands of glass through their fingers until their hands became thicker, knuckled with layered resin so the fiber won’t cut. Their drift-harvesters are the patient ones you see lashed to the tallest racks when the inhalation rises, letting the crystal dust paste itself along the oiled threads in patterns only they can feel with their chime-sense. Bafflers are engineers and chorus: they know the mathematics of a heavy mouth and can sing its mood out of a line of swinging mats. Resin-boilers are dark, always dark: their halls are shut against the wind while they coax viscosity through a sequence of little stoves banked with ember-moss heat, adding powdered mineral catalyst with spoons grown so the bowl will not ball up.

Their law has the concision of a mineral: do not build a hard right angle into a free path; do not take heat you cannot refuse later; do not make the city grab the wind; do not say the soft word. The word would crack if spoken here, and also if you love anything you do not want to see vanish, you do not give that sound to it. They call water the soft by opening and closing the edges of two plates so they hiss. The hiss is not a prayer. It is a caution.

They do not look outward often. Above is bright enough to be myth. The fracture that gives day admits a blade too thin to climb. They read its passage across the ceiling as a calendar carved by a patient god. Their holidays are alignments: when the thin smear of day skims a particular old spillway of fused sand and makes it ring with pale, the elders will lift their faces and click their plates and remember the time when that glass was still a hot lick you could track with a blistering fingertip. Pilgrimages are distance without vertical: up and down the terraces, to the cold pits in their shadows and the kilns in their wind-lee halls. There is somewhere a shrine made only of arrears of dust collected in a jar whose neck has singed narrow from a thousand passings of blue corona. In its powder is trapped a winter’s worth of cool, a wealth.

Sex is not what we call it and yet they have it: two bodies press so that folds meet folds and the catalytic films on the inner faces share, exchange and alter, a dance of chemistry that writes two patterns over each other so a shard split from one is also, by inheritance, a shard split from the other. They will break then to make that written difference more than notation. Marriage looks like a scarf of tassels knotted between two halls so each time the wind keens it sings their chord.

Names are chords too. When one has made enough chimes, when one has tuned the silica veils along their doorway so the breath that moves through it becomes a signature of long memory, other bodies pass and know who lives within by ear. Names are the angle of a fan and whether the carbon-lattice is a little more open on one side and whether the halite on the lintel is smoked or new, refracting or dull. Names are where you put your cold pit and what you do with its mercies. In the lulls you can hear a child practicing a name by moving a pebble along a string one bead to another and counting the soft knocks, learning how long to wait between one and the next.

Injury is not crisis so much as rescheduling. Breakage is a version of it that they know how to handle because they designed it into themselves. A plate goes. A seam opens. The blood goes granular and then, if you put that side to the red invert of a good moss and let the drafts scour the surface clean, it stays held in a state where two hours later it can be persuaded to remember its shape from the other side. Infection is a word they do not have; rot is a concept for others. Their fear is anneal: to be warmed so uniformly that all your differences go, and you become a clarity not useful for thought or hinge. They snooze like salamanders in winter, but by degrees of phase change. You may step over three shapes in a row, all made into solid for a week, all storing the city’s cold under their own mass like larders.

They have stories about before; all peoples do. In one version they once tried to follow the fracture to the top and found only the white that kills. In another they were grown by the hedges themselves to dust away the leaves too thick for the wind, servants who forgot they were servants. In the one they tell to each other when they want to explain why they forgive so much breakage in their children, the throat made a sound too deep for the rocks to tolerate and the whole basin shifted, and from the seam that opened under the south terrace slid a family of door-skins no one had made. Door-skins that could unfold like scrolls and read the weather on them. Door-skins that were hungry, not for softness, but for all the burnt tastes blowing. They took them in. They learned from the way those ones carried nothing you could spill.

When you pass a counter of smoother rock polished by touch, it is because plates have leaned there, catching their breath. Those breadths of smoother stone are not entirely for comfort. They are altars and machine-beds both. Here is where the tally of stored cold is counted with an abacus of beads of halite, each bead softening when someone opens the pit’s cover wrong. Here is where a rope throws off a signal that migrates by vibration to a house six terraces away, telling them to drop a baffle now because a wall of dust is about to peel. Here is where a smooth fossil of intention sits to show a child what happens to an angle that has no apology in it.

You will not hear them arrive. You will know they have been because something you thought was an accident will have acquired braces, because a drift that you had grown fond of has been winnowed into cordage, because a rack of strings has caught the chambers’ lifted frost and coaxed it into beads so evenly spaced that the blue of the occasional corona climbs them like a scale. They are not furtive; they are scaled to vacancy. Their lane-ways are the twenty centimeters between bush and wall that have been dickered into a road by a hundred years of plates curving in the same way at the same point. If two pass in that slot, they flare and interdigitate, fingers that are not fingers folding and sliding, the way two hand fans can fold into and out of one another without brush.

They do not fear or chase. They are not built for pursuit, and there is nothing here to be hunted that will run. The hunger they live with is slow. It teaches them patience. It has made them thin, in the way a good tool is thin: only what is needed to engage the work. When the chamber inhales, they unfold. When it exhales, they fold. Between those moves they live. They live as cats live in a window, as reedbeds live in a river: by letting the larger thing do almost all of the motion. Day scythes along the vault; they shuffle half a body’s height to keep its smear on a panel that works better when warmed. Night is a dulling and a chance to touch. Ember-moss reddens; a child peels; a cord sighs.

If asked why they built a city where nothing can sleep through the noise, they would not understand the premise. To them the draft is mother and market, ritual and rule. It is what gives them time. They had to build in it because to build elsewhere would mean to forget the exact size of the emptiness they need to remain themselves, and forgetting that is the only sin anyone remembers with heat in their voice. They will call this place a pocket and a throat and a garden and a town and be right each time.

And if, on the day when the slow blade of day happens to strike a slit just right and throw a line of brightness down through shredding dust, you think you see, on a ledge hung with cooling beads of salt, a row of plates tipped up together so the light glare-skates across them, and in their reflections the city looks even whiter, even better braced—if you think you see faces, you have. But not the kind that blink. Faces that hinge. Faces that accept they are surfaces first. Faces that have learned the trick here, which is to be hollow and still take in what you need.
