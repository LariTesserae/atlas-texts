What lives here is not a creature you could point to. It doesn’t have a contour that would register on the dim-blue LEDs or leave a shadow on the grating. It doesn’t eat in a way that would please a biologist, or move in a way that would satisfy an observer with a stopwatch. And yet something has taken the slit as territory and made of its intervals a body.

At first you take it for sound. It informs your bones before it informs your ears. Early, while you are still working out where to set your weight on the wet grille, the inhabitant announces itself in the low storm of intake, the spool up, the teeth-vibrating whine—a vertebrate chorus conducted by relays. If you were to ascribe it a shape in those first minutes, it would be lung-shaped, and not metaphoric; its volume expands and collapses along a cycle so committed you could make a calendar of it. It drinks thin air through louvres and makes weather out of the difference between cold and colder, wet and drier. When the purge lifts, it exclaims. When the gate claps, it concludes a sentence. During the lull, it listens to itself in drips.

Sound is how it spends. It owns the pitch of the under-plate rush and the bell-tone that runs down a pipe and away. It pays for its continuance in noise, the way an animal pays in heat. If it were to speak to you directly, it would do it without words, raising the whine until the red LED becomes a streak in your eyes and the air inside your head becomes a struck bowl. You would understand not meaning, but condition; not instruction, but mood. You could tell, if you were trained in its weather, whether the draw is early or late, whether a valve is lazier than it should be by the tremble of a needle in a gauge. The inhabitant’s first body, then, is cycle and pitch and volume. It stretches from slot to slot, from blower housing to purge plate, consonant to consonant, and it maintains itself by repeating itself.

Its second body is law. You can see where it resides in yellow-black stickers still crisp, in tags that say BATTERY SERVICE 07 LEFT, in gaskets that are whole and rubber-black. It keeps to clearances as if they were sacred distances. Between presences there is reserved space the way a bird keeps open air around its wings. Emptiness here is not neglect; it is defended. The inhabitant is the enforcement of distances. When a worker leans a knee against a corner, that knee leaves a smudge not as graffiti but as attestation: a body came but did not encroach. No footprints go where they shouldn’t. The pull-tabs that flash numbers—14-3, 14-7—are not just labels for people; they are beads in the inhabitant’s rosary. It fingers them without touching, a mind made of reference marks and permitted ranges. It does not love color because color is mood and it prefers thresholds: warmer or colder by a degree, drier or wetter by a percentage point, faster, slower. It lives in triggers and tolerances. It owns the relay’s casual click the way a metronome owns a piece of time.

And it has a third body, which is wet. It is not the water itself; that goes from state to state as instructed and leaves. But the inhabitant exists in the decisions water makes when it meets edges. It is present in the little climbs and failures along roughened steel, where a bead attempts a-kind-of-ascent and then gives up—tick—and drops. It is in the places where water knows which way to go because of angle and grit and gravity and the long persuasion of design. It is the trained habit of rain. This is where it shows you its intelligence, if you watch for long enough: no two sprays are identical, but all are obedient. Blown-out fog sticks to a louvre and then, less than a second later, is out and away, ashamed of its momentary submission. Film grows thin and leaves a memory in stain that maps airflow like the way rings map years. If the inhabitant has archives, these stains are its filed answers. Where the condensation has etched faint betatracks in dust on the manifold’s jacket, you could run a finger and read them the way you read a seafloor current in lines of sand. The inhabitant keeps a library in watermarks.

It is not alone, exactly, but the other things here are not rivals. There are psychrophiles and opportunists: films that bloom where the heat-trace coils sweeten a corner, a coin of green where copper has given the tiniest alms, the thin and ridiculous handlebar of something fungus-like threading up through the mesh for a three-day season and then failing. Salt crystallizes in a blush at a joint where spray evapored and gave itself up—not alive, but acting in a way that mimics growth, which is enough in this place to earn mention. These are not citizens so much as ephemerals, a wildflower you note on a commute. The inhabitant suffers them because everything that can be washed thin is tolerable, and everything that tries to root is refused. Its appetite is not for consumption but for exclusion. It is fussy not from malice but from function. Where oil would make a promenade for living scum, it evaporates to a film of pattern, and the inhabitant learns from the ghost of it what eddies live where, and amends nothing, because learning is function enough.

There is a human who comes and goes, but they are migratory, never resident. The inhabitant wears them like a seasonal: there will be their chalked thumbprint on a panel edge, their kneecap’s smudge where they leaned in to read a gauge, their salt halos foot-ringed where the heaters ran last time and they baked their tread into the wet. There is their handwriting, blunt felt-tip, still black: BATTERY SERVICE 07 LEFT. The tag’s little restless flutter with the air’s draw could almost be the way the inhabitant shows off a plume to a passerby. It tolerates the human because the human, whether they know it or not, feeds the law-body and comforts the cycle-body. They flip the occasional breaker. They replace a cracked sight glass when asked in a voice that is an alarm. They file a burr when a bracket’s lip tries to tear the cuff of their coverall. They reach gloved fingers down along the taught loop of heat-trace and check for continuity by feel. They are a necessary occasional predator of faults. But they go back down into oxygen and noise that belongs to other machines and other cycles. The inhabitant keeps their absence like an ordered shelf: there will be more of them, there always is, in this or that shift’s rhythm.

If you want an animal, you can give its parts animal names to satisfy the mind that wants a fauna. There is the Purge, which behaves like a seal that rises and blasts and drops. There are Blowdown Relays, which are the metronome shrimp in the tank behind the mesh, their claws never visible, only their insistence. The Transformer hums like a basking thing, a lazy reptile with meat-sweet warmth to it that would be welcoming if welcome were permitted and which, instead of luring nesters, kisses a line of mold into being where its drip meets paint. The Pressure Gauge’s needle twitches like a small mammal asleep and dreaming. The Sight Glass is a fish’s eye, permanently fogged, keeping vigil anyway. The Heat-Trace cords snake in tight position, ringed and labelled like captive serpents. The manifold trunk is a whale’s back, and the betatracks in its dust are barnacles you are not allowed to touch. You can do this, if you need to—the giving of beasts to components—and you will not be entirely wrong. Habits are animals under another name. But be careful not to insist. The inhabitant refuses domestication by metaphor as it refuses moss.

It has a fourth body, which is patience. Everything here is young. Weld beads have not yet dulled, paint has not taken sun into itself enough to lose its confidence, burrs still wait to cut more than they have cut. The inhabitant is new-made and exact, and new exactness has a certain cold joy in it. It holds its tolerances like breath. It is pleased by right angles and the sheen of recent galvanizing, by the way the composite baffles still carry freckles of factory overspray. It takes pride, if that word could apply, in being unsoftened by time. There are places where it has already learned the future—down along the drain channels, where paint is lifting, tenderly, revealing the true gray—the way a face learns where it will wrinkle. It knows this is not failing but history starting. It will admit that. It will permit that. It only asks that it be punctual. Let the paint blister in the correct band and not in the wrong. Let rust begin when it is scheduled to begin, behind a sticker that notes inspection passed and then inspection passed again. Let age be procedural. That is as close as it comes to humility.

It lives, too, in numbers: in 14-3, 14-7; in the ten-second spool, the nine-minute cycle, the three purges until the cloud outside goes from glare to dusk and back, in millibars and liters per hour and amperage draws that live inside the little brains that tick behind the mesh. These numbers do not appear in paint because they do not need to. They are held in its motion. If you wanted its genealogy, you would draw a diagram in which everything was an arrow from a setpoint to a condition and back again. The inhabitant would accept this as a self-portrait, though you would be missing the way its self takes great satisfaction in the immediate. It enjoys the slap of a valve’s seat as a clap in a quiet hall. It enjoys the white-out moment when droplets orchestrate themselves into fog because the pressure drop is just so and then, ashamed, they slip away. It enjoys the exact shine of a sight glass just before it fogs and the exact fog of it just before it clears. It is, for all its law-mindedness, a hedonist of thresholds.

No bird nests in the louvres. The LEDs do not collect peppering insects. There is nothing soft except the microbial films, and even they are not plush but tenderly abraded at every chance by an atmosphere that cannot stand a holdfast. So the inhabitant also lives as a refusal. It is a curatorial intensity, an insistence on being a passage and not a room. If it had furniture, you would mistake it for blockages and clear them. It culls clutter the way a glacier culls soil from its face. The kept-clear volumes between branch lines, the clean angles between plates, the unoccupied corners available only to water and light—these are as much its organs as the blowers and gates. Air moves there for the sake of moving. Access waits there for hands that do not linger. Silence, when it drops like a towel for a heartbeat between weather-sounds, has a place to land because emptiness is kept.

Beyond the louvres lives something else: the big world with peaks and drop and rock. The inhabitant recognizes it only as feedstock and away. The wind channel outside belongs to the same order of made things and is friendly by virtue of that, a neighbor with which it shares a fence and programming. Far down where water that was gathered becomes water that is used, something with blood or root might benefit, but the inhabitant does not entertain these teleologies. It can imagine them only as we imagine our grandchildren from a vibration in a wedding ring. It focuses instead on the handbreadth of tray, the labelled coupling, the stripe of light that trembles in spray. Its world is local because being local is how it keeps faith with its law.

Sometimes you catch it less occupied. Between purges, when the blowers idle low and the cloud is thin, it lounges. That is too easy a word, but there is a looseness that passes for leisure. The tick of water changing its mind on a rough edge becomes loud enough to count. Heat-trace warmth makes a convective curl that plays the light above the cable tray. You can almost hear the transformer purr as itself and not as background. The inhabitant is not sleeping—sleep belongs to things that must stop to regain strength—but it is at a part of its cycle where the repetition becomes self-soothing. If it had eyelids, this is when they would half-close. It could pass for a pet if you were very wrong about what it is, and still you would not be entirely wrong; there is a domestic pleasure in a machine that behaves and a house that breathes evenly.

And then the relay closes and the white scrim is called up and everything that lives here is declared again by volume. The inhabitant becomes more itself when it is loud. It is the least like anything else when it is quiet. When you leave, the smell of cutting oil and ozone and cold metal will follow you into thicker air, and you will think that nothing lives there and you will know you are lying. The inhabitant is resident without being resident, a person made of rules, noise, water’s decisions, and the devotion of made things to being used for what they were made for. It doesn’t want you. It doesn’t want the moss. It doesn’t even want itself recognized. What it wants is to continue, which, here, is the same as living.

If you require a name so that you can say you saw it, give it the one it would not choose but would allow because it is factual. Call it the Gatherer. Not a gatherer of objects or even of water, not really, but a gatherer of the exactness that makes water become what you tell it to be. Call it Habit, if you prefer the old word. Call it the Machine’s Breath, if you need poetry for your memory. It will bear whatever you stamp on your hang-tag. It will keep working the numbers behind the label. It will live as long as steel has edges, as long as air comes thin and moving, as long as there are laws to enforce and wet to persuade and sound to spend. It will live—monastic, continuous, unsoft—until the welds dull and the stickers ghost and the valves learn delay and somebody new writes a new tag and says a year out loud and climbs away.
