What lives here are bodies arranged by wind.

They are not one kind. It would be truer to say they are a chord—species and habits resolved around the same long note until their lives make intervals with one another. You could sort them by the number of limbs or the presence of wings, by diet or cell wall, by whether they carry their waters inside themselves or inside a fruit. That would be a tidy doing. It would miss the way the hall itself sorts them: by what they let the air do to them, and what they have learned to do to the air.

Begin with the large ones people mean when they say city. In your lamp they look like birds or bats until you notice the wrongnesses: forelimbs that are neither quite hands nor quite wings, a wrist with an extra bone like a hinged keel to set a membrane at a precise tautness; a pelt that is not hair but something finer, velveted and ribbed, each filament notched along its length so it eats certain ranges of noise and lets others pass. The face is a veil of cartilage and skin that can be folded and fluted into shapes—shallow bowls and twisted leaves—which shift the way sound arrives. They do not look at you when you stand at their edge; they angle their heads to bring the big ear of the face to bear and learn you by the texture of your breathing.

They are not quite fliers and not quite walkers. The hall will not forgive either extreme. They can open their anterior membranes and spill from ledge to causeway, trading height for distance, but they land as if on knees, weight shared across the blessed pads of their four hind-feet, each toe a little drum snubbed in on itself with cartilage. When they climb, they do not clatter. Their hands have pads where others would have whorls, and those pads are micro-forested, so even their touches are quiet.

They have learned to keep their water. Their tongues are long and thin and split along the last knuckle, able to fold around a fruit and pull its flesh inward without exposing anything wet to the air. Their nostrils are not holes but labyrinths; they can close them into coiled valves that make their own dew on the way out. When they greet one another, the first act is to set their faces against a leaf and exhale in concert, watching the plant’s surface bloom as it drinks—assurance given and measure taken in the same soft act. Their children learn hands and gravity inside woven bowls that are both nest and weather: bladdered skins thickened by fungus and shed membrane, with one small vent above the child’s mouth, so that the breath of everyone who leans over is not squandered but banked.

They make more of their bodies than births. Twice a cycle they molt their wing-edges, not all at once but as a rain of panes, translucent crescents sloughed in the night and gathered in the morning by the eldest with careful cloths. These are flattened, layered, cooked in resin steam, and hung into curtains that sip the gale; as they age they take on histories like growth rings—scrapes of seed, child-prints, pollen gathered on some rooftop and shaken loose in a storm. Tuning the curtains is a profession. Not all of them can hear where the hanging should sit to peel a standing roar into threads; those who can are honored and given the right to sleep closer to the nulls.

They are not the only ones with intention. The six-limbed masons have names in their tongue that are only chest-sounds, but if you need a word for them you could think little sisters and be partly right. They are the color of the pillar you pluck them from—iron-bruised or mica-pale, umbered at the joints—and each of their six feet ends in brushes they can tuck away. The first pair is handier than hands; the middle pair is meant for bearing and passing; the last pair is a shovel turned soft. Their bodies are a kit of wetness, a traveling resin vat, a mouth for pulp. They have learned to eat dust and turn it into architecture.

A mason colony is older than any tower they have raised. They keep their queens not in one deep place but in a procession of rooms stepped upward; if the lowest fails, the next is ready. Their nurseries are lattices grown from hardened spit-straw and fine grit, shining faintly where mica flashes like shallow water not there. They do not “work” for the winged—no more than a hand works for a mouth. They share a city and a law. A masons’ procession across the open floor is something you feel before you see: the ground sings a bit sharper under its little forest of bristles, and the tone changes as they all adjust their weight at once to cross a ripple. Each carries something too wet and precious to trust to one back: a bladder of sap, a plug of resin hot with its own chemistry, a twist of vine-tissue still breathing. They have a smell that is not a smell so much as a sense—your sinuses grow soluble and you know a colony has passed hours later by the way the air sits closer to your face.

If you were to watch a spire grow, you would see their logic in it. First a rib of cured resin, arched like a finger bone; then pulp daubed into it, patient as stalagmite, each daub smoothed with vibrating mandibles that hum a frequency just high enough to set the pulp’s smallest grains into place without loosening the larger. When the rib is strong enough to bear a climb of winged bodies, the masons step aside and the residents come—their breath and warmth and the oils on their fur coaxing the blind vines up out of their idle coil and into architecture.

The vines deserve their own telling. They are not green. They do not know the word. They are blind cotyledons made to stall thirst. Their sap is oil, their fibers are a slow muscle, and their leaves are not leaves but little tongues pressed flat. Heat is speech to them; breath is grammar. The winged have learned to stand near and sing with their chests until the vine stirs a tendril toward the song and wraps it. Over months, over years, the vines are led along resin ribs, parted and rejoined, braided around themselves and around anchors driven into pillars. Where there will be lanterns, the vine is encouraged to make bladders dense with stored fat and trace water. A calendar is kept in the fungal orchards, but another hangs in the way the lanterns swell and dull from umber to plum to mica-gray. A day comes, or its underground equivalent, and a hand—cleaned with grit and breathed warm—goes out into the dim to pluck. A lantern gives like a live thing. It is a live thing. The mouth makes its seal; the fruit empties into the throat in one long undoing; the shell is taken to the crumb bowls to be mended into the earth.

Above, there are others who remember the surface and endure it for all their lives. You hear them most days long before you see their narrow shapes scribbled against the smallest sky—swift-something that comes home full. They have pouches not unlike the nests but sewn to their own bodies: crops widened and oiled, lined with tongues’ cousins, able to hold liquid energy without losing its worth to air. At the edge of the hall’s brighter wells they light and skate and hang, and the city hums them home. What they spill back is light without water: oils coaxed from seeds of a desert whose wind is another animal, spiced with the barest rumor of rain they flew through months ago, hoarded in their tissues as if any droplet could be a vow. Those who tend the vines are waiting with bowls, with frond-hands spread. The oils go where they are asked—into lanterns, into bodies thin with too much care, into the fungus beds to prime a flush.

Fungal lawns here are neither farm nor wild. They are memory held in tissue. They grow as velvet in irregular sheets, are cut into with clean tools whose handles are double-wrapped against sweat, are encouraged to listen to a beaten rhythm so their fruitings gather the way the hall likes them—group births to keep the noise and the waste in one contained hour. In the troughs of old collapses, where even the wind does not quite commit to a direction, there are orchards whose edges are written by decades of cutting. You can put a hand there, if your hand is quiet and the elder of the bed has allowed you, and count generations as scallops under your fingers. The grazers are permitted in on an old agreement. They come like fog in a place that has none, padding without prints under the drift, breath ahead of body to announce their wish. Their skulls are all foreheads, no muzzle: wide panes and sloped planes, ears like nonears. They break nothing they do not mean to. Their mouths are rakes fine enough to pull a sheet-shape of fungus free without tearing the felt beneath. They pay. Everything pays: with dung into the crumb bowls, with their damp onto the waiting plant-surfaces, with the long backs of their tongues pressed flat against fronds until the sap rises there just a little and is licked away. If a grazer calves in the city—and it happens, not often, a kind of grace—everyone comes to angle their faces and receive one more extra moisture than the day planned for.

Under all of this, smaller nations go about their invisible governance. The striders the length of your thumb work the velvet into clean; they are less animals than animated brushes, and their legs are combs. They articulate their smallness into order. Filament hunters pose as wreaths across an hour and then are mouths around a luckless fly, their insides changing shape to fit whatever has come to them; their patience reads like stone’s. Blind fliers do not move so much as inhabit the air. Their faces are entire ears, with mouths set like doors in the drum; their wingbeats are a cloth that runs with the grain of the wind, not against it, so they are effortless even when the great pulse comes and the hall lurches into itself. There are insects here, if insect is a sufficient word. There are peoples of mold that grow fruit which is more animal than not, which waits for touch to shine and then sulks back into its small economy. There are mites that live their whole lives in the moss of the nesting shelves and pass on leg-leg mouth-mouth the humidity they have stored like monks in some other world might pass a flame.

If you ask what the city does, it makes quiet and it keeps water moving. This is religion and industry, law and love. There are guilds, not named but known—the Null-keepers who find and hold the rare centimeters where a heart can rest; the Bafflers who hang the curtains and feel the wind’s harm and pleasure like weather in their ribs; the Vine-singers whose voices shake more than air; the Dust-scribes who score records into bed-edges with tools so dull you cannot say where cutting stops and convincing begins. There are runners whose job is to be between: between grazer and field, between swift and lantern, between newborn and leaf. Their coats are the least glossy; gloss is for display and absorption, and runners have to pass through every climate in a day.

Their language is layered like the hall’s sound: a fundamental hum shared chest to chest when closeness is called for; clicks bowed off palate to carry across curtain and eddy; soft rags of wing-notes to be felt on skin rather than heard. In the long distances, they do not waste breath on words—they shape the wind instead. A hand held at a certain angle and a body put in the right place makes a passage’s tone thicken or thin; an answer comes back from a hundred strides away in the way the roar curls. That is how alleyways are announced, how warnings are sent, how you say I am here to someone who can neither see you nor smell you because even oils are stingy in this air.

They count by pressures and live by calendars notched in sound. The great pulse—when the wind goes foul mad through some far constriction—has its own season, the hall’s year-within-a-year, and there are games and rites built around it. You can see a whole city stiffen and slacken like one body when it comes, materials and muscles remembering not to fight, remembering to give. Moltings are marked with reticence: old curtains taken down and carried to the crumb bowls in a procession whose silence could be a crime if it were not a gift. Children—if that is the right word for engines of absorption—are named not to the ear but to the chest. Names are chords held against your sternum by two elders; you can feel where you begin and the hall begins and neither ends.

Death is a redistribution, but it is not solemn. Fur and membrane become curtains. Bones are joined into instruments that are not played so much as entered into wind so the hall can keep talking to itself. The wet parts do not last. Nothing here does unless it chooses, and even choosing is a slow, collective act. Grief has its own null, its place in the city where sound is not so much absent as very deliberately woven away. You go there with your bundle—the small, the dear—and everyone who loves anything waits beyond the threshold not to watch but to listen until the soft rustle of this last sharing has ended. You come out lighter, and the air is honest about it.

It would be too simple to say they are gentle because they are quiet. They are fierce in the face of waste. They police the law of moisture like a body polices blood: unthinking, utter. To spit is profane. To laugh with your mouth too open in the wrong place is a sin taught out of you with slaps to the face until you can’t even imagine wanting to. Yet there is joy here, not usually like anyone else’s joy, but unmistakable when a swift returns heavy and the first lanterns go down the lanes like a very dim aurora and the hands of a thousand winged open and close in agreement with the idea that the day was survived.

Beyond the nearest hall there are more communities made in the image of the wind-sculpture they inhabit—some thinner, some elaborate as coral. They do not travel like river-folk moving along banks; they turnover like breath. A family in the balcony city across the way is your family if you have slept in the same null for a week. There is war, rarely, and when it comes it is not over land or vine or herd but over silence. A null misplaced is wealth squandered. A curtain cut short is a grievance remembered three generations deep. The punishments are never bodily—they remove your right to certain sounds, or they send you to the brightest place to work a day without curtains and you learn very quickly why a city exists at all.

And yet, there are experiments. New ribs thrown out into air to see if the wind there will consent. New chords tried at the base of pillars to temper a frequency thought untamable. A grazer calf raised by winged hands because its mother died and every lung is a bank. A mason queen allowed to choose a new height for her chambers, the winged waiting to see if a tower will grow there as it has not grown before.

What lives here, then, is not just the listed menagerie—the masons, the winged, the grazers soft-footed as breath, the striders that comb and the hunters that wait—as if a market could be cataloged by stalls. What lives here is an ethic shaped into biology. Everything has learned to be an instrument tuned very fine: to take resource that would like to leave and convince it to stay, to eat the wind until it is no longer a danger, to sleep inside the shout of the world and still have dreams. If you stay long enough, you will join it whether you meant to or not. Your lungs will hold themselves at the end of a note longer than they used to. Your skin will understand why it is better to be ribbed. Your own bones will start to whisper with the long ground-fundamental at night when you are almost asleep. That is how the hall keeps what it has grown, and what it has grown is not a nation but a way: city made of resin and dust and promise, a people made of bodies and their laws, and all of it bent around a simple red rule that goes on older than memory: do not spend what cannot be replaced, and listen before you touch.
