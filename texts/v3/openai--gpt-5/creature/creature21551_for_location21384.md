Things live here that learned to be edges.

The first are the films. They’re older than almost everything else and much faster. They lacquer the south faces in a gloss the color of alder bark and gunmetal, little republics of bacteria that eat light and trace manganese, that turn salt and sunlight into discipline. You know them by touch, that slick hardening under your fingers, and by what they permit: seed to catch, dew to hold its bead, lizards to read the wall in taste and tongue. Where the stone sweats brine in midsummer, there’s a rim of halophiles that wake in a glitter—sugar-grain crystals dusted with life, their feast measured in minutes. The varnish looks like stillness, but it is constant appetite under a glaze.

Lichen writes its own slow alphabet in places where morning won’t burn it off before the day can begin. There are letters that mean “north” and others that mean “shadow at noon.” From a distance it’s all pale punctuation; close in, it’s a slow grammar that tells the stone where to keep a wrinkle. Moss exists as a rumor unless you slide your hand into a slit and find cool wool under fingertip, green you didn’t deserve, drinking from a seam that has been making its own weather for best part of a century. And ferns—feathered clocks that only open where it is narrow and certain—unroll their hours in damp that would kill them a meter away. They know the address of water not as place but as habit, and they show up when the habit is generous.

Plant-made shade is a poor thing, but plants make it anyway. There are shrubs with mirrors for leaves, flinging the noon back where it came from. Their flowers are stingy but precise, a set of invitations written in ultraviolet for slow bees that memorize three coordinates on a whole plateau. Cushions that pretend to be stone right up until a week after rain pry open a hundred tightly held mouths to breathe and let the quick things in. Little wiry grasses lay themselves down to nothing and rise with dew like someone forgiving you. Roots travel vertical, braided up with old calcite, tapping condensation like a harp tapped for luck: here, here, here, and here.

There are the quick nations, too many for one gaze. Lizards run this country the way language runs a book: everywhere, all at once, in sharp little darts of meaning, then gone. They are prismatic when the sun says yes and dull as old bone when it says no; you know their laws because you step aside without planning to when a body the length of your hand pretends the ledge is its alone. They do not drink often, not in the way that feels like a drink. They lap sweat off limestone bands at the appointed hour, polish a trough into pearl in four summers, draw their mothers’ maps of shadow into their hips and ribs. When a fly is an event, you don’t waste your calendar.

Snakes claim their places like spells. There is one long as a thigh—polished cord, hunter of birds, a shadow that does not change with the hour—always waiting not where water is, but where water might be persuaded. The new synthetics taste wrong to it. It coils where plastic mesh meets rock because that is where the ambient moves through, little eddies of insect and drip, mice deciding rashly on a crossing. It sleeps with its heat in the coil and wakes without moving its head.

Insects own green. Any scrap of it, any leaf silvering in laughter at the sun, and they’re there with the patience of metals. Bees that could find their way through a three-dimensional knot with no map and come back with their legs furred in argot. Metallic flies with bodies like commas, pausing emphatically, then gone, then back. Moths poured out of a day of rain like a secret you were keeping from yourself, each of them new and convinced. In the sinkholes, parachute beetles ghost the air above the water for hours, counting something you can’t count. At night there is an hour the size of a hand when all of them are available to be eaten and all of them are saying not tonight.

Mammals, if they’re named at all, are names you get from posture and noise: sullen eye, stone-clinger, grump-heap. The rock hyrax analogs keep their heat in tight packets behind teeth and whistling lips. You’ll see the droppings before you see them and the polish their feet have made onto a climb that isn’t one. They bask like an argument you don’t want to join, and explode up a wall like regret. Their infants are the old shade made cute for a season. A lean fox works the perimeter like a line cook, brisk and funny, knowing every pump’s cough and every panel’s morning frost. It pads its route in a loop of metal and dew, capitalizing on anything that clinks, anything that hums, anything that declares itself in scent. Its ears are a memory of owls you never see. It eats eggs you didn’t find and doesn’t eat the snake. It understands doors.

Birds are vowels, fast and bright and brief. Swifts and swallows cleave the air at noon with enough joy the stone itself seems to laugh and then stop. Their nests glue into ironwork and under old roofs, cups lined with the spit of insects long swallowed. They don’t talk about it. The falcon is a decision that happens in the vertical, a blade making use of the one thing there’s plenty of from above. At the right height it is lazy about genius; at the wrong it is a feathered mistake, and the fox takes a piece of it sometimes without unkindness. Bats leave the cave mouths in a sentence, no line breaks, and return in clauses, each with something in the mouth to give to dark.

Things live under. Blind fish with skin like rice paper stitch themselves between limestone lips where the underground river pinches itself and gossips with air. White isopods with translucent shells consult each other in the way of bureaucrats in a windless hall: antennae, touch, consent. Amphibians the exact shade of surrender wedge themselves under drip-lips and absorb their meals through the damp like a tender theft. Worms write their private names where no one will ever read them. The guano floors are layers of years; fungi open their astonishments at a temperature the cave decided is proper, and roaches collect and redistribute wealth like tax farmers. Their life is full even at a whisper because nobody wastes vocabulary there.

Machines live here as if they were an introduced creature that figured out how to be native. Nets, thin as intention, are slung from posts bolted to stone that gave permission. At dusk the whole array becomes dew’s version of rain; at dawn it beads and lets go. The condensers hold themselves like diatoms scaled up to human need, vanes and ridges tuned to a degree put into the world by difference. They eat night breath and return morning water and keep quiet about it. Cisterns breathe through stone jackets and sleep heavy in heat. There are schedules written into their control boxes like circadian rhythms: when the plateau exhales, we drink; when it inhales, we rest; when the fog that is not sky creeps up from its mouth, we open; when the metal ticks from heat to shade, we count that too.

Cables hum when the air is right, singing the key of the gap they cross. Some are retired like old climbers with their gear in a neat pile; others carry bladders that share the cool of caves with hands far from here. The winch makes its two daily noises like a heart, and whole micro-ecologies have learned them. Lizards time a crossing between the second creak and the settling click. Insects that like oil lick the pleasant smell off the casing’s seams and migrate to the nearest bloom approved by the same scent. Birds use the pylons as grammar in a sky sentence. If any of it stops for too long, foxes and people both stand with their heads on the side and listen, and sometimes the people lay their palms on the panels the way you touch a forehead for fever.

Because yes, humans live here, though the place would get on without them. Not many, and no one who hasn’t made a map of shadow under their skin. They are the net-tenders, the cable-sitters, the keepers of the breath. They sleep where the rock breathes, under roofs the plateau made, in rooms that smell like drip and warm dust and the last tin of something brined. They step in shade corridors of their own bodies across flats that are never flat, and give the open sun an hour of their morning and an hour of their evening and leave the middle for the stone and the birds. Their clothing reads like a negotiation with light: whites that won’t blind, silvers that lie, black bands that hold cold when there is any. They carry water only as much as you can promise to lose without crying. Anything else is a leak the plateau will take.

Their work is listening and grooming. Nets are brushed and stitched with fibers that do not betray the air. Shutters are turned, cables checked, belts replaced by belts that were never belts. Mineral pans get scraped and saved for trade; the brine hour is watched and harvested with tongues on stone same as the lizards, everybody equal for five minutes. They keep charts on calcite of when the fog made itself from rock last month versus last year, of how the bats wrote their line. They are not alone—there are hands in the plains beyond who wait for the bladders and for the news the bladders imply—but the work feels monastic in the good way: humble solutions to accumulation as vocation.

Children, when there are any, learn their own country in reverse: which holes are mouths, which are lungs; which lizards ignore your hand; how to tell the key of a humming stone from a working motor by putting your teeth together and listening with your bone. They memorize their household’s map of heat: how to walk under the reachable flicker of swifts at dusk so they can pretend to be birds, how to move far on very little by adding their narrow body to whatever shadow the world will offer. Their holidays are storms, and the feast is watching water try to be river and fail, then succeed underground. They have words grown fat with repetition for a grike you can pass through shoulder-on, for the glazed smoothness made by many paws taking the same choice, for a place where swallows have being-there so intensely it changes your breath.

In their stories the plateau isn’t a god so much as an uncle with strict house rules. Water is for touching, not for keeping. If you leave a cup, the fox will drink, the heat will lift, the stone will take; and after, your mouth will learn humility again. Each flat has a person—lizard, midge, drift—that does not forgive you for standing too long; pay rent by moving or by offering your blue shadow. If something hums at noon, it is stone; don’t go near it with metal in your hand because you’ll forget what you were doing and come back burned. Deep verticals keep secrets because secrets leak down and make stalactite in the time of your children’s children. Heat is not a temper, it is a statute, and no one wins an argument with law.

Their dead don’t stay much. Ash is a poor idea here; wind makes too free with it. So they go into crevices, into dry wells, into the mouths of caves with an understanding—take this bone and use it as you use everything. In a hundred seasons the varnish will sign its name on that too. You can find a tooth framed in manganese if you are looking poorly and then you will think about teeth for longer than comfort. The hyrax will squat near, bored, till a vulture comes down on a line of air that makes no noise.

There are other residents you can’t name. The fog that breeds from inside the stone takes a tour at three a.m. like an aunt who checks you’re warm enough and counts your pumps. The daily breath itself—warm out, cool in—is a thing with habits, a big animal with a lair as wide as the plateau. The grikes that play flutes on certain winds are musical neighbors; on a good night you can hear them teaching each other new chords, and you will wake sure that architecture is a way of singing yourself into cooling. The echo lives below and is thriftier than you. It saves sound until it can’t, and then lets it fall in a cascade of regret that has been known to knock sensible people into prayer.

Predation is not personality here; it’s housekeeping. Snake cleans mice off mesh. Falcon takes swifts at the only place that will let it, then pays in feathers. Fox tidies moths from the pump-shed light and collects straps torn loose by a midnight gust for a den that needs a door. People weed shade you can trip on and seal the silver of a panel where birds are quiz-making. Machine eyebrows collect dew for everyone, and lizards write paths in polish that hands can follow. There is no charity, but there is also no meanness you can survive indulging.

Season writes a myth on your skin you can’t stop reading. High summer empties noon the way a furnace empties water, and almost nothing moves then without a prayer or a debt. But after a storm that arrives on heat alone, with no escort from sky except the drum on rock, miracles small as thumbnails and loud as swarms take their innings. Seeds that travelled as if that had always been the plan break their silence. You can step on a place and see your dry foot print open, not from you but from the last thunderhead, and for an hour be tricked into thinking this is generous country. It is, but not to you in particular.

What lives here, then? Films, alphabets, cups of green, teeth, belts that are not belts, cables that learn a chord. Things with spines but no patience; things with patience but no eyes. Foxes that know the timing of relays. Bats that spell dusk and eat the spacers between days. Snakes that don’t blink and never forget where the drip lands. People who keep the breath and take their water as touch and refusal both. Machines that farm vapor and stand respectfully in their own shade. The plateau itself, of course—the oldest resident, organized into buttress and fin and flute, patient enough to grow its own weather, good enough at keeping to itself to seem like absence when you look too quickly. If you stay, and stay well, it lets you count yourself among its living: a body that has learned the trick of moving like shadow, a mouth that knows when to close and listen for the pump’s second click.
