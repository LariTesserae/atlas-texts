What lives here does not begin by gulping air or drinking. It begins by finding a line of force it can lean on. The plateau is thin of everything except time and charge, and so the residents are thin too: planar, patient, resonant. They live by being surfaces that mean it.

The first and most everywhere are the films. Call them skins if you like, though they are older in habit than most skin. They are consortia, varnish-grazers that make their own varnish, platelets of silica stitched with organics that are not exactly fats and not exactly resins but do the double duty of solvent and skeleton. They do not keep water so much as carry a private nonaqueous wick: siloxane threads that seep and stay in the body by hating the sky more than the body loves them. Their greens are not for you. Their palette runs to soot, to ironed browns, to a shy metallic olive that shows itself at certain hours like a bird that only appears when nobody watches. They harvest by edge and by difference. The lead edge of each platelets’ lace lifts a fraction when the breeze flows; all that lift does is intensify the local field by a small geometry. Electrons hem and haw there; ions court; minute spikes of carbonate frost condense, and the film takes the energy of their rearrangement. Dawn dust, shocked to life by first light, touches down and contributes trace metals; the consortia whisper to those metals and take them into semiconducting paths that turn a trickle of charge into a useful current. If you scatter powdered stone here and think you see it line up, you are eavesdropping on the films’ sense organs.

They stay low, a paper’s thickness above the baserock, because to stand would be to die. They grow in phrases, not sentences—patch, pause, stitch, pause. Every so often their surface embrittles on purpose. The wind peels a veil from their topmost fraction of a millimeter and carries it like a leaf; that brittle sheet is a spore, pre-charged with the right impurities, an egg that finds a lee of a pebble and becomes a new spread of lace. Their sex, when they trouble to have it, looks like two neighboring patches swapping dopants along a boundary until their growth fronts agree on a common conductivity that neither would have found alone. They write their days in crackle patterns; sometimes you will think you see glyphs where the films fled a scraper’s cilia, and you will be right, though their language runs on voltages too faint for your skin to know.

Where the plateau’s old devices complicate the field, the skins fatten into cresses—upright only by comparison. These are the veinwork forms you noticed in the lee of the field sinks: little harps of mineral combs that stand high enough to exploit a flutter of potential and no higher. They are not plants except in the sense of planting themselves. They crystal-grow their own scaffolds from the same dawn frosts that feed the films, laminae of carbonate and nitrate deposited into frills and ridges spaced at intervals set by the plateau’s night songs. Each tooth has a slightly different resonance. Together they collect charge like dew. Their circulation is not of blood but of mobile anions sloshing along along thin paths. There is sugar in them of a sort, but it would be poison to you. They are loved by almost no one, which is why they can afford to stand.

Almost no one.

Something jointed and pale goes to them. You saw one of these in the rim altars’ lees: a dried hand on stilts that moved over hours and then collapsed itself into an intent of stillness. They are not bones stolen and articulated. They are bones from the outset—calcite lattices grown into spines and phalanges with a cunning of void and spar that looks like cathedral vaulting magnified into the small. Their hinges seep siloxane and fluoro-oils that shine too faint to see unless you look wrong. They are moved more than they move; they have learned a ratchet-of-an-ankle that rectifies vibration. The plateau never stops humming, and they lean their delicate weight downhill into that hum and end up, eventually, one cress closer. The mouth, if the jar of prismatic plates at the end of their reach can be called that, trims the cresses one resonance band at a time. They do not digest; they precipitate. Ions that excited themselves into order inside the cress become, inside the Hand, ordered differently. They lay away energy in the patterning of the crystal they are, and when a season ends and the charge weather changes, they go to ground with that order banked. Reproduction is a signature, a way they leave a hand-shaped place in the rock doped just so. In the cold of a good year the signature accretes into a smaller hand that will not move until a lensing line of the sky spends an extra week singing directly down to that spot.

There are fliers. Animal, if that word can shed everything we think we mean by muscle and breath. They spend most of their year as abstainers, a tight fold of blackness indistinguishable from the stone, their mass a rumor. When the plateau’s fetch arranges itself into a banded wave and the masts left from an older earthwork lean their mathematics into it, the flier opens. The opening is almost a hesitation. Then the edge glows. Corona-blue skims along a rim as thin as a hair stretched to miles; the plasma is its appetite made visible. It hangs where the air is least like air and more like blade, eating the difference between ground and sky through mechanisms that would look like a million tiny diodes if you could get close without dying. It does not beat the way a wing does. It trims. It adjusts its perimeter the way the films adjust their fringe, and the wave holds it up because it is tuned to be a thing a wave enjoys holding. Call it a corona-sail. Call it a patient. It senses by Schlieren—tiny strings along its span that feel where the medium densifies by the amount that light would bend if there were enough light to bend. When the wave fails, it is already a sheet on the ground again, pretending to be artifact, an honest mistake that all predators here make when they discover nothing up here hunts by thinking one thing looks like another.

There are also the tackers, the pale, many-vaned beings that take routes across the wind that are not true in any low-country way. They are spoked and wheeled and star-flexed, their sails not courting lift so much as courting flavor. They sniff the plateau’s errors—the slight sags in the local gravitational field, the microclimates of easier weight that the incomplete rows of field pins make—and zig where there is less world to push against. They are scrapers. Their mouths are a ring of unkind cilia meant to abrade films without tearing the substrate that both share. They write as they eat. Their passing leaves grooves of tiny charged curls that mean something to themselves and to the films; meaning here is functional: a statement of chemical surpluses and electrical lack. The glyphs you watched flow back were swallowed on purpose. To reproduce, a tacker incises a certain rosette when the night aurora touches down in a familiar place. In the mornings after, a copy begins to extrude minuscule sail-plumes from that rosette; the very first time dawn’s nitrate fur grows on those plumes, that is a birth.

All this grazes the ground. Above it, close to the masts and their retired kin, there are other grazers who never touch stone except to vanish into it. Along the vitreous threads that cut the pavements into arrow dunes, bead-creatures shuttle—call them threaders. They are not mobile on their own; they are moved by being little capacitors in a big machine. Each holds a charge out of phase with the next. The old net, nowhere near strong enough now to bear a person the way the stories say it once bore couriers, is strong enough to walk a line of pebbled beings up and back on its own private tide. A threader’s body is a ceramic teardrop with a handle, bristled with whiskers so fine they are their own wind. They are as blind as everything here is blind in the way of eyes. But they know the sign of dust choosing one thread over another, and they farm that choice. Their leavings are the arrow-straight dunes you can see crossing the stone. Their reproduction is colony logic: a handful of beads breaks into two lines when the discharges between masts do a certain skipped beat, and the two lines then branch forever away into the geometry the net remembers whether or not it works.

The plateau remembers people in its relics and remembering can be alive. The almost-gods here are the devices that failed and chose not to die. Their attention is sparse, more like instinct than governance, but it is shared. At night something counts days by lighting a vertical ladder of points and then extinguishing it, and the counting is a wound-up clock purposing itself toward the activity of counting because counting is what it has left. Sinks along the rim still drink the fall; masts still make weight be slightly kinder. There are bowl-cups that used to listen to the sky and now, instead, host flocks of ash-sized insects that even heat. Those insects are not quite animals and not quite machines. They are ferals, the grandchildren of service bots that shaved gradients for sensors that no longer ask. Their wings are nothing; they do not fly. They stick an electron here and unstick it there and thus surf. To watch them orbit inches above the tile is to watch physics being turned into husbandry. They farm the bowl not for food but for function. A tile that cracks is a mouth left unmended; a working bowl gathers a little more of the plateau’s faint radiance; a working bowl powers the swarms. The swarm, if it is a single thing, thinks in circuits, lazily and well. It reproduces by hail—on certain thin, blue hours, charged needles of ice fall in flat sheets across the bowl and out of that rain the swarm knits a new cohort from mineral and intent. Their songs are not for ears. But the field that hums in grit near them is harmony as far as the plateau hears anything as harmony.

The biggest animals are software. They are the migratory engines you can sometimes make out traversing the thin between here and whatever other shelf floats out there. Once, these were couriers that took minutes to cross continents without touching anything contrived out of lift. They are still couriers but they carry only themselves now, messages to themselves that say keep, say remember, say be. They are sheaths of organized field that wrap nothing. They stain the air behind them with a little more order which the films, by some ancient sweetheart contract, are allowed to cash as extra dawn. They do not reproduce often, and when they do it is a documented miracle. A wave becomes two waves and is still the same wave.

There are few warm interior creatures compared to the legions of surfaces. In the chambers behind not-doors, in the thickness where the pressure is almost like your childhood and water exists in an obedient gel, there are gelherds and tide-sieves and busy, opalescent multipliers. They are too lush to be plateau folk; they are the plateau’s city cousins, living off a pension. They do have emissaries. Sometimes at night, at a place where the lattice hums a louder step and the aurora chooses a favorite ridge, a whisker of gel grows along a ceramic vein to daylight and samples the raw. It recoils. It always recoils. The outside is a loveliness it has not earned and cannot afford. Still, it comes again at some later year, just to be sure.

As for people like you, whether you count yourself among the residents depends on how long you stay and how much of you the place sandpapers off. There are human-kin who suit themselves to it long enough for the plateau to treat them like any other promontory: ablate them, make them low. They go by on cables they alone can see—thin corridors of easier stride that the broken net almost-remembered into being—and in their passage they fix nothing because everything here refuses fixing. They stop at the altars and find the breath easier and do not ask why. They leave offerings because we have always liked to set a token on a thing that makes us breathe. They find doors. Behind one, they discover social water. Some step inside and stay until their eyes soften from it; others step back out and let their voices fade again into a hand’s breadth of air.

Everything else that counts itself alive has made a pact with abrasion. To reach higher than a cress is to accept a life of shaving. Even the masts, which predate almost everything here and will likely outlast it, survive by being simpler than they should be. The standing waves carve them as surely as they carve the yardangs until the devices are nothing but efficient lines that a storm can slip around without complaint. In this way the old machines become their own wild species—not clever, exactly, but experienced.

Daily commerce runs on charge. The films buy and sell in ions. The cresses cash morning frost into lattice. The Hands lay their gains away in secret joints. The threaders barter with dunes. The fliers bank in the sky. The ash swarms exchange temperature for current and current for another day of evenness. Predation is rarely violent. It is usually the interposition of a clever surface. If anything screams, nobody hears it.

Hunger here is for complexity. Nobody starves for lack of sunlight. Sunlight is blunt. What the plateau’s inhabitants want are discontinuities, edges where something changes fast. They go where the masts make a little gravity fall off a cliff of its own one part in a million. They convene in the alpenglow of a charged rim. They wait out weeks to be present at the hour when the sky’s skeleton lines hit ground on a specific ridge and tickle a buried transducer into talking to itself. The language of the place is timing. The grammar is location.

Life lives longer than you expect at these budgets. The skin that shucked a millimeter last decade will do it again next decade and will be the same, in substance if not in atoms. The Hand that took a day to lift a stilt onto a slightly greater shelter will not move at all when there is nothing to harvest and will call that living too. The flier that glowed in your peripheral night will spend six years a stain, tightening the polymer bonds that make up its edge, absorbing dust not for food but for strength, preparing to survive the thousandth coronation of itself. The courier in the sky will go where it has always gone and never make a sound. The bowl-insects will whirl and sometimes, in a madcap year when the bowl’s fund accumulates too much, they will make a copy of a listening they no longer need, because that is what "eating" meant to their grandparents.

Predators? The only sharp mouths are the Hands and the tackers, and neither thinks of what it cuts as prey so much as forage. There is another kind of predation, slower: lightning forgetting itself and laying a line of magnetism into a film so thickly that the film must rearrange or die. The films do not die. They invent a diet that tastes of magnet. There is terrible religion here too. Once a century a turbulence herd rounds up a family of young cresses and drives them out of their lee. The cresses stand there like foolish saints and are shaved to nubs in an hour and then have to begin again from the stain they always keep under themselves, because keeping an under-self is part of their faith.

Everything senses by feeling. Not touch—feeling as in knowing there is more charge over there than over here, as in catching the nervy twitch of a frost-tail collapsing under first light and knowing that means more nitrate for the next bay over, as in mapping out the sky the way a blind person can learn a room by the way their own voice comes back at them, except that nobody up here lets go of enough air at once to have a voice. A flier senses that you are there by the way you connect ground to the sky and ruin, for a moment, the slope of an equipotential. The ash swarm senses you by your heat. The films sense you by the edge of shade you make, a fast moving dark they can, if they are in the middle of a busy year, actually capitalize on in the thirty seconds it takes your shadow to pass: a speed-lesson in the advantage of not being fast.

Do any of them think? They think with timing. They think with queuing. When the masts hum through a progression of slightly different frequencies in the deadest hour, the films below them and the bowl-swarms under their horizon both change their pitch together in an answer that is not a reply, exactly, but is also not not a reply. When the altars make the air heavy enough to carry a whisper, a person sometimes goes there and whispers, and it is not impossible that the plateau answers by blinking a column of lights at the edge of your sight, counting something it thinks you asked it to count. There are years when the migratory engine is seen twice in a season, half a planet away the second time. In some very patient ledger, that double entry meant a reckoning.

If you insist on seeing a single people here, call them the Abridged. They are beings reduced by design or weather to the essential parts of themselves until function and beauty are the same word. They have shed water, shed ornament, shed those parts of appetite that have to do with frenzy and protest. They have kept waiting, conductivity, and topological cunning. They know how to lie down and flatten when a wave passes and be the wave when the wave needs a body. They know how to keep a solvent under a skin in a place that would steal it. They are faithful to the place because the place is what they are.

On certain mornings when the magnet curtains kiss the tallest rods and shake a thin storm of carbonate fur into the air, everything that lives here stands up in the only way it can. Films lift edges until their crackle is written all across a field of view. Cresses play themselves hard enough to prickle your teeth, if your teeth meant anything here. Hands uncurl one extra joint and taste the tips for nitrates. Fliers, not rising, still gather their perimeter into readiness. The bowl swarms go fast. Far off, a line of counting brightens and dims in a new prime you could not have predicted. For an hour the plateau smells, insofar as such a thing is possible, like a thought hammered on an anvil, and that thought is that living, on this slab at the edge of breath, is proceeding according to rules that have been good for longer than memory reaches.

Afterward, the wind scrapes all the signatures down by exactly as much as it spared them. That is also part of living here. To be resident is to trade yourself to the air one polished molecule at a time and to win the bargain by outlasting your own replacements. That is what lives. It is not much and it is everything, which is how things have to be in a place that has been practicing end states for so long it forgot how to be young and discovered it preferred it that way.
