More than one thing lives here, and none of them insist on being first.

The place itself is alive in the way a tree is alive when you stop calling it wood. It has a pulse—turbines that think in revolutions you could count on your fingers if you had hours to spend on each finger—and it has a skin that sweats and heals. Where a weld split once, the ferrofita sealed it with green fur and breathed the iron back into coherence. Where a door’s gasket rotted, sponge and mineral froth grew to meet the gap, a white ruff that hummed slightly where water nuzzled through. The hall is a creature that never needed a spine. It remembers its jobs the way a body remembers walking, with muscles that keep moving long after your attention is elsewhere. When you put your palm on a column, the warmth under that algal suede is not just geology; it is the slow metabolism of a room metabolizing being a room.

The green country that covers everything is alive, too, and not as passive as you think when you say “plant.” It is a confederation that has colonized metal the way cities colonize riverbanks. Rope-plants throw hairy blooms into eddies to catch the drift of mineral-rich silt; colonials of bead-weed stitch seams shut against a harsh turn of current; filamentous banners loft themselves under warm lamps and fold up in the shadow between them, like laundry learning wind; ferrofita reach their velvet tongues into iron and drink, leaving arabesques on pipes you could read like weather maps if you were patient. When the heat of a lamp rises or falls a degree, gardens shift their neighborhoods by microns, then millimeters, then inches, so slowly that by the time you call it change, it has already finished arranging the chairs. They talk to one another through the tiny constellations you see along certain wires—the freckles that contract when you whisper—slow, patient Morse under your hand. If you ask them for bare metal, if you stroke them with a polite blade and hum the room’s note back into itself, they let go in umber veils and wait at the edge to be called again.

Between this animal architecture and this plant city move the few deliberate bodies that could be called people, if you broaden “person” enough to include anything that can be said to keep a promise.

There is the caretaker you can imagine from the cut vines and the golden clips: a tender whose face is as much schedule as skin. They are not often seen because there is no spectacle to what they do. Their lungs are a compromise with the water; their breath is smaller than yours because they have learned not to hurry the room with the wrong kind of bubbles. When you glimpse them, they are a pale figure weighted where the world would make them drift, shoulders squared under a harness quilted in little brass scales the algae won’t colonize. Their tools are not original to any store: a long-handled knife whose blade is not blade anymore but lace, honed along its remaining filigree by a thousand asking touches; a set of spanners softened to ovals by lime; a spool of cord that is also vine, taught to loop and lie docile until tied. They wear a mask when they need to, but as often they prefer the little rebreather that sits where a brooch would sit and trades carbon slowly for oxygen won from the room’s own gardens. They keep shells in a jar the height of their head; each one is punctured not for beauty but for passage, strung when a job is done, hung when a job is still speaking. Money, maybe, but more like a rosary of tasks.

They do not talk to you; they are talking all the time to the room. You see it in the way they enter a current and let it take their hips, in the way the bioluminescent freckles along a conduit draw tight and then relax as they pass. They move lamps. Not often, not in ways that would look like design to an impatient eye; but a lamp shifted the breadth of a hand is enough to ask a colony to move its kitchen, enough to coax oxygen to a darkened corner where a valve will tire in a month’s time and would like someone to be there with a key when it does. They trim, not to tidy so much as to make clearings for hands; hands, here, means anyone’s—human, eel, machine-fish. They turn handwheels in quarter-turns learned from a set of teachings that never became a book. When they part a chain curtain to enter a maintenance cove, the silt lifts and resettles as if embarrassed to have shown them its underskirts. They leave prints that last days, and nothing in their posture ever says they want those days back.

Whatever learned them their craft, it was not a school. It was the kind of adoption that happens between a place and a person when the stair you take becomes the stair you maintain, when your day bends at the same arcs as the pipes under your hands. If they dream of a surface, it is filtered through glazing brick, a rumor of paler green. If they have a name, it is something like “Keep,” a syllable that looks like the lift of a column when you write it beside a sketch of the hall.

Smaller assistants live nearer to the bolts and the lamps: the pale caretaker fish, if fish is what they are. They are the size of a cat because that is the size of the crevices that were built for hands. Their backs are covered in little plates stamped like coins—scale-stamped brass that is not brass, not anymore, but an alloy that does not sicken in this sweet water. On each plate, a shallow number, a history of replacements, but not the same number on each side. Their bellies are soft, wrinkled with the kind of skin that is more organ than covering. Where a fish would have a mouth, they have a small hand of whiskers, five or seven, with velveted pin-jaws at each tip that can take hold of a screw without stripping it, that can feel a thread the way a tongue measures the difference between seed and sand. They see light as temperature and pressure as a language. They ride lamplight as if it were a slope.

You hear them before you register their bodies: a respectful click of feet on grille, the faint brush of whiskers on glass. When a lamp goes with that little click that is also a sigh, one arrives from whatever market of currents it had been attending, noses the housing, counts the fasteners with gentle taps, and releases the spare by degrees, slow enough not to startle the algae in their sleep. Its lateral line thrums with the 50-hertz hum of the old dynamos; it knows the difference between a healthy sound and a cancerous one the way you know the difference between your own breath and a stranger’s. After it has made a change, it lingers, angled like a comma in the paragraph of the current, until the freckles on the adjacent wire relax to their usual spatter.

They breed in the digesters—not inside, but in the warm iso-edges where bacteria throw off enough light to feel like a cove at sunset. They lay bead-bright ova on the undersides of valves, and when the young hatch, they eat foam and rust and the softest new growth of ferrofita until their plate-backs knit. They are not pets, though the human tender could touch one if they wished; to touch is to ask a favor, and you feel how such economies could be abused. They have what you would, in a bird, call a route. At an hour the hall would call a note, each follows the same pipe, rubs its side along the same run of mineral like a prayer wheel, stops at a jar to sort shells by hole-pattern into arrangements that mean each to each what stations mean to a commuter: valve-should-be-here; do-not-strip-this-one; leave-bare; begin-again.

Other animals have a place less by decision than by fit, and they live here as punctuation. The eel is a thread of memory, a pale ribbon that knows the beadings of a pipe the way a blind hand knows keys on a piano. Its eyes are there because eyes come on the template of life, not because seeing is useful. You could time your own visits by its passing: of a morning it will turn its head into a current that comes from the clarifier; of an evening it will find where the water stacks and press its jaw into that brief false gravity, resting in work the way a bell rests in its arch.

The little oil-colored snails are scribes. Their mouths are raspers and their raspings leave tracks that catch silt or fail to, and in that silty script the day’s moods are recorded: a stumble where a larger creature’s fin brushed them out of their lane; a flourish where the current curled; a long blank where a gauge stuck and made the hall hold its breath. The shrimp appear only as a subtraction of water, a rectangular ghost that resolves into shell and joint and goes again, impossible and nearly not there, but they are there, and their job is to find edges and make sure edges remain edges.

No larger fish come. They would have nothing to be large for. The terms here are negotiated slowly between leaf and bacterium, and in the middle of the contract sits the hall, warm and pleased to be useful. Predation happens, of course, but it happens politely, when the eel takes a shrimp, or when a snail dies and is unbuilt in place, and its calcium is turned into the lacy chew-marks in the tender’s knife.

There are rumors of other tenders in other rooms, like galaxies repeating down a spiral, each with their own slow dialect of hum and glow. Sometimes a scrap floats in—half a label that says INTA and will never be restored past guesswork; a tuft of moss that belongs to a different temperature—and the room reads it like news. When something outside changes, the first to know are the gauges: their needles drift by degrees you could write a poem about, an ode to asymptote. Then the lamps know, their weather shifting, their little auroras flaring and dampening. Then the plants rearrange kitchens again, and the caretaker lets that be a day’s work and calls it done by tying a vine away from a wheel, a gesture with the grace of punctuation at the end of a long sentence.

If there is a god in this cathedral, it is the rule that water remembers shapes. Everything that lives here worships it without worshiping, by compliance. The human tender sleeps in a curtained cell when they sleep, their heartbeat small to keep from tangling the hum. The brassback fish bring them a nut they cannot grip without hands when something is too tight for whiskers alone. The ferrofita loosen and retake the iron as asked. The eel rubs; the snails write; the shrimp erase; the hall breathes.

So who lives here? The answer is layered like the room. The city-as-idea, scaled down until it wears algae and grows hayfields of moss on its filters, lives here. The plants, with their mandarinate attentions to temperature and angle and adjacency, live here. The small machines whose front halves are creatures and whose back halves are armor live here, and they are old enough to be breeding true. A person lives here—one, or a few across years—whose face is less important than their patience. And above all, what lives here is the agreement they all keep: that nothing is urgent, that every line will curve in time, that light belongs to its water, that currents are the streets, and that emptiness is not a virtue but a mistake the hall has corrected. If you hang and let yourself be held, you can feel that life take you in, slow your intentions, soften your edges, and whisper its last gentle boast through the freckled wires: We are full. We are working. We have all the time we need.
