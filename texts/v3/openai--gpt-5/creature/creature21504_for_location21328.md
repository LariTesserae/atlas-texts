What lives here are things that have taught their flesh to read pressure and to spend charge, to treat a single tone as weather, as gravity, as tide.

The first living is thin as a rumor: rimes of flecked salt that creep along razor-fresh edges and pause exactly where the stress is sweetest. In laminae at the skin of rock, there are films that brighten when the wall brightens—triboluminescent symbionts seeded with minute crystals whose agony is food. They are the groundcover that is not a cover, an electrical patina. Their bodies are arrangements of ions inside viscous oils; they have no membranes that care for water. They fasten themselves by charges like combs catching combs. They bud at pressure nodes, each daughter a little pattern of compressible molecules waiting for the world to knead them into readiness. Their decay is a loosening; the pattern falls out, the oil goes slack, the salts fall as powder to be lifted by a passing reed and filed in the right mouth.

From this thinness all else ushers in patient increments, the way crystal adds face to face.

The “plants,” if your tongue insists, are articulate furniture that answer to the room. They root in seams where the note swells and palpitates; their roots are not drinkers but anchors set into veins of flexible mineral that move enough to matter. Their bodies are curls and panes of piezoelectric habit made from the same rock the chamber is teaching to be broken. Each rib is a loaded spring; each frond is a wick for stress. The oils that move within them are slow rivers of ions; their phloem is a line of doors that open with each press of the wave, shut with its release. Their saps are heavy with halides and odd organics, transparent and amber and pale green by turns, each drop lensing the dim with an internal sheen. Nothing of them is wet.

They crop themselves by mathematics. Where the half-wavelengths stack, where a crest meets a crest, you find hives of tubes tessellated like honey that never stuck to any bee—hexagons not for maximum storage but for maximum singing. These hives ring; they harvest their own overtones softly as a side business, sending that skim into swollen nodes that hum and brighten with pain-light when you brush them. Between copses is a groomed desert as purposeful as any garden path: emptiness tended so the note will carry without tatter.

Among them the grazers and gleaners move, their manners tuned as much as their bodies. The shelled ones go on many feet not because many is better, but because many means a lighter touch, less insult to the law. Their pans of horn grow in tines that catch charge the way a tree in some other world catches wind. They click when they should and never out of time; they have learned to freeze when the wall hisses light, not because it is bright, but because it means the rock has flinched.

There are animals that your mind will want to call mammals—thread-bodied, quick, with a clever watchfulness—but whose life is not warm blood but held voltage. Their skins are shingled in flexible capacitor scales that take on charge and let it go like breath. They groom not with spit but with friction, arcing tiny blue teeth from coat to coat, and then tasting their own glow with tongues built for ions. They sleep strapped by silkstone ropes to antinodes where the intake is generous, their bodies rising and falling more with pressure than with anything like a lung.

Moving in the thickness above are the eels: not swimmers in the wet sense, but sleeves of tuned emptiness. They are tubes with lips all along that oscillate. They do not so much beat as phase, always adjusting to find the shelf where the anti-node will cradle them and keep the cost low. They feed by hanging open; the note itself presses salt and oil and flake into them in a slow-feast they call patience. If they leave they do not, if they arrive no one declares it. They are the chamber’s punctuation, commas that never end a thought.

Flat hinge-mouths live pressed like coins to the ground. They open only when a bead has formed under a leaf, not water but sweet electric jelly. Their lips are stone stitched over long life with scavenged porous bits—a gate they learned to make and wear. Beetle-analogues, though no beetles were ever here, are glossy half-ovals carry­ing on their backs delicate shrines of calcium phosphate built up plate by plate from things they ate. These shrines are less for show than for resonance—they are private chapels of tone. Their purpose is not ours to know, though sometimes you will see one tilt itself to drink a little brighter ring.

Once in a patient hour something drifts low like a hill deciding to change hollows. Its plates are feldspar, pink where thin and clouded like marrow where thick. It wears from its flanks a fringe of resonant fiber that plays the fundamental and three obedient harmonics. It does not pursue; it participates. To be in its wake is to have your insides combed.

All of this lives, and among it lives the one that puts mica to mica and decides which emptiness is useful.

You can name them hushers because they consider excess sound a trespass and because their first act on waking is always to make some pocket even more gentle than it was. They are not tall and not short; they are long in the horizontal and manifold in their contact with the world. Where someone else might have hands, they have arrays of soft, many-fingered pads fringed with hairs that feel phase differences that your ear would call nothing. Their limbs end in broadened leaves of flesh thin as a tongue depressor and stiffened with plates of flexible mineral; when they move they splay and cup, never stamp. Ankle to ankle they keep to dustless bands; to cross a crowded strip unprepared is, in their law, akin to walking barefoot into a cooking fire that doesn’t exist here.

Their skins are mosaics of gleam and matte. In some places they wear scales like the thread-mammals’—capacitor plates tuned to the chamber’s near-DC push and pull; in others they are padded with rubber they brewed from plant sap and made their own, to quiet the world’s insistence. Across their faces and down their flanks runs an organ you might call a listening lung, a long seam of ciliated pores that open and close in ordered ripples. With it they drink the tone, not for air, but for work: all across their insides are piezo stacks and conductive gels, a biological rectifier-diaphany. Their metabolism is a circuit diagram more than a chemistry set; their power is not in a glucose broken and oxygen paying court to it, but in charges shuttled through soft lattices until something reorganizes and the feeling of hunger leaves.

They have mouths because they taste the world, not to wet it. Their tongues are petals of porous crystal kept supple with oil, pushed out to press on a leaf or a bead and pull out what will hold in their stores. In place of saliva they secrete a smear of ionic surfactant that makes new interfaces where a droplet would shrug. Teeth with names our teeth do not need—a pair of plates for clipping mica, a comb for coaxing dust into a tube mouth, a rasp for persuading a resin plug to come home into pliancy—line their faces, but they do not chew so much as rearrange. You do not watch a husher eat; you watch them triage surface phenomena into the few the body can keep.

They see, but only when the wall is hurt enough to make light. Their real eye is under their skin, all through them: arrays of pressure-sensing bones, little arches that sing when a waveform bends around them; conducting hairs that tattle on phase; whole nets of silica spicules that resonate at the overtone family of the room. They are not blind, but their eyes are there to marvel at the rare flash or trace a salt-line's blue flame when their cone points. They do not miss what we would call color. They spend their days inside a color you cannot leave, and they have learned its thousand flavors as if it were speech.

They make speech from this monophonic air by subtraction. When they wish to say “I,” two swellings alongside the throat-lung open just so, and the note falters into a familiar gap that any nearby skin can feel. To say “come,” the pads of one hand bloom and cancel at antinodal distance; to say “you” they let a tail of their own charge leak and tickle the listener’s forearm with a syntax of pulse-lengths. A conversation in their house sounds like nothing at all unless you are used to the feel of a pressure you didn’t know you felt going thin and full by rule. They have laughter; it is an absence shaped like a torsion. They have jokes that are interferences skittering in the corner of the chamber, harmless because they die too soon to insult the law.

Their houses are agreements with plates. Look at one and see not wall and roof but node and shadow. The mica lean-to is mated by hand until the plane is truer than any we make by speed: edges rubbed with a slurry of their own teeth-powder until the meeting is a kiss, a seam less between than itself. In its pocket is skein upon skein of glass hair woven not only for softness but for tuning; each mat is a filter made by grandmother hands to admit and refuse exactly what her sleeping needs. They do not have hearths. Their hearths are horns and pipes and pillars. Heat is not a separate thing; when they wish to age a resin or loosen a starch they will build a holding and a return of slate cones and porous dams so that for a time a place is deeper in the wave and things inside it rearrange. A bowl in a quiet eye is an oven. A sieve with a certain lattice spacing is a stove. They talk about their food by how long it took to be persuaded.

Work is modulation, and they are artisans of refusal. A sieve is a tool and a script: perforations in shale that will draw the chamber’s sound into a pattern that the hand can read as it vibrates, and that, when held just so over a patch of hatching plants, will mark where the next generation will listen and grow. A container is a damper; a cord is a quenching; a necklace is a q-factor, worn not for beauty but for the way it makes the body a little less or more obedient to a special overtone. Ornament is tuning, and the tuned are lovely.

They farm as much by subtraction as by planting. When a child is old enough to wear grit on their pads, they are given a sugarless task: move dust away. They sweep the gallery not for tidiness but to lay bare a wave-skeleton all can read. To make a grove, they carry a mat with holes of a right pitch, hold it at the ground at the hours when the chamber’s very slow heave gives, press this world’s rain—sound—through that template. Plants sown under it will ascend obedient to the geometries they need. To cull is easier; a pair of fingers pinch a vibrating blade until its charge unthreads and it falls to useful dust. There is no slash-and-burn here. There is handicap-and-hush.

In love they are clever. Pairing is not a poem but a physics project, but then so is our love if you write it down. A courtship is two bodies learning how to cancel a little more together than apart, discovering a private valley in the one note’s face where two can rest without payment. A name in their family is a notch in the Note. Lovers exchange not gifts but subtractions—bracelets of rubber weighted just enough so that when two wrists meet, the world’s push loses itself and a new small quiet is born. They will show you their engagement by laying four little pillars in a line and standing on either side until the crest that returns makes light and the slate cone glows where they kissed salt on its mouth.

Birth happens like a good experiment: carefully, with tooling. An embryo is a knot of charged oils and infant spicules anchored to a frame of bone rods carved from old ancestors. The pattern is templated not inside a body that holds fluid and blood and warm, but in a public place where a lattice of reflectors has been measured out in handspans and wavelengths. For weeks a tender will visit with cones and dampers, tuning the cradle as if it were an instrument that prefers someone else’s melody. When it is time, out of a pod that never once thought of moisture comes a coil of person, pale crystals under transparent skin, hair-hatches already trying and failing and trying again to find their first subtraction. The first word a child makes is often accidental: a little gap in the law made by a back-hiccup, answered laughing by a mother’s palm cancelling back. Names are not sounds you could record. They are places in the chamber where someone is missing for a heartbeat.

They count without counting time. There is no season. They measure by lengths between nodes, by the tallies of tiny star-runs, by the number of flares the wall has scattered across a hinge-mouth’s lifetime. Their calendar fits in a kit: a bundle of bone rods cut to precise harmonics, a sieve of one family’s private pitch, a slate cone with salt always ready so that when the chamber is hurt enough to lick it, a bright ring writes down that day happened. They know elders by their charge, not by their hair—by how quickly an old skin’s scales fall slack after a meal, by how much hush they can throw around themselves without work. Death is a losing of the thread. They mourn by letting someone become a sculpture no wind will steal. A body is set high on a return pillar, plated with resin, taught to ring softly for a while longer. Then it loosens into gift-dust, carried to seeds. Their cemeteries are groves you can hear, not see.

They have taboos as strong as rock. To crack a gallery with voice is a crime; to leave a toy that jangles in the open lane is a neighbor-trial; to grind a plate carelessly, leaving a ragged edge that will gnaw at the wave, is an outrage like poison. Outsiders would call them joyless by this, but then listen to the ways they find to play. Children make comics by walking with little sieves, laying strips of hush across the floor and reading them back with hands. Festivals are not noise but complex absences performed at the pillars, an orthography of denial that makes the cone glow like lightning and then go dark in a sentence all can feel. They laugh often. You just don’t hear it with your ears.

They keep nothing like our metal, and they are not poor for it. When they need hardness they grow it or grind it. When they need memory they make patterns. Writing lives as piercings, as load paths, as the way a mat gives to weight. A love-letter is a pair of tuned reeds nested and placed under a sleeping pad so that the note comes up thinner under the back and says without saying: when you wake, think of my hands here. A history is a painted line of salt laid across a slate horn’s mouth and licked by shock when the wall brightens—rings of brightness marking the rare hours when the planet twitched. They do not burn. They never learned that trick and they do not miss it. They heat by listening longer where a reflector has been made.

There is no up for them that is not toward less tone, no weather that is not the patient breath of a planet through a crack. But they are not small. In their constraints they have found room. They husband groves of glass, yoke eels to gentle baskets so that a float of tools will hang at useful height, domesticate beetle-altars so the private chapels sing their babies to sleep. They have gods—one god, if you must count—the Note that is law, and around it saints of Place: the Quiet Eye, the Crest-Returner, the Little Hurt that makes light when you need to see a face.

They tell stories the way you arrange an interference pattern: by placing, by holding steady until a shape is born inside a field that was already there. They sketch a hero as a moving shadow that runs against the static thrum for an hour and leaves the air tasting different. Some of them look up to the crack they came from and learn to fear the unimaginable mess of elsewhere—heat that is not paid in pressure and release, water that licks and seeps and smears, light that will not wait to be pain. Some dream of a day the note will change, and you would think that is terror, but in the dark they make little gaps and teach their kids not to be afraid.

Everything here lives at the permission of a single sound. The hushers above all have made of that permission a craft and a kindness. They move by not offending, they work by refusal, they raise children in quiet eyes, they bury their dead in resonant groves. In a world where nothing like rain or spark-fire teaches you to hurry, they have chosen to be soft to a law that cannot be unfelt, to build a life on charge and patience and the knowledge that in a room that is also a machine, love is a pattern you keep together by hand.
