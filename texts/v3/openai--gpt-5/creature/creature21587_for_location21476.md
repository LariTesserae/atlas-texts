What lives here is employment.

The bowl hires bodies to fill functions, and those that live are the ones that can hold a beat. They are individuals as much as a clapper is an individual apart from the bell. If you try to inventory them as species, they slip into tasks; if you try to list tasks, they resolve into mouths and skins and axles. They do not make the factory; they are how the place manages to continue.

Begin with the breathers, because the stone keeps a chest. The exhalations that fog your lips are not geological accidents only. Threads of soft animal occupy the seams—the seam-keepers. You can see them when the warm breath rolls out like a held note: a slither of pale tissue that seals and unseals like a valve, beaded with a comb of cilia. They live thin, spread like the membrane that makes them, and never come loose. When they do reproduce, they don’t walk away; they bud into an adjacent seam and take on a share of the opening schedule. They taste the mineral soup in the fissure and decide when to press and when to ease, and their decisions add up to a rhythm you could set a clock by. They are not lungs, not muscles; they are punctuation inside the stone’s sentence.

All along the gutters and bead-ways live the Drip Throats. You saw them right away: the cuffed ones with the petal mouths. Each is a tube curled into a ring so that its intake kisses its outflow, a kind of simple machine that drinks a drop and expresses it as a colder, slightly bluer bead. Their skin carries veins at the surface in neat geometry, webbed like printed circuits. They are not plants, not animals, but wet intentions with a pulse. They pulse on the beat, gathering the falling spheres as if swallowing notes off a staff. Under their translucent hides, you can watch ions shuffle and heat kneel. IIIII in their little organs act like gates, open-shut-open in exacting patterned kindness. If you mark one and come back a week later, it has not moved; the ring is ring still, but a clone now shares the gutter two fingers downslope, and the register of drops they serve has been extended a fraction, as if the gutter had asked for more capacity and gotten it.

Something else shares their line—the Gravity-Forgers. You notice them when a bead takes an impossible line. Looking closely is like looking for wind: you see only an effect. They are films more than bodies, slick, slicker than oil, draped over the hinge-places of the rock. Under certain lichens that wear velvet like a fur, under the nap of green felt, you can sometimes see one lift itself and crawl a thumb’s width, laying a gloss that reverses beaded loyalties. Their lives are measured in vectors. They have no mouths, but when they fail, their bodies collapse to a dull stain that other films, hungrier or earlier, drink. No one taught them this—it is a knack the bowl has grown on its own skin the way a scar is a habit of healing.

In the peat, whole companies labor: the Bellows Beetles. Beetle is convenient, because they are shelled and jointed, but you can pry one shell and find that it’s more like a portable press wearing legs. Their backs are varnished dark, each plate lacquered in a checkered pattern like a chessboard whose squares can flex. They settle at the edges of the hummocks and begin to squat and rise, slow and faithful. With each press, a sigh routes through the peat and into reed pipes that only ever do two pitches. From that, the beetles derive instruction. They listen through their feet and match the under-hum with their bodies. A new recruit arrives when a hummock fattens beyond its set circumference; you can watch it shuffle to the end of the file like an apprentice, placing its feet on the glinting press-marks of the one ahead. Their eggs are neither laid nor guarded. Each press stencils a tiny lozenge of goo into a safe pocket of peat; the heat of the process keeps it viable; at a certain number counted by pulses, the lozenge hatches, a perfect miniature press that simply drops into place like another tooth in a gear and begins to pump.

Their cousins do the opposite of breath: the Peat Drinkers. Pink, clear, all column and ripple, they stand upright in the seeps like organ pipes. They are made of throat. Look long and their stillness disturbs you, because they do not hunt; the world is arranged so that what can be taken drifts to them. They post on duty in squads—call them an array. Each throat takes from the film-surface anything that has drifted off its schedule or rounded too soon: spore dust, debris, an insect exhausted by the discipline of this place. It is not cruelty. Their patience is the place’s way of collecting its mistakes and turning them back into solution. Once in a while, one throat loosens and floats a short distance like an eel, then stands again. That is how they migrate. If one is knocked from its post, the others adjust spacing by exact measure, as if the empty spot still occupies them.

Air carries citizens too—charge-eaters, birds. The Cloud Swallows, the ones with iron tails, are not all swallows. Some are bats with plated membranes; some are moths scaled like razor wire; some are exactly birds, slender and pale with copper filaments where tail feathers should be. What unites them is that they diet on the seam where voltage pools like a thin broth. They fly the oval that everyone knows around the fixed lightning hinge. To watch them is to understand restraint as an appetite. Every third wingbeat they arc slightly, and the filament-bunch at their rear makes a polite little pop, brightens from ash to pearl, and returns to ash. Their nests are an alloy of living vine and iron filings, spun together by humid breath; the grapes of their eggs hang under ledges like ball bearings, frost-white, taking a charge just like their parents, never burned. The shells are slightly ferromagnetic and chime if you stroke them with a nail. Hatchlings do not beg. They go right to neutral: first flights are not flutters but regulated orbits, and from their first day they observe the guard around the hinge in the cloud, never encroaching the radius that would ground them.

Not far below them work the Hinge Frogs. You could believe they were invented the day the rock cooled. The hinge in their name is not anatomical only. That jaw you hear slam against the vibrating leaf—like sheet metal struck at the exact node—is also a hinge in time. Their feeding is a metronome. The panel leaf is a partner; the plant likes to be rung. Each blow shakes a flat of air that knocks true on the gnats that flock like looms, square against square, and the frogs’ tongues come out on count. Their tympanum is sheeted over with a glaze that makes it ring faint-brown when tapped; when it rains, drops bead up on the glaze instead of rolling in, as if the frogs decline improvisation. Tadpoles never crazy-spin in puddles; they queue. They grow legs at a number of lightning-counts that never varies, and on the day their tails are light enough to lift from the water but heavy enough to feel like memory, the frogs leave the sump’s rim in a procession like a weld bead.

You will see the Raspwings on the stone whenever the mold thickens. They are nightlike, in that they transform quiet into shape. Their wings serrate a fine burr, and in flight they keep a blade hum you feel in your teeth. When one lands on a patch of fungus, it drags the wing’s saw-toothed edge across the fuzz and feeds the dust through a slot along its abdomen that must be a mouth. The stone gleams for a minute. The moth brightens then dims—a factory work light turned on then off to show you the tool’s line. They do not rasp for themselves alone. The place dislikes occlusion, and the Raspwings are its polishers. Their larvae are not caterpillars in any simple way. They are small, low gaskets that seal the pores of stone worn too smooth; when they pupate, they unzip the gasket around its circumference with a tidy noise, step out dry, and leave the ring where it made sense.

The ones that make mathematics in the air are the Squaring Gnats. Each individual is not clever, but like needles pointed by a magnet, they find straight. You can watch a square swell and shrink around an obstacle without losing right angles. Their perception is made of what is shared. A whole square can turn ninety degrees in a blink that is also several dull blinks added up. They drink nothing but the thin sweetness the lichens exhale and offer their bodies to frogs when the panels knock the air just so. It should be sad to them, to be knocked cleanly out of shape and into a mouth, but nothing here studies sorrow. The consolation is that their squares survive them—half the time literally, in that a missing gnat is simply accounted for by a tighter spacing and the shape holds.

Dragons do not live here, but something that has borrowed their word for wing does. Call them Plotters. They are the dragonflies that do not waver. Even their blur is on the grid. When you first notice one, you think a pin should be stuck through it; it seems so museumed. But what the eye calls still is an endurance running like a lathe. The Plotter holds its point in the air until the timing in its thorax strikes, then writes a figure on the thickened air with crisp angles—L, L, Z, L—and lands again in the same air-location precisely. No two write the same figure, or rather, no two write the same figure at once. Over a day, the total of their tracings cancels to a wound coil that does not unwind. They hunt squares, yes, but after a set of lines, always, even if prey passes within an incorrect distance, they end the figure and rest the same rest as before. That is how you know you do not matter here. Your breath could gust them off their mark, but their mark reappears as if it were an architectural feature of the bowl and they a caretaker of it.

On the rim, one large animal keeps a post in the way a minute hand keeps a post around a clockface. It is goat-like and not. The copper hair is not affect; each shaft conducts along its length and hums very faintly under your palm, if you are allowed. Its pupils tick side to side as though catching a pendulum’s last inches, and if you stand with it for an hour and a half, you will see one step, exact, the step it owes. Lichen will have grown into the seams of its hooves in the interim; the hooves give like crust on bread, flaking away at the moment of movement to reveal a polished metal of keratin so tight it could take a spark. It eats little, almost nothing you can see. It stands where wind knifes, placing itself where two blades meet, as if the geology needs a mammal to complete a circuit up high. Kids are ungainly only in the way new instruments are: they overring. By the second week they have found their one step and take it with such satisfaction their ribs relax.

Between hummocks, there are bridges that think with their backs. Centipedes, if centipedes could appoint themselves spans. When a route must exist that did not, a series of them snake out of the green and join spines. Each spine ridge fits into the next like zippers agreeing on how to hold a seam. The bridge quivers itself taut and then permits—only then—the crossing of many small, wet, black things that cross and have no other way. The small things are shy and numerous, maybe isopods like obsidian or maybe something else with folded origami bellies, and as soon as they have passed and moisture is restored from whatever deficit was being solved, the bridge unstitches and walks away. Once every many bridges, a predator ruins the crossing by taking one in its prowl—the Plotter makes a mistake, the Hinge Frog misses, or some other creature whose life you have not learned yet breaks the rule. There is no pause. Another spine slides into the lost place and the span holds. The loss is accounted for later, in pulses through the peat, in press counts, in reeds that whistle one pitch a single time extra in the afternoon.

All this rides the scaffolding of plants that are not idle scaffolds. The pads of fungus that stamp spore into the damp make patterns like part numbers because the patterns are read by the next organism down the line. The spore-presses have caps like platen heads; they make a hiss when they kiss the peat. A few hours after, you can find the emboss read by a worm the thin color of malt, its skin ridged in a way that catches the mold’s print. The worm is the Reader. It consumes only numerals, turns them into gut-syllables that mean “more breath here” or “clamp this seam.” The cushion domes—the battery-mosses, the readies—shine without shining, an electrical pallor you feel with your tongue as a taste. They accept the charge the cloud seam feeds down the copper outcrop and hold it without drama. If you lick one, your mouth goes metallic for the rest of the day, whether or not you washed. They do not like to be moved. You can try to pry a puck of it up, and within hours the bare stone will bead a thin, wrong sweat until a dozen Drip Throats kink into place to carry the mistake away. The moss pings you with scent: fern, tin, readiness. It does not die under your hand; it stops happening until your hand leaves.

It all rings around the sump, which is not a pool so much as a polished question. Under its skin of geometry live Fish That Agree With Themselves. They are one and they are several. Sometimes you count three, then the skin quivers and you count five, then you laugh at yourself and count one the rest of the hour. If you sit long enough, you will see a mouth kiss the undersurface—a mouth like a notch, not like a lip. What they eat is repetition. They knock the underside with their heads in a private code that runs alongside the audible code of frogs and beetles. When you notice that the knock below and the slap of the frog’s jaw above are the same rhythm, just half a measure out, you are near understanding how the bowl keeps any middle from wandering. The fish blow no bubbles. When they die, the geometry that will not let flies in will not let death up either; the body is accepted as a case, digested, and the surface resolves.

There are salamanders too, membrane-literate. The clap you made, the temporary wall that your sound drew between two stones—that is a thing they can see. Or if not see, respect. They stand under the bruise of air you have marked and hold their heads a fraction lower than they would in open path, knowing in the bones of their skulls the ceiling will hold until the peat has drunk the last of your echo. Their bodies are stacked commas, soft and black and glistening. Their tongues flick not for prey only, but to read the state of walls in air, the stress in mist. They will not cross lines of stamped spore. Trying to herd them is like trying to argue with punctuation; they will not break a sentence made for them.

Someone might ask you, do the people live here? People like you? The answer is no, and also yes, if you will allow that a place can burgeon itself into role after role until every urge you carry into it—the urge to make, time, solve, rinse, store—has a native here who does the same, but by its own light and without your need of it. The nearest thing to a mind you will meet is distributed. It is housed in the sum of intervals. It corrects for rain that never comes and lightning that always does. It uses meat where meat makes sense—jaws to knock a panel, pupils to measure the wind’s blade—and it uses leaf and lichen where those are appropriate—vane to shiver a frog’s dinner, velvet to persuade water up its nap. There are no bosses. There are clocks, and the clocks are alive.

Even the predators obey. There are owls with square faces that are not faces—they are collectors of space. They take small moving things, yes, but only in quantities that leave the hummock’s breath undisturbed. Their pellets are tidy like invoices. There are snakes that are almost equations: bands not of color but of interval, wide-narrow-narrow-wide along their length, and so they move by solving for friction. There are foxes with wet heels that stamp algae in mirrored prints and in doing so set a direction for algae to spread, then follow the trail for reasons that have nothing to do with hunger. Their teeth are clean, because rot does not accumulate here; it is serviced.

You think, perhaps, at the edge of such order, that something must be kept out to make it. But arrivals are adoptions, not invasions. A seed out of context becomes a spore-emboss; a metal tool furs and joins the haircap contact net, surrenders its private gleam for a public bite of ozone; a bottle poured turns itself into the since-ever bead-train and marches, empty of your intention, full of the place’s. What lives here is not a list of creatures so much as a vocabulary of continuities. They are the words the bowl says to itself in order to keep being this particular bowl. If you turn your back on it, it does not miss you. It will be spoken by moss and beetle and frog and cloud and seam until the ridgeline dulls and the schedule relaxes and something else fills those posts with a different beat.

And if, after you go, you taste iron and peat and that green tartness of electricity, and hear the clean panel-smack of a frog as if in your own chest, that is not the place following you. It is employment lingering, the sensation of having been, very briefly, part of someone else’s shift.
