Who lives here are things that do not cast themselves into bodies. They keep to films and seams, to the glaze a hand would miss if a hand could pass. They are the sort of life that takes its shape from whatever it is on, knowing better than to raise an edge.

They are green first, where the noon is richest. Thin cloths of cells run up the sunward ribs of the chimneys, bright where the face is square to the light, olive where it slips into shade. Each sheet is a braid of hair-fine filaments, each filament a string of rooms inside which daylight is taken apart and paid out as sugar and a breath of oxygen that no animal will claim. They do not heap. They stop at their own shadow. They lace themselves to the carbonate with clear gels, smooth as saliva, that hold them and, when the chemistry is right, call down a little more stone. In the shallows along a ridge you can see the trick in cross-section: a millimeter of green, a millimeter of precipitate, a millimeter more green, each season’s errand kept in laminae so slight that even the silt respects them.

They adjust without stirring. No fronds, no flutter. Where the light leans a degree to the left, they lean one cell to meet it. Where a pore begins to whisper out a different recipe—more dissolved gas, a sharper base—they alter their pigments, thicken their skin, choose a slower enzyme that works cold. Some of them carry, sealed in certain beads, the ability to make their own nitrogen from the water’s blankness. In these beads they keep oxygen out and the ancient work in, a tiny monastery of careful breath dotted through the green.

Mixed through this lacquer are pinpoints of other makers: small, glass-clad cells that lay down their own skins from the room’s silica and build them in pairs, in trios, in the simple clocks the light allows. When they are finished—when a frond-wide patch has all the light it can use—they do not spread. They let time take them loose, frustule after frustule, a chalk of glitter that settles into the room’s careful accounting below. You would call it ash if it were not translucent.

There is a second nation under the first, and it does not need daylight. It occupies the stone itself, the wet pores that branch and rejoin inside the chimneys like capillaries frozen mid-branch. In there the seep’s last habits continue in remarks too small to feel. You cannot see them, but you can read their presence in the faint rust of an iron seam put back to brown, in the quarter-tone of a manganese band grown a shade more violet. These are the keepers of differences: cells that make a living on the line where one mineral hands off to another. Some of them drink the thin trickle of methane that rises from whatever deep, long-closed pocket the spring still taps, and spend it at the outer boundary where oxygen—minted by the greens—seeps in. Some of them take ammonium where it is born from slow decay and press it up a step, and others take it back down and send it to nitrogen again, the slow hiss the pores exhale. They are not a single sheet but a gradient’s worth of citizens, arranged as neatly as a slide rule: oxidizers at the brightward edge; reducers in the blue; in the middle, a few that do nothing more than pass electrons along with wires in their walls, bridging rust to violet so both can continue. If you put a pin in one seam and another in the next and measured, you would find a whisper of current, and their filaments would be the only hands against it.

Beneath the brightest panes the ceiling also grows its life flat. The ice is streaked very faintly with a tea-colored bloom—cells made for glare and cold, sheltering in the centimeter of light that the ice itself remembers and keeps. They are not much more than stain. When sun fails, they poise at the edge of work and wait in arrangements they inherited from last spring. Here and there, within the sealed tubes of clear salt that hang like paused drips, there are survivors folded down to their smallest self: halophiles locked in a brine the texture of glass, proteins held in a winter that does not trouble them, instructions stitched to endure. They are not busy now. They are present.

The silt is a conservatory. Everything that has finished above comes to rest as the finest punctuation, a grammar of light solved and let go. In it are spores as dull and perfect as seeds—the answers to bad weather written with the same patience that made the shelves. There are chains of bacteria that keep their work to the uppermost millimeter, wires a child’s eye could not see, moving charge from the layer that breathes oxygen to the layer that will not. There are exudates, sugars and slimes, too slow to call food, that trap the nearby and glue it into the slow stone to come. There are archives: loops of DNA clipped from last summer’s brighter week and stored on mineral, drift that will not disintegrate in the cold and can be read back into being when a living cell washes close enough to learn it.

Between all these sheets and threads swims the smallest, most numerous inhabitant of all: the virus, a verb reduced to its bare instruction. It does not drift far—there is no current to ride—but it waits so well that it does not need to. Now and then it finds its host in the film, writes the slightest correction into that host’s way of living, and ensures, by cutting and reminder, that growth is a seam and not a tide. It keeps the room from becoming felted. It keeps the script crisp.

No creature here sets forth. There are no eyes. There are no mouths, unless you count the pores. There is movement, but it is the sort that a microscope calls motion: a glide of a bacterial raft along a ridge by the width of its own body in an afternoon; the adhesion of a spore that will open next winter; a bubble born too slowly to deserve the word. Everything else is metabolism finding a place to stand.

They are old talents wearing young coats. Alkaline water laid these chimneys down; a film taught the stone to harden faster by taking carbon from it; precipitate made a house for the next film; and so on, until the plant stopped itself with its own success. The living that remain work in the husk like a final shift that tends the quiet machines—keeping their bearings oiled with electrons, spilling a bare measure of oxygen into the water so iron can keep its time, catching a photon with the same relief, the same appetite, each day. They do not miss what they have never had: grazers, storms, alarms. They do not ornament themselves to be chosen. They wear, instead, the minimum that endures.

At the edges, where the light thins and the ice grows thick, even these go transparent. There the residents are mostly chemistry, which is to say almost everyone has stepped back into matter. In the center, under the panes that read as noon, life is legible: patches, veils, green arithmetic. If you could take a census, you would count by surfaces and interfaces. If you could ask them their years, you would get answers in layers of lime. If you could touch them, you would come away with nothing in your fingers, and still they would remain, whole, in their places, continuing the room’s single practice: to take what comes through the ice and make of it, at a pace you can hardly imagine, more stone, more clarity, and more of themselves, which are mostly edges—edges that endure.
