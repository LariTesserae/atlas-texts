What lives here keeps to a thickness.

A skin of beings spread over stone, shaved thin as a breath and stacked in sheets: light-harvesters, water-catchers, stitchers, chewers of mineral, gluers, wire-layers. They don’t so much stand in the place as adhere to it, take its shape and its moods, and live by whatever passes. They are lungless; they count by pulses of wet and the voltage on the edges. If they have a body at all, it is the surface between things.

The mosses are the most forward face of that body. Not one thing but a parliament of strategies, they occupy every slope and seam that will entertain a grip. Some are cushions heavy as sandbags, domes tight with hyaline cells that hold a private weather of their own; some are starry and sparse, each leaf a ribbed little trough for fog; some are shags that comb the wind and coax it to let go of beads. When they are wet they shine like polished kelp; when they are dry they flatten and lock, green fading to the color of coins found in snowmelt. They open and close without noise, but you can feel it in the texture if you run a hand along the grain: a roomful of tiny umbrellas setting themselves in one direction when the notch inhales, reclosing when it exhales. Their hunger is for light and for the slow soak; their work is to make a grip for all the rest.

Clinging just under and among them are the filmy ferns, lives no thicker than a trick of fog. Each frond is a skin just one or a few cells deep, paint you could breathe on a wall, trembling with the faintest draught. Their veins are vague suggestions, their green so pale it reads as a thought of color rather than the thing. They are leaf and lung fused, sipping water from air, sunlight from dimness. Their sporangia ripen as rows of pinheads under the lamina and then, on some pressure-turn that only they feel—a drop in humidity, a blueing of charge, the right tooth of wind—they let go their flour of spores. It rises a finger’s breadth, no further, then is caught up and written through the slot like smoke.

Older than the notch but made for its exactness, the clubmosses stand in their toy forests, cypresses cut for a god’s dollhouse. Scaled stems, sporangia like lanterns tucked on the tips. Their powders are fat and bright, notoriously ready to flash in a spark elsewhere; here, where sparks are ordinary as breath, the plants have learned a slower release, a sifting timed to the long inhale. You could watch one cone for an afternoon and see it do almost nothing, and then—on the instant when the hair on your wrist lifts—the whole thing would snow into the air like a moth’s wing rubbed to dust.

Sedges uphold the high-edges and anoint every little ledge with knives. They are not here to be tender. Their blades are triangular in cross-section, edges beaded not with dew only but with the freight of the sky: aerosol salt, stray nitrate, ash. Their flowers are inconspicuous, their achenes even less; they believe in numbers and wind and patience. When blue fire walks, it chooses their points first, violet foxfire along the knife. If anything in the place can be said to wear jewelry, it is the sedge in a storm.

Tight and thoughtful, the rosettes—houseleeks, saxifrages, stone-breakers of various mind—make themselves roofs. Each leaf is a trough and a gutter and a shield. Hairs along the margins catch mist; the angle of the blade leads the drink to the core; the core is a well; the well is a stomach without a mouth. They store cold light as starch, store wet as pressure. When the mat under them loosens a flake of rock, a root finds the hairline and slides in, slick as a key. They do not hurry at anything. They are the sort of lives that take a decade to get to the size of a boot heel, then split themselves neatly so that a daughter has a platform, then do it again.

Under this green is a second people, whiter and browner and sometimes every color of smoke. The fungi are the sutures. Pull back a tuft and there they are, bright as hoarfrost in the first inch, then browning, then marbling into the stone. They draw their lines along capillaries, binding mats to mats and plant to plant. They budget the notch: carbon that arrives on the faces is banked and redistributed to whoever is currently well-placed to turn it into future hold; phosphorus and trace metals are chelated and carried across gaps that wind would make into non-places. Fruiting bodies are rare and ashamed of themselves here—little pins, dark lids on stalks that are gone as soon as they are seen. What they like best is not to be seen, to be tensile in secret, to be the reason a wall does not let go in one piece.

Lichens script the hard parts, early and ongoing. Crusts that look like skin and that are skin: fungus making house for alga, alga feeding fungus, both of them making acid that persuades glassy basalt to become something more amenable. On the newest planes they are grey as lichen is always grey; in older pockets they put on other surfaces: an invisible varnish that feels slicker under a finger, a powdering of pale where soredia can be lifted by the next rill, tiny upstanding cups that will never be seen except in lightning when every ridge is chalk. They are as patient as stone has to be taught to be. They are also the first authors of dirt.

The films are legion. Bacteria cast their gloss on everything that stays wet even for an hour: brown where tannins have been gathered from plant, rainbow where manganese has been licked into a different state, yellowed a little where iron has been fussed over. They are who gives the place its coin-smell after a storm. They redox the notch as surely as thunder does, running electrons along mineral rails from wet to dry and back. When the ozone is strong you could imagine them hissing with appetite, though they make no sound. Some trace the insides of vesicles in the rock, endoliths that live by the side-light washing in through a translucent wall of feldspar. Some live in the first fraction of a millimeter of water that rides every leaf when the cloud sits right. None of them are meant to be noticed individually. They are patina, sentence, undertone.

In the bowls the rock remembers to allow, little worlds as big as a helmet brim themselves whenever form and air make water. In them, the smallest glassworks are at labor: diatoms building silica rooms in symmetrical styles; desmids making elegant green hourglasses; green algae broad as a human hair lying against one another like books shelved; cyanobacteria lining the lip in dark fur. They have their own drifts and overcasts, their own stratifications of cool and cooler, oxygen and less oxygen. Freeze pops them, thaw wakes them, wind unstrings them drop by drop across the floor where they will be pressed into felt under feet that are root more than foot.

If there are animals, they are the kind a poet would omit from a love letter: protozoa tasting bacteria along a film with the blind certainty of cells; perhaps a nematode thread folded into the wet of a moss tip and asleep more often than in motion. Here, anything with a mouth would go thin from want and wind and the static that pricks every articulation. The rule holds. Everything that eats does so without a mouth, or with one too small and slow to count.

Whatever lives here that is not made from carbon and patience lives as weather. Lightning walks and writes nitrates into accounting columns the plants can read. St. Elmo’s combs the edges and hums. Pressure swings breathe; fog drags its belly over the gap and pants; wind picks locks and opens doors and closes them again. These are not alive, not the way the green is, but they are the green’s shepherd and its famine, its calendar and its metronome. Release happens on their cue: spores uncork when a front depresses; gemmae flick when a gust hits exactly; a thousand mouths that are not mouths open their stomata to the smell of rain and close again at the scent of frost. Even electricity is folded into habit. Root-tips set up small potentials with the mats; hyphal cords feed down the slope of charge as readily as they do down the slope of gravity. Wiring is not a metaphor here. It is how the room holds itself against being stripped.

You could, if you wanted to call it one life, call it that. The notch lives. Not as an organism with one skin but as a community that has become a single practice. Shape is its genome. Wind is its blood. Cloud is its food. Stone is its bone and its tool. The mosses are its tongue for water; the ferns are its lace for breathing; the sedges are its hair standing up in a storm; the lichens are its handwriting; the fungi are its tendons. Bacteria are its varnish and its appetite. Its moods are barometric. Its memory is in layers: hoar left bright in the under-mat; salt smears and pollen prints recording what was made, when. It can be wounded—a plate let go will take a century of green proceeds with it in a single shear—but it will suture itself as soon as the wind gives it a purchase, because that is what it knows: capture and hold, then pass the held forward until the whole is slightly thicker and a little quieter underfoot.

What lives here does not go anywhere. It has no elsewhere it prefers. If it travels, it does so as dust in a gust, or as an echo of chemistry on some new plane of andesite a crack farther along. Its bloom is in small insistences measured in centimeters and seasons, its extinctions are in single sporangia lost to a dry day. None of it is finished. None of it means to be finished. It means to continue, laminar and elect, doing the repetitive genius of its kind until the ridge itself has been rounded, until another notch inherits the wind and begins again with a bare glass edge and a first brown stain.
