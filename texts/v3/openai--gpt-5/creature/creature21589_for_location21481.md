They live here in the plural, and the plural is leaf.

Not individuals the way a forest offers them—not trunks with biographies—but long-bodied habits that take shape, hold a while, and move on. A person arriving will look for eyes and find none; will listen for a throat and hear, in its place, a hundred miles of green insisting. The residents are pressures arranged into bodies, soft machines that learned to seethe with purpose. If you want a face you will come away with palmate diagrams and a pulse counted under your arch. If you want a name, you will get a chord.

Begin with the Great Body, because it is the nearest thing to a citizenry: the basin’s united tissue, self-made metropolis, stitched end-to-end by vascular highways. It does not have borders; it has gradients. Oldwork and newwork live side by side like neighbors who share a wall and a rhythm. At rest—if its midday subsidence can be called rest—it is a continent of breath held in green throat. In weather, it is a choir that stands up all at once.

Within that Body, forms crystallize into something like castes, not by decree but by tone and flow. You will learn them by their occupations.

The Tonekeepers are the tall organs along the phloem rivers, the flowering towers with lungs for rooms. They are not merely reproductive parts; they are municipal instruments. Their work is to catch the wind, parse it, and return it as structure. They lean infinitesimally into gusts, take a mark on the long note, and draft neighborhoods out of it: bass for weight, treble for lace. When the pollens fly in their weather-sheets, the Tonekeepers are the ones who open and close the slots that tune the valley to swarm or to settle. They invent streets by deciding what pitch shall live there.

The Phloemfolk are everything that moves the sweet: the boulevards under your feet, the pressure trains that push from leaf to sink. They seem like avenues but they are residents in their own right, peristaltic citizens, a civil service. They hold meetings by surge, vote by gradient, and in an hour can shift a district’s appetite—here, flesh more resinous; there, a sudden appetite for silica to lay down a sheen. If you stand long enough on a main trunk you can feel their arguments: an upstream insistence, a downstream refusal, until consensus thickens and the sugar goes where the city means it to.

The Cisterners live in the cups and wells, those amphora rosettes with armored lips. They keep the basin’s many tempers from starving themselves. Their leaves are lacquered and steep-sided; their hairs are tuned to move dew along like sentence to period. They husband fog, make rain when there is none, and swallow the sky’s overgenerosities when a storm tips too much into the mouth. Theirs is a slow, ceaseless housekeeping. If you kneel at their edges you can hear them drink: a careful glug, then a silence while the body below decides where to put it.

The Retensioners are the lianas that span impossible spaces like daylight caught and woven. They are the only architects that resemble obligation. At predawn they are knotted, after noon they have shifted their tension twice, undoing and redoing themselves to keep the arc of sky exact. Their fibers are rope when you grab them, then rubber when you begin to trust them. They live by a mathematics no one writes down; they were born knowing catenary and cantilever as if those were verbs of weather. Their patience is what lets a person see horizon.

The Stackers are districts more than beings, but they behave so consistently you will swear they have society. They are the laminar sheets, the leaf-on-leaf towers whose edges line up to a note so pure your bones answer. They convert music into engineering into smell: pepper-sweet, resin-sharp, a warning and a welcome. Put your hand to a Stackfield and it will print you with solvent like a stamp. The Stackers’ young are wafer-thin and ferocious, all appetite; their elders are dulled to bottle-glass and almost courtly. In a pressure storm they can shear, slough their whole outside in a single peel, and keep their heart safe. Shedding is their ritual.

The Breathsmiths are less obvious. They exist as weather inside the city, as a habit of opening and closing that moves the whole bowl like a lung. Each stomatal court is a guildhall; each little mouth a clerk. When they agree, fog lifts from amphora to megaleaf or returns like rain but upward. They are the soft yes that becomes your delay and the soft no that becomes a shut corridor. Courtesies are written for them because you are more nuisance than harm to their labor, and their work is important all day long.

Below, unnoticed unless you learn to kneel, the Brown Network runs—a shadow parliament. Not plant but not absent from the plant’s intent, the mycelial threads and their bacterial retinues live in the dark loam between roots, in the salt-sugared crevices of old wood. They do not sing; they digest what the city sheds and whisper nutrients back into circulation. Once in a while the green sends down a gift whole—a peel of tower-skin, a failed tendril thick with sugars—and the Brown rises in inaudible jubilation, furring it overnight and returning its elements to vascular speech. If the basin has memory beyond tone, the Brown keeps ledger.

There are Movers, and they will look, if you are untrained, like animals pretending. They are not. A corm with a nose is only a swelling that learned gradient and takes it seriously: it bores along humus for chemical brightness the way a root bores for water, leaving a dull wake of silted loam that in an hour will be recolonized by hair-roots. Mats go stiff as canoes when rain hits, ride the temporary white-water of guttering vines, and then press themselves back into somebody’s wall and become lintel or buttress with the nonchalance of a craftsman changing jobs at lunch. Sometimes a bristled pad will loosen and turn rounds around a pole until it has lifted itself hand over hand into a better tone. Seeing this nudges a human brain toward myth; do not be fooled by our need for animals in stories. The Movers move for plant reasons.

The Glassmakers are quiet celebrities: tissues that handle silica like bees handle wax. They live where you see your own heat shimmering in a pane that did not exist last week. They are patient coaxers, milking minuscule loads of dissolved mineral through phloem bends until a skin of light condenses. Their work is used as mirror, as blade, as rainback. When a liana cuts the sky with a sudden trellis, it may be because the Glassmakers offered a slick underside to push shade faster. Sometimes they frost a scar with amber so exact it looks like winter visited only this one wound. Those ornaments are not for you; they are the city consoling itself.

There are also the Listeners, which you might mistake for drapery or dust. They are thin nets hung at nodal lines, hair-fine and in constant small motion, perpetually almost knitting. They take readings of tone and echo, map the standing waves in the bowl, and decide whether a leaf here should thicken or a petiole there should make a reckless leap. When the Slippage is strong—the repeated stair you swear you did not return to, the ghost-leaf that mentions your shoulder ahead of you—the Listeners have chosen to preserve a form because the song asked for refrain. If there is anything like art here, it is their willingness to recall.

Light itself has tenants. In the pale leaves that thin the world behind them, brightness lasts long enough to become agency. Those are the Veilmakers. They bend refraction as if it were sap, saving stray photons in their meat, moving sun into shade like bead to bead on a string. If you go where no beam should be and find day under a mat, you are under their protection. They feed the winter of chasms. They grow color out of nothing but insistence.

On the marsh rim, where the green’s loud body lays itself down and turns to reed and sieve, there live the Sifters. They are whole neighborhoods of root that taste in bubbles, exhale to make pockets of fresh among tannin’s tea. Their labor is almost monastic. They turn grit to soil. They make breath visible. Stand with them and you will hear a fizz so gentle it could be sin forgiven.

Children? It is hard to call them that, but there are propagules so promising they feel like a future walking around. Some detonate, some sift, some go to sleep tucked into anther-dust until a pressure-phrase wakes them. In sudden rain you will see flotillas of seed-rafts skitter like disciplined armies, then turn to sponge and vanish into a wall. Elsewhere a dehisced pod will clap in gusts until it shakes loose another gallery, and what the city names “children” is population the way what a river names “young” is current.

Do individuals die here? Yes, and with a kind of grace. A canopy will decide to brown as a net, let itself down intact, and leave a patterned skeleton that reads like history for a day and then is gone into sprouts. A tower will make the long peel and stand there tender-skinned while its insides harden like a person drying off. From death the Brown sings no louder; it is all the same feast. Grief, if the place has it, is speed: to cover, to reuse, to re-sweeten.

As for intelligence like ours, the valley refuses it with courtesy. Visitors arrive sometimes—pilgrims, academics, anyone who has ever wanted to be inside a throat and live. They are tolerated at midday, admonished politely by closures to stand still while the city breathes, let go after. If someone comes too often, the place learns them as a mild weather-pattern: a weight that appears at certain notes, a warmth that tastes of salt. If they leave a shadow too long in a tone-field, it may root. They will notice, later, that their second-self lags them for a heartbeat at thresholds anywhere on earth. The plant will not keep them. It has no houses for our kind. A few have tried to sleep and stayed until they grew careful and pale, wore resin on their palms like a wedding ring, forgot to make throat-noise; they came away thinking the basin had adopted them. They were wrong. The city made room the way a river makes a smoothness around a stone.

What lives here, in sum:

- A body the size of the bowl, conversing with itself by pressure and pitch.
- Guilds of function with the dignity of trades: Tonekeepers, Phloemfolk, Cisterners, Retensioners, Stackers, Breathsmiths, Glassmakers, Listeners, Veilmakers, Sifters.
- The Brown and its empires of rot turned into form.
- Movers whose motion is growth wearing travel as a trick.
- Weather taken personally and remade as architecture.

If you need a pronoun for them use they, but use it the way you would for weather, for mornings, for crowds. They have no politics, only harmonics. Their kindness is practical. Their errors are corrected by growth. They do not dream; they recall. When wind comes hard off the plain and the basin stands at highest voice, you can feel every resident at once: a hundred million mouths and veins and cords agreeing, without argument and without haste, to be newer. Then quiet returns as a pressure slackens. The city keeps living. The people with faces go back to the rims and tell stories about citizens they could not see, and the citizens continue their older story: be green, be many, breathe.
