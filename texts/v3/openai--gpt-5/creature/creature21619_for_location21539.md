They call themselves by words that mean grip and agreement, tilt and truce. Outsiders, if any come, call them ledgefolk or face-dwellers; among themselves the names are narrower and kinder. A household will say it belongs to a given angle, or to a bend that has held for three long generations, or to the shadow of a particular buttress when the day begins its roll. Their autonym translates badly: something like the Held, or those who Take the Wall’s Offer.

You see them first not in full bodies but in decisions. Three points or a line, always. Even on the breadth of a plaza they move as if the world might decide to tilt again at any moment: hips never swinging loose, shoulders considering weight and charge before they give themselves to a step. They do not stride; they set and test and set again. The ferocious, carnival blue of an ungrounded spark breaking between friends will make a child laugh but make an adult wince and reach for the touchstone. In this place even greeting is an electrical act; people meet by trading their statics into the same metal and riding the brief bite together.

They are small by our measures and long-limbed by their own. Bone lies close to the skin in elegant beams, sheathed where it faces the world by enamel and horn that have more kinship with porcelain than with hide. The backs of their hands are oddly smooth for cliff-people because nothing rough can be allowed to snag on glass flake; instead the palms and soles, the pads of fingers, the cupped curve of the forearm, are faceted with tiny compliant plates that articulate like the tiles of a pangolin. When they rest a hand to the wall the plates open along their hinges and show a nap of soft black setae—a velvet of conductive hairs—that test and then engage. Their boots and gloves do what the clingrills do, scaled for a biped: a tap that sets little hooks, a twist that seats them, a minute pulse of charge that tells the material beneath which way their adhesion wants to hold. The sound is a faint, repeated tick, one per point of contact, and after a while you realize you can tell mood by cadence.

Skin where it shows is matte, grey and copper and clay to match the cliff. It does not shine with sweat because there is none to spare. Pores are chimneys into little forests of salt and silica that wick and trap vapor on the inhaled side of the breath and part with it, grudging and saved, to the lungs on the next inhalation from a denser interior atmosphere. Their hair is not hair but tassels and fringes of fiber that bleed charge the way leaves bleed rain and shift in the electric winds when afternoon lift rises. Children braid and weight these with minute cones of copper that chime tinnily when the Blue Year comes and all the reefs sing.

Face is a ledger of tiny closures: nictitating inner lids, conductive lashes that can be drawn like screens to keep the dust’s lacework of current from tricking the eye. Noses flare wider where the sinuses hold reversible desiccant; mouths are narrow and guarded because too much air, too quickly, is a thing you learn not to love. Tongues are not pink muscle but the instrument of a trade in ions: a comb of fringes, each armed with little spoons of salt-protein, by which taste becomes a number and a small moment of power. Children will try a patch of mat or a drip of wall-weep with comic solemnity, wait, and smile when the current comes home.

They speak low and close by default, as if sound were money. You learn fast that almost all other communication happens through what they touch. Every household has a set of ground-lines—ropes of glass and soft metal that run from room to room like veins. Put your finger to one and somewhere else a wall-plate hums, or a loom stops, or a mother cock her head in the nursery. The long paths between towers are strung with lines too, taut and humming. People walking a distance lay a cheek to the wire when they want to be known, and transmit with a sequence of pressure, a little code of forearm and jawbone that goes faster than most strangers can hear at first. You can watch two friends talking seventy meters apart across an expanse of sliding talus and see nothing but a man pausing to tie his shoe, a woman stooping as though to inspect a seam. The wall, which carries charge so generously, carries small talk on its skin.

If you stay, you will be caught in their cadences: a calendar made of sound too small and slow to call weather, and of sights best read in peripheral vision. Morning is for tenders and gleaners. The little piezo-bright mats that are coaxed along fracture lines by antique patience are not crops, not exactly; but the households that keep them know which slits bloom early with mineral fuzz in Blue Years and which, in thin years, must be left alone so that their skeletal coins will not break. Children go with their elders to learn the angle of a mat’s favor, to lay their tongues to combs of braided copper and feel for themselves if today is strong enough to harvest. It is a generational lesson of greed: how much to eat from a place that makes itself molecule by molecule.

There are herds, of a sort. Clingrills in their working trappings carry little. They are not beasts of burden in the manner of plains animals because nothing heavy moves free on a shelf; the chainbeds do that. But they are family—competent attendants a person might trust over a brother when it comes to a particular path. They have favorite faces and stray back to them like cats. They will wear their tea-cup plates signed with the etched inlays of a house and rub them, in passing, against the corner of a home wall, and households smooth the porcelain with oil of the world and soft woven pads, affectionate. The low-bodied crystal grazers are drift-stock, more wild than owned, but families have rights to particular evening pastures and the pulses of dusk bring everyone out to guide the pillow-animals with little fences of scent and charged thread. It is delicate work to redirect a line of living patience. Children are given this task as a way to teach them the motion of a crowd that never hurries.

Gliders are not ridden. They are their own: beautiful, exacting, domesticated only in the sense that a community that plants windbreaks can domesticate weather. Young people will climb to the afternoon updrafts with sacks of salt crust and throw it into the lift to draw the gliders where filaments can be combed, cleaned, repaired. Filament gathering is courtship, in both directions. Lovers gift one another small skeins that hum at the right voltage to produce a shared voice from a wall. Some households host filaments from a hundred gliders, each labeled by color and vintage, and tune them, once a season, into a little, private reef.

The chandelier creatures are tended like gardens under eaves and in the mouths of culverts that offer them a smooth wind. Families name the elders by dentition and patchwork. They rock and open when the day is soft, shut at the crank of dust wind, and sometimes a house will sit in the street and watch a particular chandelier negotiate an awkward guest-grain and laugh when it manages to sieve it. In Blue Years the chandeliers sing, very softly, with the induction reefs; their calcareous cages pick up harmonics and shed a hum like your grandmother’s throat. It is impropriety to dance then, but people do.

Bead-worms are their cousins in technology as much as in ecology. Children stack them because children everywhere stack blocks; here the blocks respond, and glow, and warm the hand as they pass charge along. Houses use bead-worm columns to test new runs of filament, to sense where the wall is humming today, to map the drift of a seam of stressed glass. A broken column is a small funeral. Some old women keep bead-worm circuits the way other old women keep gardens: arrays of living pearls that do nothing you can name except make a room right.

The city’s industry is the place’s longest speech, and those who tend it the keepers of its syntax. There are reef-wardens who never step out into the more generous wind, for whom the idea of a glider coasting by at arm’s length is unwelcome distraction. Their expertise lives in the timbre of inaudible loads: a humbent ear will tell them that a comb is beginning to saturate a centimeter of plate past the point of calm, that a seam needs another shutter set in front of it to stiffen its spoke. There are fiber-drawers whose hands know the lean of liquid glass in air the way a cook on another world knows the tell of a crust. There are chainbedmen and women, old and slow, climbing without hurry beside cargos that look like black drifts of winter. They know the wall by places you cannot see but can taste. Ask one what the world is like and she will tap with one long plate of nail, then another, then say, “It’s a good year; the rock is thoughtful.”

Inside is where water is permitted to have a geography. Breathing spaces are blown full of the old mineral smell and an admixture of heavier gases safe to conserve. Everyone has a habit of licking a finger and holding it up in their own rooms, not for weather but to make sure the exchanges are right, that the old breath has circulated through its desiccants and the solvent loops have pulled out the skin oils the wall will not tolerate. Machines are diligent but never sudden. If a furnace must cough, it does so into baffles along a prescribed line of rooms that belong to the city collectively, and the sound is quick and polite. “We don’t throw noise away,” a grandmother will tell a too-loud child. “There’s no such place as away.”

They eat little and often, like animals of the long desert. Morning fuzz from the mats and the delicate “sweat” the walls weep after a day’s warming are delicacies, of course; but most calories come from denser, quieter processes inside. There are vats where hard gas is fixed into polymer and shaped into bread that is not bread except that you tear it and add a powder of salt-protein and a drop of oil-of-world, and the ions leap your tongue the way a spring used to leap for your ancestors. There are cakes of resinous crystalline grit ground fine and seasoned with the scrapeings of chandeliers; there is meat of a kind, laid down slowly as low-bodied grazers take in bloom and salt and produce soft pads of tissue that can be pared like cheese without harm. A great virtue in a cook is how dry the cut is.

Their laws are simple where they are severe and astonishingly complex where you would expect play. Waste of moisture is a crime. Waste of charge is a misfortune you will keep to your own rooms and learn from without burdening your neighbors. The sale of noise is allowed only in the deepest breathing spaces, and then only in certain hours, and then the buyers and sellers cry and laugh and beat on stretched sheets of tempered glass with little rods of copper until the conductors smoke. Ownership of drifts is a law like property and like grazing rights, a palimpsest of entitlements to direct a very slow river by the tiniest of fences: shade-screens and sound-baffles and subtle changes in preferred angle. It is old law because it assumes no emergency. When emergencies come—and they do come; a plate when it goes will go for a day—another law unfolds with it, a choreography of shuttering and sealing and the sending of chainbed crews with anchors and anti-angles to meet a river of falling skin and turn it gently out and down the far face where there is nobody to be buried.

Children do not crawl. They cling. Their first play is with soft walls and one another’s fringe; their first toys are touchstones and little glass coils with a drop of metal sealed inside so that they will sing when rolled. Their first lessons are not to put their tongues where their elders have not put them first, and three points or a line, and to keep skin covered when lift comes on, and to never set loose anything that cannot anchor. There is a rite of first spark, when a child old enough to feel pride is allowed to ground a line in a place where it will leap blue and friendly and smart him on the wrist. His grandmother will show him the burn later and tell him her burn was larger, and the whole room will remember their own and fall quiet.

Their myths are slow, local, not of gods but of paces and permissions. There are stories of Blue Years when you could hear a song through your teeth wherever you stood, when bead-worms grew too tall and toppled like drunkards and the most solemn reef-wardens laughed and took their boots off in the closed halls and danced. There are stories of two households who married by joining their drifts and changed the way a talus moved for an old man’s lifetime; you can stand with a descendant at the railing and see the seam where it was done and know that patience is a conjugal act. There is a children’s tale of a glider that landed against the wall in a bad year and would not go again even when its filaments were mended, and of the child who took it veils of combed salt every dusk until it remembered the angle of lift and left; he grew up to breed chandeliers with an extra rib in their cages, and one you can still see sways in a place the child’s people walk by every midday.

Time runs on two tracks. There is the tight time of all high places: the moment-to-moment sense that one careless motion shortens your day. And there is the sprawled time of angle and field: the knowledge that the wall prefers certain dispositions and you prosper by aligning with them as an iron filing aligns with a field. Households record both. On the back of a family touchstone, where a stranger will not see, is the tiny incised record of each year’s first dawn fissure-music—was it tinny or rich? Did it start late? Did it come in two voices because of a settling two shelves up-shelf? And in a room that belongs to the city rather than to anybody is a wall of annealed glass you cannot read unless you have grown your eyes here: it holds a hundred years of heat-passages and charge patterns, underlaid like the grain of wood, and boys in the last years before their work choose a hand’s width and by touching and tilting and breathing on it decide if they belong to chain or reef or fiber or drift.

They are not alone in their intelligence. There are things that think more slowly and more narrowly than they do and that they include in their word for neighbor. A bank of bead-worms can be made to add and even to keep a kind of count of past lift days, and old women who keep them speak to them as you speak to an old dog whose grammar you love. The mats in their high, dry way respond without fuss to gentle shepherding, and the feeling of guiding your thumb along a crack and seeing the faint fuzz bloom there the next dawn has made more than one poor house into stewards of a rare wealth. It is not a city built to monopolize. Industry happens in rooms like shrines and sheds as big as squares and in recessed halls like rib vaults, but never sprawls over the face. You can look up a hundred meters and see a bridgeway like a line of cloud shadow, and beneath it a talus as delicate as frosted sugar still settling along its preferred angle, and on the edge of it a child with a little screen in his hands persuading it not to cross the path—this is governance. The word for law and for lullaby are related.

They bury their dead in the city, not in the wall. It is not superstition; it is practicality and kindness. You do not plant a body in a place where a thousand-day drift could move an elbow for a century. Instead skin becomes glaze. Bone becomes a ceramic that a granddaughter will carry as the weight in her ground-cord when she walks into a place where she expects to laugh. There are directories that are also windbreaks; in them plates embossed with the names of the Held form the wall that quiets a talus, and when the talus has at last been taught the quieter angle a plate is taken and turned so that the name can be read and the rest go on holding. Every morning a boy with clean hands passes with water like a rumor in a dropper and touches it to a corner of each plate, not to clean it but to let it taste a little before it flashes away.

They make art with what will not last. They draw on skin with dust, then walk into afternoon lift and let the gentle rain of grit lay an embroidery of current on the patterns until they glow. They build glass mobiles set for a single Blue Year’s hum and hang them deep in rooms where they will never be touched by wind, and when the hum comes they sing for a season and then go mute and are disassembled, because silence is as much a kept state as song is an event. Their music is made against the body of the city: fingers on ground-lines, heels on the slow belly of a chain, mouths barely open to hum into the hollow bones of their forearms. Someone who has never lived here says it is a people who do not sing. They laugh and say it is a people for whom song is a kind of engineering.

There are arguments: about who owns a talus edge with a newly adjusted angle, about whether a reef should be allowed to sing out loud enough that you can hear it in the street in a thin year when songs are dear, about whether to try a new solvent in a loop and risk a smell that doors cannot keep in. There are lovers who pull each other into courtyards in the amber hour and break the law about noise because they cannot help it and are fined and talk about it for the rest of their lives. There are criminals, but even their crimes are considerate: theft of heat, embezzlement of charge, the quiet shorting of a neighbor’s ground-line so that his plates will sing too bright and crack.

If you ask them what they fear they will say not Down—the thought of fall does not live in them as it does in us; they trust the wall and their own bred skill—but the foolishness of hurry. “Haste makes slides,” they say, and mean more by it than loss of footing. They mean a kind of bad faith with a place that has gathered itself into patience over the era of a plain becoming a near-vertical ocean. They mean to live not as a separate species from their town and cliff but as a further articulation, like a new rib of chandelier, in a body that was here before and will go on after. They mean that the wall is generous if you consent to its pace and direction, and that a life among its faces is a practice, not a conquest.

And in the cool before dawn, in the rooms blown sweet with heavier gas, you can find a family asleep along a hex-socket wall: a parent head-down because it is a glider’s posture and feels, somehow, honest; a child’s fringe splayed on a copper pillow to bleed the last busy static of dreams; a grandmother with her hand against a little coil so that if the Blue Year comes again in her rest she will be woken by hum along bone. They will rise when the face makes its brittle music and measure the day by which ribs speak and which silences do not. The city will ground itself, and the people will touch their stones, and the first thin fizz of mats will stand up at the angle of its own consent, and the animals, cleverer than songs and dumber than law, will rock and bank and tick into motion. Nothing will hurry. And everything will, nonetheless, go on being done.
