They live by ear.

Not because they lack eyes, though many of them do, and many more have traded color for the confidence of darkness. It is because the bowl has taught every body here that to persist is to participate in a single, unending act of attention. A life in this place is a kind of listening, whether you are root or skin, chitin or quartz.

The first residents you notice are the ones that make noticing possible. The chorus is not incidental; it is the architecture of presence. Frog after frog after frog, each throat a bellows for a particular pitch, each body staked to a patch of wet as a monk is to a cell. They don’t live in ponds so much as in sentences. Their calls are not lures or love songs alone but positions announced and renewed: I am here; there is space around me; I will leave room at my edges for you to make your own noise. They hatch in the bromeliad cisterns, in the leaf-cups that never empty all the way, in the old pans that are more memory than water. Their tadpoles eat incident and drift; their metamorphoses happen in syllables. You can track a generation by the way one note gives way to another, the octave-stepping of a lineage.

Under and between the frog nations, insects pursue their different literacies of vibration. Crickets tow threads of air with their legs until the threads sing; katydids carve notches in the night and move their names through them, a calligraphy you can taste on your molars. Beetles keep the low, steady friction that makes the bowl’s heat feel inhabited. Moths gloss the air with wing-velvet and read the bats’ braille; some of them have learned to throw false echoes like chaff. Ants use the ribs of rock as resonant corridors and talk by tapping; their undergrounds are shallow but wide, an architecture that honors the basin’s refusal to stack lives.

Bats do not roost here in numbers that would shift the temperature, but they transit like swift ideas, articulating the bowl with echolocation so fine you could sketch its cross-section from their account alone. Each pass is a measurement; each mouth is a ruler thrown into the air. They own the wingstorms that come without warning, a sudden cooling wash that sweeps from rim to root as if the basin itself needed to be brushed and set.

On the stone and within it are the slower, denser citizens: millipedes carrying lacquered histories, reading with their legs; snails pulling stories behind them, leaving syntax wet and iridescent; flatworms that are the negative space of light, dissolving their paths into coolness; salamanders sheening themselves along fern ribs, taking turns because turn-taking is survival when everything else is pulse and pool. Spiders don’t hazard webs to light; they lay lines like frail fences in the habitual lanes where rodents and other soft-stepping mammals (a handful, sensing edges rather than horizons) have learned the bowl’s perimeters by heart.

Plant lives here are neither background nor scaffolding; they are hosts with their own schedules. Cloud-trees stand far apart because conversation needs air, not canopy. Each one raises a sky of cups and pockets. Orchids have accepted a covenant with shadow and produce complicated, understated promises that pay in fragrances no day-blooming bee would recognize. Bromeliads arrange themselves in levels like an audience, catching drips with theatrical precision. Liverwort writes cursive in gloss and matte, drafting new sentences of moisture every time a breath passes. Lianas tune themselves to tension and rainweight; their beards wick the bowl’s wetness down to roots that have no patience for thirst. The meadows of moss are less lawn than lung, inhaling footfalls, exhaling spring.

There are others you will not see unless you kneel: rotifers corkscrewing through the meniscus-world, copepods rowing their transparent boats in bromeliad oceans, tardigrades grinding their bear-mouths in the folds of a moss leaf. Fungal hyphae lace the peat into a memory foam for time. Bacteria graze on the electrical whispers of mineral and root; some of them glow when no one is looking, and even when everyone is looking, the place decides not to show that trick.

This is the obvious multitude, the flitter and tremor you could inventory if you allowed yourself to betray the bowl’s third rule. But there are also the less comfortable answers to the question who lives here.

There is the bowl itself, which is not stone alone. The mountain holds its syllable open, and that open becomes a patient organism. Its bedrock is tuned, its pans remember, its flutings are not decorative but the recorded trial of water and time discovering how to speak without boiling. When a deeper boom goes under the chorus every twenty heartbeats, as regular as a vow, that is the basin releasing one thought and accepting another. Its microclimate is an organ it plays to keep its own shape. Pressure-currents sink and idle here; humidity pools because the bowl has learned to make gravity choose a path.

At the rim, in arcs planted like punctuation at the thresholds, live instruments. They have been mistaken for machines and for bones; they are—depending on your willingness to count making as a form of life—both, or neither, but certainly alive to the extent that they have habits. They hum when the bowl is hungry. Fog beads along their lengths as if reminded of what it wanted all along; dew collects as if it had been invited across a threshold. They do not corrode because decay is for materials that do not enjoy being themselves; these arcs are pleased with their work. Their warmth is not a fire but an intention, a quiet adjustment that tips the balance from almost-water to water. They are tuned by patience. A pole in the peat tastes the wind for them, spins its minute vanes and tells them, by angles so slight you would call them fiction, when to lean into the world’s dryness and when to back off. They adjust themselves by daylight nobody here watches.

Who tends the arcs? Now you begin to approach the persons of this place, and you will not get a clean answer. There are marks of former hands—stepping stones blackened and then no more stepping stones; a path held open so long the roots began to remember it; a tree sealed with not-sap that has become beehive arithmetic—and there is a house that is mostly absence, with a round door, with slot-windows, with the feeling of listening built into it like insulation. The house keeps its shape without insisting on it. If it contains anyone now, that anyone is a verb more than a noun.

Call them listeners to have a handle. They are not a species you could sketch and not a family you could induct. They are an office the bowl created and certain hands agreed to fill. When they go about their work, they do it mostly by subtraction. They carry no lamps. They teach mist to prefer certain edges by brushing those edges with mineral and thread. They tip an arc by a finger-width over a decade. They lay a stepping stone into a pool and let frogs decide whether to sanctify it. They seal a wound in a tree with something made of plant and patience and ground bone so the future can graft over it without remembering the hurt. If a wingstorm begins to come too often, they change a ceiling in the canopy by a leaf or two so that bats feel the corridor that is better for them and for the moths. They don’t farm; they edit. They don’t harvest; they encourage.

If a word is needed for their body, use “porous.” They are built to admit sound. Their skulls are more ear than vault; their ribs are tuned so that bass settles between them like a cat. Skin that can drink humidity and keep its salt. Eyes that close to a nail-width and open just enough to count leaf-shapes against the color of dark. Soles that shrug off moss with courtesy. Palms that can feel a drip and tell you how far it fell and how long the air took to make it. Their tools are light enough to make no comment to gravity. A line and a weight. A paddle of mineral paper that suggests where fog might prefer to bead. A bundle of vanes like a child’s toy windmill, inflected to read more precisely the language the pole has been reading for centuries.

They do not call this place home in the way valleys are claimed. They arrive and depart like weather fronts. Their visits are days braided into years; their sleep is diurnal, pressed into the hours when the bowl is quiet and the quiet becomes loud with its own mass. They keep records, but the records are scores of sound and charts of drip—long cords strung with knots naming a night when the deeper boom slipped by a heartbeat; a slab etched with notches for the century when a fern first colonized a buttress and the decade when its shadow asked for a new angle. They are careful with names because everything else here is careful with edges.

Are they alone? No, in the way tidepoolers are never alone on a rock that looks like emptiness from a distance. They are accompanied by apprentices that are not children and not human—owls that have learned to wait for a hum of a certain pitch before eating, bees that have been taught a square flight path so the arcs do not accrue grit to their leading edges, a snake that has understood a stepping-stone path is not for it without having to be hurt to learn. There are also successors that are not kin: the arcs themselves which, if no hand would come, would still tilt a little over centuries because fog and gravity and the shape of air remember how to be courted.

Everything else that lives here lives here in the first sense: plants rooted and not; animals whose circuits are ironed into lanes; microthings that make peat and undo it at equal rates so time feels gelled. They do not think of themselves as ruled, but as arranged; they participate in a composition the mountain has been writing and erasing since before the plateau had a name in any mouth.

From time to time, someone from outside steps over the low lip without meaning to, and their boot enters the piece as an added instrument. They will be assigned a note; the bowl does that, not the listeners. The note is not moral but factual: you are a body of this size and temperature, and you are disturbing the air in this pattern, and this is how you will be woven in until you leave. If you do not bring a light, if you learn to pace your heart to the twenty-heartbeat boom and let your breath be counted by a drip, you will have the sensation—not false—of being tolerated. If you hurry or glare, the abundance here will appear to thin as eyes and throats and hyphae pull back from edges.

In the dim that calls itself morning, the frogs close their instruments and put them under their tongues; the insects disassemble their teeth from silence; bats knit themselves into leaves that are not there. The arcs alter their hum. The house hums a counter. Something that might be a person and might be an office presses a palm to a wet stone and says nothing, because that is the right way to live here. They are not keepers of a garden, though the place is garden-green; they are keepers of an O, of a shaped breath in the mountain’s mouth. They make sure it stays a vowel you can step into, and not a cave-in, not a choke, not an echo that has forgotten what spoke.

So the answer is a plural with no center. The bowl lives here. Sound lives here. Water in its always-almost state lives here. Frogs and bats and moss and spiders and microteeth live here. Instruments live here that pull drink out of fog by remembering what cold is. And a kind of person lives here, now or once or again, whose labor is to listen until listening becomes action, to adjust a future by the thickness of a hair, and to leave so little trace that you think they are no one. They are the ones the place lets through, because they behave like weather and not like will. They are not the point. The point is the chorus, the spacing, the fact of a breathing held without strain for as long as a mountain is able.

That is what lives there.
