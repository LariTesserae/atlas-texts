What lives here has learned that constancy is a kind of shelter. Heat that never stops being heat. Water that never stops being water, only cycles between skin and air and back again. A machine beat that does not surprise you. In a room that knows its edges, anything that can make a life out of a rule does.

The colony you notice first is not human. The ceiling owns itself and them together. Tens of thousands of bats live like shingles and logic: mothers with thumbs hooked into the fat seam where two pours of concrete met; bachelors clustered at the chill edges of the vent housing; pups the size and temperature of a small apple tucked against ribs they can feel through their faces. They speak in an unceasing pressure that only sometimes resolves, if you stop, into tiny lines of meaning—hunger, heat, the right distance between you and yours. They time themselves against the pumps and the fans. The dusk lift is a weather change—those brief minutes when the shaft becomes a throat and the room breathes out warm, fur-scented air and a respirable rain; at dawn, the world draws back in, everything new-swarmed with the night’s caught protein. In between, they keep their own industries: grooming lines, heat exchanges, mothers reminding the room that mammals live here by letting milk be smelled by things that have not smelled it before.

In the walls and under the lights live the ones who are not fussy. Roaches make and remake their city in the grout lines, glossy as licorice when you catch them with a lamp, shredded cinnamon when they’re young. They surf long waves of paper when someone breaks a rule and leaves a list to pulp; they tenderize crusts into molecules and teach their larvae to recognize the kiosk woman’s bread bag by vibration. Cave crickets hang like punctuation marks and fling themselves like errors, so uncanny that men with steady hands will startle anyway. Millipedes move like handwriting, dark and articulate, slow enough that you can count segments if you are of a mind to count anything. There are mites so fine they are mist with legs; springtails that take off when the surface tension snaps under their feet; little larval furies boring perfect radius holes through the gray scum, turning the sump’s skin into a lace you would not put to your face.

Water is busy, and the sump owns a population of its own. The fish are pale not because they have never seen light but because light here is a rumor. Somebody seeded them once—mosquito fish meant to keep summers liveable down the corridor—and they grew a temperament for dark and warmth. They live where the pipe mouths jet—narrow sleeks that stitch the pool into territories as if it were cloth, learning the schedule of drips like a clock face. They take guano snow the way cities take leaves: unremarked but structuring. In the same continuities, there are glassy amphipods whose whole career is to scribble at the meniscus; threadlike planarians with eyes that are two dots, comically certain of each other; a freshwater shrimp so small you have to want to see it to see it at all.

There is always one long thing. Call it an eel because that is what it looks like in the white flash when a spanner bites into a live busbar and the whole chamber blooms into daylight for the quarter of a second it takes to make your heart lose a step. It is old because length is a way to measure time when you cannot count birthdays. It has learned the room. It is not blind the way cave names for blindness go; it simply does not ask light for anything. It turns its jaw into a hook and hangs in the warm jets where a valve constantly leaks riches. The workers know about it without ever having planned to know: they talk about the Line, the Cable, the Thing With No Shoulders. They leave it alone; it is an agreement like all the agreements here.

Amphibians set up their patient camp at the edges of the pools of heat. The frogs like radiators, flat-backed and the color of whatever they last sat on, throats turning lantern-white and lazy as the heartbeat they mirror from the big pump. They feel along the grills with their hands and memorize the taste of the condensation; they know which screws shed a drink, and when. Salamanders are rumor at first. A thumb-long, bone-pale head disappearing into a pipe mouth gives away the lineage. They are the understatement of animals. Egg clutches appear like snot bubbles under valve shoulders and hatch out at night, and the ones that live become the ones that ghost the seam six months later. Workers give them names—Coal Baby, Candle, Tooth—because anything that never comes to the surface like a proper thing should be made particular to be defended.

On the catwalk rails and along the base of the panels, rats keep a fast, thin world parallel to the thick one in which people work. They have a highway culture. They learn a rung count like anyone else who lives by hands and feet. The big gray with the notched ear uses the handrail as if it were a streetlight wire, upside down and smug about it. They crack open fallen beetles, stash desiccated bat pups the way children once kept marbles in tins. They have a calendar you cannot see: they know paydays and new pipe deliveries, recognize the slack heat of a shift turnover and the brief chance of lunch crumbs carried tucked in jackets because the kiosk is out of whatever was sweet today. They are self-respecters, which is to say you will never quite catch one who did not plan to be seen.

Lower still, too small to be watched in the manner of a mammal, live the engines that weather in place. You can smell them when the humidity picks up and the switchgear throws whiskers: the metallic breath of oxidizers, the charm-shop tang of sulfides. Iron bacteria paint tiger stripes where the condensation runs regular; others tidy nitrogen and leave the air in the vent a fraction cleaner than it was. Algae ghost a mat under the LEDs, so thin and the color of ash that you won’t know it’s there until your boot betrays you. Molds know which walls have been painted poorly and blister them back toward their honest wet. Slimes set their tables on the backs of every cloth tied to every valve so that the rule about not spinning what a rag can solve is not simply thrift but also husbandry; the room eats and the rags are its tongue.

Plants are few and have the gravity of decisions. The vine that remembers green drifts down pale as tendon; it grew toward the pump warmth all winter, embarrassingly earnest, and then plainly forgot what a leaf should be by spring. Workers talk to it. They tuck it back behind a conduit like a loose wire, then untuck it again because it feels wrong to trap something that has actively chosen to be here. Above, roots taste for the chamber. They bead, glass their skins, sip. They are very slow and therefore utterly sure.

People live here in the way standing water lives here: always moving, regular enough to trust. They sleep and cook somewhere else down a corridor where orange light soaks towels and a fan purrs like a cat that does not care about you. But if you asked them where it is that makes them think of work like a home, they would point with a dirty thumb back toward the sump. The pump has a cycle you begin to feel in bone and blood when a month of watching it has made it reflex. People time themselves to it. Braveries and lazinesses slot between the thrum and the surge. Men and women who climb in under the same lintel every day learn the same quiet: they hood their lamps without being told, they wrap their radios not to be scolded but because the bats’ panic would blow the morning and make their friends’ hours worse. They talk in the shorthand that trades use when they stand somewhere too loud for dates and nouns. “Valve five’s weeping.” “Guano’s fat, get the covers.” “Don’t go up; they’re hot.” “Hear that? Call it in.”

They keep a small tin money culture beside the great currency of heat: a kiosk welded out of sheet that would cut you if you leaned wrong, with tea that will skin if you let it, cheap batteries that take a charge if you talk nice to them, the day’s gaskets nested like coins in oily sandwiches of paper. The woman at the kiosk is everyone’s Auntie because nobody remembers what her badge once said. She lets kids hook their finger into the frayed cloth below the taps and feel how the leak makes a life. On off-hours the plastic chairs face the sump. Men in wet socks and women with hair under scarves and boys who are not yet trusted with a ladder sit and allow themselves the human decency of staring into a moving thing that does not need them.

They have rules that make a community good at not dying and good at not being needless trouble to the other lives that have found a steady state here. They hang the plates that say the ceiling belongs to them because someone broke it once and made a hail of mammals that do not understand falling. They scold each other about paper because they have swept enough paste to feel old when they bend to do it again. They do not use the word “vermin” out loud; the rats are neighbors like the people in the building you do not love but borrow sugar from. They will throw a nut to a frog—everyone does it once—and then, later, stand in line to sign the sheet that says who did what because nuts do not help frogs and rules are there because you are not always your best.

Because the city is a creature and this is where some of its organs live, the city itself lives here too. It breathes up and down the shaft on weather that is heat and negative pressure. It sweats where the rock pulls water from its own blood and the air can hold no more. It feeds on rain you cannot see and on the spill of men whose days are full of carrying and wiping and calling in. Its nerves are hung on porcelain like worry beads. Its rib cage is painted to look new until the place reminds you whose house it actually is by peeling the paint back in long, tender curls. It has a smell like old coins and like hair after rain and like something a hand remembers from a handle it has held a thousand times.

Visitors arrive by mistake or design. Moths choked by sodium light spiral down like burnt stars and end in the fish. A swift, once, looking for a cave of its own and finding a ceiling already spoken for, writes a single scream down the shaft and leaves. A pigeon wanders in and never learns a way back; for a while there is a hush of feathers in a corner and then only a curl of gray under the stairs. Every season there is a school trip disguised as a safety tour; children whose families do not live under the fan walk with their heads tipped back until they cannot help it and a drop takes the inside corner of one eye and the space wakes them up with a cold blink. A vagrant is found asleep on the plastic chair one dawn, arms crossed, face rained on by condensation, so governed by the room’s sleep you cannot bring yourself to be angry. He leaves behind the smell of outside and a newspaper glued shut by humidity by noon.

Time lives here and moves in two gears. There is the fast, which is work and wings and rat runs and valves clattering when a man loses his patience. And there is the slow, which is rock discovering under city how to be cave, scale learning to think like stone, tufa telling the sump what it wants to be, rust bagging a bolt head the way vines take back a fence. Children mark the soda straws with pencil lead at their tips and come back months later with their thumbs like calipers, and because they have proof they recruit their friends: look, it grew. The old men track rust up chair legs and laugh at it, and in the same laugh is a sorrow for the belovedness of objects that will not last.

If there is a spirit here—and someone who sleeps beside fans and heat long enough will give that thought its own chair—it is not a single ghost. It is a composite animal made out of bats’ pulse and pumps’ hum and the patience of water and the hands of people who carry wrenches like priests carry books. It is the behavior of life drawn to warm and wet with a promise that, if you mind the rules and clean your shoes before you step onto the paint stripe, there will be tomorrow. It is not grand. It does not want to be. It is the modest market-sized square of a world making itself over and over: beetle wings mortared into tufa; saliva-slick pups gripping concrete seams with flowers for thumbs; rats going highway hands over heels along a pipe; Auntie tapping a spoon on the side of a dented kettle; a thousand pale stitches of fish working the sump as if to mend it in place; light glancing from a maintenance arc and showing, hard-white and briefly, all the lives mid-breath—and then the lid of darkness sliding back on and everything, all of it, carrying on.
