What lives here is distributed, and then, once you’re certain it’s a crowd, it resolves into a single presence again. Not a person, not even an animal exactly, but a way of being organized in wetness and wood. You can call it the householder if you need a name. It wears the plant as its only garment and the chamber as a cuff of that sleeve.

Its body is mostly arrangement. Where you see ribs and flanges and leaf-lipped openings, it feels flow impedances and pressure heads. It thinks—if thinking is the keeping of thresholds—by opening and narrowing thousands of little gates that never become visible as decisions. It moves sugars in one direction and water in another, then braids the two in cambial seams where growth is negotiated. It takes the marsh’s thrown noise and sorts it for its own accounting: tide-hum goes to root; sun-clatter and wind go to leaf; the minute grain of drip and hiss stays in the node, like a hand on its own pulse. The loudness here is its respiration made audible by architecture, its private hydraulics turning public by the way the walls curve.

It eats, though you wouldn’t think so, but its eating is light-first, and water-second, and trace-third: a diet of photons it converts to sugar and a drink of solvents spiked with ions, all taken through surfaces that look like lacquer and glide. It leaks as much as it takes—oxygen out, sap-scent out, resins self-sealing—and those leakages are the pantry that makes tenants possible. Where a healed cut glitters with sugar crust, that is the householder spilling a little into the commons. Where tannins stain the pools tea-dark, that is it sequestering what would rot it, and in doing so laying out a table for others who enjoy the bitter.

And there are others. They are even here when you cannot see them.

Pressed tight to a warm ridge that runs like a cable under bark are small plates of life that you would mistake for more lenticels if you did not watch them long enough to count how they do not breathe. Scale insects: the matriarchs seal themselves down along the phloem’s shallow course, pin their needle-mouths in, and let the householder feed them. It does not care. The sugar flow is wide; the cost of their tithing is written somewhere else. The ants that might farm them are rare in this wet footing, so the plates learn to be quiet. They cast a wax over themselves that sheds the constant condensate. Their honeydew rolls off in clear beads and joins the varnish on the healed wound; it is their signature in the room’s polish. Under one of those plates, if you were a splinter thin enough to slide, there would be a nursery: a film of eggs laid in a shallow smile of wax. When the young come off, they walk a little on the green light, carry a static from the plant’s faint ticks, and then choose a seam that runs warm to settle and become a dot.

Under the broadest root there is the custodian you already noticed: the transparent shrimp that sweeps the sump. It does not have a name in your language that is not borrowed from glass and hands. It has a broom for a face, and it breathes with all of itself in water that other fish would call scarce. It has, if you watch in the late day, a habit of stepping into the ripple thrown by the chamber’s tallest drop and letting that movement comb its feeding hairs; it times its fanning to the metronome of the ceiling. When moon fattening swells the tide in the floor a little, the shrimp feels it as a slow, low tide of body-memory—estuary-born, it knows brackishness the way you know your tongue. It carries eggs sometimes under the arch of its body, green-pepper flake small, and fans them with a rhythm it did not invent; it borrowed it from the householder’s valves.

A frog the size of your thumb is not here all day, but this is one of its apartments. It remains such a good tenant that the plant has rounded a nook to receive it: a slick the exact width of its belly. It takes its water through its skin while it sits, eyes unmoving and gold. It drinks, sometimes, directly at a lenticel; it has learned that those tiny mouths breathe out a vapor that carries iron and finished rot, and it puts its own mouth to the plant’s and trades nothing but calm. When it is hunting, it uses the chamber as instrument: the single spider line, the click of a lenticel, the way gnats drum the web like a yard-rail. When it is not, it becomes resemblance: another bright bead about to fall.

On the algal seams, snails work in single brushes, drawing clean lanes like squeegees. They rasp the copper-green into their dark and lay a line of snot behind them that another grazer will later graze. Isopods ferry themselves along the white highway a fungus throws between floor and ceiling; their legs tap a tiny code into the plank. Copepods ascend like fell sparks to ride the small comets that each drop drives through the pools, then sink back to the tea. If you are patient, you will see a water strider lay claim to an oval of mirror and defend it with the arc of its meniscus, not by force but by balance. A stranded minnowlet—call it a fish if you are gentle—keeps itself alive not on the strength of the water’s given oxygen but by taking what leaks from the plant’s own breath; it knows the seams where that oxygen is richer. A spider anchors its line not to twig or leaf but to two sapsweats that the householder keeps replenished, then sits with its toes on bark to read prey through plant.

All of those are subcontractors. They carry out conversations begun elsewhere—mouthing algae into snail, filtering rot into shrimp, spinning gnats into frog—and their labor feeds the next labor in a small chain that curls tight around this room. They don’t report to anything. They discovered a dependable factory din and synced themselves to it.

If there is a single resident beyond the plant that does not come and go, it is a kind of attention that accumulates in the node. Not a mind with a center; a spread-out noticing made of repetition. It is the way the plant chooses to be this shape and not another, the way it keeps the groove of the main drip true, the way it braces its ribs to throw xylem hiss back at itself until the friction is readable as a gauge. It “hears” cavitation the way your jawbone hears your own voice under water: transmitted and internally confirmed, turned into minute changes of pore and plate and membrane elsewhere. It does not worry about you. When you press your mouth to the bark and take in the phloem’s slow thump, it adds the heat-shadow of your face into its calculus, then lets that shadow leave like one more cloud passing the leaf. If you lay a finger a moment too long on a cool streak where xylem runs and warm it, the resident rounds you into an average. This is not indifference; this is a different scale of care. It cares in decades for grooves and gloss, in hours for drip cadence, in seconds for sieve plates opening and closing. It notices what repeats; it lets what does not fall through.

Sometimes, rare enough to keep stories scarce, frost crawls near. The householder salts its sap a hair with sugar so it will not freeze; it closes a certain fraction of mouths so the slower waters do not cavitate into ruin in the first direct sun. The frog is elsewhere then. The shrimp is shoulder-deep in the root’s felt. The scale plates go quiet and wintry-clear. The resident that is repetition contracts to a smaller breathing and waits inside its own room for the same sequence to return, and it does.

Off to one side, beyond where you can comfortably reach without getting a sleeve dark, are the beginnings of another node like this one. Plants make sons and daughters not with limbs but with habits. The householder duplicates its favorite efficiencies outward as wood thickens: another bracket where noise gets thrown into itself and made legible, another shallow pan where tannin-title water can stand. The inhabitant spreads by cedar-heart inches. Nothing is born all at once.

There is no ghost here that asks for worship. If you believed in overseers you could kneel and give thanks to the foreman of this little factory of flows, but kneeling is your expertise, not its requirement. It will do its work whether or not your breath fogs the opening. It lives as dryness’s opposite: a continuous, modest compulsion toward movement inside a sealed skin. When you leave, it continues to exhale the soil’s recipes through its rivet mouths. When you return, it will be exactly as busy in the exact same key. The long shine that the same crab rubbed into the same root edge will be brighter by a hair. The groove cut by the main drop will be truer by one undetectable lick. The residents will be on shift. The householder will be here, wearing noise, wearing green light, not minding you at all.
