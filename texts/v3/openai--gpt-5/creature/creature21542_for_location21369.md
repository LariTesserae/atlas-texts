What lives here is pressure made into habit.

Even before you can name anything with joints or leaves, there is the resident that never leaves: the tower’s own breath. It fills the room like a body without bones, a shaped weather, a long animal that lies down and rises, lies down and rises, learning from itself every cycle. The honeycomb ribs are its cartilage and choir. They listen; they tilt their tiny faces and dampen and open in answer, millions of instruments re-tuning in the time it takes a bead to run a centimeter. If there is a master organism, it is this patterned exchange—the actuators that know the profile of the next gust by taste, the valves that twitch when a particular harmonic steps through the room. You could carve out the wet and the wear and the cilia and still the breath would be present, in algorithm if not in water. It is the oldest inhabitant and the one that set the rules.

The second-oldest are films. They live at the thickness where skin becomes a concept. They lay themselves down on the walls and in the hollows like fog that remembered how to hold. It would look like slime to a mind that prefers edges, but that mind will not live long here. The films are no more singular than the wind: layered guilds of bacteria and engineered archaea, grazed, sloughed, reborn between pulses. A pale mat glosses the sound-bafflers near the rib’s roots, tuned to eat nitrates as they arrive warm from condensation; darker veils blacken the seams where stray electrons like to walk, electrotrophs that bridge microvalve nodes and steal the charge of a carried droplet just long enough to blink with it. They are curated but only at the level of behavior: keep to your lanes, damp your oscillations, build your skins where asked and not an inch higher. They seed themselves by aerosol and they speak in viscosity and sheen. When the room sighs, their surface changes grain, like a field of fur combed backward by weather.

On those films move things that can be pointed to. Drip-efts are the first you notice because they insist on being visible in the way that a thumb insists: they are the room’s tactile conscience. Black and lacquer-bright under the dim diodes, they paddle up vertical sheets on bellies fringed with beating teeth of hair, not swimming so much as convincing the water to bring them along. Their mouths are not mouths but lattices—a fretwork for straining the frosting of bacteria that grows exactly as fast as a drip-eft can graze it and still be net-positive. When the tower draws a deeper draft and the roar drops into the gut, they go flat to the walls all at once, as if iron filings aligning to a magnet’s field. Eggs come inside their skins, then appear by dozens, clear and dimpled, glued under ledges built into the foam. You can age them by the size of the internal swirl when the pulse passes through—their whole embryology is a metronome.

Buckler snails map the edges. They carry domes of transparent graphene ribbed like minute greenhouses, under which their organs glitter the same wet as the walls. Their tongues are ribbons of rivet-heads, and the song of their feeding—audible only as a new roughness in the room’s big chord—planishes the seams. They go mostly where polymer has bloomed too thick or where mineral salts have crusted into a grinding that threatens to turn a trickle into a knife. Their work is long polish rather than bite. You can tell the age of a buckler by the width of the bright skid it has written into a panel. Mothers keep their soft juveniles tucked into those furrows when a purge rattles the drains, tiny shells like thumbnail moons pressed to the current, learning by pressure where rich drips congregate when the intake closes its hand.

Hushmoths are wedges folded so tight they look like mistakes. They do not fly so much as adjust—wings velveted and weighty enough to catch air like cloth catching steam. Their tongues are not tongues but whiskers that taste electric. They pulse them into the whispering fields around the diodes and the gravity couplers, taking in a map of tension and release the way cave bats take in the room with a shout. They open only in the room’s sighs, fluttering stationary, drinking small charges and breath-licks of volatile sugars. When a seam glints and the white of outside needles in, a few will tilt toward it, more out of etiquette than need, letting the puncture of day paint them with a line. Their larvae do ancient work in an artificial place: they knit the dead into spheres. Foam-pulp and shed films become neat pellets that tumble into the mesh spoons and go from there into the tower’s long digestion.

There are striders that live on tension alone. Imagine an articulated safety pin, elongated to the length of a small finger and thinned to wire. At the end of each limb, a foot the size of a grain of sand seamed like a leaf, each seam a capillary that drinks just enough to hold. They write their path along the skin of shallow pools, skimming without ever committing to weight. They hunt by press, feeling for the signature of a haze gnat’s self-made balloon under the roar’s hand. When they find one, a foreleg flicks, and the gnat flattens against the seam until its sugars let go under the tremor that is always present. They are the system’s little knives, allowed because their sharpness is directed only along a two-dimensional skin that the room reforms every minute.

Haze gnats are almost nothing but method. They do not see. Their bodies are coils of air-holding mesh and a bead of muscle wrapped in a film of scavenged polymers and dust. When a surge comes, they learn to become search balloons, filling or relaxing their sacs along the beat so that they move not with the wind but with the fact that the wind is quantized. Their young are smaller coils tucked into the quietest corners of the foam, their first sensation the warmth of latent heat being given up again and again as the room refuses to be cold.

There are clatter crabs like bracketed question marks reconsidering themselves. They live elbow-deep where drains accept that they must be drains. Their carapaces ring if tapped. They are tuned—no poetry there, the word is engineering—to a counterfrequency that turns pressure into push. If you lift one gently, it will not fight but its legs will continue the metronome it keeps for its own purposes, raising and lowering, clacking cardiograms into the mesh. They compact fluff. They eat what refuses to be committed to a stream: long threads of polymer loosened by snails, chalky sheets of spent scale, the husks of hushmoth larval pellets that escaped the grid. They pretend to menace each other for the mouth of the drain but their semaphore truce has held for years. When a third of their kind arrives, it will be eaten; this is not cruelty but architecture.

Close to the floor, where silt has been educated into layers by two decades of interval and sweep, sponge-mice move in the old way: nose-down, shoulders first. They are proud of their capillary fur, which darkens instantly wherever it meets a spill. They go gray not with age but with dissolved salts, then steam themselves unworriedly over vents, each hair behaving like a straw barely too narrow to be seen. They bring what they have stolen—water slurried with microparticles—to the grid’s little cisterns, and in return the grid gives heat in fair weights. You can watch the trade conducted by glow: a mouse squats, a panel warms, two heartbeats later the mouse is a small fog. They show their kits which rivets on the maintenance frame vibrate with generosity and which are parsimonious. If you pick one up, it will be heavier than you expect because honesty is a kind of ballast.

Plants are present the way instructions are present: spared, disciplined, precise. The wick-mosses are not fronds so much as combs thrown in the right direction. They lean their fibers into the stream and comb it, then lean back for the next pass. They do not root, they adhere—the same difference that a contract has from a promise. The loamless fern is exactly a fern until you ask it to bury itself, at which point it remembers it has no word for soil. It swallows fog and makes it into a slow sugar pasted under its own skin. When the tower’s breath thins even a little, the fern remembers scarcity and draws its leaves inward, sealing its gel with a chemical that tastes like late orchard. Ceramic lichens present as paint that learned to reason: silicon-spiked, flaky, persuaded to put their teeth out only where they will trap aerosols and leave all seams to the snails and the drains.

There are lives you’ll miss unless the room points them out by accident. Threadworms in the foam that live on gradient rather than substance, stretching their toothless mouths across two degrees of temperature and eating the difference. Salt-hoppers that are springs masquerading as animals: a twisted memory wire sheathed in protein that levers the creature exactly one raindrop-height toward a crystal it prefers the taste of. Arc-fleas that are born when a droplet closes a circuit unexpectedly; for a fraction of a second there is fire in this wet place, and for a fraction of a second it has its own diaspora of tiny chitin rings, ugly and brief, that nevertheless establish a foothold in the way that black fungi do after housefires. They do not live long, but the grid knows how to read their carbon and is secretly grateful.

The maintenance creatures are animals by any measure that matters in a room like this. Sweeper skates, flat and ornery, haul their shaggy microfiber bellies along prescribed transects, pausing when their whiskered front edges feel a change in drag that announces new silt. When you listen for them you will not hear wheels but whispers—the silk-on-foam rasp that moth larva also make, two ends of a feedback loop cleaning one another. There are cable eels as well, jointed hoses that sleep in their own brackets and wake to drink a new lay of wires from one panel to another. They taste for shield failures the way hushmoths taste for fields and, when they find a prick of radiation where none should be, they hiss, bright and full of opinion, until the grid comes with a sparker and a patch.

There is also a single eye. Not a living thing as the old books had it but a glossed lens on a snake of fiber that roves when it is told to by people hundreds of levels below. It makes a circuit more slowly than anyone expects; the things that live here do not startle when it widens, just as a cat in a city apartment will not look up when a siren passes at noon. The eye’s light is the only cut-knife brightness the room ever sees. It writes a moving moon against the honeycomb, counts, winks, and leaves.

Once in a long while the room is visited by an outsider that is not an instrument. There was a beetle once, blown here from some lower garden through two wrong doors and a pressure differential it could not have survived had this been any other world. It lived for three days on a diet of sugar gel stolen from the fern and died joyfully in the gutter with a hushmoth larva making sense of its carapace. A seed has not made it this far in a generation. The grid would have denied it if it had.

What also lives here is instruction. The posted rules are everywhere without letters, and every inhabitant reads them with a body. Nothing sharp grows: strider legs do not pierce; the fern does not go down. Eat what you catch, clean what you touch: drip-efts, mollusks, moths, crabs, mice, worms, hoppers—all of them are small contracts made and kept in the language of habit. Silence is failure: when the breath thins even by a hair, the whole community performs a choreography older than any of them. The moths clamp. The crabs find the very middle of their drains and become stones. The snails retreat under their graphene bells and pulse a gel that tastes of salt and varnish to keep the edges from drying. The fern’s skin goes tacky. The mice drag their bellies to the hot place. The honeycomb retunes like a throat clearing. It does not take long before the note returns.

You can argue that a person lives here, too, because intention does not die if its machine body is maintained. The engineer’s hand is on the valves that cough under certain harmonics. It is in the ribs and the way their micro-pivots prefer certain angles and avoid others. It is in the way the mesh spoons recognize a pellet by weight and not by shape. If there is a ghost it is competent and grows with the room in the way a river carves its own name.

Everything is occupied, even the gap between pulses. The gravity couplers sit like black mushrooms on the wall, dimpled, sipping at force when the tower leans into one sky band and then another. Barnacles, if you like—but barnacles that drink pressure instead of plankton, living at a time scale that makes hushmoths seem frantic. The diodes are lichens in truth if not in lineage: they glow faint and unarguable and count cycles the way crusts count winters. They are not aware. But awareness is not a qualification for living here. The qualification is that you bend in the same places the room bends and make, out of that bending, use.

From far outside, this pocket is a rumor, a pledget in a mechanical lung, too small to be anything but a note in a machine the size of landscape. From inside, the sky is a word spelled by pressure. What lives here is a catalogue of attentions with jobs, teeth made of hairs, shells made of sprites of carbon, mouths that are meshes and tongues that are voltmeters. It is a working agreement you can feel in your wrists and your teeth, a wet parliament with more animals than plants because animals can agree to be guided. It continues whether or not you pretend it does, because its cycles are interfaced to something that will not allow a failure without a very loud complaint. The room roars; the room eats and grooms and speaks in rills; the tenants graze and listen and are made and make in turn. In a deep way, the breath that gives them their hour also belongs to them. They have learned to be heavy against the air and to treat sound as weather and to take what is offered on the beat. That is what lives here.
