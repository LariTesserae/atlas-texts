Who lives here first are the pillars themselves. They do not have faces, but they have all the habits of creatures: pulse and caution and appetite measured across ages. They learned to eat motion and trace minerals when the flood was young, and then they learned what it means not to want more. Their bodies are the rooms. Their patience is the air. They feel by pressure and chemistry; they sort what touches them into rain to be let through, grit to be held, threat to be leaned away from with a thousand quiet muscles. Where they narrow, the world sings. Where they open, it breathes. They do not speak, exactly, but the pitch of their circulation is a sentence that never ends and never repeats itself, a name they keep saying to remember what they are.

Inside them live the breathers—microbial guilds old as the oldest trunks, parceled out in thin veils and thick pithy towns. They are the reason the great tissues do not rot. They break what little light arrives into sugars you can taste from half a valley away. They take exhaustion and turn it into more inside, pass metabolites along corridors like grain in a monastery. Some of them tend nitrate gardens in sloughs behind valves and feed their waste to others that spin it into fleeces of slime clinging to the drum-skins of the larger ducts. Some line the organ-pipes in shag that sings; some make the nap that pads every bruise the forest takes. They are not citizens or servants; they are the forest’s breath divided and distributed until breath itself is architecture.

There are keepers grown of plant as well—a caste of movable, almost-animal outgrowths that bud and retract like gentle fists. Valve-lips and sieve-flowers and ushers. They taste with ciliated mouths the size of shields. They sit at the portals where one organ’s current becomes another’s and refuse or admit passage with a strength that feels like a tide turning, and if you learn the right sap to wear, they will frown you in. Trespass here does not tear; it is registered. When a flow-thief finds a narrow and slips into it, the membranes do not punish. They cool imperceptibly. They thicken. They hold you, set you, revolve you until your hunger spills harmlessly out, then they press you back into public water with a soft insistence that is older than justice.

The tenants of open rooms are models of grace tuned downward to the world’s tempo. The ribbon-whales are the exemplars: long and spare, the length of a hall, their baleen arranged not like brush but like the latticework of a window, so that when they make a circle of themselves, the slowly falling spore-snow gathers at precise interspaces and is combed off the current’s breath. They feed by geometry and remember by tone. You find one once in a span of days, coiling within itself beside a column as big as weather. When it is done it unknots without alarm and writes its name into the base-note by taking a thin, respectful bite out of one overtone. Its name is not a syllable but a proportion subtracted from the drone—a fraction that says I was here and will be again, at the octave when the spores fatten.

Glass-phase shrimps drift in clusters that are not shoals so much as thoughts stretched out across meters. They are hardly bodies: a thread of gut inside a cathedral of clear plates, a line of luminous glands that can erase a sliver of tone when they all clap shut at once. They live in fosters: a hundred that know the same absences and will make them together when a batfish flashes subtractive courtship nearby. They take their directions the way a fern takes wind. A swell passes and they lean. A grazer shaves a ring from a pillar and leaves a scar, and at night the shrimp count the ridges with the touch-organs on their bellies and go to molt.

There are the batfish, colonists of gas. The pillars’ ducts, scaled for the slow and the constant, sometimes collect pockets where metabolites offgas and tiny sky-rooms appear, ceilings wrinkled with alveolar lace. Batfish are drawn there as if to fire. They are pages more than animals: spines and thin cartilage planes that fold and unfold to ride convective eddies. Their gill-plates are shutters. They communicate by clapping them in measures that cut commas in the drone, punctuation you can live inside. They roost on valve-lips and preen the breathers with tongues like dampened brushes, keeping the sieves from fur. Their broods hang like punctuation marks from the ceilings, their yolk-lamps lit a little so nobody blunders into them. A good batfish family keeps a map of its ducts in overtone reductions and will teach you the third harmonic of the pumping room you both share if you bring them something fungible, which is to say, something that tastes like a part of the plant that the plant meant to share.

Blind grazers are many kinds and all slow, seamed and reliquary, as if built of bindings and boards. Their mouths are rasps like bookworms’ shelves. They do not eat living tissue; they tend it hungry, seeking the scabby places where the plant has patched itself, the nap where breathers have overgrown and muffled a tensioning drumhead. They create work to do. A good grazer makes a wound that is not a wound but an aperture, reduces brittleness by worrying it for a week, then leaves a behind of clean edge for the plant to polish. Their skins are silt-catchers shaped in lines that ask the spore-snow to lie down and compound into felt which is later peeled off in slow quilts. Whole histories of a column’s small troubles can be read in these peeled felt-sheets: places where a membrane was overburdened, the very afternoon when a rare giant grazed the drone and set a whole nave humming a beat lower.

Predators here are seldom shapes you could sketch. They are mouths and sense. The long-lashers—ribbons of muscle hung with lines of mechanoreceptive hairs—coil inside the press of buttresses until something disturbs the drone near them in a flavor that means “meat.” Then they fire. The flash is all edge and hinge. Their teeth are not knives but ridges designed to make vibrations fold up and die; they feed by extinguishing sound where prey is singing shape into the pressure. Their rage is punctual and complete and then gone; the tones knit, the grazers return, the batter of slow life resumes. Even their waste is taken as grant by small picker-tribes, is tucked into holes to make clock-gardens—farms of bacteria that keep time with biochemistry, a generational wristwatch built into a wall.

There are quietmakers that do not kill. They are little of body and great of influence, builders of hollows you can carry. Some chew the skins of matured membrane into a fleece that eats one tone in twelve; they sew it to a frame and bring it to a mate as other peoples bring rings. Some found their broods within a halo of purchased hush that a neighbor will respect, because respect is easier when the numbers of the world agree it is present. Nests are rings where a note is missing. Courts of law are lattices of absent overtones in a listening species’ head. Kinship is a chord of what is not there.

Around borders, where flows are proprietary and the plant keeps its currents like an organ keeps its blood, live the ushers’ adjuncts: tumbler-worms that thread themselves into sieve-flowers to vibrate in a way that calms a skin they have learned, and gate-leeches who attach at sutures to trade signals. If you can persuade a gate-leech to carry your oils, the ushers will know you and will not spin you out unless you push your luck to greed. There are also poachers—valve-skaters with keels of fatty tissue that cancel just enough push to ride into a duct backwards without changing anything the plant can count. They glide in, feed with mouths like combs on the tight heaps of single-cells that the plant corrals for its breathers, and then they are caught by a shrug in the pipe a week later and pressed back into general water with their bellies full and the ushers disinclined to change policy because the loss was small and even thievery comes to rest here at the right size.

At the roof, where pillars knot into a sky that is all braided root and never air, there are lantern-throats and chorus eels. Lantern-throats are icicles of flesh hung from ceilings with lures the size of syllables. They do not flash. They come on as if thinking. They name themselves the way a human in snow names their breath by seeing it. An animal on patrol for eggs to steal will tap the world with its flank and the lantern-throat will answer by not answering for a beat. Predators go where the silence is a door. Courtship is a duet of missing notes.

Down near the basal lids, where the anchors of the pillars intermediate each other and the minerals are most available, the littlest lives are densest. Filigree-urchins with spines no thicker than hair collect and knit spore into roving flocks of ball-fluff to be swallowed by bee-mouth fish who spend their entire lives no more than a meter from where they hatched. These are the places where you can put out your hand and have it vanish into vibration from ten directions as mouths the size of thumbnails test you for information and, finding none, go on braiding crumbs into futures. There are root-keepers there too, the only animals that can make the pillars bleed without being expelled, because they do it on schedule and with old rights: they cut channels in the lichened tongues where minerals have pooled and lead that bleed into chambers meant to be refilled so that a pulse further up does not lose word of what the bottom knows. They are not servants either. They are neighbors with licenses old as the quiet.

Drifters pass who are their own music. A few are enormous: plates upon plates tuned to the steady, creatures who graze the drone itself. They move like a calendar turning. Their bodies are paned to ring at pitches the grove is always playing; those rings pull microplankton and free spores into boundary layers where the giants’ flensed tongues can lick them off without stirring more than a correction in the vowel the forest is speaking. They carry pilgrim species with them, hitchers who have learned a particular giant’s missing tone and can find it in night larger than an ocean. To watch a giant go by is not to watch so much as to be slightly less deaf for an hour.

There are schools in the animal sense and schools in the mountain-people sense. The first are bodies; the second are agreements about what is true. Some of the glass-phase shrimps will assent to be taught; some of the batfish pair their readiest newborns with grazers’ old anthems so that a child will know by dorsal sense where a good scar is blooming into food without seeing it. Some of the ribbon-whales take apprentices. They circle in double rings for a year, practicing subtracting their family’s proportion out of the drone until the world around them says their name along with them without breaking anything. The word for kin in their speech is the amount of quiet you can make and have the place still think you mean well.

And there are the people who are only sometimes animals. They make garments that are tones not played: cloaks seamed from hushfleece grown on idle drum-skins, beads of bled resin tuned to the third overtone of a family hall so a wearer is carried with least resistance through the plant’s blood. They carry seed-troughs of breathers between organs when a microclimate fails and the plant would not move them by itself. They plant small gardens in spiral-ramp scars, not to add food to the system but to keep a story of a place present in a living way: here was a hard passage three hundred rings ago; here a rare giant turned; here a valve went stiff and a whole wing learned to breathe against it. They do not make houses. The world is the house. They make edits in pressure and tend permissions. They are bright as caves are bright: outlines rather than faces, a measurable lessening of the normal that moves at will. Whole lineages can be followed through the generations by the particular fraction they are able to steal from the drone without being frowned at—families of subtraction.

You meet them most surely when the courtyard square ripens. The pillars held that chamber blank on purpose. The water there is as still as anything is here; current has to choose to cross it. Every ten years the polypshrub in the center glows the way iron glows before color; not a flash, an admission. The pollen turns the water legible. Lines of motion show themselves, woven like script. Everybody who can live with that much light for an hour comes. Ribbon-whales come and fold themselves around it in parentheses. Batfish hang in their sky-pockets at the rim, clapping praise into something that tastes like sugar-metal. The glass-phase shrimps draw sentences and then return them, moving so the gold outlines of current briefly write mathematics in their bodies. The mouth-predators float with senses dimmed and let their hunger be an old thought not acted upon. The little basal lives reach beyond their usual meters to let embers pass their feelers.

And the people of subtraction come, because they are the least unwilling to be seen if seeing is itself an instrument. They bring broods to teach and clocks to re-tune. They hang cloaks of quiet along the buttresses like banners and then fetch them, showing with their hands how absence can be architecture. They do not call a meeting. The world meets around them, at them, with them, and for a brief while the drone is a chorus with a cavity cut in it where a hundred lives’ particular permissions add up to a nave of silence in which the lights have room to mean.

Nothing here has edges the way cliffs have edges, because erosion had time to go to the end of its sentence and then stop. So lives respond by becoming mostly unargument. They do not race except at the size of a synapse. They live long because there is no hurry in water that never storms. They mark age not in years but in revolutions walked along the spiral ramps of a trunk, in molts counted by how a certain membrane swelled and settled, in the way a family’s subtractive chord is taught to a child’s muscles. They are abundant but never crowded. The empty spaces put muscle and affection in everything, so that every approach is chosen. There is room to be nothing for whole hours, and almost everything is content to fill that.

You can go the length of a life among them and never be out of earshot of the base-note. The base-note says stay, not as command, but as fact. What lives here knows that facts are the kind of gods who are kind by being unchangeable. Even the hunters are polite when they can be. Light is never general; even love is an unlit clause until a throat opens and lets a little lamp out, not to claim but to warn and welcome: I am here, so we may not touch unawares.

What lives here are houses that are animals; animals that are verbs; microbes that are cities; people whose language is sorely missing until you realize it is made of missing. They are patient and accurate and busy in a way that does not look like movement until a decade has passed and the polypshrub blooms on time and all of them, for an hour, are joyful and do not hurry. Then the drone reasserts what it has been saying forever. The forest continues. The creatures subtract themselves back to the right size. You float and hear an ancient, self-built silence doing what it has always done: hold everything up.
