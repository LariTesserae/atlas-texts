What lives here first is wind. It is not a visitor or a force to be beaten back; it has a route through the place, is given mouths and throats and bellies in scoops, louvers, long gravel beds. It learns the habits of shutters, it sleeps light and wakes quickly, it hums in mesh and moans in the gaps of enamel numbers. You can watch it herd mist, carry pollen, ring the copper chains in the dew courts like a hand brushing a beaded curtain. Some days it is a courier of the cold; some days it feels like the soft bellows of a great animal, patient in its breathing.

Next is water, but not as a wanderer. Here it is resident, enrolled, apprenticed. It arrives as absence, as a promise that leaf and mesh keep, that copper chain and lip fulfill. It stands in the bellies of cisterns with a frog on its back and an overhead circle of light; it treads the hand-wide channel in a slow black run that has memorized the mountain’s tilt; it beads on the midribs of obedient leaves and then thinks better of falling anywhere it has not been invited. Water here has a calendar and a ledger. It has rumors: a sound in the float-cocks when pressure is rising down-valley, a cut on the wind that says a cloud bank has been clipped on a far spur. It has futures: a planned braid of drop-lines to a terrace that will not be finished until two children have grown into their harnesses.

Plants live here as citizens rather than ornaments. They have jobs and routines and pay in shadow and sugars. The ribbed species whose leaves scoop their dew like a careful hand have old studs driven through their collars where cords take their per diems away. Vines clock in by waking the cable they occupy with a slight additional drag; epiphytes sit in the rafters like bottle-fed cats, cupped throats keeping custody of sips until it is time to decant to a thirsty neighbor. Trees take their turns on their hinged collars, turning against the worst and toward the best, and the ones that have weathered five repaintings of a truss are given names and small plaques on their masts with the dates of their own storms. In the cradle walls, roots have fingernailed stories into felt and brick-crush. Moss files reports in the language of green on seams where designers wanted a slower drip. Hedges of peas move in to knit gaps. In very deep shade, lichens draw a living from light that feels like the afterthought of brightness and seem content about it. You will not find a bedrock profile anywhere that isn’t padded into comfort; even the mountain itself has learned to wear upholstery.

Machines live here with temperaments the way barnyard animals do. There are the steady ones—the conveyors that tick in their sleep, the little turbines that spin like watch gears and would fuss if someone oiled them out of their familiar resistance. There are the skittish tools that prefer one person’s hand on their toggles and jam when a stranger leans close. There are the fog-nets, all nerves and hum, whose skin changes voice from a dry speaking to a wet whisper as the morning ripens, and there are cooling fans that sigh all the way through a sentence before deciding whether to stop. The place is not dominated by engines so much as inhabited by them. Their schedules are posted, their moods noted in chalk on boards at the workshop doors. Their waste heat is another citizen: laminated into walls, coaxed across seedling beds. When the belts stop and the big doors are slid to, you feel the silence like a resting herd—not absence, but the agreed-upon ending of a shift.

Birds live here like handwriting scribbled everywhere. The swifts are daggers and jokes, the kind of spirits that wait for the second after you decide nothing will rip past your ear. Pigeons are inevitable and endearing and cynical as shop-foremen; they love the ledgers of corn spilled at lunch. Bright small-things—tanagers with hot coins for faces, sunbirds that make metal look dull—arrived as rumors from lower slopes and did not leave. They have figured out how to draw mosquitoes from the dew courts without tangling themselves in the chains. They nest in places that were meant to stay clean and their soft vandalism gets tolerated in proportion to how much color they lend to a corridor in the rainy season. On the upper catwalks, raptors come sometimes and reorganize the smaller lives for a day or two, and the wind writes again in different lines.

Insects build a city that you can only notice in the second hour. Dragonflies lay glossy borders around the widened reaches of the channel and duel with the gravity of gnats. Mantises, installed by someone who talked too much about balance and was right, sway with their back legs hooked in a basket’s wicker, priests with the time to wait for sinners. Bees keep clean narrow ways between trellis flowers and brass bolts that have warmed enough to smell sweet. Ants understand more about the place than anyone: they have runs through cable trays, they keep maps of the most reliable drips, they know whose lunchbox reliably drops a crumb on the same bolt head every day.

Frogs sleep in water voices, and bats unspool themselves into the turbine shadows at dusk and make night a mesh of moving black. Skinks array themselves beside warmth—pipe skins in morning, brick ribs in evening—and doze with their eyes open like competent old mechanics. Mice go in and out of root baskets and are seen as a cost of doing business, and it is not a cost that anyone complains about unless the ledger has had a bad month.

People live here most obviously, and also most fittedly, until you have trouble deciding which parts of them are gear and which are habit. They walk with three points of contact learned before they could write their names, and when they laugh on a parapet they hook a boot over a bar without seeming to think about it. Their calves are used to ladders; their palms have the memory of cable slack and dew-wet chain. Above the goggles-line their skin is sun-poached; beneath it, leaf-stained. They smell of iron and sap both, and every work shirt has a pocket where a folded page lives, because if a thing is not written down it is not really here.

They speak with their hands as much as their mouths, and in their hands there is a grammar of pitches and bolts and drips. There is a whistle code that rides long corridors and hatchways like swifts—two notes to call a cage up from level four; three to ask for a door shepherded in the wind. There is a metal-voice, too: tapping on rails with a wrench in patterns that can say someone’s name, an alarm, the joke of the day. Voices are low because the place already speaks enough; songs are matched to the speed of a belt, to the thirty swings it takes to lower a louver without it clacking. Children learn to count in droplets that fall from chain to tile on fog mornings. They trace the age of their home in the color ladders of paint on a friendly truss and grow up with favorites among the enamel numbers on the lift cages as if those tens were cousins.

Their work has guilds, though they might not call them that. There are Net-tenders who read fog the way their grandparents read clouds, who can tell you if the black mesh is whistling because of a wind shift or because a seam has gone stale. There are Channelers who keep the hand-wide run clean with brushes that look like the tails of exotic fish and have small calendars written on the undersides of their wrists where you and I would have watches. There are Bafflewrights who set the pitch of shade so seedling and knuckle have places to cool; Cistern-keepers who fish leaves and frogs from bellies and know each float-rod’s private peculiar sigh. There are Armaturists in the galleries behind long windows who hot-rivet joints with gestures so practiced someone once danced them on a decking at a festival. There are Soil-Makers who smell a scoop of crushed fired brick and call it sweet or not yet; grafting hands who can persuade two branches that they are better as one. Ledger-folk, of course, mind the small balances; their rooms are cool and perfumed with damp paper, and they have the respect due to anyone whose subject is both mathematics and mercy.

Food is not an industry so much as an accompaniment. You will find it in the quilted walls—beans and peas first, then soft leaves, then trellised fruits that surprise you by tasting like sun reflected off metal. Trees in masts bear slow crops—figs and small citrus—that are accounted for with feasts. Mushrooms live in rooms with the feel of ship’s holds, lit as if by the idea of a lamp. Honey moves through the place like a rumor you can taste.

Families live in hangs and hollows hung back from the bright fronts. Their doors look onto small courtyards shaded by baffles that have been stitched with the same care as quilts; the courtyards hold water bells and basil in clay jars and a bench where the sun reaches at an angle you could set a clock by. Children sleep swaddled in webbing and dream of being mantises. Older people nap in shirts that smell of paint and say, without shame, how many repaints ago this or that thing happened. When they die they are spent carefully. It would be wrong to pretend otherwise. They go into the soil, into the brick-crush, into the long slow chains of phosphorus and names. A leaf catches their breath and hands it forward. Somewhere a plaque appears—a small enamel number on a lift cage has a second number, faint, companioned—and those who understand nod at it in passing.

The place has seasons that have nothing to do with calendars. There are runs of days named by the mouthfeel of air: Knife-days when light cuts and you do bolt-work; Milk-days when fog and mesh sing and you pull on the small, careful gloves. There is Paintyear, which is not a single year but a cycle, each truss stepping down its color ladder notch by notch across a generation. There are Feastfalls where the first water of a deep storm is welcomed loudly and then contained, and where, afterward, ledger-keepers go pale with pleasure at tidy columns. Shift-change is a migratory event; the cranes creak and call to one another and small wagons trade levels along rails, all their enamel numbers gleaming like a string of beads shifting on a dancer’s neck.

Strangers live here, for a time, in the form of forearms and jokes from the next escarpment along, because yes, there are more. People come for a season to teach a way of tying a net so it hums a half note lower, and they leave behind a child or a word that makes it into the whistle code. Trade runs lightly. Those long conveyors are the ribs of it: baskets for the next cliff, bolts for some valley town’s old wall, a spool of mesh wrapped like a wedding gift, a set of ground-slate sacks that smell like rain’s cousin. In return come ore and salts, a crate of dried fish, a coil of copper someone coaxed from an exhausted mine.

The legal code here is mostly made of rules about where the wetness goes and when. No water goes unaccounted does not mean no water is shared. It means that the sharing is careful and remembered. It is a favor paid back in basins refilled, a sweetmelons’ rind left where the ants expect it, an extra handful of mash for the birds on a bad gnat morning. You can tell where the law lives by where the charts are hung: in cool rooms with brick bellies around them, in shade, in quiet.

If you sit on the topmost catwalk as evening removes its jewelry, you will see everything that lives here adjust itself by touch rather than hurry. A hand on a cable. A leaf folding its catchment edge in. A dragonfly expanding the ownership of its pool by another palm-width. A net-tender counting the first five bells of dew court chime under her breath and then turning for home. A child walking a line of bolts with the seriousness of someone treading a coastline. Somewhere below, a ledger closes like a good door.

What lives here is not a city precisely or a farm precisely or a machine with vines growing in it. It is all of those and none. It is an arrangement of attentions, a place that teaches its inhabitants to see water even when it is air, to hear metal when it is plant, to feel time not as a tyrant or a blur but as a friend that has agreed to walk alongside. It is bright and loud and living, as promised, and it admits no haste not because it cannot move quickly but because it has learned what kind of quickness lasts. Over years even the rivets round their shoulders, and so do the people. They become themselves in the manner of bolts polished by repeated hands: not made smooth by neglect, but by touch.
