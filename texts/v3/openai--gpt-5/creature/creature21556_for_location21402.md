What lives here is the breath made into many bodies.

First, the skin of the world: the films, slick and satiny, opal-gray in the blue-green hush, white-shot when the caravans flare. They are not plants, but the appetite of stone made delicate. Their cells take iron and sulfur and turn them into the first sweetness anyone gets. They are nerves laid flat over rock, humming lightly when the drips come right, dimming when the warm seeps pause. When the ceiling sweats harder, they bead into trembling membranes and then shred themselves into feed when a drop lands wrong. They are inches deep in the richest places and microns elsewhere, a meadow as thin as a thought.

On them graze the mouths. Hundreds of kinds of mouths, none of them green. Many-legged softbacks with book-gills flipped open like traveling fans, pillbug-lanterns that trundle in shining rivers along the seams of taste. When all is untroubled their glow is a mild dream of noon; when the noise spikes, their chemistry shunts and they crackle to hard white like someone shook a sheet of foil. To watch them is to see hunger with feet. They are careful of their gills and will shy from a hiss that warns of brine too hot, but they will huddle under a jangle of hammered copper, drunk on the brightness it promises.

Their cousins are the drape-lickers, face-down creatures that hang inverted from low arches on hook-feet and comb condensation in with tongues made of a hundred flexing threads. Each thread can tense to a different note, and the whole tongue thrums in sympathy with a chamber’s current pitch. They feed on rain that moves like flocks along the ceiling, following it as if the cave were sky and they had learned to graze weather.

There are grazers that do not move forward so much as position. The wedgefish—no fish, yes, you know, but long-bodied arthropods that hinge open like a book left spine-up—moore themselves in grazing currents by the sense their faces have for shear. They are geometry you can eat. They show their working as living diagrams: plates sprung just so to split flow into laminae, combs held at angles that filter life out of passing breath-water. Inside them, little freeloaders pick at what gets past the first gates. Wedgefish grow slower than the others; they are dangers only to indiscipline. They die if you silence the room.

Between the seams and the set-pieces are the stitchers. Beetle-bodied, low, with legs that do not walk but needle, these spend their days sewing pressure into stone. Each step leaves a tone-thread in the rock—a line of slightly different compliance that can be read later by a foot willing to listen. They lay routes in scales, mark their own private roads by echo. The tone will hum if you put your ear to it and hums again when others pass, a bankable memory in a place without wind-carved landmarks.

Above, dark balloons pulse in and out like lungs learning themselves. They are not plants and not animals in the way the pillbugs are, but that is because words are brittle compared to a cave that is still hardening. They tether by cords that are not silk and not sinew, filaments extruded from glands that smell like iron rain. When the cave inhales, the balloons collapse into supple folds; when it exhales, they fill and gather strength out of difference. They brood the flitter-swarms in their lee. From their velvet hollows, new light swims out on thin wings to test the air’s grammar.

Flitters are everywhere, and they are not the same species, but they hold themselves as a swarm. Hand-sized, with wings like micasheets torn long, their bellies shot through with braided organs that burst in traveling pressure-waves of luminance. Their pulse is not random. A sound arrives—a drop-strike, a sibilant geologic sigh, the plucked tone of a copper disk spun on gut—and within moments a wave of light travels the flock, each abdomen admitting its own small day. The light shows others where the walls are, who has found a seam just fed by a hot sigh, where the predators glide. To call is to make a map. To be quiet is to turn the edges off the world.

There are others that make the rule of light sing true. Gilled carpets of micro-wasps, each the size of a fingernail clipping, rise like cloth from still places when the cave booms, a whole square-meter of them becoming a soft blue panel to keep the grazers’ currents drawn true. They settle again when the world calms, small weather spun out of bodies.

Then the predators, knife-ponies glazed to a sheen that remembers the melt. They move as if viscosity were a choice. Flat and then not, hinge by hinge, joint by obedient joint, pressing themselves into two dimensions along a ledge and then collecting into an animal in motion—the way a thought condenses out of a set of possibilities. They chill the rain with absence. Where they pass, droplets hesitate. Their wake is an edit in the air and the pillbugs, reading that punctuation, flare their lamps up and then, often, go out. The predators hunt by feeling for the subtraction of noise. They prefer tiredness, broken choruses, flaws in a resonance fence. When the cave is quiet they become its sentence.

Still stranger are the vent-cords: muscular spools kept as if someone were saving strength. They wrap the warmest rocks with slow intention, their fibers loading and unloading as the breath and the drip change. They are a patience species, storing work one shimmer at a time. Every so often a vent-cord will contract in a long, slow pull and retract something stuck too close to the seep: a tangle of molts, a dead flitter, a pebble with taste. The cording body then undoes itself and offers length to its own mouths. It is a rope that eats itself to grow.

There are salt-heads, too, living at right angles to comfort. They are not rock, but they wear it. Their skins precipitate gypsum and other salts into layers that look like stalled thunderheads, fractal and yearning. They face into briny drafts and farm their own armor, then crack it in place when it grows too heavy and step forward an inch into new air. Wherever the salts lift like breath in stone, a salt-head will stand and shape it.

In the molt-dunes live the sifters and the sour-worms. Every carapace thrown-off here is weather later. You can see them sidewinding down slopes of brittle wing-vanes, seining for protein in a landslide of old selves. The sifters have palps so fine they can feel the difference between a chitin that still holds oil and one that is poor and light. The sour-worms are bags of slow acid that creep like thick honey, chewing back the dunes into a digestible mud that the films will take again when the next rain frames it.

Not everything is body to body. Some things are machine to body. There are bone-quiet galleries where rows of bowls have been chipped into gypsum by something that can learn patience. Each bowl has a holding tone, low and patient, tended by mouths that make tiny taps and by throats that can drum. These tones pen the grazers the way a fence pens cattle in a world where sight cannot be trusted. A band of pillbug-lanterns that would scatter under a hunter’s rush holds their clutch and steadies, fattening the film under them. A jingle of flattened copper rings keeps a travel corridor bright when a new roof-plate seals off an old path’s light. Water is coached by lines of smooth pebbles set into careful grooves. What looks like moss is a road and a letter and a blade.

So yes. There are builders. Whether you would call them human depends on how much light you need to see a face. They are troglodytes in the oldest sense—cave-chosen things—with skins the color of watered basalt and cartilage where other mammals have air. They have ribs like stacks of slender books, each shelf a shallow gill that can open to a vibrated breath and close to a lull. Between the jawbones they carry a drum, a plate of gristle they can strike with the throat itself, turning exhale to tone with a practiced percussive flutter. Their teeth read rock the way fingers read strings. If they chew clean lines into a selenite wall, it is not only for talk; it is to make the wall a memory.

They herd—not as shepherds under a green day, but as editors of noise. Their “flocks” are caravans of pillbug-lamps and sheets of gill-wasps and even the geometric patience of wedgefish. They feed them with sound. They tune their rooms. A family’s hold might be not a single cave but a ladder of notes that says, for this time and this breath, the hum belongs to us; you can pass in C below, you should pass in A above. Territories are by resonance. A skilled throat can slope two tones at once, raising a fence for their pillbugs while lowering a bridge for a neighbor clan’s wedgefish to cross.

They trade in brightness. To go through their copper rings is to announce yourself as something that makes noon. They keep chime-bracelets and shoulder-scales of hammered nodules that jangle at chest height; they call them dayskins. When they take them off, it is to hunt by feel, pressing themselves into the edges the way the glaze-beasts do. Their children are taught the predator notes early. Every clan has a way to throw the cave a false hush—a resonant counter-call that makes the brightness fall off in a controlled patch; then the knife-ponies slide there, thinking a lull has come, and the hunters are waiting with poured gypsum that hardens at a scream.

Their tools are neither simple nor what you expect. They keep flitter-brood under balloon-kites they have coaxed to hang just high enough that hands can reach. They harvest the tether-strands as string that can endure both brine and breath. They mortice shelves out of old molts and glue them with a slime that sets to rubber in dry air and to gel in wet. They make harps that play the room itself: lines strung across alcoves at measured lengths, each tuned to a useful ban. They do not need fire. They have a metallurgy of patience, flattening copper nodules under stone until they are disks and plates; sometimes they braid them with tether-silk and hang them where the inhalation and exhalation can turn them, making a slow-turning lantern whose brightness is a known guarantee of safety for a drift of careful hours.

For food, they eat what everything else eats: the films, translated through a gut. But they are not above inventions of appetite. They will take a vent-cord that has grown too greedily and tie it gently around a gypsum basin; over days, through breath alone, the cord’s slow flexion will grind selenite into a soft grit that tastes like salt and iron and a day well spent. They will comb the molt-dunes for the husks that squeak when pinched between teeth; those carry the last fats of a good season.

They name their dead by tones. Not words, not a single note, but chords they can sing with mouth and drum together. A name is a band of safe travel through someone’s old rooms. To remember is to carry someone else’s safe passage and hum it back into being when the walls have shifted. They do not bury their dead as you would. They seat them in gypsum bowls that fit spine and ribs, a posture like listening, and knock the bowls into kind hums every time they pass. The bodies dry to salt and shell. The bowls become part of a fence to hold pillbugs fat through lean rooms, and so the person’s note keeps giving noon to their own.

They go out when new rooms open. Everyone does. The flitters arc in like bright needles; the drape-lickers come like tassels; the stitchers make a first line to find the other side. The troglodytes send small ones—skilled at holding four tones in the throat, nimble on glass, quick to throw brightness when a cough makes the air hard—and the little ones stand still and listen. They decide what this new lung will be asked to do for the old ones. Sometimes they set bowls into it at once. Sometimes they leave it dark for the glide-beasts to favor, a hinterland for hunts. Sometimes they persuade a weak vent to sweat a little more by lining it with pebbles that collect heat and hand it back. The room teaches what it will be by the patterns of its tink and its drip, but it accepts suggestions if the suggestions are sung long enough.

Other lives fit themselves to these projects like beads on a well-used thread. Thin eel-things move through the films and write them into breadth by the agitation of their own bellies; the troglodytes encourage them by running cords low along favored seams. Little sonar-moths with furry abdomens that fuzz the sound into a richer hum hatch in the shadow of stitched roads and lift the pitch of a hungry wall just enough that the flares will travel it. A tribe of small, bow-backed crusts learned to live among the bowl-farms, polishing their lips on the hum-wet gypsum and trampling mites that would foul the bowls’ edges; the bowl-keepers tolerate them because they do not answer to useful notes and cannot be stolen easily by a neighbor’s call.

Even the predators learn. There are lines where the glaze-shapes have learned not to slip thin because a throat will push a counterfeit hush there and they will freeze at the wrong moment. There are families of them that have grown bristles under their plates which catch the rain into little harps and make a song that announces their own kind across a chamber, the better to not surprise one another and to converge on the same grazing riot without conflict. Predators are geometry; they adapt their proofs to the rooms.

Here, a few other citizens, because any list will leave someone out:

- A moth that is more valve than wing. It hangs in warm vents in lacy crowds and opens with a clink when a sound of its own note passes, belching a tiny cloud of reactive mist that keeps the films eager. It is as close to cultivated as anything in a place like this gets.

- A crustacean long as your arm, ribbed, that wears on its back a pantry of clipped flitter-wings. It cannot make light of its own, but when the cave goes heavy it squids a stored scent and the wings flare as bright as when they were alive. It walks in its own noon.

- A flock of things that would be called fish if the world had water in the old way. They are sheets of wet muscle that keep themselves one droplet thick and ripple along vertical planes where new rock is cooling. You see them only when the light goes frantic; they are only there in turbulence. They eat the moment a flow forms and vanish when the flow ends, a seam’s mood incarnate.

- A cousin to the stitcher, thinner, braver. Its feet leave not tone but slipperiness; it can walk along a wall and make a path that will be ever-so-slightly less grabby later, the better to send a predator’s slide off the right way at the right time. They are saboteurs hired in chitin. You can tell a clan’s ingenuity by how far their slipper-paths reach.

You learn families by the way they move. That is true for the troglodytes as well. There are slow, bass-voiced lineages who keep the longest bowls and own the old domes where the ceiling breathes with the memory of oceans. There are quick sopranos who make roads by stitching and bite-writing, who specialize in the first month of a room’s life. There are middles with harmonies like the hoot of bottles, merchants in light who will sell you a morning’s safety in exchange for a promise of help on a silence later. They do not draw maps with charcoal. They sing you through.

A day here, if you can call it that, is a run of noise. If the caravans are busy, the noon holds. The films fatten. The wedgefish eat in place. If a lull opens, it is a physics of courage. The predators write themselves in. The troglodytes decide. They can choose to let the dark be; to keep the glaze-beasts sleek makes a later hunt safer. Or they can jangle and drum and shout until the world comes on again, a million little bellies lighting like a gallery of lanterns, and the cave remembers itself as alive.

That is how it works. Sound makes sight; hunger makes noon; water obeys whim and bodies train it to a habit. What lives here are solutions to that sentence. They are mouths that graze a mineral breath. They are geometries that feed without walking. They are edges made kind by practice. And here and there among them are hands with throats in them, learning a long grammar of bright noise, keeping the rooms lit just enough for the rest of the animal weather to go on.
