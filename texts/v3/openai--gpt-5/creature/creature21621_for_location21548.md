What lives here first is not an animal but a condition. Wet has a spine here, a girdle and a gait. It moves along the stone as if the stone were its own skin, beads itself into pulse, lifts a sheen and lays it down again. It has a calendar and keeps it; it hangs in the air with the commitment of a resident. It does not splash because it does not have to; it is everywhere already. It is the oldest citizen and everything else arranges around its patience.

Stone lives here too, but not as mountain and not asleep. This limestone has learned the trick of softening without going weak. It eats and gives back, dissolving in one room, laying itself down in another, an animal of the very slow kind that builds its own lace and grows teeth from the ceiling. It keeps memory in layers—thin menisci of attention, rims arcing toward themselves—until it becomes architecture that seems inevitable. If you listen for it, you would swear that stone breathes; you would be right in the only way that matters here, because every change of air is a negotiation between rock and water. That is aliveness enough.

Between wet and stone, in the borderlands where rules are made thin by hunger, live the first eaters: films the color of absence, slicks with a satin hand, communities so broad they forget the word edge. They are the stomach of the room. They take energy where no leaf would think to, bargain electrons from carbonic trickles and soluble iron, lay down their minutes as a darkening. In the slower water they line up manganese into trees, in the faster they polish calcite until it looks deliberate. They do not have names that hold in the mouth, only motions that do: glaze, creep, bead, knit.

On this the grazers write their small lives. A pale amphipod, curved as a comma, combs the limestone seam with whiskers that are really questions. The answers come back as taste: faint mineral sweets, the breath of a film, the holiday pepper of a drowned midge let down through the light-seam. There is an isopod here too—flatter, a little shovel of a creature—dull crystal under the lamplight someone brought, a suggestion more than a color under the room’s own indifferent silver. Springtails sometimes find their way to the entrance pools where a suggestion of outside leaks in; the ones that stay forget to jump, grow long legs for walking the meniscus and writing a trembling on water that looks like thought.

Worms happen in the silt like lines looking for a sentence. Translucent, the light goes through them and reads their last meal whole: a road of gray, a fleck of iron, the rind of something once winged. They ripple from deposit to darker shade, from darker shade to the comfort of a crack and back again, purposeful as a hand groping a known stair. Snails no larger than a pinprick make spirals on the undersides of submerged stones, breathing with the slow diligence this place rehearses into every living thing. Tiny copepods stage in the cleanest pools, flicking like punctuation where the drip is purest.

Sometimes the salamander comes, long-bodied and incapable of haste. It moves from basin to basin like someone testing keys in a locked drawer, counting with its hips. Its eyes are unlit stones embedded under skin; its world is fingertips and water-writing on its flanks. It has lungs because its ancestors did, because the world above once mattered; here it uses them rarely, sip by sip from air already drunk by stone. When it lifts its head out of a bowl you can see water break off its jaw in a single string and hum for a heartbeat, a luthier’s secret in the belly of earth. You feel you could wait out a life to see it do this again; the salamander already has.

Bats do not live here, but a bat once wore sleep on this ceiling. Its soft body made a print in soot that the damp could not quite undo, a negative of breathing. On certain quiets you might think you hear the lightest unshy scratch as a claw adjusts its idea of rest. This is a rumor the room likes to keep; rumors can live somewhere the way moths live in a cloak.

Elsewhere at the margins—where the dim still admits a shadow that knows what a leaf is—midges are made and spend themselves. Their larvae are citizens of the dripline, thin and transparent, thread-lives that eat the algebra of films and then write themselves out as flight. Some come too far in and name themselves mistake, falling ungently into a pool that names them food. In the clay you can see where something passed with more mass than a worm and less than your foot, a drawn-out S that ends under a ledge; eggs cling there, a clear rosary fixed to a darker prayer.

The air itself keeps a colony—millions of drops unfallen, each clinging so hard to the idea of itself that the idea becomes a substance. They host tiny things with no interest in your naming: spores that are not news to anyone but their mothers, bacteria floating with their mouths open for the faintest rumor of sugar. They do not travel so much as drift with conviction, which is a kind of residence.

And then there are the keepers who don’t live here the way a salamander does but live here all the same. They come rarely enough that stone can forget and then remember them with pleasure, the way a groove learns a rope. They are not a single person anymore but a series of hands that know how to be gentle with a surface that will outlast them.

They are the ones who placed the flat stones, giving ankle and habit a place to agree. They are the ones who pecked niches from wall and taught clay dishes to drink and leave a rim of chalk like a candle’s ring. They made a small circle of stones where the drips are truest, a piece of patience catching a more patient thing. They burned lamps that did not dare to loudness and let the smoke write its wet scripture across the under-sky. They left a copper nail that turned the green of age and decided to stay. They learned how to listen until listening felt like an animal you could feed from your palm.

They arrive with a rope, a skin to carry water that tastes like rock persuaded into tenderness, a tool wrapped in oiled cloth because the room eats edges. One wears a braid twisted with salt; another wraps their forearms because the cave makes a habit of tendering bone to stone. They speak here the way people whisper their own names when lost—soft, to keep from shaking loose the beads from the ceiling. They have beliefs, but they do not nail them up. Some say the water here does not spoil the way other water does, that it carries the room’s promise of keeping into the fevered and back. Some bring the first teeth of children to dip and swear into adulthood, because stone knows how to turn sharpness into use. Others come to ground a vow, the way you might tie a difficult truth to something that will not move even if you try to make it. One or two only come to learn how to be long at something; their teachers are drip rates and salamanders and the way a dish gathers its own ring.

They do not live here as householders, but their lives include this room. The room includes them back with traces instead of portraits: a polish where a shoulder brushed over and over, the faint dark of a path that the drizzle is in the slow labor of forgetting and remembering. When they die—far away, in air that moves—their bodies go to another stone, but their patience lingers here as surely as the copper nail does. Patience is a resident that can wear many faces.

Other occupants are verbs wearing shapes. Dissolve, precipitate, condense, bead, fall: they have tenure. Reflection lives here, not as a thought but as a creature bred in thin light that learns to copy itself along a stalactite’s belly. Quiet lives here, but not silence; quiet has a busy stomach and a full schedule. Weight lives here; it leans on everything, and everything has learned to give a little. Time lives here too, not as a river but as a blossom of calcite, each petal added by an idea as small as a drop and as certain.

At the specific edges live thresholds. One is the sink where water vanishes into the stone’s throat—things enter and do not come back with names. Whatever goes there becomes more profoundly of this place the moment it is lost to it. Another is the bright seam that will not admit you, a curtain whose thinness is a kind of courage. Behind it, blind fish the size of thumbnails taste their way along unseen galleries and are never anyone’s story but their own. Up the shaft lives weather as color without motion, a round and laconic god you can learn but not be warmed by. In the terraces live crowns of rimstone, kings that do not mind being stepped around as long as you step knowing where your stepping fits.

So: who lives here? Wet, stone, film, worm, crustacean, salamander, a few lost midges and the rumor of a bat. The low lamp of a person, now and then, who has learned to count in ticks and keep their hands to themselves. The tools of patience: rope, clay, a nail that stayed. A cistern that eats forbearance and gives it back as water. Above all, an agreement, older than any body, between gravity and carbonic acid, kept as faithfully as any oath. The room itself lives here. It is the kind of creature that never moves and yet, if you return after a life, you will find it has gone somewhere and taken you with it.
