What lives here is what you stand on.

Not beneath, not hidden in a soil someone else made. The living is the surface itself—layered into stone, veined with salts, skinned with films that are more decision than tissue. If you try to look for individuals, you lose them. If you look for functions, you see them everywhere.

They are builders first. They take what small water there is and make it behave, and the way they make it behave is by growing the correct edges in the correct places. Their bodies are carbonate raised out of solution by daylight, silica scootered into mortar by threads so thin they’re almost a rumor, salt trained to crystal into ribs. The work reads as architecture because that’s what it is, but nothing in it is overseen. The guilds know one law—to place—and they obey it with exactness that looks like taste.

Closest to your foot is the skin guild. You could pass them by if they did not shine in the morning like a damp page. They cling to the substrate in quilts a few grains thick, gluing dust and airborne ions into a film that can be water when there is none. Their topmost members meet the light, harvest it into sugars and pH shifts, and spend those shifts where the surface gives a hint it wants to become edge. They do not march forward; they thicken and they thin, so that surface turns to lip along a line they sense by capillary pull and temperature slope. Beneath them are layers that used to be top, bodies collapsed into glassy sheets in which your heel-print mends as you walk away. In their depth the old work is stored like a conversation—thin bands of pale, thick bands of honey, a green so faint it is almost an afterimage—every stripe a day the same rule was applied with different weather.

At the low fence lines they raise, the morning fluoresces. That sheen is not show; it is the toolkit laid on the bench. Pigments tuned to first light make a blaze your eyes register as a glow; at that time and angle they are most efficient at coaxing carbonate out of water. Out of the film they extrude a wafer, not unlike a nail paring, that hardens in place into the new terrace lip. A thousand millions of those bracts line up to become the curb you trace with your gaze. Where two curbs cross, they do something with intention: they leave a cup dark and cool as the inside of a wrist. That emptiness is as much them as the lip—the absence of growth is also placement, a kept hole where water can think slowly before it is asked to move.

The candelabra are a different expression of the same life. Copper-green because they must bleed heat quickly, each frond is a comb the width of a hand stacked for two or three human heights, all surface and little mass. Their hairs are not hairs but microgrooved blades, alternately wetting and refusing water on a scale that would look like a stitched herringbone if you could see it. They do not drink. They invite. Night breath beads on them and, compelled by grooves whose angle wriggles by micrometers along each filament, the gloss resolves into filaments of its own and runs to the base. Their roots are not roots either; they are wicks, thick with channel, that conduct those threads into the stone fence the skins have grown around them. By dawn they have poured a river you must kneel to notice. By afternoon they become still monuments, but not idle. Heat drives a gentle exhale through their stalks, water stored not for themselves but to lift a temporary cloud above the grove. That self-made shade is not indulgence. It slows the waste just enough for vapor to live another hour and enter some later cistern. They use temperature as a routing instruction.

Inside the crust, below, are the breathers. That bass sound in the junctions is theirs. They architect alveoli and tunnels whose sizes match the way warm air wants to rise and cool air wants to descend. Their membranes are not fleshly but mineral spun with gel; by swelling and softening, then firming again as light shifts, they tune the speed at which the basin respires. This is not ventilation for lungs; it is a way to keep the dew where it ought to be. They sense by resistance. When meniscus after meniscus at a cistern mouth climbs just so and won’t climb further, they widen a thousand pores a little until the surface relaxes. When a cloud dims the flatland and the breath slackens, they tighten to hold, and the place keeps its cool.

There are salt-keepers too, pale as old sugar and just as busy. On the plates where evaporation concentrates brine, they insinuate into the crystals’ birthplaces and insist that planes of growth pause and redirect. You hear their work more than you see it: a dry rasp, a sketching as crystals shuffle by micrometers, aligning into micro-terraces that can step a bead along where a smooth plate would leave it stranded. Their metabolism, if you call it that, tolerates saturations that would pickle other guilds; they fold salt into their ceramics, make wafers that accept and release with the day’s arc. They are the ones who turn spills of rain into deltas of glass.

Farther inward, in the wells the labyrinths keep from the sun, another color takes over. The tarnish-gold mats are shade-workers that read energy in a longer, lazier part of the light. They lay down not white but straw, not honey but bronze, and the stone stains accordingly. To visit their chambers is to see a perfect record of how long water lingers: the gradient around their pool is not decoration but a chronicle they grew as slowly as the hands of a patient clock. Where their films meet the porosity of the cistern wall, there live dissolvers who speak in acids so gentle you would not taste them. They nibble at old carbonate, free up ions locked too far from the surface, and hand them upslope to builders in return for sugars pooled in the shade. That traffic is as much community as any flock.

Everywhere, in the thinness, you will find the tuckers. They are the ones that fold the dead into the next layer without ceremony. No grazers come to keep the carpets clipped; here, the sun trims and then the tuckers tuck, fastening each spent sheet to the one below with gels that are glue when they are wet and glass when they are not. Their work makes strata that can lift dew up through themselves by capillary, a kind of inherited generosity. Their offspring do not look like them; they are dust, each grit bright with an oil that refuses to wet, so the night breath can carry them across an open kilometer without pinning them down. They choose where to land by waiting—not days but cycles. A spore will not sprout unless it has been moistened and dried in the same rhythm three consecutive mornings running, and even then it divides into different fates according to where it rests. On the lip it becomes a ridge-worker. On the plate it becomes a salt-keeper. Under a frond, if the shade feels like this shade did before, it makes another comb.

There is a blister caste—audible when the day is hottest, tick by diminutive tick. They live down in the honeycombs under the morning face of the mats and spend the forenoon trading light for gas. As heat increases, pressure points build along the paths they have laid; their bodies give way predictably along seams they have grown for that exact collapse. Each pop is a valve, the tiniest punctuation of the daily mechanic. The sound is not their language, but it is part of the basin’s grammar; the outward breath moves a hint of air through pores, ripples a line of dew uphill in response, and a builder somewhere responds by brightening its fluorescence.

The buttresses that look like lung capillaries are nobody in particular and everybody at once. They are communal artifact, the result of millions of choices to place shade at a step you could walk, then to stack those steps into ladders for water. No one decided to raise a nave. But when the ladders touch, vaults appear, and the air under them obliges by slowing and cooling. On a new scarp face, after a bright sheet failed and lay like a card on the table, pioneers arrive within an hour of the next mist. Their oils make the film bead instead of spread; they eat dust and lay gel; by noon a faint net exists, by dawn it is coherent enough to hold its own surprise: a meniscus where none has ever been. In a week there is a lip. In a season there is a gutter so trained that the next rain knows exactly where to go.

Because there are no mouths that move across this world, no teeth that correct mistakes by biting them away, the lives that live as surface must be their own editors. They do not hurry. When a vault collapses at the end of a long drying, the place does not lament. The skin guild thins where the shade is gone and thickens where the new edge tells it to. Repair is indistinguishable from growth, and both are indistinguishable from living. The only time something like appetite is visible is after rain, when everything makes more of itself at once—not out of greed, but because chemistry is briefly easy. Then lines you could not see a week ago are confident as pottery. Deluges leave behind glassy fans that glitter until the skin puts its nap over them and the glitter becomes a wiring diagram.

If there is mind here, it is made of gradients and held together by habits. Calcium waves travel the mats the way a rumor might travel a town, a pressure felt at a dozen points that becomes a decision to thicken at sunrise or to leave a hole open through noon. The condensers angle themselves by growing a little more on one side than the other, aligning to the faintest persistent draft. Salt-keepers adjust their lattices when the basin as a whole exhales, because the ease of breath bears on how fast saturation will rise in the plates. Everything consults everything else without leaving its place.

You could attempt names. Skin guild, comb guild, breathers, blisters, tuckers, salts; the shade-gold dwellers at the labyrinth bottoms; the pioneers who arrive where something has broken; the archivists who turn last year’s growth into this year’s subgrade. But any taxonomy you draw will smear at the edges. A skin-thickener under a frond will be more frond than plain; a salt-worker on a rain-fed fan will act half architect, half sieve. What looks like species is more like posture. What they have is not difference but repertoire.

They do not court seasons as forests do; they court hours. Their calendar is not twelve months of weather but the thousand repeatable gestures a day offers: glossing, beading, waking a breath, flattening a shadow, stiffening to release a tick. Morning is when matter feels most malleable to them, when photons fall at the right slant to draw stone out of air. Midday is arranging and holding. Evening is for letting go—a little vapor, a little pressure, a little attachment from the last lamina so it can settle and harden. Night is for sending—spores lofted by exhalation, the oil on their skins taking humors from nearby lives so that when they land, the basin knows where it has sent them.

On the edges of their world, where erosion has not yet learned to curl its finger and hook, they are bold. They drive channels straight because straight is easiest to keep clean. They accept a ninety-degree corner because nothing has come along to rub it round. They grow confidence into the shape of stone while stone is still young enough to be persuaded. Later, when some future of wind and an older kind of water has taught this basin to forget, they will either have been buried under their own good work or they will have receded to niches—the undersides of steps, the lee of fronds, the undershine of a salt plate. For now, they are the map.

Who lives here? Not animals. Not plants as you know them. Makers of lips and ladders. Combers of night and keepers of breath. They are unions of thin-limbed filaments and films, capable of lifting a ton of water an inch or two if it means twenty more minutes of dew somewhere else later. They are green only as punctuation and copper when it matters, gold where the sun has been tamed. Their bodies are their buildings. Their dead are their subfloors. Their hunger is to place one more edge where gravity will be obliged to help.

Stand still, and you become part of their technique. Your shape interrupts a draft and they answer by angling a hundred distant hairs. Your shade lengthens a meniscus and for a moment a cup holds its breath. Then you move on, and so do they, without moving at all. In a day you could learn the syllables of their speech: gloss, trickle, tick, sigh. In a life you could learn their one sentence, spoken again and again across a bowl large enough to be a country: we will make the surface behave.
