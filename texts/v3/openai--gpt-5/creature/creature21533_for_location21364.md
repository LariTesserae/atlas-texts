They live here in the same way a bell tone lives inside wood: not housed, but resonant. If you try to catch them early—fix them to a single body with a single word—they slip, the way a bead of water won’t be held by dry fingers. They are multiple and they are method; they keep to the density.

Outsiders call them people because they have hands and ache, because their backs are mapped with the same long muscles you know from your own reflection. But what lives here has been educated by ropes and cane until the body is partly instrument. Ribs answer wind with a held note, and—by long imitation—so do they. Their breath is practiced, kept warm and heavy so it falls when spoken, and when it falls it binds a name. That trick is not an accident of weather to them; it is a craft, and all crafts here are manners of gravity.

They do not keep names in the manner a town might. In the dawn-heat they lean over mats and trays and the wet edges of clay and put vowels into the air until droplets pearl on the things called. Those syllables, quick and short, belong to the mats for the short life of the beads, and then they belong to no one. Any name that stays longer is braided into rope and hidden in a knot. If you must call one of them, and you must, try “Keeper” and say it while your own breath is still warm on your lips. It will take for a while.

Their feet know rope without looking. The soles are split by callus into four pads with a reasoning space between; eyelashes stay wet; the clavicles tilt a little forward from a childhood spent suspended in nets. They carry their weight low, knees bent the way cane bends when it has just agreed to be a curve. Echo lives in their chests from lying awake under the drum’s long note; they can feel a gust coming through the ribs before the skin of the chamber answers, and their fingers move for ropes that have not yet tugged them. Their skins are varnished with seed-lac where air would otherwise wick their heat; hair is corded not for fashion but for catch, so tendrils of vine can take them lightly when they lean into blossom.

They are not many. Rooms like this teach smallness as a courtesy. The elders—if years count at all in a place measured by thickening clay—wear the place like a smooth second hide. Their gestures are the rules repeated until the rules agree to be rules. One will sit cross-legged over the hollow log that ticks and turn its end a finger-width to slow a heartbeat; one will stand listening at the valves, counting chatter against the feather-brush swing; another will peep through the oculus, not for scenery, but to test wind-bite by cheek alone and give the drum four taps soft as a moth. Children are everywhere that a lap of rope will hold a hip. They are taught first to lie still and name without waste. It is difficult to coax a child away from the knowledge that sound makes things tip; difficult and necessary. The first thing they give a child is a reed with a hole and a rule: low for fall, high for lift. The first thing the room gives back is proof.

When they speak, they do so in weather. A question is set inside a fog; an answer falls as a drop off a leaf-hair. They will not laugh close to a mat that is not meant to grow proud; laughter is a high keen and would tip the trays wrong. They will, however, sing when a valve slips and storms begin—the singing that makes parallel edges out of corners, the old singing the clay remembers by thickness.

Do they sleep? Yes, but never flat. They sleep cupped into nets, back to back, so someone’s shoulder stays against the skin and translates wind into a dream that can be answered by the drum even at night. Sleep is an instrument too. Dreams are used to teach the body to reach without waking for a reed’s mouth and bring the drizzle down one breath-width. Infants are strapped belly-out above fermenters in the safe hours, so that the first heat they know is the friendly fever of living rot, and their lungs learn humidity before language. When it is time to tend the fermenters, faces are undone: masks of waxed felt are pulled over eyes and mouth so that nothing with a face does the tending. That is how the rule reads, and even if you have not understood why, you will obey it after the first time the ferment sees you and quickens with you, and the clock of dawn comes out wrong.

They eat what is coaxed, what drips sweet, what is walked into their mouths by climate. The ferment gives them a broth that is part grass and part tongue. A lily of green meat is slit with a bone shell and spit into bowls made of a gourd that cannot decide if it is still a fruit. They will lean close to the seamstress-spider’s web, collect the slowness that shines there on a fingertip, mix it with ash-milk, and say it is enough. Once a month, at no fixed hour, a leather valve coughs a perfect fish out of air, and the nearest hand catches a fold of humidity in a woven scoop and lays it, still wrinkled, onto a hot tile until it becomes water again and then sugar. They do not tell outsiders this story. The snail with antlers is not eaten. It is the conductor. They watch which way the horns lean at a boom and reset the gutters by that small agreement.

There is sex and there is the other version of growth. Sex is quiet and tied, a braid of bodies fed warm water by wicks—do not romanticize it; it is also grinning and clumsy and full of the bedding’s sour-sweet. The other version is the way long fibers root, the way a sash forgotten on the frame for nine nights puts out hair. When one of theirs becomes old enough to be considered structure, the family binds a strip of bast from that person’s belt to the rib behind where they always sit. After nine nights they free the knot and see if it has taken. If it has, the person is “Posted” and you will see them afterward choosing chairs like a tree chooses shadows, if there were chairs. When one dies, some of the hair is carded into rope that will root, and the law in the rope will remember the watch that person kept. This is not figurative. A year later, the new leg of frame will “decide” to thicken in the hour they once sang, and the room will lean toward the old habit.

Not all of them are always inside. You saw their kin in the oculus’ edge, hung out in the roar with bone awls and sky-nets. They call those “edgehands,” and the inside keepers greet them with bruised arms and bead-licked cheeks when the harnesses change. Edgehands come home with wind knitted into their hair and a look that says the mountain is still learning where to be. What is true is that no one lives only in one chamber for long. The place is a lung in a body with many lungs, and they are the blood of it. They circulate, taking with them what each room has taught them about slope and wet naming and how not to scare the ferment. You can tell where one has just arrived from by their cadence; the ones who have come down from a colder hall walk with tighter steps and coddle the lamp-fungi with eyes turned away. The ones up from a hotter belly clap as they step to keep drizzle obedient.

Their speech, when it is not the work of drops, becomes cords. They keep a grammar of knots, a song of resin-tears. The back of a valve-frame will have a story about a mistake in stringing and the storm it brought; the story is kept by crossing one twist over another so the future will not forget how to sing the mistake back into parallel the next time someone gets clever with a slipknot. They can read a fingerprint in clay the way a scholar reads a page. The whorl says whose palm and what hour, what warmth, which set of teeth were clenched, and whether the solver was hungry when they pressed and listened. They do not write prophecies. They make rules until the rules are strong enough to hold their own weight without shaking. If they do dream ahead, it is in gross structures: a cliff that is not a cliff yet, a lung that will be grafted, a weave that will take song the way water takes a gutter.

There are taboos and there are courtesies. Do not dry your hands too fast; the chamber loves to take moisture and will take it from you if you do not give it in teatime measures. Do not eye the clock-fungi and do not put your tongue on the rope-river between trays, not even a little, not even to test your own speed. Do not laugh early; the frog will blink and the jasmine will waste its lift on your joke. Do not harvest the moss that looks like animal tongue. It is animal enough to be called by it, and once named it will stick to your teeth. Do name what frightens you, facing away from it, into your palm; bind fear to drip and let it fall into the bell where it belongs. When the leather valves flick and water runs uphill like a conviction, stand still with your eyes closed. The room does not need your help then. It is remembering.

They do not worship gods. They worship causality, which here is near enough to god to pass. They sit with ears on cane and feel the chamber talk to itself, and when the words the water uses for itself are disputed by a root’s slow insistence, they do not adjudicate; they make room for both to be right. Religion is the keeping of edges where two truths slide into a single plank and hang at height without tearing loose. Ceremony is a tool anyone can pick up so long as your hands are wet.

How do they quarrel? With angle and beat. One will set the drum a finger west and let the middle rain fall where it should not; the other will bring a line of bells there and make the water obey a different truth. They never shout names because names bind. They thump their chests to adjust the slope of the air between them until eventually a droplet grows heavy on someone's eyebrow and breaks. Argument ends in agreement or in clean wet faces. After an hour they put the drum back.

There is a boy who can speak a drip to a standstill. He is nearly a man, and the elders do not know whether to be proud or frightened of him. He calls drizzle up and makes it hesitate, a ceiling that thinks about falling and then does not. He will go to the edgehands in a season; he has the look of those who prefer the animal roar. There is a woman whose wrist takes the exact curve of the cane; when she presses clay it never slumps wrong afterward. She is mother to three who will never have their own knots—they are too light to root—but they are kept anyway because keeping is what makes a place. There is someone with a scar across their mouth from a leather valve that stuttered when it should have hissed; they laugh with their belly and nod with their whole skull when the drum hums at low C, so that rain from the middle comes fatter and then clean.

And there is the other thing that lives here, which you already suspected before your words turned slick on your tongue. The room is not only made; it is mannered into personhood by repetition and heat. It holds grudge and favor. It rewards handshapes it knows. It leans for some people and against others. It is not quite an animal, but it does not act like a bowl. When a new tender arrives and puts their ear to a rib for the first time, the cane gives them a note that is true and kind if they have been polite to knots in other chambers; if they have not, the cane remembers. The place collects decisions the way a tree collects rings. You can count storms that became rules by the depth of those clay bands and know who was brave enough to make the mistake and smart enough to sing it back.

This is not a solitary residence. The frog, the antlered snail, the spider like a drop of pitch—they are not pets but colleagues. When the builders hang outside and the wind eats at their clothes like salt, the snail lifts its horns and the room tells them, without words, how fast to beat. When the frog blinks during the shout that calls dawn, those who sing the day into being leave a gap wide enough for silence to stand up and stretch. The spider has swallowed a drip that would have fallen wrong; someone will remember to name it and they will send a sliver of fermented sugar to its web in thanks. Even the shadow-fish appears, once, to the child with the reed, and the child chooses to let it wriggle back into humidity rather than pull it into water, and that choice becomes a small kindness baked into the next week’s weather.

What they fear is not collapse or drought. The cliff is faithful, because it is their own patient delamination; water is continuous as breath. They fear forgetting the shape of their listening, growing a hunger for emptiness that would make them careless. They fear drying their hands too quickly and cracking a law. They tell stories of a time someone spoke too long, too clean, and their name stuck to the wrong place and the room loved them there until they could not leave. The story is a warning and also a wish. To be kept by a room like this is to stop being singular. Some of them want that. Some already have it.

If you stay, they will ask for your heat and a little of your voice. They will show you where to place your palm so a root learns it can push there, how to put a bead on a leaf without making the leaf proud when it should be humble. They will make a place for your breath to be useful, which is what they mean by hospitality. You will find yourself wearing their smell: resin, hot sweetness, hide oil, clay not yet decided. You will stand very still while a droplet grows on your knuckle and then falls, and when it strikes a bell you will feel the near world tip by an amount too small to be part of any other life. It will occur to you that what lives here is not only the ones with hands, and not only the apparatus of drip and drum, but the consent among them—the agreement to keep weather at arm’s length and then bring it in close, to sew laws until they hold, to be persons together with a place so earnestly that even the rope grows hair.
