The ones who live here have trained their voices to stay under their skin. They talk with the pads of their fingers on the rail, with wrist-bones tapped once, twice, with breath pressed out through their noses so the message travels without a bump. A laugh, when it breaks free, is swallowed into a sleeve. A dropped wrench is a story about who you are, and no one wants to tell that story.

They are warm people—glossed at the temples, always damp along the spine. Their hair lives high on their heads or braided into a single rope pinned between their shoulders so the neck can inhale. Children go with bare feet first, then learn sandals lined with felt that never harden, the soles brushed daily with oil so the step is a rumor and not a report. The old keep their toes uncut, because nails carry humidity back into the leg if you lie still enough. Everyone has a rag tied to the hip for the green film that grows on metal like a suggestion of currency; they pay their machines with it, wiping until the cloth comes away darker and the wire is clean.

They are neither many nor few—enough hands to fill the day without making a crowd. Families are something you inherit and something you foster: a tin-and-timber box under an arch, a mark stenciled in lampblack on bladder and barrel, a series of times your elders have a right to—the long-night exhale at early hot-season, the brief breath when the cold above shakes loose its fog. Here, wealth is an hour, a corner of fall you can lay a net across, a tuning you heard early enough to slide a turnbuckle and take the water while it was inclined to come.

Skin tones run from the brown of wet rope to the white of tea left too long in milk; all are altered by the light, by the bruise-amber and the lichen’s drowned green. Some have freckles that darken not with sun but with heat, constellations that mark who sleeps closest to which vent. The babies come without ceremony and without sound; a midwife hands the mother a strap of leather to bite and counts in a whisper for when to breathe with the stone. You do not wake the chamber in its inhale. The father waits apart with his palm on a wire, the note a proof that the world will continue while their own enlarges.

They have jobs that are really postures—ways of being in a place. The Attendings keep chalk soft and pockets damp. They walk the middle deck with one hand dragging the cable trays and the other planted to the sternum, finding the seam where hum and heartbeat share a beat. Their nod starts the climb: small forms going into the rafters, faces moth-pale, felt-wrapped wrench biting into turnbuckle without the lick of metal on metal. These are the high-walkers, string-keepers, whose thighs carry fine crosshatches where the copper kissed them years ago and never exactly let go.

There are pump-minders with the patience of stones. They sit where the old-olive belts turn, shoulders touching the basalt because that is where the warmth comes true. Their work is to listen for a belt’s opinion and to answer it with a new notch or a smear more grease. There are runnel-keepers whose hands never dry; they know the way a frog’s egg looks when it will split by night and the exact hour lilies prefer to be tilted. There are combers who climb the uprights to part the moss so the wires will not rest in too much green and go soft in their mind about where to drop. There are salt-bringers who collect a knife-tip of the moths’ left-behind from lamp housings, pinch it along a string with a care that is never called superstition.

They cook where the rock is most generous, you can taste it in the food: steam gathered in the hinged belly of a pot until it makes peace with the bean, a trickle of hot air pulled through a crate of greens so they wilt the way you would, tired and satisfied. There are long-fermenters whose ear belongs to jars the way the Attending’s belongs to wire. They tap a bladder and wince a little when the tone says not yet. They know turnips will sweeten if their bed is fed with this week’s lees and not last, that mushrooms fed with onion peels shine like varnished wood.

Dress is practical and mild: shirts unbuttoned to the breastbone, sleeves always rolled because forearms say more than mouths. Belts bristle with cloths, with hook and line for lifting trays of soil, with a tiny tin to catch a stray drip when a line gifts you a bead. Copper wire binds hair, strings beads made of ceramic scraps on bracelets so they can exchange their own music at wrist level without bothering the harp above. The children wear cords around their necks threaded with tiny pressed leaves or the spent skins of moths, proof that they did not try to touch a lamp and learned the better way to sit in a halo’s edge.

Their calendar is a string of minutes, an abacus of drips. Birthdays mean little, but people remember the day you first matched a wire’s voice with your own, the first time you returned from the maintenance corridor without fleeing the perfect quiet, the day you were trusted to take the railcar two lengths past the boar generator to bring up compost without waking the belts into complaint. Their oaths are small and binding: I will not scream. I will wrap the hammer. I will not take my neighbor’s fall unless I looked them in the eyes and sang the change back to them first. Debt here is measured in refills, in someone coming down the catwalk at the exact minute your bladder should be pulled and standing there with it like a second spine.

They keep stories low: the basalt was once a river of fire that cooled so fast it locked its pattern in the hexagon like a beehive, and because bees live by hum, this stone remembers hum too. Or: the river above is mercurial and leans into the fractures when it is melancholy, and those are the days the chamber moans. Or: the machine’s heart is what gives it breath and we live under the ribcage, so be gentle; we are all the wrong kind of trespasser. They tell these to children with their mouths at their ears, so the words arrive already as a hush.

They send out parties now and then, three or four with lines of damp cloth wrapped around their boots so the gravel won’t tattle. They return with what the upper drainage gives—the broken yard of pallet someone couldn’t burn, the smooth log that smells of a tree’s dream, a new ladder with a company name as if printed yesterday, to be leaned against their own letterless world. They shave slivers of rubber to plug the heavy curtain at the hot vein access, to keep the egg-stink behind it, to satisfy the superstition that smells, too, can knock a droplet off course.

They are hospitable the way moss is: by offering you what space there is between ribs and machine, by softening the hard edges so you can live against them. If you come to them, you’ll be pressed a cup from a bladder marked with a family’s sign and told the minute it was gathered, as if that were part of its flavor, which here it is. You’ll be taught to listen before you hang anything; you will be handed felt and shame together, the way they hand children both at once so the lesson takes.

Other lives live here with as much right and as much caution.

The moths dint themselves against lamp glass and do not burn because no flame is allowed. They are white like old paper in rain, their pupils hard beads. Their fringed feet comb the wire and leave a memory. Some say the salt trail teaches the water to be brave, to gather where it might have let go. The people do not argue about it aloud. You can watch a boy hold his breath and hold his hand out and the moth step onto him bold as if his skin were a home it had always had. There are nets sometimes, soft as hair, that corral the moths away from the spindles when a retune is delicate. When a moth dies, they press it into damp paper and the powder prints will show you how soft can be geometry.

There is the one gecko, fat from heat and moths, who owns the corner where the control panel warms the wall. It is not named, because names assume more numbers of a thing, but it is acknowledged. A little bell has been threaded on its favorite route across the catwalk rail; it makes a sound like a lake tapping a glass, and the sleepers turn their necks for it, making a path. No one minds the gecko walking them like a bridge. They say if you wake with the ring at your ear you will not drop a tool that day.

Sometimes a frog, glass as a thought you can’t quite catch, arrives in the runnel. You can see its mean little heart beating, the pale fish of its organs cruising under the thin green. It takes one day. No more. It stares with amphibian courage, eats something you cannot see, and then finds the hairline in the rock that the rest of you could have sworn did not exist. People tell each other, in hand-talk, where it was last, and for a morning the work moves like a shadow that will not be startled.

Fungi make their rounds of the room on their own schedule. They build shelves in the corners the harp cannot dry, they bruise like an apple left on a sill. There are harvesters who can dent the rim with a thumb and know by resistance what stew it is intended for. The spent cakes go to the terraced trays of soil and cloth, their mycelium unraveling into food for small waxy leaves, tight tomatoes nobody up top remembers ever seeing on a vine. These, too, are inhabitants; they bend toward a fall-line they know nothing of, and that not-knowing is as valid as the Attending’s chalk.

There are the greens that are not color but profession: moss combed like wool, ferns that lean out in the exact angle of faith, heart-shaped leaves cupped where water prefers to pause. They do not so much live here as suggest to the people a way of living: make yourself into catchment, make your surfaces into invitation, linger your own wet against the harshness.

Smaller, smaller lives also: the lichen that beads along handrails and helps you see without being asked to glow too much; the damp-loving bacteria that bloom in the condensate’s thin film and will emerald a switch if you do not love it back with oil; the rot that is not moral but legal here, because what else can a thing do in damp but continue underground into a new shape?

The harp lives here and is counted among the breathing. The people say they do not play it, but their hands are everywhere in its posture; they pay attention to it the way you would to a relative with moods. The motor that thuds like a patient heart is encased and deaf, but it speaks in the body language of wires—what lifts, what droops, what wants to be a road for a thread of river. It is the law, they say, and also the god, and also the weather. It is as alive as anything that learns; when a droplet hesitates at a spindle and then splits and proceeds along other lines, the whole web has done a kind of thinking.

The stone itself rules without ruling, a massive tenant who came first and never moved out. It keeps its heat behind the ribs, then lets it sigh through cracks and vents, telling the chamber when to undress and when to put the cloths back on their hooks. In this room you sit with your back to basalt and know where you are in the long pulse. The children are asked at supper, what note did the east ladder whisper? They answer, and that is how they learn compass as sound.

There are also those who pass through and do not belong. They come from the light that crushes shadows flat, slick with dry sweat, talk too loud and at first do not understand why all the faces around them go still. If they learn quickly, if they let their words go flax-soft and watch what a single shout does to a ribbon of water, they may sleep on a pallet near a leaf-cup and wake with dew on their forehead like a blessing. If they do not—if they slam something that cannot be unslammed—they will feel the room lean away from them, the hum shift to a note with no place to put your foot, and then someone will stand and look them in the eye and sing not unkindly the way out.

What lives here, in the end, is the practice of attention. It moves through cords braided with plant fibers, curing into living cable. It waits in bladders labeled by time, not by liters. It makes itself at home in whispered counting, in tools wrapped in felt to kill the clink, in the minute where the whole chamber hears rain thin as rice on a roof and every leaf lifts. It is people, certainly—people who lean into a world that asks for their quiet—but it is also the moth and the mindful gecko, the frog that is really the river taking a breath inside the wall, the fungus that eats delicately, the lichen that is the lamp that decided to be a plant, the instrument that is weather, the stone that is a clock.

They live by the harp’s calendar and the stone’s breath. They accept that here lushness is a function of patience and angle, that abundance is a behavior not a hoard. Outside, a river does not care about wires. Inside, they make the river care, a minute at a time. They listen to themselves listening, and in that echo they build a city no louder than a heartbeat.
