Those who live here have been worn to fit the place the way a thumb wears to fit a tool. You can see it in their feet: broad pads that know the shallow ribs of the floor without looking, skin glazed to a dark softness where terracotta kisses it day after day. Their hands have small glossed hollows in the meat between thumb and forefinger, a twin to the polished thumb-places on the pillars, as if they and the wood have taught one another how to be held. Lime dust has traced the habit of their breaths in the hairs along their arms. Their shoulders know the width of ladders. Their necks kink without complaint to look up into the net of shafts and drip-needles and light.

They are few, and they are enough. In the sleeping niche, six hammocks sway lightly under their banked lamp: two for the elder pair whose ears keep the hall from spilling or starving; two for the women who tend the fungus rooms farther in, who walk with a night smell in their hair; two for a man and a girl, father and daughter, who climb like swifts to hitch and un-hitch the slats at the shaft mouths. A boy sometimes sleeps where the ferret does, hand draped over a spotted back so the bell does not confuse the dark.

Their age is hard to place because the place keeps them. The elders move slowly in the dry hours and quickly in the wet, as if the hall oils their joints as it does its wood. Children learn speed only where speed belongs: in the reach to a rope before a surge, in the hand placed firm, unhurried on a weir stone, in the dart to snatch a moth away from a flame-hood. They take the measure of themselves from the measures around them. They count in drops and lamp-wicks and the crickets’ brief crescendos in the louvered flutes. Each child can tell by ear which gourd has slipped its pitch and needs turning, can tell by nose whether the drip is night-water or sky-water come newly down.

Clothes do not snag. Their garments are cut close and soft, woven of reed-fiber and twisted bark and stitched with hair-thin cane that bows but never breaks. When they need weight, they wear it as bracelets: copper disks, river stones in tiny nets, a length of bone drilled through and threaded. Tools hang low and silent at the waist: a key of carved hardwood to fit the shutter-latches; a lime-scraper with its own wisp of cloth to keep it from chattering against brick; a knife with a chipped-glass edge that lives in a leather sleeve as oiled as skin.

They eat like those who have learned the scale of their place. From the grain room comes a smell faint and sour, the comfort of a promise you have kept before. They beat the husks off millet in shallow bowls whose rims have been thumbed thin over generations and will be patched with a slip of clay when they break; they boil the millet slow, over a flame kept small and kind to the pitch in the seams. Fish are taken with respect from the cistern pans only when nets raised in the confluence have bitten well and the frogs are fat enough not to mind a morning without atriums of gnats. In wet years the hair-mesh weir is set and eels tremble like black ropes in the basin; those are skinned and smoked in the organ-breath of the flutes. Fungus from the night-lamps is sliced like meat. There is goat’s milk sometimes, traded along the sacred shafts for rope, for baskets, for a jar of the hall’s oil whose smell the surface people call boat and the hall people call home.

They sing like carpenters sing: to test their work and to tell one another where their bodies are in the heights. When a shutter needs lifting, the man at the rope chants the count and the woman at the counterweight answers in thirds, the vaults returning their notes with an accuracy that is also affection. There is no silence in their good hours—not because they fear it, but because they know that some silences mean absence and some mean rest and those two cannot be confused. The law against silencing the drip-plates all at once is a law against forgetting their own pulse.

Their governance is a habit more than a council. Decisions are made at the moment the sounds ask. If the flutes in the west turn thin, the elder with the listening ear lifts her half-mask from its peg, the one carved to sit gently on the bones of her face and narrow the hearing the way fingers narrow a reed pipe. She walks the hall with it, tuning head and note, and the others see the slight bend of her wrist and know: open the second slat, check the leather over the east drain, set a stone the weight of a child’s heel on the fourth weir. A child might protest, for the sake of practice, “Why the fourth and not the third?” and be answered with a story about the spring two decades past, when the third saw to itself but the fourth learned stubbornness.

Their rules live in the skin. Never block a channel even for a moment—the hall forgets more slowly than water does. Leave two gourds ringing, even at night, so that those who wake in the new darkness know where the edges have stayed. Feed the frogs at dusk and dawn, because a frog disgruntled is a moth fast lost and six lost moths are a webless night and twenty lost nights are mildew where you do not expect it. Lay a hand, always, on the snake-stick before you take it up, to remember that turning is different from striking. If you must move the little stone at the fourth weir from the west, tell the wall first, by tapping three times on the carved hollow at shoulder height; it costs nothing to be polite to a load-bearing thing.

They are caretakers of fast things. Water does not belong to them except in its arrangements. Light visits and they usher it, parceled, through the day. Wind is a musician at whose whim they stand and listen, and then they tune his instrument under him. Even pests are walked out rather than warred upon. The rat that runs the dark hall knows that the ferret rings for people, and not for him, and by the time he knows it, it is already too late. The huntsman spider is left to her business and given a ring of copper around the beam she favors, a charm against her moving farther in; they harvest her shed skins to line the flood-baffles, because silk speaks to water and water listens.

The swiftlets are not stolen from. Their nests cup the shaft-edges like small ears pressed close to the weather. There is a day each season when the ledges are brushed clean of dust and combed with the warm palm so the birds feel a home renewed, and there is a day when the children count eggs by breath alone, each quick inhale not to be finished until the fingers on the rope feel the tug that means “stop here, do not sway, it is enough.” The geckos are counted only in play. The girl and the boy name them for stains in the plaster, for seams in the ribs, for the shapes of week-old falls of dust. They know that a lizard who grows too bold can be persuaded to decamp by polishing his path to a sheen that says “no grip, not for you,” and they like the trick as much as they like any trick that uses care to reach its end.

Work is a braid. Morning dew fattening at the clay needles brings out the little stools and the quiet: each gourd is checked with a nail touched lightly to its lip, the notes pearl and line, the elders’ ears assembling a symphony of enoughs. Brooms appear from corners they do not logically fit, and dust lifts like a spell and finds the reed mats as the day finds the shafts. Shell whistles at the mouths of the vents choose their keys; north means one thing to the people of the grates above and another to the people of the hall, and in that difference lies why strangers come for advice. Midday brightness brings out repair—the pitchpot warmed till it pours the way thick rain pours, the seam sealed, the ash rubbed in with a cloth softer than a chick. Afternoon is rope-work: cords walked through hands to feel where they will fray, bark strands spliced, knots taught to the children whose fingers are still more clever than strong.

Toward evening, food passes hand to hand, but the word for it is not passing; it is “rounding,” because it moves in circles, always back to center. The lamp hoods go on and make domes of downward light; faces come up out of shadow. The frogs begin, and the frogs’ song is an assent to the day. Once a week, after the feeding, someone strikes one of the larger gourds on the low side with the heel of a hand: one, three, five; a rhythm that does not belong to counting but to remembering. Names are said. They do not carve their dead into walls or sweep their ashes into mortar locks—not here. The dead are put by in clay along the cool corridors where the air tastes of mushroom spores, little lidded bowls set on little shelves at eye height. You see them when you pass. You put your thumb to the bowl’s rim. You tell them where the drips have moved today.

They do not go up often, though they can, through the sacred shafts that end under grates. Sometimes a goat’s hoof stamps and the dust sifts down; sometimes the shaft gives back a gust that smells of thyme or dung or a street of hot stone. Traders’ voices filter like water: a basket lowered on a rope, a jar raised in answer, the simple arithmetic of exchange that is also a vow. It is not a secret life, merely a quiet one. The surface knows of them, and they know of the surface in the ways that matter: what the sky has been doing, how deep the runnel is likely to be by dusk, how light will act between noon and three if the clouds come three at a time and each has a burnished belly.

There are no titles, but there are names that are not names. The one with the listening ear is called Aunt by anyone allowed to joke. The mat-tender has palms inked by earth reds and smoke blacks and wears the faint green of damp at his cuffs. The rope-keeper has scars across the inside of her fingers where little stone weights have taught her lessons. The ferret’s bell has its own name, and children are not allowed to speak it in the sleeping niche. The little stone on the lip of the fourth weir from the west is always The Stone; there is no other stone with a definite article. If a stranger moves it in ignorance, the hall does not punish. The hall remembers. The next time a surge runs tight, a jar will chatter, and the old woman will stand and smile and put the thing back and tell the story again for the pleasure of its ending.

They dream in squares of sky. They dream in circles of dripping. The language they make has verbs for what water does to stone, to light, to sound, and the same verbs fit what children do to rules: soften, round, redirect, polish until the thing remembers a better way to be. They do not have two different words for work and worship. The closest they have to a blessing is “May your rope fall true,” which is to say, may the path between your hand and what is heavy be kind.

And there are others who live here besides. The frogs inhabit the law as surely as the people do and obey the schedule without understanding it. The swiftlets teach the shafts their use and stitch the air until it is tight enough to climb. The geckos go house-to-house along the undersides of ribs and keep other small lives small. The fish accrue and disperse like thoughts. Dust arrives and is shepherded like a tribe. Sound breeds in corners that aren’t corners and takes up residence with the legality of air. A huntsman sits behind a beam, queen of a web that celebrates more than it snags. The ferret keeps a private counsel and considers herself a citizen.

What lives here, then, are keepers: of water that is mostly not here yet, of sunlight coaxed into squares, of a breath that moves through ribs and hollows and calls itself a room. The people and the animals and the rules and the sounds keep one another. The place is kept, and in the keeping is the living. When strangers pass in and stand awed by the pared brightness at noon or the frog-choir at dusk, the hall people will put a gourd in their hands and show them how to listen to its ring. They will not ask for names. The question here is not who you are. It is: can you learn the pitch of this water, and will you love it enough to leave it ringing when you go.
