Patience lives here, made into mouths and hair and hinge. It began as a cleanliness and a schedule, and then it put down a foot, a glue, a comb. What answers the fan is an order of animals that takes the same shape as the room: narrow, strapped close, working in films and lattices and corners, with no appetite for vistas and no need for change.

Closest to your eye, because they read as decoration if you don’t stare, are the seam-goblets. They live where soft meets hard—under the flange lips, along the compliant edges of hatches, any place a gasket is asked to be both grip and give. Each is a transparent thimble with a flexed waist, a fluted rim edged in cilia so fine they look like dust until they move. They don’t flex to drama; their opening and closing is the tide at this latitude. They graze film: not water as such, but the lace of what water carries when air gives up its freight—proteins that learned to fly and were caught, sugars blown off the tongue of a worker three levels down and filtered through a world of louvers, the faintest oil from a bearing too far away to see. The gobletmouths bud new goblets from scars on their own bases; a skin puckers, domes, grows a ring of hair, lets go with the first cough of a pump and is already feeding before it has made its first decision. They are not fussy. Their discrimination is chemical: sour is bad, a certain copper is too much, sweet is a picnic. That is all the ethics they require.

Across the baffles where air is bent and made laminar, the room grows a nap that is not nap. The bristle-colonies anchor to paint with glues that weep and set and weep again, over and over, their chemistry cued by shear. A single bristle is a hundred animals—tube-bodied, their tips a hooked trellis—grown together at their feet so they cannot leave. If you could be their size you would see the joints in their spines; you would see each mouth ringed in hard light, opening out of a bundle of muscles no thicker than a dot in the fog. They do not wave. They do not brush. They are present at the boundary layer and the boundary layer is their pasture. They intercept what the goblets miss: airborne, abraded: paint-chalk; oxidized wire; a wing-scales’ worth of the midges that never get to fly; microplastics shaved down to what passes for dust at this height. They take nothing personal from the room. When the fan deepens a half-tone, they lean the fraction that is all they can. When it settles, so do they. They survive abrasion as forests survive winter: by being larger in number than the scrapes that will take them.

At the bottom edge of everything, where water remembers it is a sheet before it becomes a drop, the rice-shells scour. They are not shrimps but their knees know how to look like shrimps know. Pale, glassy, knuckled with hairs that leave little wakes in the thin as they move, they work in chords and counter-rotations, each the size of a comma if you wrote big. They skate more than they walk because the floor is never floor; it is always surface. Where a rivulet has organized itself across a slope they will meet head to head like shoppers at a narrow door, neither making a fuss. They shovel with their feathered arms. They are responsible for most of the room’s hygiene and none of its beauty. They abrade algae back into suggestion before it can raise itself to a noun. They scratch biofilm down to a strength that the drop will carry away in its next widening. They know nothing of the world except inclination, thickness, and the constant low percussion of pump through metal. They lay their eggs—if you want that word—under the lip of an anti-slip strip, glueing sacs into the serration, and the young come out already cleaning, because there is nothing else to do.

Under the louvers where the air is coldest and the light is most like an idea of light, the room has grown its own glands. They look like the neoprene folds you’d use to seal a door in a bad storm, slick and heavy, a black that reflects nothing. They are animals, all length and patience, and at intervals they unzip themselves into rows of tongues the color of uncooked fat. There are so many tongues that the word stops being useful—a comb, a curtain, a field—and they do not move in any mammal way. They taste. They taste the acid the rain brings in memory; they taste the nickel the summer gave up; they taste the breath-signs of bloom and rot and the flat tin of over-sterilized air. When they decide—if that is the right word; they decide with a distribution of signals that never makes it into a thought—they secrete. The secretion is a thin, mean chemistry that etches the paint along a vanishing path and leaves the goblets nothing to eat for a day. This is not malice. This is specification embodied. People used to check dials for this work. Now a curtain of quiet flesh keeps the drift inside its numbers and remembers what winter had to say to the lungs.

Everything that is round wears jewelry. Bolt-heads show collars of bivalves too small to be a meal even to a patient child. Their shells are hinged sapphire-blue—not pigment, but interference, a trick of layer and light—and when the pump shifts phase they all open as if on one idea, and they all close again when the shift is done. They will live for as long as the pump is under service contract, and when it is replaced by a pump that makes a different hiccup once an hour they will die there and be taken up by the spiders you cannot see unless the light fails and a net catches enough dust for a second star to be born. Their foot—each has the one—reaches far under the paint to the metal itself, a drill that is not a drill but a series of little licks you would hear if you were living at that scale, and they fasten themselves to the room the way children fasten to vows.

Between girder and sump there are threads, and on the threads are breakfasts. The threads are spun by anything with leftover glue and a boredom with corners. On their ends hang lenses of jellied water, each cupping a garnish of midges that are not midges except in the way any tiny gnat-like animal is a midge if it comes through air and should have left by now. Some of the threads belong to animals that harvest things out of the flow, beating cilia slowly to keep the lens fat and sweet; some belong to animals that have nothing at all to do with feeding and everything to do with the way condensation wants somewhere to turn when it is asked to be a string instead of a page. The carpentry of moisture is a kind of music here and every chord is held forever. These threads are one instrument the room plays and it is not a bad instrument, though it never learns a new song.

Up inside the fan’s throat, where there is no place to be if you are not spin or duct, the crosswinds have grown their own terns. They are not birds. They are trapmakers, all waist and wing, planted like burrs in seam-shelter. Doubled and redoubled threads spread from them across the least little drafts like soap film shows rainbows across the rings of a child’s toy. On those threads the dust that remembers streets but has forgotten which ones will find a resting place, and in that dust will be oils and spores and the skins of bacteria and, sometimes, a single hair that made it a hundred meters upward in a week because it rode everything that meant to go down. The trapmakers eat all of it in a chemistry without teeth. They are not great hunters. They are good hooks.

Plants don’t live here so much as they annotate. Now and then a pellet of crustacean leavings will hang and dry in a place where spray kisses, and on that pellet a sheet will grow itself the color of institutional hallways in snowlight: dead green, not an option anyone would have picked. It will be so thin a glance will blow it away, which is how it spreads. It respires when mist lays itself down and sleeps when things begin to bead. It is not proud. It is the equivalent of privacy in this machine. Algae exist, too, as a rumor in the bottoms of gutters, as a promise the grazers never let materialize into a film thick enough to skid a boot. If they have flowers or seasons they are not for us; they open and close along the tick of the drips.

The smallest lives are the most sure. There are bacteria that ride on rust as it tries to be rust and make it be less so; they take electrons the way some animals take warmth. There are consortia that old engineers would have called slime and then written papers about: interleavings of archaea and fungi that knit to one another and pass on the weather in ions and words we never learned. Viruses travel like gossip from goblet to goblet, meaning nothing until the day a fan blade is replaced and stress opens a seam, and then they mean everything. The whole underlife flickers with quorum and quorum-broken, with trails of chemicals left for others to read like chalk marks on a back alley, with anchors dissolved and laid down again. If you wanted names you would need a book. It would be a long book, mostly about sugars and arrangements.

All of these animals know time the way this place can afford to keep it. None of them sleep in the dark because this room has only one dim and it is always lit. None of them wake at dawn because they have never seen a horizon that was not made of louvers. They take their hours from the pump’s cough, their days from the service cycle that cools the fan to a fraction less and warms it again, their years from the deposits of salt on the heads of the rivets, one bead per—how long? They have calendars, but their calendars are grooves and welds and the pace at which a machined line becomes a shallow valley and then is scraped back to a line. They court, if that is what you want to call it, by standing where the flow is right and being the right shape to catch what their mates release on the hour. They grow, and when they are not useful they are dislodged by the next sudden sneeze of pressure at the purge-iris, and when they are dislodged they fall and are eaten by the shrimp that are not shrimp, or they fuse into a plate that will read as stone if you visit three years later.

There is a creature that lives in the iris-valve’s lip like a ring eats a finger. It is only awake when the room exhales to the outside. It is not adapted to the fan; it is adapted to difference. When the hatch opens and the world beyond comes in like a punch, this ring blooms in a second, flipping a row of tiny cups inside-out. Each cup is a tongue and a barnacle and a parachute. They taste the sharp, they taste the thin, they drink the microcrystals caught in the sudden fog, and then, just as quickly as they came, they fold back into a seam and become gasket again. They reproduce in winter, which in here means the month the deliveries are late and the fan runs an extra beat lean. No one put them there and yet they are perfect at what they do. This is how most of the room’s residents seem if you bother to care about where they came from: custom-fit and untraceable.

There are larger things, but “larger” is a lie the eye tells because it wants to be comforted. A handful of white moths the size of thought arrive once a year when a warm day surprises the factory and tricks a loading-bay into letting a cloud in. They make it high and higher and then find themselves in this room and unmake themselves on the louvers like wet ash. Their bodies feed a generation they were never told about. Once, years ago, a rat made the climb. It did not live. What lives here lives inside a narrower alphabet.

No one tends them. People visit, suited to be other places and careful not to be too interesting. They bring with them a warm edge and a handful of salts and a pulse too loud to ignore; they leave when the checklist says go. The animals receive them not as omens but as weather: a favorable gust, a cruel drop. The bat-gasket tongues take a taste and learn a new number. The gobletmouths close for a minute and then open again, because there is always the next breath to eat. The shrimp fall back into a seam and let the boot pass and afterwards their tracks overprint the scuff as if it had never been anything but a change in slope.

If you need a story to make it tidy you can say this: someone designed a machine to carve water out of air at height and then acquainted it, on purpose, with microfauna that could do the work in crevices machines dislike. The list of parts includes pumps and plates and valves and then, near the end where the buying officer stops reading, goblet polyps for seam hygiene; louver bristle colonies for airborne particulate interception; sump-scrapers for biofilm control; taste-curtains for pH and microbial monitoring; bolt-head clams for condensate transient capture; trapmakers for aerosol load reduction. You can imagine serial numbers and suppliers and a field manual with photos of each, faded, with parts circled. It is a comforting lie, but not a harmful one. It matches the way the room behaves, which is what matters.

If you would rather have it be wilder, make it this instead: the world is a built thing and animals are very good at building themselves into it when it promises a steady rain and an unchanging wind. They select for adhesion and appetite. They sand themselves down to what the baffles will allow. They learn to keep time with the only clock that runs. They grow not toward sunlight but toward a pressure sensor’s steady readout. The shape of the place writes itself into the bodies that can stand to be here, and then those bodies keep the shape sound because that is how they live. Accretion, specification, hunger, the fall of a drop: the same four notes, played clean.

So: who lives here? Mouths like thimbles; hair that is not hair; scrapers that make the floor be floor by never letting it be anything else; tongues arranged like a census of taste; shells bright as a mistake someone loved; nets spun in the throats of weather; a paint-thin plant that will not take no for an answer and takes no as all it needs. They live within the budget of the room: cold, noise, thin light, constant fall, always moving wet. They live as pressure lives in a line. If you touch the back of a louver, one of them will make you part of their ledger for a second, then erase you more cleanly than apology could. They are the body of the schedule. They do not bustle. They are presence, pressed into every seam, agreeing over and over to go on.
