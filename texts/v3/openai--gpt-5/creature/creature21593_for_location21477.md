What lives here begins as breath and film. The first eaters are sheets and laces, damp as tongues and patient as old stains. They grip the stone with chemistry instead of roots, coaxing carbon from water and stone-sour and making it into bruise-colored mats that darken in exhale and ghost to ash-pale when the air stills. They are the thin rinds and vitreous panes that wake to color only when a wet wind speaks across them. They are the frost-beards under lips of rock, the slicks at the mouths of holes that go down forever. They taste bad until you understand the heat and salt that soothe them; then they are the basis of everything.

On the mats graze things that are almost furniture. Low and oval, armored in stacked plates that tremble against one another when they hurry, they look like coins given legs and purpose. If you lift one it hums at a pitch so low you feel it in your wrists, as if the underside of it were not rasping teeth but a little drum rasped by sand. They like to work in lines with just enough gap between them that each gets the mat at the felt’s richest edge. Smaller forms hide under their plates, quick tails plucking for loosened bits. They are placid until a particular chord comes off the plain; then their lids flicker and their whole bodies stutter like kettle lids, and you tie them down or you lose them to some slot that breath will never surrender.

Behind them and between them, the velvet ones come on. Set a hand to a fresh trail and your skin comes away oiled with a cool shine and a little bite of bitter. These are slugs with their slicks disciplined, velvets whose bodies breath-polish themselves. They pull themselves long, then compact to rest, long, then rest, an admonition written in quicksilver and spit. They are honorable thieves and live on the crumbs of the armored. Their slime is kept, salted, smoked without flame over vents; later it will set as a varnish that shrugs off damp and dulls a bright to safe.

If the breath comes up fat with aerosol and dust, the ringed ones open. They look all winter like bracelets left on stone and forgotten; then a gust throws a little froth at them and they go to flower—fans of hair that comb the mist for spores, for motes of fat, for life you can eat without touching. They pulse so slowly you might think you are watching the wind and not the worm at all. Their fans fold again before you can stand up, and you will be hard-pressed to find them when the air steadies; they dissolve back into ring and wait.

Where a rock lip makes a little cave in the lee of tone—where sound is broken and made thick—humped things the color of old knuckles anchor themselves and open their underbodies. The plates of their backs form a mouth facing downward into the hush; inside is all curvature and resonance, a set of cavities tuned by age and accident. Dust hears those bowls and decides to stop being air; it lets itself be gathered. The humped ones—throat-crabs, cupbacks, resonants—sift what falls, sliding filaments across the hollows to lick up mineral and spore and the secret dark sweets of the mats. People keep them near their doorways to pull the breath’s grit out of the house. In the morning there is a soft cake to collect: salt, pollen, a little fat, a little iron, a pinch of day.

In the long slots the plain has made for its own exhalations, hunters set their patience. Gray on gray, limbs like grate-tines or crooks, they keep their weight balanced on tendons as fine as a rhythm in the hand. When the tone drops and the great sponge inhales, anything shallow goes deeper; anything with nerves drags itself the other way, and that is when the hunters feed, snapping up what loses its mind to a draft. You will not hear them over the organ. You will know them by the way a long crack, unremarkable a heartbeat before, acquires corners like thoughts.

Above, where other worlds would keep birds, the kites live. They keep themselves pressed to stone until the vent below them shakes into a note that means lift. When it does they stand, and each unveils a sail of skin the size of a small table and the thickness of a blister. The edges of the sails have little hands, more clever than fingers and less fussy; they pluck in the wet wind and bring back fistfuls of gnats, of fly-shadows, of a juvenile grazer that leapt the wrong way and found itself light. A sky with a hundred kites up looks like a flattened garden in an old etching: all brown flowers, all nervous veining. They do not leave this place. The plain breathes them into existence twice a day, and they fold and lie down again when the tone slackens, all quiver shot and eyes bright as new iron.

At the permanent mouths, the ones that steam in low weather and breathe to a rhythm you could set a year by, white things keep their faces up. They are somewhere between fish and lizard and a bad plan by an old god. The skin on their heads is calloused into blind masks, their teeth are made for seizing: whatever watches the lip too long and forgets that the land inhales will be corrected. You can see their jaw-corners glitter at night if you keep the lamps low. People take them, but not alone; you charm them with a slatted mouth over a hole elsewhere, make the exhale there into tapped invitation with shutters and weighted flaps; when the near hole slakes, the far one hungers, and you have set the table at a place where your spears already wait.

And then there are the people. They look like whatever patience looks like when it grows bones and learns to make knots. They are short to the wind and wide in the foot, wrapped not to be warm so much as to refuse any note that would shiver their membranes into rips. Over their ears—because ears are dangerous luxury here—they set layered caps of fungus-felt and hide, thick as loaves, quilted with channels for breath to run through without catching a pitch. Their hands are clean always; their nails are kept round so that they will not catch on tendon or membrane. They are so used to watching that their eyes take in a room’s air and its corners before they make a face.

They live in the lee of what can be leaned. If the breath hits a ridge and spills, you will find their house behind that spill—a frame of bone and a wall of gypsum, a roof of stitched membrane pegged with stone so it does not sing itself to death. They do not waste corners. Everything curves or is braced against a curve to deny a standing wave. Along the windward line of a house are hung bladder-curtains and tendon braids, not as decoration but as devices; you pull a cord and a bladder lifts and a particular hole’s roar goes from long to chopped, and someone miles away knows the signal and knocks their own shutters to answer. Each settlement keeps the mouth of a single hole as an instrument. The tune is simple—this hole is not a flute, nothing here is a flute—but it is enough to say danger or come or feast.

When they talk they do it with their wrists and their ribs, with a tap that is not a percussive in the air but a code on the skin. The stories are thrown with fingers and caught with eyes. They have verbs for angle, for the way a hush feels at the edge, for the exact wet of a day. They name their children with gestures that live in the bones of the hands: a name you can sign even if your lips are pressed and the whole plain is blowing. When the breath lessens enough that a whisper will not go from friendly into foolish, they whisper into the soft underside of each other’s caps and grin like conspirators.

Their tools are small and too serious to shine. The best awls came down as rain and were not rain when they arrived; they were star-teeth, hammered until they stopped acting like stone and started acting like promise. Knives are ideas more than edges: you pet the shale against a wet stone every night until the knife remembers what you want it for. Nets are tendon twisted with a fungal pith that people raise in darkness and dry above vents until it stops smelling like bread and starts smelling like rope. Around their hearths—little as hearts, the heat borrowed from the planet’s leak and the patient alchemy of salt on fat—they stack curls of old chitin and plates shaped into bowls and mantles. Fire is a guest almost never asked; the air carries too much mood.

They have herds, but their herds are hardly animals at all when you first look. The armor-ovals are tethered at the edges of gardens. Gardens here are not plots of green; they are lengths of wind shifted and sipped. They set stones around a patch to knock an ugly note out of the air that makes the mats sicken; they lay a skin across a small vent to make it cough in a way that brings a rain of dust. The grazers take what grows then and hum while they mill, and the hum reminds the mats to thicken. In the same way they cultivate throat-crabs under the eaves of their houses and sponge the cups in the morning for cakes of salt and dark-sugar. The velvets come and go in tolerated crime; a child will smudge a finger in a fresh trail and lick and make a face and laugh.

Hunting, for them, is not chase. It is arrangement. They listen to how two holes speak to each other under the rock, how blocking one with a weighted flap fattens the breath somewhere else. They wedge a bluestone here, lay a line of pebbles there, set a resonant skin to drink the bad tone and leave the good. Then they sit in the hush they have engineered and wait while the hunters of the plain behave like themselves. A kite rises where it should not. Something with white teeth finds that hunger has moved house and goes to see. When it comes to the lip you chose, you do not have to throw a spear very far.

A way between settlements is not a path but a chain of silences. They have built cairns along them whose mouths say syllables if a breeze hits them right. You travel with your ears swaddled and your eyes reading the wind like letters; you count the cairns, you tally the syllables, you step from hush to hush. A child can be taken that way with both ears wrapped and both hands in a friendly belt. You point to a stone that sings and the child learns the meaning of that song. Later they will know to tie a bell—a pebble in a pouch, nothing more—to a running cloth at the edge of a crevasse; in a certain tone it shakes and the cloth goes rigid and someone does not fall.

They have few walls that do not breathe. A door here is a membrane pegged, a flap you lift on three stones and let fall when the plain begins to sing a collapse-note. In their houses there are chests for keeping dry—lined with the resin the velvets gave—but otherwise most goods are hung. They collect plates from the dead, both their own and their animals and anything else that will offer the curve of its back to a beam. They will not set a human skull in a wall, but they will keep the thick bones of the jaw, because jawbones know about resonance and make quiet places quieter. The bodies otherwise go into the safest vents in the small hours. It is not reverent and it is not not. It is practical and patient and includes the dead in the work of keeping the living.

They have games that can be played inside the ear. Children set two thin skins up and adjust their pebbles until the breath moving through the house makes one of them flick but not the other. They race by running their hands over walls and counting cuts. There is a whole poetry of knots: you can tell someone where you are, in what tone of weather and wash of sound, by the way you lace a tendon and leave it under a marked rock. Some of the old things are too worn to read in any other way. The fossils in the stone—once monsters, or once dinners, or once no one knows—have been rubbed to patterns like weather. So the people carve their stories into things that bend the air. There are canopies hung in the center of town-hushes, lattices of tendon and bladder and split bone, that quiver to certain notes; if they tremble, everyone moves. If they do not, someone tells a tale.

On a good night, when the air above steadies and the organ sags to a low that lives just under hearing, the pools throw up light like kept secrets. The mats at the edges glow where they are kissed by the thing they have been making. People pad without caps then, heads bare to the humid cold, and they can hear the little domestic world happen, not just the old wet kingdom under. They hear the scratch of a grazer’s comb-teeth on a stone. They hear their own clothes whisper. They hear a story finished with a hand, and a hand touched back on the knee to say, I have it.

They are not alone in their minds. They keep a map of notes in the same part of themselves that keeps the names of people. The plain has tones for danger and for luck and for a sustained mood of generous damp; these have been memorized for so long that a person who has lived here all their life will flinch or ease before anything obvious changes. The coming-of-age, such as it is, involves standing out on a certain flat in a certain hour with one’s caps unbound, with kin ready to put hands over ears if the mood turns, so that the body can find its resonant frequency with the land. Afterward you are given a small rod carved from a jawbone and planed to your note. You hang it at your belt. When it thrums against your thigh, you move.

They trade with distance by breath and law. There are other settlements—five, eight, a dozen dwellings tucked into their particular ridges and lees. Messages run upvalley by pipe and shutter. You say, in a two-stroke gasp from a mile of stone, that you have a glut of velvet-cured skins and a need for gypsum slabs; the answer knocks back like heartbeats from the next mouth: wait two breaths, we will carry them in low tide. The moots—the times when a half-dozen little hushes align and you can stand in the open and have a room’s worth of quiet—are brief and planned months ahead. People marry then, trade then, cut children’s caps to size then. At the last moot before a cold shift, they lace a net of tendon across a small ravine and show the youngsters how to feel the bone speak when the inward tide begins.

Names for them would be the wrong shape if made in a noisy mouth. To each other they are a cupped palm turned and a small twitch of the shoulder and a knuckle laid against a lip; they are the Hushkind if you must make a word. They are not heroic. They do not aspire to climb or to claim. They keep edges blunted and plates stacked and skins weighted. They listen, and when the old, flat, wet thing they live on draws a long breath, they brace for it, and when it lets that breath go they lift their bladders and make a single hole in a continent say yes, we hear you, yes, we are here.
