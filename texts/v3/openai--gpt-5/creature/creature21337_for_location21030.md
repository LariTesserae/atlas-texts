Who lives here are listeners first, eaters second, and only then anything like shapes. They are the kind of bodies that figured out long ago that light is an unreliable rumor, that the most trustworthy maps are the ones your feet and whiskers and water-feelers can read.

Pinned to the surge-line are the mouths that never close. Barnacles, set against the stone like a mosaic of stubborn alphabets, open their ponytail fans when the ocean presses in and comb the volume for the edible dust of the world. They were once tiny boats—free-swimming triangles of possibility—until each one decided, with a last minute’s eye, where to glue its life. They cement themselves head-down and spend the rest of their days as a door that knows when to be a net. Between them, mussels hold fast with silk they spit into cords that out-labor steel, each thread a compromise with violence. Their shells are not blue in this dark but they carry the memory of sky as a gloss that collects the cold. Gooseneck barnacles lean from flexible necks, white calves of limestone, and clutch at the pulses. Anemones fold themselves to fists between beatings, then let go into hundreds of sensing arms; they fish with touch, reading the fine script of currents for the word meat.

Every vacant angle that is not truly vacant is an animal translated into architecture. Bryozoans lay down calcareous quilts so finely stitched that only a pin’s whisper of water finds the filters underneath. Tube-worms splice their secreted stone to the cave like beads, withdrawing fern-frills when the sea’s breath turns to spit. Encrusting sponges—soft rock with pores—drink the place by the gallon and exhale it altered, their chemistry a quiet industry that never shuts. The hydroids and other small emperors that look like plants—because our eyes are lazy—thread the walls in transparent republics and set out stinging flags to trap what the barnacles miss. There are no fronds here, but many things that wear the verb of fronding.

Grazers follow the low tide like a clock hand. Limpets, small shields that remember a home as exactly as a needle remembers north, trace wet signatures between their feeding and their resting. Each one keeps a “home scar,” a notch filed into the stone by years of kissing the same point. Chitons with their eight-shelled backs creep as if welded to the wall, radulas rasping. In their tooth-rows there is real metal, iron bright as a thought—magnetite for biting rock. Under their roofs, dozens of tiny eyes are fused to shell, half-made and mostly useless here, but good for feeling the absence of wave-shadow, for measuring a glare that never arrives. Whelks pass among them like interrogation. Snails with drills find a mussel’s seam, borrow a patient hour, and make a hole impossible to deny. Every evening a few periwinkles attempt a pilgrimage uphill, and a few make it. Most are read by something else first.

Things with jointed legs own the in-between. Crabs with claws like quotation marks move from exclamation to exclamation along the walls, arguing with the surges, flattening themselves into punctuation when necessary. Porcelain crabs rake the water with elegant fans and backbite at insults that only they can hear. Hermits heft new homes like critics, tap, weigh, decide, and become their own furniture. In the blacker bowls, there are sculpins and gobies the size of a man’s thumb, wearing colors no eye in this place rewards; they feel change along their flanks, an undulation that is information.

Sometimes, when storms breathe in, an octopus takes a room and makes a midden: a rim of opened shells that says this hollow is rented to thought and invention. She learns the signature of each surge and meets it with her own math of arms, rearranging the furniture of stones to suit her hunger and her temper. For weeks, the cave’s economy tilts around her quiet empire.

Between the sea’s reach and the ceiling’s colony, the stone sweats a polity of small scavengers and opportunists. Amphipods crowd like sand with opinions, leaping without looking because looking is less efficient than reflex. They pick meat from ghost feathers and groom the damp where a wing brushed. Springtails—little punctuation marks—bob in agreeing herds wherever a bead of fresh roosts long enough to ring a sentence. Isopods as flat as ideas you can’t get rid of creep the films, tasting with their whole bodies. The ones born to darkness are translucent, legs too long by a sunlit logic, eyes dissolved into memory. They do not guess where the world ends because their antennae are sure of it. Millipedes harvest the threads of fungus and rewrite the threads as themselves. Centipedes patrol like sharpened verbs, angular as hunger, reading vibrations with their many feet and administering revisions to the careless.

The guano is a province with its own laws. It receives the bat’s flying taxes and hires immediately: pale cockroaches that never quite leave the page, booklice that are older than paper as a technology, rove beetles built like opinion, tails held up as if to sting when they only mean to bluff. Pseudoscorpions stand with their small harvesters lifted, waiting to be hired by gravity; when a bird or bat returns, they seize a sea of fur with those pincers and ride to a new economy on purpose, phoresy as strategy. Mites measure square foot by square foot and make ownership a swarm. Filaments of fungus stitch white embroidery through the damp piles and bloom shyly, fruiting with no sunlight to finish the thought. The air over the mounds is warm and learned, scribbled with ammonia’s sharp handwriting and the sweeter adjectives of rot.

Up under the roof, the sleep is communal and deliberate. Bats cork the ceiling until it is fur grown on stone. They have allies they will never know: nitrogen-loving bacteria that take their exhalations and leavings and turn them, step by courteous step, into nitrate, into the bitter crystals that fret the limestone, into the slow etchings that widen a room like a sigh. The colony itself is a weather system. It flutters when the sea shouts, settles when the sea withdraws, collapses into a thicker hush when a few thousand points of warmth detach and leave for their nightly harvest. They return heavy with moth and beetle and gnat; what they cannot make bone or milk their bodies offer back to the cave, and so the cave fattens not on light, but on the insect rains of the world beyond.

Threaded among them, installed where the rock fits the curved ambition of a cup, are the birds that learned the cave the way a mouth learns to whistle. They do not own the sound here; they place their clicks into it. Swiftlets—brown, exact, all efficiency—bat their wings in black rooms and name the walls with sonar syllables. They mortar their nests from spit that cures to china and lay eggs that are milk-white and unargued with. In some places, far away, humans climb to steal those nests. Here the nests grow a lacquering of drip and salt, a pale fungus patina, and serve as punctuation for another year. Storm-petrels may rent a side chamber, coming back to their dust-letters by smell alone; their voices are small bells, ceramic and haunted.

The fresh lenses that ride the brine like a thought ride a fear as well. Here live the things that know two waters and belong to neither. Transparent shrimp ghost through the boundary, flashing for a second when your toe makes a seismic event. Copepods orbit each other like ideas that haven’t decided what they are. Ostracods shoal as coins. Planarians—soft knives—pare slugs of flesh from anything slow and soft enough to be improved. Threading longest through the clearest bowls are the white hungers, eel-thin and undone by eyes they do not have. They hold in place when your attention falls on them, a stillness that pretends to be stone. They smell you. Their superstitions run through their skins, through lateral lines that hear the sea think to itself. They rise to the ceiling of their own sky for a bubble that tastes like daytime. When storm brine overturns their tiny world, those who have the trick of it retreat into cracks and wait for arithmetic: the fresh will settle back on the salt as surely as silence will settle back on echo.

The halocline—where one water sits atop another and the world holds two truths without argument—is busy with chemists. Bacteria that make a living on difference bloom there, harvesting the gradient the way others harvest light. They take a little sulfur born of rot, a little oxygen bled in from outside, and run a slow ledger that ends in more of themselves. Their bodies are not much to look at unless you are them; their mathematics feeds the amphipods, who feed the centipedes, who feed the spiders, who feed the bats, who feed the molds, who feed the bacteria again. The cave is a circle of done with very few straight lines.

The black glow that answers your foot is the gossip of plankton tucked into a place that rarely deserves night. In summer, in currents that smell like warm continents, dinoflagellates stumble into the cave and set up their small factories of light. They do not care who uses the signal; they only insist that disturbance be answered. Your toe inscribes a comet in their dark; a fish writes an alphabet it cannot read. Some seasons, ostracods take over the lamps and write in blue sparks so brief you doubt them. All of it says: here is motion. All of it says: now.

Predators exist as clarifications. Shore eels thread into tidepools half-sand, half-breath, their ribs learning the metric of slopes; they are most themselves in the second before they strike. Sea stars—austere and patient—arrive like five pale suggestions and reframe an argument by climbing on it and digesting its heart. Octopus, crabs, rove beetles, centipedes, little spiders with long legs like question marks—they all own teeth in some form and the will to place them. No one is sovereign. No one starves alone.

On the margins, where the rock lifts a degree and the air isn’t shoved inward and pulled out by the ocean’s chest, live the quieter permanent citizens: the troglobites, the ones who have never asked the question what is sunlight. They are small and low-metabolic as abbots. They move when they must. Legs grew longer because distances became maps of touch. Antennae grew extravagant because nothing is more valuable here than an advanced report on the near future of a surface. Pigments melted off like manners. Eyes closed and did not open again. Their world extends exactly as far as their whiskers reach, and it is enough. They do not fall prey to nostalgia. Outside is an idea that does not pay rent.

Who else lives here are the rules. Attachment is life. The surface of anything is an opportunity and an argument. The first second you stop, a film arrives; the next minute, a barnacle larva tests you with its face; a day later, you are property. Space is rationed, so bodies adapt to fold—in layers, in strings, in bouquets. They live on top of each other and inside each other and through each other. Filters become flesh; flesh becomes droppings; droppings become filaments; filaments become meat for mouths that have never seen anything except touch. Orphans from storms, children of moon-led tides, ghosts of insects from faraway forests, bird-boned traders who move nitrogen along the coast, bats who refine the night’s moth-harvest into heat—all of them are more themselves here than somewhere else.

Sometimes a winter seal shoulders its way into the lowest chamber and sleeps like a polished boulder that dreams of barking. Sometimes a human, sagging with curiosity and ropes, fumbles into the breath and sound and names things wrong. Neither stays; neither needs to. The true inhabitants have arranged to be present whether the cave is crowded by waves or left quiet as a lung between sighs.

You could call them by their natural-history names if that made it safer to love them: barnacle, mussel, anemone, chiton, limpet, whelk, crab, shrimp, goby, sculpin, octopus, amphipod, isopod, springtail, millipede, centipede, spider, cockroach, rove beetle, pseudoscorpion, mite, fungus, bacterium, swiftlet, storm-petrel, bat. But the list would lie about the experience. What lives here is a grammar of touch and appetite that writes itself new every tide. It is a choir that rehearses chaos until it becomes a pattern. It is particular: a hundred thousand small economies reconciled into weather. If you stand very still and stop asking for faces, you can feel them reorganizing you into their map—another vertical in the manifold. For a breath, you are part of what lives here too, a tall thing that learned to decide the world with sound and pressure when the lights went out. Then you move, and the place folds back around the gap you leave, ferrying your shape away as quickly as water will let it.
