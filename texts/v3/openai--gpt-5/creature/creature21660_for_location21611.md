What lives here has taken the place’s habits as a body-plan.

At first you don’t see them. You see their preferences: a notch pinched into a rimstone lip just enough that overflow stops tearing moss from a corner; a gullied beadline where someone has worried at a calcite film with a bone scraper until two drips learn to become one; a willow twig combed and fan-set across a rill not to dam it but to give it teeth. You see an alder lattice thickened on one side and thinned on the other, a correction for winter’s weak noon. You see, under a moss mat, an air-space the exact size of a fist that never floods because a patient hand has kept it porous. Stand long enough, and the work coheres into its makers the way water makes a current visible.

They are small and water-dark, the length of your forearm from nose to tail tip, fur laid short and slick except for a pale hoarfrost of guard hairs along the spine that breaks the shine and turns their outline moss-colored under dapples. The head is wedgey, the muzzle cleft slightly up the middle and whiskered with a long, fine rake of vibrissae that taste draught and difference. The ears are modest and can be closed by a fold of skin when they go under. Their eyes are lacquer-black with a second, milky lid that slides sideways; when the lid is down, skylight writes itself as a soft glyph on the surface, which unmarks them from above. The hands are not human, but you will think so at first glance: forepaws with five digits and half-webbed second and third fingers, built to feel and place and comb. The feet plant soft, the heel long, the webbing between toes delicate and tense like willow withies. The tail is not a swimmer’s blade; it is a counter and a rudder, furred all around, carried low and used, often, as a third contact to the rock to keep the body from blocking flow.

Call them the porous, for they name themselves with a gesture—palms cupped, then spread, and air let through. They do not permit a skin on water if they can help it, and they do not present skins of their own. You won’t hear their speech unless you sit out a day and a night and cheat by ear on the rock: a thin tapping passed along the underlips of gours like a sentence underlined; a breath-shaped syllable straightened through the hands as if the hands were reeds; a whisker-strike on wetted limestone that leaves no mark but carries position. Sound is intent here. They measure how quiet they are the way others measure how strong.

They live everywhere the roots make space enough for a small body to turn and for water to sigh without complaint. Under alder buttresses in peat that has been taught to drain; in peat-rooms under beaver lodges, approached from the air-side by a tunnel that always has a second door; in rimstone caves that never quite wall themselves off because someone keeps their lower edge thinned with a thumb. They lay their nests in the driest of these, nests woven of willow bast and the gloss-strand of caddis cases, poured over with a thin milk of limestone they have coaxed out of seep lines and rubbed into the fabric until the nest takes the room’s temperature and never sweats. You could pass a hand over one and feel only moss. They leave a pocket in the weave—a little emptiness near the rim—kin’s doorway and their best rule made visible.

Their work looks like the place learning quicker than it otherwise would. They are rimwrights: they pinch new lips shorter on one side to teach a pool which way to overtop, so an eddy stops plucking trout from their corners and a vole path keeps its gloss. They are seamcombers: they rake ceilings with a fan of willow strips to persuade droplets to march in even lines instead of blotting out a wall with seep, because a wall that weeps evenly ices evenly, and even ice breaks with a voice that warns. They sweeten the beavers’ grade, cribbing stickwork into thin runnels where calcite has not had time to build, twigs set like capillaries so a sudden water can diffuse instead of cut. They rake spruce pollen (on the rare gusts that bring it down) and bat droppings from the seam-rooms into seeping places, feed for the algae they husband on driplines so the lines hold well and green and pull their share of light downward. They peel a ring of willow bark here and there, and the willow answers with a hundred small shoots that can be braided into a trellis the size of someone’s two hands. Their mark is in how edges last.

They eat the place small. Amphipods shelled between the side teeth and swilled before swallowing; isopods tasting of metal and leaf; caddis larva plucked delicately from its case and thanked by a nibble of algae placed back in the now-empty tube; snail eggs a brief, rare treat, and snails themselves left alone more often than not because they are respectable writers and better left to draw their lines. They bob the drips for copepods, they gather alder catkins wet and soften them between palms, they chew willow cambium in hungry years and spit the strips back into baskets to ferment. Eggs of trout are taken at flood only, in token and with risk, and always some left to the under-stones because they keep their own larders by other means. If you look for their granaries, look down: gours turned pantry by nothing more than a finger laid in the right place so a lip becomes a spill that can be blocked by a pebble when danger comes, the food inside bathing in water cold enough to keep sweet and too lively to grow still. If you are thinking of fire, do not. They know heat as water knows it. Their warmth is a lodger’s warmth, co-opted where the beavers swim, where the river grumbles behind a hand-wide wall, where a rim holds a deeper pool that is never quite cold as the air.

They keep time by noon. There is a way each season writes a bright parallelogram upon a certain wall, and the porous learn those writings the way other people learn letters. They know what hour the bats flit by blue and black the air, because it changes the lisp of wings at seam-height. They can read mud as calmly as you read this page—sideslip of vole, an owl’s toe undone in a skid, the slick passage of a weasel around a lip too polished to grip. They wait to cross open pools until the swallows’ lanes careen and knot—a sign of updraft and the kind of light that makes owl eyes lazy. In storm, they listen not to thunder but to the pitch of feed-water on stone: six notes, seven, and the particular step when a hidden tributary shows itself by voice and you must be elsewhere.

They have rules, and the rules are almost comical in their specificity until you live by them and they become sense. Never lay anything flush across a run. If you must cross, cross with fingers. If you must sleep where a rise could put water to you, sleep with your ear to stone and your hand to a notch you’ve made that will wake you when it over-touches. Never take a thing at its youngest bright. If an ice-skin has set in the last hour, it belongs to the pool. If a calcite lip glows the way new shells do, you step beside it, on the pale stone that does not remember your foot. Leave a pocket. This for lodges and for talk both: every story keeps an open place where someone else can pass. Count the drips in threes at the change of light. If you cannot count them because they are one, be loud; go.

They are not alone, and they are careful about neighbors. The beaver kind are large kin who work wide and heavy and make mistakes you can see from winter; the porous watch their notches and make nicer ones alongside that the beavers forgive because the whole holds. With the bats they exchange nothing but respect and guano. Swifts skim hair from their backs when it loosens in spring and mortar it into their cups; in return, the porous keep two or three danger-lines open under the honeycombs so rat snakes and weasels have to think too long about trying. Of the weasels themselves they do not speak—there is silence around that kind of certainty. The owls they map with absence, staying to the underlips of bowls until blue goes gunmetal and the skylights are only cold rumors.

They are a people of small ceremonies. A new light-room—a skylight punched fresh and smelling like crushed shells and winter-sour dust—draws them from three terraces to stand along the edges like a scallop’s mantle, each with a willow switch that has one leaf on it. They count the seconds until the first drop falls from the fresh-broken edge, and the one whose hand is there names the run with that number and that day. At the first freeze of a pool that kept itself water to the last breath of autumn, they hang broken amphipod cases along the rim, case after case, until some minute tilt of air moves them and they strike the ice; this is their singing, a shy, thin, many-belled joy that can last for the span of a vole’s nap. When a terrace fails—when bowls pour out in a run like the chimes tossed from a lap—they come after thunder, little heads low, and touch the newly-plain with their noses, and there is a word they make then with breath through hand that means both grief and good map.

They make and wear things, but all their making bows to the rule of not-sheathing flow. Their cloaks are lattices, not skins: willow-bark strips feathered with caddis silk and set with a nap of alder pulp, dipped in thin milk of limestone and flexed until set. When still, they are a rock’s understory. When moving, they whisper of themselves only as a rearrangement of gloss. Their gloves are leaves sewn into a cup that sheds water and never drips from a fingertip but passes it along; their tools are light. A forearm-long deer-bone ground into a blade for shaving new lips thinner; a porcupine quill lashed to a twig as a pointer for teaching drips to agree; a stone sliver honed on gourd-undersides to score a drainage in fresh mud before it learns another mind. They carry those in their mouths often; hands are kept for touch.

They raise their young in rooms where water is near but not seen. The first lesson is to lie in a nest and hear three drips, then tell with the hand which one is a risk and which one is only pretty, and which one is food. The second is to open a drain with the pad of a thumb and to stop again before it is empty, because empty things call. Names come the day a kit first finds the noon in a place no elder had taught them—the white parallelogram in summer on a wall under a cowl of willow, three hand-heights left of the brown line of the flood whose year is told. The kit lays both palms against the bright and then to the chest, taking the light in; thereafter, in tap and breath, that angle and hour are theirs.

They die as the place does, by quiet failings mostly. An unnoticed film can grow where breath warms often, a bright new lip can overtop and drown a pocket, a weasel can be faster than luck must be. They do not make graves. They return bodies to rooms that feed: a root-lattice scaffold where water clicks around bone and calcite begins its slow white; a bowl of peat that will remember as stain; under a beaver lodge where the water is always warm enough to keep slender fingers soft for a last week and let kin come and press whisker to whisker good-bye. A generation later, you cannot tell where a body was, except that a rim somewhere is thicker in one place enough to change a trickle’s mind.

If another people had found this trench and taken it as city, they would have given it a voice. These do not. Their governance is a kind of distributed attention: whoever is first and best at a place becomes its muscle-memory for a time, and when they leave or die the place keeps some of their habits by what they taught the water and the plants. When floods lay a new grammar down, new will comes. Disagreement is enacted, not argued—a lip left thicker and one thinned more, and the test watched at noon. If the place puts its hand on your shoulder and says you are wrong, you were wrong. They are not moral in any way you can drag upright; they are obedient to something older and colder.

If you meet one—if it chooses to let you see it as more than a smudge caught in your side-eye—there will be no startle and no greeting. It will place one hand palm-up toward you and hold it under a drip, and the drop will fall and explode its small white on their skin without running off. If you answer by putting your own hand there and feeling the cold numb you like a thought you cannot finish, you will have accepted the way time passes here. Then it will take your hand and put it, lightly, on the palest stone to teach you where to step next, and your foot will remember the lesson elsewhere.

You leave, and you carry them anyhow. The sense of second doors in every room. The knowledge that a run can be corrected by the smallest pressure in the right place. A new reluctance to lay anything flat across a flow, even in speech. A willingness to count by drops. And if you come back, if you voice their name (which is not a word but a release of breath through cupped fingers) into the cool and silver, you will not be answered—but some lip will have been made thinner new, some twig set just so, and you will feel, along the bones of your forearm where the cold found it, that someone was here and is still.
