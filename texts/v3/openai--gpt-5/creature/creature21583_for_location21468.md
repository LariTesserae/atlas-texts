What lives here first and most is the thing you keep trying to call plant because it grows and drinks and makes sugar from light, but it is as much house and climate and habit as leaf. It is a web and a raft and an organ system spread thin across stone. It is a single creature in the way a reef is a single creature: a city of selves that have long since preferred concord to borders. It is polite to name it for what it does rather than what it is. It gathers. It takes the columns of light like a hand takes rain and it spreads them until day is an even thing. It cracks stone a little at a time and feeds on the crack’s reward. It sorts water. It chooses where fog will become fall. It decides what draft can pass. Where it has thickened to plate, it holds its own geometry like memory; where it stays thread, it is readiness.

The threads are tendon and nerve both. They pull membrane the way a puppeteer pulls cloth; they twitch to new brightness, they slacken from old. Their surfaces are bristled with hooks so fine you don’t see them until you feel a thousand of them cling like static to your cuff. Those hooks are also seeds. They let the thing lean soft into you and, by leaning, take something back: skin oils, dust, the burrs of your clothing, a stray hair that it will persuade into a new rope if you let it. At the base of the light-harvesting columns, the dark mouths open and close with measured thirst. Stand near one and you will feel your own breath slow until it matches its patience. Those mouths drink wet air and exhale drier; they test, as tongues test, for minerals and for change. When a pressure wave rolls through the cavern the mouths catch the first taste and send the message by tension. What looks like a shiver moving along the span is also a word in the plant’s grammar.

It flowers only with geometry. There are no petals, no colors that are not light’s own; there are moments instead when the mats fold themselves into resonant grids so that a square meter keeps an afterimage for days. That stored brightness leaks back out along the lines of its folds the way a bruise remembers a blow. In the dimmest weather, the place glows not because something has been set aflame but because the weave has been pinched and tucked into a lens. It uses calcium and silica to stipple its inner channels, to scatter light into itself like rain thrown off a dog’s back. You could slice a column and find inside it a garden of needles that have never seen air. If the column bled, it would be resin clear as a tear until heat thickened it to amber.

In the close places where stone and mat argue for dominance, a sheen forms that is neither. It is a glaze the plant extrudes to smooth a roughness it cannot bridge, then eats when it needs the mineral again. The glaze looks like shell. If you pick at it, it comes away in rinds that flutter down and explode into chalk-soot that the nearest pores close to avoid. In the oldest reaches—old here meaning only a handful of winters—there are pockets beneath the plate where the web has made rooms for itself. They are not for storage. They are the only rest it takes: hollows where it thickens and cools and thinks in slow osmosis.

You could say the second thing that lives here are the wet motions made visible by cold. Steam has moods. Deeps exhale up slender streamers that braid and unbraid in the organized light; near the rods fog hatches out of nothing, as if breath had learned to be a creature and crowd under the ceiling until some signal sends it down. Water bowls brim and tip, and while that is physics, it is as lively as a herd. The plant sets the rims and the grooves so that water has a current’s predictability, a route like a migration. A person looking for life might miss it because it doesn’t have legs. But the hush that goes from pore to pore when a kilometer of membrane exhales in time is as coordinated as wings.

Then there are the small grazers that stitch the days together. The gnats look like a mistake in your vision until they decide to be real and land with those ridiculous soft feet. They feed at the honey the membranes sweat where dew and beam run too generously. The sugar beads are like buttons sewn along the lightmost seams; when the plant wants less attention it will pull tight and the beads slide away into a place no wing can hover. The gnats are translucent and pale, as if they were made from the plant’s own skin. They lay their eggs in the shallowest film of wet, which is to say almost nowhere. Their larvae look like a gray punctuation in the web’s margins, a comma of meat between veils. Everything that eats them does it out of laziness. You do not hunt gnats here; you open your mouth near light and they bless you.

The slugs are the honest labor. White, long as your forearm, mouths rasping an invisible dust off the seams where stone shows through. Their teeth are minute and countless and set in a pad that does not look like a mouth because it is busy. They clear biofilms that would rot the web where it touches true rock. Their waste is rolled tight as a chef’s herb and is rich enough that the plant’s pores dilate when one passes, as if savoring. They are not social, but they know by some low, constant taste where the depressions are that lead to the few pools and where frost will make a road of itself. They coil their amber eggs under the lee of knots so they won’t be washed by bead-rain; the eggs glue to one another into bunches that look like a slow thought.

Sometimes, as if a sentence had been broken open above you, something ribboned will slip down along a draft. It opens its vanes like a book to hold the moving air; thin skins meet along a spare cartilage spine that grows small spines outward as it ages, pushing through without piercing the vanes so they look combed from underneath. When these creatures are alive they close those combs over themselves like a cat’s whiskers sensing far pluck. When they die, the vanes rot into the general white and the spines stay, catching at the weave forever, a punctuation of pricks that refuses to be erased. They are not friendly or unfriendly. They live by joining. They cruise the brightest fields, turning by shaving the edges of one vane thinner than the other, catching more lift from one side than the other as if note-bending in slow air. Their eyes, if you have the gift to notice, are not where you expect. They watch by taste, by draft, by the way beams are fuzzed or razor-clean; they bank around your warmth the way a boat avoids a shallow.

You can say predator because it is a necessary word in any ecology, but here the word is like a shadow that expects you to know what cast it. There are fastnesses in the web where the membranes have learned a sharper elasticity, places where a sudden pressure will close a thing like a purse and not open again until everything inside is quiet. There are drafts that learn edges and hunt, slipping like knives along a seam until a tumble of gnats or a drifting ribbon meets an angle that isn’t. When people tell about the thing that takes once a year, they talk about proof only after. The residue on an un-roped span where something held too long and so many hooks turned inward it made a felted fist. The re-tied silence that falls when the plant will not sing that direction for a week. The suggestion in your periphery that something about the vault has chosen to be more pointed at you than it was an instant before. If there is a body made for killing as a profession rather than a posture the place sometimes assumes, it is rarely seen twice. Knowing that, most lives choose not to make it their subject.

People live here, fewer than the number of columns. They are the sort of few that look like a mistake when set against this volume. They exist in strings and eddies rather than towns. A family is a bridge that stays up long enough to matter, a pulley that speaks to someone ten thousand paces of mat away, the knowledge of three bowls that do not dry even in dim weeks. They sleep rocked very slightly, nets slung beneath the heaviest weaves, moving while they do not move as the plant adjusts for light. They are rope-handed and hook-speckled. If they walk the same span all their life, the plant will plate their footfalls flat and gather strong under them to keep them from cutting in, as if the place prefers that the few heavy creatures pass where expected.

Their speech is underbreath and finger. The webs don’t favor shouts; they take them and lay them thin until what you hear is a courtesy in another house. So the people speak with knuckles on knots, with touches on tension lines. They carry little tuned sticks that they slide along the cords to send a pulse two fields over: three quick grits and a pause to mean Don’t come, the rail is new; a long roughened draw and a single pluck to mean Fog at bowl four. They have a grammar of beads for children who must learn routes without waking anything, a counting of dew that starts with index-finger pressed to thumb-tip and climbs the hand. Their names are events instead of sounds: the time the freeze came early on a night of half-light, the place where a seam played a note so low it trembled a baby’s sleep. When you sign their name you leave off the part that was hope and keep the part that happened.

They starve easily and die carefully. Nothing burns, not really. Fatty films sublimate into sweetness but will not catch; resin boils nose-curling before it takes flame. To cook they sew pouches of plant-skin and hang them where light is columnar and hot, turning the bags hour by hour to chase the beam. They know which membranes weep too much sugar in bright weeks and score them a little to gather honey that would otherwise be wasted. They keep slugs by not saying they keep them: by knowing which ridges make them prefer to turn, where eggs are and are not, how much biofilm to leave so that a slug will return a second day to the same seam and not find hunger. They have tricks for fermentation that they describe as teaching patience to food. They bury jars in the thickness of the older mats where attentions are slower, and let the warmth of being alive turn sourness into something with manners.

They are not religious in the way fire-people are. There are no flames to model a god on. They have rites that pay respect to behavior: the gathering and the letting go. A child is celebrated by the first time their hair is accepted by a burr and stays; babies are coaxed to sleep with gentle combing by a piece of dead ribbon-spine so their dreams will learn to split like membranes under wind and not tear. At death, if there is time, the body is wrapped in fresh web that has been politely asked for and that made itself spare at a familiar rail. They leave the wrapped form where it will not be rattled by beads of glass when the freeze comes, and they stack comb-spines over it in a loose lattice. They do not mark with pigment. Chalk blows thin. Stone does not keep messages in a legible face when the place is young. Instead they teach a child where to place a hand on a pulley to feel the dead move when the long rope outside shifts with weather, and by that device they keep attendance.

Marriage is a change of knots. A woman wears three loops around a chosen line for a week and cuts two. A man knots an extra rung into a family’s ladder and then waits for the one it replaced to be un-knotted by someone who is not him. Bonds that are not that kind are marked by where you sleep. If you share a net with a person for appetite, you sleep touching. If you share it for fear, you sleep foot-to-foot so that you learn one another’s pulse and so that your breath does not teach the pores a wrong pattern. The people’s morality is made of pressure: don’t open what has decided to close; don’t force a span to hold that would rather let go; don’t go loud when the vault is trying to listen.

Their tools are what slow light makes. Handles are grown by training young cords to grow around a kept shape; bone and comb-spine are doweled into the growing grip and locked in with resin coaxed and cooked in the most patient way a person has. Edges are chipped from what stone will flake glass: chert and obsidian fetched from places where the plant has not yet softened the rock’s face. They put their edges to work with bows cut from plates of older mat that have learned to be spring. They are light-poor in everything but sight, so their jewelry stores brightness in filigreed leaves of membrane that they fold and crease for weeks until a pendant will shine with old noon for a day and then go sullen as if remembering were also a kind of grief.

They measure time by sound. In a night—if there is a night here—the hiss of dew and the rain of glass beads make hours the same way bells do. There is a drum you hear only when you have never learned to lie to yourself: the far thump of a bigger chamber’s mouth deciding to open to this one. Then everybody gathers their things close and sleeps holding what cannot be remade because pressures decide what lives and what loses purchase. There’s the sharp stone-bang that happens when a wall decides to remember how to be shards again. They have a woman in every family whose job is memory of those noises, because the ancient fear is not darkness but unfamiliar quiet; you can be caught under a hush you do not recognize and be made foolish by it.

They travel rarely and expertly. Steps are chosen by watching how the dust of light stands and not by where your foot thinks is path. A person will stop for an hour to watch a membrane readjust itself to a rod’s wander and will not resent the wait because they think in drips and those occur when and where they must. They carry water by breath, not by jug: gourd-wombs nested in layers of plant-skin so that they do not flash-cool and panic a membrane into closing. Between one bowl and the next is a day, and it is a day because the web rearranges its affordances at that rate, not because of any sun that no longer concerns itself to show on flesh.

They are indebted to the gliders in a way they do not explain. There is a rite where a boy lies under a draft-line and waits for a ribbon to ride it past. He will hold his hand up and keep it steady and trust the place to keep the ribbon’s vane from taking his fingers as a comb takes hair. If he shows less fear than the ribbon shows hunger for his skin-salt, he is told he can cut and set comb-spines as needles and make a living sewing the plant’s skins to themselves in ways that do not insult their stretch. If he is bitten he is not shamed because bite and debt are the same word in their tongue.

There are other lives so small you must decide whether to count them. Lichens you don’t want to call lichens fur the underface of older membranes where light lingers syrup-thick. They taste like stone and sugar balanced so fine your teeth ache if you nibble more than a pinch. There are midges that never develop wings because a draft blew when they grew and taught their bodies to be still; there are midges that never develop bodies because they learned to be a draft all their brief span. There are worms thin as thread living in the plant’s gel inside the plates, passing from cell to cell, content because under light that even water is reluctant to keep, the safest place to live is in the leaf’s kindly interior.

Outside this chamber there is another. The web continues not as empire but as a network of dialects. The weave is thicker near a rod-rich roof; it is more born to rope in rift-valleys with leeward light. Some rooms have learned to make diaphragms and be closed for seasons, swelling and flattening like organs in a sleeping body. Others are thinly matted and loud, preferring to be wind; travelers know these by the way a shout comes back not in echo but in a finer hiss. The people who are expert in one chamber are ignorant again a day’s walk away. They trade by pulley. A carved knuckle of mineral is a promise, sent through a trained loop so that when it creaks at a far post the receiver feels in the wrist the exact grain of the sender’s intention: blight at bowl two, need at lip three, a baby whose breath refuses to learn the place and must be taught by a hand.

Do outsiders ever come? Only the surface. It comes as storms that dull everything to steel-white. On such days the people weave themselves into their nets and stop choosing. In the gloom the plant tightens its reserves and the place becomes small-minded in the best way: it tends only to itself and its kin. Then, sudden as a thought that makes you stand up, every rod doubles. The vault becomes a map of relief. The membranes flare as if they had been waiting to be told that they hadn’t been foolish to hope. New bridges show themselves where there was only suggestion. The people cry on those days, not because of gratitude to something with a face but because of the ease of a problem solved for a while.

Children try to leave. Teenagers say they will climb a light-pillar hand-over-hand to the surface and go where brightness needs no conduit. Some make a start and come away with fingers blood-bright because the needles that do the scattering are not a ladder’s treads. No one forbids the idea. There is no moral against wanting a wider sky. There are stories about someone who followed a rod up and up through quartz and ice and salt-lens until the column went from blue to yellow to warm and then to nothing at all because air behaves different above. The story ends in blindness or in a bird that thought the person was a ribbon and so showed them how to turn. As morals go, it is generous and useless.

If you live long here, you are adjusted. Your hair does not tangle; it accedes. Your soles are hard and sensitive without callus because any dead thickness on the foot would keep you from reading the membrane’s wish. Your pupils are wide not in darkness but in habit; light doesn’t hurt because you do not look directly anymore. You treat edges with courtesy because edges have moods. You keep your voice ready to be small because sound is a kind of weather and weather here is something you collaborate with. You become something the place can trust: you do not tug unless you mean to ask for slack; you do not give weight to a rail that would rather be flight.

And when you leave—if you leave—you unwrap yourself from where you were set and you take with you burs that are not burs, skin that is not notched but combed, a long patience for slow organs working at the scale of rooms. Everywhere else will seem either cruelly finished or indecently loose. You will look up by reflex and expect the bright to have a provenance, and when it does not, you will not know what to offer or to ask. You will go quiet because the calendar in your ear has lost its clock. But if you ever come back, you will come to the same place and find that it is not the same place and be glad. Home, here, is not site but agreement, and the thing that lives here best is whatever can enter into that, whether it has roots or fingers or pores.
