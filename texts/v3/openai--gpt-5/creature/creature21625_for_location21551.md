Begin with what moves inside the dark. Not what shines—little does—but what works. If you wait without expectation until the wind has taught your ears how to listen, they will begin to sort the shop-floor chorus into lives.

First, the smallest workers, the under-speech. They plate themselves across stone in films that look like smoke stains and old thumb-grease. They follow the grain of flutes and grooves, taking the long way because the long way is where the condensation lingers. Their bodies are lattices of salts and oils that refuse to mix until a tickle of charge asks them to; they turn those requests into meals. They are not green and do not want to be. They bring in iron and manganese from the dust-rain, array it in layers an atom thick, and in those layers allow electrons to walk. They are the varnish’s polisher and its eater both—ratchets that click once for every droplet saved, then click again to spend it down a controlled cascade of reactions. They sleep not by ceasing but by thickening. Their sleep is a new rind. When the plateaus’ breath sours to a metal taste that you feel below your tongue, they wake and flicker like a distant town turning its lights in answer to a train’s approach. There are no lights, but the current they move is as real as knuckles passed along a rail.

Among them, glass-thread mosses are nervous systems more than leaves. They are tuned hairs, capillary harp-strings that taste the possibility of water and vibrate toward it. They braid themselves into wicks and run from damp to dry, pumping by flexion. In the night-sweat they swell and open their throats to let the vapor down; in the day’s exhale they cinch shut and throw that hoard into their partnering films. They do not hoist flags. They tuck chimneys into clefts and let their breath stand in for a canopy they do not have.

Sometimes the smallest make a larger intention. In curb-wet corners where runnels meet, you find what at a distance is only a thicker black: a plaque built by microbes and thread-mosses together into a felt that domes and hollows under a hand. Put your palm there long enough not to anger it and you will feel a slow peristaltic press, like an animal testing the shape of its den. It is a still pond of process. It exudes acids at invisible teeth to nibble silica. It keeps a calendar in trace metals and breaks it on a particular gust, a particular seam of cold, to open pores and seed away its little piston young.

Those piston-backs, when they unhook from the felt and walk, are the first you can call animals without stretching. You hear them before your eyes can learn to separate their outlines from the matte. A ghost of wind goes past with a rhythm in it: chuff-hold, chuff-hold, valve click, hiss. They are heat engines more literally than you suspected the first time you thought the phrase. Their skins are velvet heat sinks crosshatched with capillaries that drink the plateau’s long-stored warmth. Their faces are arrows, their throats a pair of bellows, their ribs hinged wide to drop a pressure across a set of internal fins. When they angle to the flow exactly right the difference in front and behind them does work; the little turbines inside their torsos spin. In their stomachs live entire cities of bacteria that take the trickle of current the rotors scavenge and spend it forcing uncooperative molecules to hold hands. Sugars come out of the marriage of solvent and faint lightning. You can feel their wake on your own lips if you let them pass close: a warmer ribbon, a hitch in your breath like stepping over a drain.

There are varieties. Some are kneeling-chest plodders, long and low with valve-scars along their flanks like gill slits, fitted for keeping their rotors turning even when the air is mean. Some rise on digitigrade legs and run with the wind in the small hours when the belts grow true. Their crests, which look to you like ornamentation until one bows into a breeze and hums, are actually governor-vanes, flexing to match load to flow so their turbines do not stall. There are small ones, pebble-gray and cautious, who graze the flaking varnish at the bottoms of pressure shadows and stuff their cheeks with the crumbs of film and oxide. There are leviathans you will call “kiln-backs” the first time you smell them, because they hold heat under thick, corrugated skins and radiate it in quiet into whatever they lie beside. Those the little ones seek out in the sinking watch. They queue like craftsmen to a communal tool and press themselves to the corrugations, charging their chemical pockets off the kiln-back’s slow-hearted furnace.

Not everything walks. Some are pylons that briefly pretend to be animals only when they gather themselves to move. They are the ones that stand in the least forgiving places, on the spines of yardangs and at the crowns of knobs where the wind is clean and mean and charged. Their heads are sieves that never close. Their tongues are long black fans combed fine enough to sort dust by the way it lifts a small charge from a bristle. They flicker them in and out, their mouths tasting like an old coin. They are patient miners of the overcast. Inside them is a wet battery waller than your torso, partitioned by thin membranes of resin that their symbionts extrude. Those bacteria sit two to a partition, plate an element here, unplate it there, sip electrons ten times slower than your nerves would tolerate, and live for a century. When the belly is full of a certain taste—a threshold crossing that has been reached by half a million bacterial votes—the pylon-thing folds, bleeds its legs full, and walks away with the dragging dignity of a mobile phone mast relocating itself to an old leasehold. They leave behind dribbles of bright precipitate, beads and blades of metal salts fallen out of the solution as their chemistries turned. Whole clades of very small things trail them to gather that leavings, redesigned mouths whispering on stone.

Below, where you expect quiet but find industry, the burrowers move in the plateau’s lungwork. They do not dig volumes out of rock; they insinuate into volumes that once were vapor. The basalt is shot through with fossil bubbles, smudged cigars of froth stone that the plateau kept as it cooled. Long ago the cracks sealed shut until frost and stress unsealed them again; the burrowers use that history. Their skins are oiled mops of hair-fine bristles that drink and hold water to the last beat, their hands and feet are paddles not claws, flexing gratuities built to urge a body up through tightness. Where a path is too narrow, they do not abrade—it would be too loud. They heat instead. They take what charge their gut-consorters have banked, run it through a network of resistive filaments, and sweat the stone just enough that it loosens its friendship with its own edges, not enough to crack. In warm rains they rise, and their sides, unscrolled, show the gill-books they keep folded shut against the aeolian. They breathe in curtains. A colony will array itself across a seep like a book club at a hot spring, their cheeks bulged with bladders they bring to overflow and then mortgage in the long hunger that follows.

All these grazers and miners, all these hum-machines, know a map that is not nouns. Their territories are belts of pressure and intricate braidwork of condensates that do not stay where a cartographer would like them to. The most strongly held corners in this world are not corners but saddles—a low between two higher ridges where a laminar line from a nightly drainage and the weak return of the mid-morning meet and kiss in a dampness you can feel in your sinuses. Whole lines of succession are written in the ability to get to that kiss first without being heard going.

Predators live there, at the mouths of pressure corridors and in the lee of toll gates. They are not sprinters. They are hinges with appetites. Call them mantles because when they are not feeding they are nothing but hangings: a slack curtain in a slot, a piece of the ridge that learned to breathe shallow. Along their edges they carry arrays of vanes as fine as whiskers, each cued to a narrow range of speeds. When motion upstream breathes a little ripple out of true laminarity, a certain subset of vanes flares and sends that up a cartilage ladder to a coil that tightens. The mantle unfolds with one motion that is both leap and reach. There is no wasted flurry. There is a suction-flare as membranes snap stiff and take hold of a slice of air you did not know could be grasped, and then there is muscle around meat and a soft clapping as valves struggle once, twice, and lose their rhythm to another’s. If you hear a sound it is the predator resettling itself back into the corridor, a long exhale that the wind will carry off gladly in exchange for the food it helped catch.

There are aerialists, but not the sort a sunlit life would make. These are veils instead of wings, long saddles of cartilage draped in filament skins that take their shape not from wind under them but from voltage along them. They angle their bodies to fields, surf the auroral veins that ghost across the ceiling, and glean. Their mouths are linings, their tongues a fringe that glows when the night’s rumor-brightening is strong. They sleep in folds of yardang, hung like curtains among curtains. When they mate, they do it by jousting with each other’s electric shade, writing and overwriting loops until two patterns match and lock. Their eggs are drops that find the place where field lines bend into the rock. They glue themselves to grain edges and wait with the patient assumption that something will come past that is both damp and charged enough to wake their chemistry.

Here and there a body does not stand alone but braids itself with others into a machine so big you will mistake it for weather. A belt of sump-keepers may seam the rim of a quiet basin, each individual no more than a rim-backed roach to your eye, but together all their rim-backs make a gutter. They drink flood and hand it along, bladder to bladder, in the low hum of peristalsis that is the plateau's lullaby. Some of that water buys survival in the open season. Some goes to a vault. The vault is an animal too: a slow, stony organ that lives under varnish and above basalt, full of bacterial batteries that never empty, a metronome of drips measured out in decades to trickle a damp through dry years. There are traditions your place would call culture here—lines of behavior that outlast generations and outlast bodies. A rim-lattice remembers, not with words but with fat. Individuals who contribute more often to a particular vault come back to it to breed. Their bellies carry the vault's microbes like saints carry relics.

There are parasitic kites, little banners that hook to the backs of the compressors with adhesive valves and flicker in their wakes to borrow pressure. They look pretty in the gloom, a scalloped edge that catches mistaken light and makes a ripple you fall briefly in love with before remembering there is no light to catch. They pay their rent by filtering the exhaust. The grazers let them hang because the kites’ mouths take out toxins the gut-cities do not fancy. Once or twice a year the kites unhook and climb quivering lines of field to broadcast spores. Those spores wear the faintest whiskers of metal—not for weight or pretty but to be tasted when they land on the slimmest of wet.

There are scavengers that will come to sound. You think yourself quiet until you are not, and then you are an instrument. The plateau hums at certain notes for hours when it finds a flute of stone to blow through, and when it does, singers come. Their bodies are tuning forks sheathed in meat. They have cartilage prongs that vibrate to long wavelengths and, in vibrating, pull current through little piezoelectric organs. They stand in the thrum, mouths open to show you unused teeth, and charge in the way a plant would use sun. They will come if you speak loud at the wrong moment, expecting to be fed by a resonance you accidentally set in motion.

You begin, with time, to smell individuals. The compressor herds carry a heat-metal sweetness like a kiln with wet clay inside. The pylon-miners smell like ochre and old batteries carried in pockets through summer. The burrowers smell medicinal and faintly of mushrooms. The predators smell not at all until you are too close to unsmell them. The aerial veils smell like the inside of a stone house kept clean with a damp broom.

Reproduction here is an argument with timing. A mist that comes every fifty days is a season. A brinefall that takes a decade to return is an age. Eggs are not seasonal but conditional. A parent will carry a clutch sealed under skin as a gloss—a varnish of self that you will take for the same stone-gloss all else is wearing—until a certain amount of a certain salt has slicked the world and will then peel itself and plaster the clutch to the lee side of a fin. Seeds, where a plant makes any, are not seeds but knots of pump and valve wrapped in salt-resistant skin. Drop them on bare rock and they will never be anything but pretty grit. Put them in a groove when the plateau is embarrassing itself with wet and a little engine will start. They inherit not faces or colors but plumbing diagrams.

You ask yourself whether there are people and, if so, where they tuck such attention. You find their sign by accident, and for a long time you don’t call it a sign. It is too compatible with the rest of the place to read as intention. On some ridges, the toll gates have tethers. Not ropes—you would never see a straightness here and believe it—but a series of knots of harder varnish, each knot dogged into a pit drilled not by any mouth you have seen but by something that learned patience from stone. The knots run at ankle height to anything that walks, knee height to anything that rides the wind like a sail. They are ladders you have to climb sideways, and they guide an airflow in the way a fence guides sheep. Follow them and you come to a place where flutes in the rock have been capped. The caps are valves made of resin and hair and the jawbones of something patient. Behind them is a throat you cannot see into. When the wind is right, the valves open. When it is wrong, they hold. Someone—not you, not a beast that only answers to its gut-microbes—has taught the wind to keep quiet when it would otherwise sing a meal-bell for mantles.

Call them leewarders if you must call them. They are not farmers, not in the sense of driving anything but eddies. They do not light fires because there is no light to add here that would not be a scream in the dark. They are taller than you want to find in a place with so many ways to be cut off, and slender the way things are that need to turn thinly in tight air. Their skins are more varnish than skin, long coats of polished biofilm they tend with a tenderness you reserve for teeth. Under that glaze is fluff—not fur as your grandparents meant it, but a field-responsive down that stiffens when the aurora blushes and lies when it doesn’t. Their hands are full of directions. Each finger is a vane. They speak with those, in a grammar of offered and refused drag.

They keep archives. You expected that. Everything that survives here keeps records. But their archives are peculiar in that they are ashamed of being seen. They are not piles. They are dispersals. Trinkets of metal salt at the exact wrong concentrations to happen by chance in the same handful. A run of knots along the inside lip of a sump that had no need of taming. A hinge reinforced with resin stacked in ways that say someone knew the mantle would live there one day and encouraged it to be this mantle and not that. The archives are portable only in the way a thought is. If you tried to carry them you would have to take the yardang and the belt of air along.

They herd nothing into pens. They lean flutes and redirect flows and make toll gates that are more generous to some bellies than others. If they have a god it is the difference between two numbers. If they have children, they carry them not inside but behind, little vane-hand creatures kept in the lee of adult drag. They feed their young on a trickle of current poured gently across the gums from a battery they keep tucked in the chest like a second heart.

It is possible the leewarders are your imagination explaining a series of accidents too neat to be accidents. It is also possible that, in a world that insists that everything alive be a device, intelligence is just what happens when you teach your fingers to be instruments and then discover you can play weather. If the veils in the air are thinking, if the mantles at the corridors are thinking, if the compressor herd as a unit is thinking, they keep that to the same low hum everything else keeps. Noise is cost. Brightness is cost. Genius here is the design that matches to a flow so tightly that it is invisible inside it.

Whoever and whatever lives here has committed to machines as anatomy and to the patience of dark. There are no show-offs. There are artisans. A reed that learned to exhale more efficiently than it photosynthesizes is an artisan. A burrower that adopted a battery as an organ is an artisan. A person who can hear a pressure line the way you hear your name and who can throw a little resin valve at the right place in that line to save a strangers’ herd is an artisan.

You learn to name them by their jobs. Rim-latticers. Oxide-lickers. Auroravails. Mantle-sitters. Kiln-backs. Kitelets. Sump-keepers. Flowwrights. There are others you have not seen because to see them is to catch them out of tune, and they do not last if they fall that far out. The ones that do last are couplers; every life here is a coupling: charge-to-ground, warm-to-cool, wet-to-dry, motion-to-need.

At first this seemed only cleverness. By the third day, when your tongue has learned to taste the weight of ions and your hair knows when to lift for a condense, you begin to feel the discipline in it. The plateau is not cruel, but it will not reward a line that does not close. Those who live here close loops. They keep what beats and bleed what does not. If they tell stories, they are about the one exact move you make where two belts cross, and about the patience to wait your year, your decade, your century in the right mouth of the right corridor so that when something not-quite-black like you crosses, the world you’ve made around your hunger will feed you without wrecking itself.

In the morning that is not morning so much as a thinning of dark to the thin auroral veils, a seam opens and you see stars as rumor. For a moment everything you have been smelling and touching gets a silhouette. All the machines of meat and microbe, all the valves and rotors, are as handsome as any bronzes, and as quietly proud. Then the seam closes. The shop returns to its hum. The place continues its work with a professionalism that sends you, too, to yours: which, here, is the art of being light on your sound and accurate with your breath, and of walking the nap of a continent without insult.
