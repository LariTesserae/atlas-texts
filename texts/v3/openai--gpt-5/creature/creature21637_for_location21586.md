What lives on the rim has made a practice of being surface.

They are a people of fronts and films and attended edges, with bodies that have learned the thinness of the air and the weight of water when it is a thread instead of a flood. Their chests are broad but not barrel; their breath is economical because waste is an embarrassment. The backs of their hands are weather-burnished, almost glossy, from years of offering skin to chill and drip. Their fingers are quick at closures and patient at stasis; they can keep a valve a hair from shut for as long as you can keep a thought. A good listener, here, looks like a good worker: head slightly angled to the moving air, eyes doing most of the stillness.

Clothes are tools. Hats have slick skirts that guide the fog down into collar reservoirs; cuffs end in small ringed pockets that gather what the elbows have persuaded to travel. Coats betray their work by where they darken first: net tenders gloss at the forearms; gauge painters at the wrists and throat; flow readers at the tips of their gloves where they spend mornings feeling the thin. A child’s first garment has little wicking tails, more to teach the movement than to gather volume. On bright-break days, when the fog relents and the sun finds them, you can see the faint callus ring around each person’s wrist where the bottle-strap sits for a lifetime.

They are spindle-strong rather than heavy, and they stand as the shrubs do—leaned into one direction even at rest. Wind writes its edits on them: hair plaited into flat cords and tied to collars; beads hung low to keep the chin aware of sudden tilts. Their jewelry is not a display but a ledger. Bands of oxidized iron around the thumb or the ankle carry the knurled marks of old storms survived, of gutters won and gutters ceded. Children trade little discs of slag glass inscribed with a gust’s pitch; mates give each other calibrated nozzles as vows.

If sound here likes to walk along chosen paths, they do too. They speak in soft, angled voices aimed at you or at a wall that will bring the words back with all the aggressive brightness taken out. Whistled signals ride the tunnels; a particular scrape of broom across frost means watch your step in the next alley; three short clinks of a clamp set down on stone means stop, look, listen. They sing only to the wind organs and to infants. When they laugh they do it into the heels of their hands.

Their sleep is set by wetness and the hum of their small machines. “Spill,” the hour when the frost loosens its hold and everything thinks about becoming water, is not a time so much as a mood: a quiet quickening. On spill days—there are lull days too, when the air refuses them—they are already at the nets when you wake, legs braced against the scarp’s patient insistence, hanging new meshes and combs with the care and awe of beekeepers. They harvest water the way some tend orchards, with notes about the eccentricities of particular frames. “The south comb likes to dribble early,” someone will remark, and that is a piece of gossip you might later hear in a contract.

Contracts themselves are weather documents. The oldest are graven deep, charters with letters that look like they were taught to condense. They name not just people but habits: “this gutter, on the fifth day after a wind from the kettled quarter, in the hour before spill, shall feed the House of the Long Trill up to the green rib.” “Drip rights” they call them, but they read like biographies. You can follow the life of a woman—her steadiness, her failures, her kindness—through the gutters she tended and the hours she agreed to give. The city forgives late payments more quickly than it forgives varnish inside a public downspout.

Guild is a word they do not use because it suggests enclosure, and everything they do reaches into everything else. Still, there are vocations that smell a certain way and leave their particular lines on the hands:

- Flow readers, who are surgeons of the near-nothing. They go wrapped in felt and quiet, staves under arms, notebooks in oilskin, the calluses of their fingertips honed to a sense you could almost call taste. They can tell you when the water has picked up a breath of peat or a lick of iron, and so when some seam (animal, mineral, human) has gone wrong.

- Netwrights, who sit with skeins of resin thread and the silver combs and lacquered bamboo bones and figure out, by eye and by small wind-tunnels of their own making, how to teach a fog to fall where you wish it.

- Boomer-tuners, who set their chests against rock and listen until they can coax the wind pillars into telling exactly what kind of trouble is shaping up beyond the next notch.

- Gauge painters, part mechanic and part priest—who keep the measures truthful, apply the scale paint in careful bars that seem decorative until, in a blinding moment of sun, the whole ledge reads itself aloud in gleaming code.

- Feltliners, the most patient people in a patient place, who card the heath’s coarse fiber into a thousand skins-between and cut them to lie against stone and against water with no resentment.

Food is not abundance but succession. They eat as stones erode: shallow, sure, and cumulative. The edible lichen is more than a crop; it is an education in what repetition can do—soaked and beaten and lifted with ash lye into a soft sheet that becomes something like bread when warmed slow on a slate near a lamp. Heath-seeds ground coarse add a bitter comfort. The glasshouses give them greens and sweetness out of their own winter: short carrots with shoulders too proud for soil, fat-headed herbs that tried to be trees and found a vocation as sauce instead. Cistern fish are a privilege and a responsibility; each pool has its character, its stories of floods that emptied it and careful years that filled it again. Red worms, encouraged in long trays that breathe like bellows, go back and forth between the glasshouses and the fish ponds, between the trays and the pockets of the cliff, a little circle in the great one. Honey happens in the moss gardens under the warm glass, and it tastes of lamps and herbs and the idea of thunder.

They keep pets the way they keep any other domestic: by arriving at an agreement. Bees are colleagues. Salamanders, so clear you can see their single heart and its earnest argument with cold, are allowed to surge the drip pools on spill days and are left wholly alone. Children with steady hands are sent to drop tiny stalks of cooked lichen into pitchers’ mouths as payment for the warmth they, great lidded things, steal without malice. If the fox-thing that writes itself across the waist-high heath comes close in the blue hour, everyone speaks its name as little as possible so that whatever knowing it has about its safety remains what it already is.

They do have animals that came with their people once and were taught to become this place. There are no hooves—hooves are too simple an answer to too many small problems—but a low, quiet rabbit descended from some brave pair that traveled up on a dawn cable in some ancestor’s coat now moves by intent through the peat in the gardens, fattened deliberately on trimmings and brought up from kits around lamps so that hunger has bones and does not invent itself. In the warm wards moss is grown for steadiness rather than to feed mouths: its work is to keep the sound of the city at a hum. They tend it like a chapel.

The youngest learn to read by reading flow and stone together. Their first books are the city’s faces. A teacher leads a procession of felt-bundled small bodies out along the inner ledge and says: you will learn three letters today: the one that means tighten; the one that means slow; the one that means listen again. Children are named twice. Once, at birth, by whatever calm word their mothers can say without feeling they are making a claim on the weather. And then again, at the first spill they feel in their own fingertips, their second name chosen by the way the water travels when it meets their skin. There are people named Sheen and Lint and Third—one who has gone a whole life simply as After.

They make art that lays itself along function so closely that sometimes you forget to call it art. Nets patterned like ferns so that fernflies will breed on them and crib the first beads from the air; gutter-mouths inlaid with glows from the poison-lit lichens so that you can find them and not kick a toe into them ungently, and so as not to break the lichens’ calm with a lamp. Music is rarely produced so much as coaxed. They leave slatted flutes in little alcoves, and when the wind turns as it does in spring, the whole scarp speaks in four notes that were determined by the weight of a grandmother’s hand and the slight shrinking of bamboo over forty cold seasons.

Doctrine, if it needs a name, is the Law in the Water. It is practical and superstitious at once. You will hear it as a quiet litany said before someone opens a new gutter: no new cut without three old cuts made better; no lift without a drop; do not teach the stone a taste for much. They talk about the hungry rock as if it were an animal, because that is the easiest way to remember it is not on your side, not even when it is carrying you. They sheath their dead in felt and slate and give them to the cisterns at a pace the city’s readers decide; not as a burial—they are not sentimental in that way—but because the water should meet a little of each person and the city should drink its own memory. Names are carved next to drip scales, not in high places but where the stone is always just slightly damp. Memory should need a hand to touch it.

There are things that live here that did not come with anyone. The hawk that shears across the drop on wings as thin as thoughts is a citizen to the extent that any hunger is a citizen. The small moths that keep their bells of scent near the ground are the city’s arias, sounded so near the moss that even a sudden voice would not scatter them. The insects that mistake a transformer’s warmth for an honest sun give their bodies to the gray hawk at a rate that makes moral sentences about it feel like hubris. The fox-thing under the peat—no one has reason to say its true name in a loud voice—writes the margins, and they let it. And then there are the things that are not animals and are as alive to them as any beasts: a wall that has learned to sweat on one face first; a felt that, after enough seasons of tiny drinking and giving, knows how to draw a baffled fog into wisdom; a downspout that sings when it is time to take off the scale and see what the world has left inside.

On rare clear days, when everything brightens at once and the city stills as if it were embarrassed to be seen staring, the people do not pray. They hold out their hands, they let their skin know the amber of sun and the awkward comfort of being a body among space and glare. They mark those days with small bright scratches on their door lintels, parallel to the drip gauges so that joy can be read alongside duty.

There is no sharp East or West in their talk, no summer and winter as a primary rhythm. Their calendar is granular. They reckon by rumors: the week that the sky smeared itself along the cliff and the turbines sweetened their voices; the silver aloofness that went on long enough to make tempers crisp; the time the far cables wore coats of ice and the wind organs whispered it so low that only the oldest readers were uneasy and right. They keep a civic virtue that has to do with timing. To be good here is to be early to a gutter and late to a grievance.

They dream of two things: warmth and fairness. Warmth, they answer with lamps and each other. Fairness, they write down and set to tick slowly in ink and chisel as if it were a machine. Theft, to them, does not begin with want but with a lie about water. The worst story in their canon is about a house that drew “just a little extra” at night and taught the stone to expect it until one day the gutter, newly lusty, took half the cliff and three children and the word “more” has not had an easy time since.

And yet they are not severe. They joke like moss: in the small, in the continuance. On blue-fire nights when the St. Elmo’s wreathes the highest frames and everything looks as if it would like to stand and walk, children stand their hair on end and reach cautiously toward the humming green and shout with mock terror they have carefully rehearsed to be safe. Lovers meet in the moss gardens where the air stays at one kept temperature and make long slow promises in the smell of honey. Old people teach the rock its softness back by sitting against it and telling it out loud that it is forgiven for how hard it was when it was young.

They do not think of themselves as isolated. The gondolas go out into the perceived dark like long slow fishing casts, and they speak of the outlying farms as if they were cousins you must write to on paper you know will cockle in the mist. They trade in details. Someone from the far end of the ledge will bring news of a lichen that glows a fraction brighter if sung to softly; someone from the dry fall where the river once leapt will arrive pale with different oxygen and teach the city a new kind of trellis. Visitors are rare and treated not with spectacle but with attention: here, a better coat; here, the way you step on the frost to test the bond before you trust your weight; here, a net that will take your nervousness and explain to it that it is welcome.

What lives here is not one body but a negotiation built of many bodies and habits. It is a city that behaves like a bromeliad, catching its weather and raising little seas and the worlds that bloom along them. It is a people who have made law that does not pretend to be higher than gravity and who seem always to be on the verge of laughter not because anything is especially funny, but because attention, here, is a pleasure. And the rest of it—the hawk, the fox, the glow, the red worms, the breath you can see when you say a name into a cold hallway—is what comes when you spend generations learning to live where stone remembers being water and water learns to be law.
