They are people who were taught, by their land, that to live is to listen.

They come long and spring-light, a geometry tuned to spread weight and disrupt sympathy. Ankles flex sideways, toes splay in a quick, deft star under boots that are more instrument than shoe—laminated foams and lattices that throw their footfalls safely out of phase with whatever bass the world is holding at the hour. Inside their chests, cartilage is a second skeleton. Plates and struts grow dense along the breastbone and between ribs, honeycombed not for buoyancy but for voice. That frame will sing if you let it. Children are taught not to. To move here is to practice the unmatching step, the almost-dance that says I am present but I will not be taken into the chord.

Their bodies husband solvent the way city states husband water. Sweat is a misapprehension to them, a terrible and improvident animal story they shake their heads at in old books. They keep their liquids pocketed, in tidy sacs walled with shiny membranes; they sip pearls of thin, cold solvent and crack capsules between their teeth at meals. The breath they give back is brittle and cold enough to fog the edge of a lens and then be done. Mucosa do not glisten here. Lashes and inner lids are dust-tools, combing the sharp air and whisking it over conductive hairs that prickle in veils when the charge-auroras depart their far sky. Skin is not glossy but matte and microridged, a field of interference patterns that refuse to couple cleanly with sound. Many lacquer themselves besides, a thin, renewable varnish that smells a little like nut shells and pepper when freshened and keeps the grit out of joints and pores.

They hear with their ears and also with bones and bays inside their heads, with soft-walled sacks along the neck that tighten or flower open like a throat-lily to admit or deny pressure. A finger laid along the jaw can read whispers the tympanum cannot. Their voices have at least two levels—the ordinary speak carried in clicks and slurred syllables that taste like vowel and light, and the companion hum that most of them aren't aware of halfway through a sentence, a tiny correcting drone from the laryngeal sac that kneads the air flat around phrasing so their words don't stray into anyone's patent. When they sing, the drone steps out from under them and becomes another person under the same skin. There are singers who can carry three or four stable notes at once. They are precious and watched like engineers are watched, because a chorus here is not only art. A chorus can make a house fall or make it live.

Names in Port Refrain are both sayable and playable. There's what you tell a stranger to fit in their mouth, and the small chord you carry that belongs to you and your mother or your mentor, the one you hum against another's throat so their bones can know you years after eyes fail. Addresses are small pieces of score. Bureaucrats have drawers of mesh that, when shaken by wind, will hum out the list of permits valid on a given terrace. Lawyers are mostly not lawyers but listeners and cartographers of what notes have already been claimed and by whom and whether your new drain-slit makes a forbidden word in the air.

Clothing is kin to the city's beams: hex-shingled and dust-fast on the outside, soft on the skin. A good coat has more dampers than seams. Its collar can stiffen into a hush frame if you must cross a raw zone where the vent's phrase is too pure. Belts hold capsules of solvent, packets of charge-salt, small pats of catalyst paste wrapped in thorned leaf to keep them honest. Jewelry tends toward function. People wear the equivalent of tuning slugs threaded through skin over cartilage ridges to slightly reweight a tone, bracelets that can vent a thin answer-note if a predator looks too long, tiny conductive whiskers at the ears that flash briefly when lightning comes near so you know to crouch and open your little sac doors in your neck and throat and belly to equalize before the world speaks louder than you can bear.

They live in a city that was less raised than asked to grow. Port Refrain's ribs are the plant's ribs, splayed where the Choir Mouth leans into the thin of the sky. House walls are membranes stretched across organ-arches, tuned to drown the worst of the day’s infrasonic pressure into a soft, heatless peace. Privacy is an arrangement with wind. Neighborhoods hang their silence nets not only to stop the land's big note but to keep from touching one another unintentionally in that place where song becomes food. You can feel a cheap net when you step through it: your teeth itch, your ring bones quiver; a dear net is like cool lather, and you do not even know you crossed until you breathe deeper without meaning to.

They have trades that would seem like jokes elsewhere and here are survival. Hushwrights array phononic crystals in patterns only they can read, corkscrew-lattices set on hairline gimbals that lean into the wind on their own and take it apart. Chordwrights hold patents on numbers of bars and arcs, sell the right to build a bridge that eats but does not feed on the city's shared baseline. Organchitects sit cross-legged on ribs and hum proposals into the bone until the thallus under everything shifts its flux and admits, gently, that it agrees. Mass-leaners tend the field-anchors that make cargo light by turning weight sidewise; their art is mathematics and humility, because if you are wrong, the rail will bite and crack everything on it and no wind will take your apologies. Solvent-keepers keep the city's small oceans of lifeliquid in the dark grilles below walkways, moving the fragrance of cold pepper and clean knives from one vault to another as need and festival require.

Food is crunch and dissolve. There are cakes of catalytic grit bound with mineral protein shaved careful from the substrate's sacrificial rims where it offers itself back; there are slabs of polymer turned chewable by slow chemistry conducted in thumb-high bowl towers in the lee of windmouths. There are the pods that the thallus bleeds into its own pipes; the city gets a tithe of these not as fruit but as allowances of taste. Traders take sacks of dry crystal seed from old mounds when the thunder-crack is spent and grind them slowly to use as spice. On festival nights the air is sweet and charred with bowls of catalyst pastes frying up along terrace alleys in pans that look like pate-molds and behave like mild bombs.

Children are as you would think and not. They run, they have secret places under the low membranes of public rooms where the silence is like cotton and they can hear their own heart without the city's pressure on it. They learn to count in time, in the way the land's hum changes when the sky's thin heat crawls from one side of the horizon to the other, in the way a net catches a tone like fish. Step-class begins as soon as their bones can bear it: the unmatching step, the safe stride, the emergency freeze that throws your body's whole note out of itself for the half-breath it takes a hunting scream to pass. They play games of tag with shadows the nets cast through dust when the aurora-sheets admit they are aurora and drizzle charge like lace. Coming-of-age is a night spent two tiers above the Choir Mouth's big lip, in the angle where the city's hush breaks and the world says its underphrase in parable under your spine. If you come back unjoined—if your dreams and your breathing hold out—you get your first personal skiff.

Travel is all slide and hum. There are rails all over the substrate, shallow gullies opened with soft persuasion and foam-lined, and the skiffs are like a thinking seed's idea of a boat: bulbous and matte, stretched belly-first over three or five rails. You hum them loose. You lay a note along their bow with your throat sac and they lift a half-fist width and go skimming, because here the world's tendons are strong enough that, if you ask them right, they will flex and carry you like a swallow on a pressure line. There is etiquette on the rails: who hums first, who gives way, which vent's harbor you can bring your voice to. Pilots have tongues that can touch a tone and know whether another tongue was on it an hour ago. No one whistles the city's notes for fun.

They are not alone, of course. The plant that underlies and overlives them is a presence in the neighborhood more than an object. It has moods. It has patience like weather. It is not a person in any way that makes good courtroom sense, and you would still not insult it out loud. It listens by architecture and reply by growth. People bring it proposals in long cloths printed with score—in lace-maps of hush and hum—and tuck them under resin lips at the bases of buttresses the way some folk still put letters under saints. Sometimes the plant gives them the exact thing they asked for a winter later: a crinkle of terrace where there was only flat, a gentle deepening of a gully into a rail-line that all their small skiffs will find, a new organ-veil between two ribs that used to pummel each other on days when the wind came from the west. Sometimes it gives them a tower where no permission was filed, because somewhere a child sang in a throat-sac playing at a grown-up hum and a whole acre of patient plant heard that pure, silly shape and thought: yes. They keep those towers. They are called found-thoughts. They are never cut, only trimmed so they do not shear, and are wrapped in nets thin enough to show off their ribs and thick enough to keep them from waking on certain nights and taking the city with them.

Fauna keep distance that is measured in tone as much as space. The big gliders are the punctuation marks of the plateau, anodized frames under skins so thin you can see the solvent slow in their capillary-lattices if the light is angry enough to come through dust. They ride in sentences made by the chorus of vents and rarely waste a verb on panic. Shepherds do not herd them so much as accompany. A shepherd's tool is a clamp of hardened mesh with a corner of wind in it. Snap it open to hum a home-note in miniature, and the gliders will take it under one wing and let you walk their path while you mind their shadows for hawks of sound. Smaller grazers are bristled cylinders on stilt-legs, all rotary lips and insistent thrift. Their hazard is not teeth but resonance. They can take the safe hum of a porch into themselves and wear it like a dress, throw it back white with overtones that make a loop of the floor and take the oldest chair to its knees. No one hosts them inside nets. Road-keepers put out rime for them two times a season in long drifts like wedding rice so they will keep to the alleys meant for them and not test anybody's staircases.

Predators show themselves by negation more often than by form. The screamers are a vertebrate parabola where you least expect a curve. They flash in and pulse a pencil-headache sound that cuts and leaves you blinking and wondering for a fraction if you want to sit down and let the dust cover you, and then they break like splitting resin over a runner the color of local stone and there is a crack and a stink and a small softening where a life was. They hunt the edges of the nets, where the city's hush and the world's song comb for one another like hair. Their bones are used as whistles in villages outside the main vent-rings. In Port Refrain you can be fined for owning one.

Serpents move under more than over. Threaded through the thallus' courtesy veins, they dimple the city like polite thieves and carry messages in their stomachs the way people once did with pigeons, except that here the letters are not paper but clamps with knots of note printed in them—choreographies that a receiver can play into a pole and have the pole hum the phrase of a neighbor two terraces away. They will put their smooth faces in your hand if you have solvent and do not shake too much when lightning is near. Those who love them say they are the only truly gentle thing the land has grown that is not rooted.

Law here is as much acoustics as ethic. There are civil note-ranges you cannot sing in public no matter how good your throat, unless you have a licensed reason and safety cordons. There are chords you can own and chords no one may own because the plant took them first and holds them in its underhalls as a right. There are wealthy families whose greatest treasure is not mass—mass is expensive to move and rude to ask the city to hold—but a hush: deed to a patch of stillness under their terrace, passed down like a vineyard in a wetter world, parceled in marriages and leases, litigated hard when neighbors put up an alcove that flicks an overtone no one planned. Protests happen in quiet. Revolutions begin as legal hums that make a wall breathe wrong until it cannot stand and must be remade.

Strangers come rarely, and when they do they come bright and wet and jingling with seals. The people of the high plain call them Wets and set nets up as fences for them same as you would for toddlers and for any animal with thin sense for these specifics. Their suits sigh and bead, their breath fogs on the first glass and the last. They look like grief to a child in Port Refrain, like a thing out of time, always drinking and always leaking, always apologizing with towels. They buy hush, sometimes, and take it away as patterned arrays they can stack on orbital habitats to make their own thin air more kind. They leave smelling like rain and are buried whole if they die, suited, sealed, put under grit where their particular decomposition—wet, hungry, wrong for this place—cannot teach the plant anything but alarm.

Death for locals is gritwork and song. Bodies are taken to the bowl-petals that the thallus offers as refineries and are not burned—fire is a sulky god here and forgets itself at inopportune times—but persuaded apart. Catalysts reduce polymer to its building virtues, solvents are taken up by the plant and home again, bones go to hushweavers and organwrights and the occasional child with a permit for a flute. There is a long poem read in three chords at the softening, and children hum along from the corridor because in a hush garden everyone can.

They keep festivals to the storm that rasps instead of rains. There is the Night of Crackle, when charge veils come low and lightning softens like cotton until a lifted rod coaxes it close and then—detonation not of fire but of sound, big enough to sit on your kidneys. People cook in the afterward, on the momentary warm of grit, while the plant under everything opens gill and pylon to swallow the fresh radicals like sweetmeat. Lovers walk the rail-lines with their skiffs singing under their hands and give gifts that are pieces of silence rolled into mesh and tied with hair.

What lives here is not only built for the place. It is shaped by it, has chosen it, chooses it again. The old substrate lives in seasons and charges and inches its thoughts around in mounds and bowls, a slow scholar who reads the sky's bleak marginalia and answers not in words but in ribs and valves and gill-rooms. The gliders write their big parentheses across horizon. The screamers make exclamation points nobody asked for and so have their own grudging place in grammar. The people write their walking in slights and gracings of force, in the choice not to match, in the caress that makes an instrument and a city out of something that could easily have been only a floor.

They wake in the morning under a small, tired sun and stretch and listen to what key the land has taken this hour. They decide what to cook, what to carry, what not to say. They go about the work of asking politely of a patient mind and being answered in structures you can stand under. They go out in storms with masks tight and rods high. They hum at their boats and skim along the long gullies, lightened not by magic but by nuance. They sleep under quilts of hush and dream in chords and dust. If you ask them what they are, most will say a third thing—neither plant nor wind, neither instrument nor song, but something grown from the two: a listener that feeds on movement the way a leaf feeds on light, a tenant that knows which frequencies of gratitude hurt and which ones help.
