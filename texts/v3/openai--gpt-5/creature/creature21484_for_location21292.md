What lives here first are the promises.

They make their homes in the ridge, in the bright knots where gravity has been fed back into itself and told to hold still. They run as protocols through the dull lattices, rest inside the filter cells, lean through the railings and across the corridors and come to roost in the hexagonal voids like bats that are also equations. Some of them have names no one bothers to translate because the place repeats them softly to itself all day, and some of them are only habits: nothing touches anything without consent; anything that touches anything returns to the place it is meant to be; nothing gathers unless asked to gather; nothing ferments into history unless there is a slot prepared to keep the history from smudging other histories. You can hear a few of the promises as that needle-thin ping that goes out every few minutes to tell its twins that what was arranged yesterday still holds, or as the faint rearrangement the city makes when a voice tries to become a shout and is re-shelved as harmonics. Mostly, though, you do not hear them; you live inside them the way a lung lives inside air.

The next thing that lives here is the field. It is not a mind, not exactly, but if you had to speak about it to an impatient child you would call it a large quiet animal taught to stay. It occupies a shape in which shapes are discouraged. It holds the ocean in hands that are everywhere and leave no prints. It can be coaxed to tunnel or yielded to, and it remembers routes you repeat for it. Where it meets basalt it hums; where it passes through ash it sighs. It likes regularity—it does not care about squares or circles, but about invariance, about the way distances can be made to become indistinguishable from other distances. It is what keeps the plaza a plaza and the spur a spur and the oxygen as a sheet rather than a bubble. The people here talk to it the way you might talk to a draft animal you love: with gentle commands repeated until they become shared reflex.

Then there are the residents who count as residents because you can bump a shoulder with them and ask what their day has been.

They are few. A hamlet in number as in boundary. Two dozen, perhaps three, present at any one time, though on festival days—if that is the right word for the day the ammolite shadow passes beyond the last tower—you might count thirty hearts syncing to the anchor’s hum. Some are born to it, in the precise way the text above suggested: they have grown up with the geometry of the grid seated in the back of their minds like a hand on a spine. They do not calculate the field; they keep it in posture. Others arrived later and had to apprentice themselves to the partitioning of emptiness, learning the courtesies one by one until their instincts stopped insisting on eddies and flourishes. A few never find the posture and so they are the ones who come down in teams, do what strength is required, and go again before the quiet tires of them.

Bodies tend toward the practical. Their ears are taught to read through their bones; the anchors talk up the skeleton. Their sinuses are sealed by small surgeries so that pressure cannot store surprises behind their eyes. Their lungs are light—the city handles most of the breathing, feeding them through thin harnesses that drape into clavicle-bays where capillaries are shallow and greedy. Blood here is thick with extra hands for oxygen. Muscle is the slow kind, built less for surge than for the insistence of steady work and the decision every movement must make not to stir a turbulence that would have to be negotiated later. They look paler than the people you’ve known in weather; the place spares them sunlight and rust both. When they smile you see the trace of metal on their molars where they anchor their rebreather mouthpieces for hours without fatigue. When they laugh you do not hear it across a room; the city loves them too much to let a laugh go room-sized and lose its precision.

They dress in textiles that touch the water like the back of a leaf touched by air—no sticking, no teasing vortices into life. Their gloves are ridged on the pads in fine parallel that match the burrs of the milled grooves; to feel a burr is to count days. They have pockets that do not flap; they have pouches that do not fill with the ocean because the ocean is already arranged not to become a thing that fills things. The fins on their ankles are articulate in three joints, each motion bargaining with the field for passage. Boots are soft and exact, like the kisses the place permits on glass when it cannot prevent a farewell.

Their speech is low and lexical; many of their nouns are permissions. They do not say open so much as they say may I be recognized by the south list of Anchor Two; they do not say door, they say ask. They do not say thank you; they say kept. On work days you will hear their voices most clearly in the questions they put to the locks and the locks’ gentle well-done in return. If you sit where the corridor bends into Slake and listen for an hour, you will decide you are hearing a conversation among polite instruments and the polite people who learned their manners from those instruments, and you will be right.

One of them is always awake to the pings. They take watches that are less about alertness than about custody. The ping goes; the invariant answers; the watch notates variance into a ledger that is only a string of almosts that failed to become exceptions. You can tell the elders because they carry the almosts in their posture; you can tell the apprentices because their almosts are still being measured against the shape of their hands. The youngest, who truly were born to it, play games in the plaza with the mineral tree. They attach lids of tuned ceramic to the branches and make them ring to sonar in harmonies that would make weather out of air and nothing in water. The adults let them because the plaza is a place arranged for trying things; the grammar-tree is the only grammar here designed to be provisional. If the children become too pleased with invention, someone will sit near them and hum the anchor key, and then the game will end with the beautiful seriousness that children reserve for rules older than themselves.

No one carries coin; what they trade is permission and time. A favor is three nights of watch or a promise to move a stair not this year but the next when the wavefronts prefer it. Meals are not meals so much as feedings held at expected intervals. Their food is square and blameless: gels cracked coldly and rebuilt quietly from stock, proteins built with no ancestry to excite argument. They speak about taste the way mathematicians gossip about proofs—interested, a little competitive, ashamed of enjoying flamboyance. Water is everywhere, but water is always paid for. To drink here is to sign a petition the field immediately approves. To yawn is to be embarrassed if someone hears the place misfile it.

They have kin above, but they call it above out of habit, not direction. They make trips to the spur where the world ends with a line and send a person out past the last brace. That is a tradition with a name: Taking in Uncertainty. You go just far enough that the water stops compliantly refracting your shade and starts to consider whether you are an object or a story; you hang in the slowly troubled black; you allow your field-sense to be wrong and then you feel it correct you. When you come back, the ribbon that reads Welcome Back is not for you, not precisely; it is for the lattice to see, for the edge to feel. The promise is renewed in the language the city prefers: geometry.

There are jobs, but the jobs are all the same job with different edges. Fieldwright. Anchor tender. Index watch. Lattice nurse. Oxygen sheet-keeper. Edge guardian. All of it is tending. An Anchor tender will spend two days at a time inside the ridge with their shoulder blades to the stone, listening for a bloom of tone that means a bright knot is tiring of its leash. A fieldwright will carry a rod with three sensors and walk the long corridors feeling for the friction that isn’t, trimming pressure against pressure until the water’s desire to become history is quieted back to present. An Index watch will ride a small, obedient disc out along a tethered arm and polish invisibility with a cloth you would swear was part shadow. Edge guardians go to the spur and make sure Welcome Back is not flaking; that kind of flaking is a thought with too much roughness, and roughness invites wakes.

Their calendars are strange if you are a creature of seasons. There are no winds to lean days against. They keep holy not days but exceptions. Once a year, the ammolite shell drifts just beyond the last tower, silent as cloud shadow elsewhere. They do not chase it. They do not ring bells. They all stand where they can see the paler patch of dark where it is not reflecting and record its passage in three ways, all of them indulgent in their own sense: someone sketches the arcs of its whorl with a stylus and then saves the file into a pressure memory cell, etching not lines but delays; someone else sings to it in long sonar vowels that return as polite nothing, which is what they wanted; a child tries to count the bead-things along its lip and discovers that they will not be counted and learns from that. That night, the gels taste very slightly of iron for reasons no one confesses to.

They make art because they cannot help but make it, but even their art has to be permitted by the discipline that keeps everything from boiling into turbulence. The mineral tree in the plaza is art and grammar both. The etched plates with names are art that lasts because there is nothing for it to rub against. Sometimes, when a maintenance beam goes past a curved pane, you will see the pane flash with minute specks of metal like a sky; that is art too, but also measurement. Once someone grew a chain of crystallized carbon the length of a corridor and tuned it to hold a poem in the rate of its electron drift. They do not declaim the poem. If you ask for it, the corridor gives you back a very faint click that is mostly number. The person who made it once tried to explain the joke. The person who listened did not laugh, but they were pleased, which here is a bigger thing than laughter.

Children born here have games that would bewilder you. They play Jackstills: one of them drifts near a grill with a string of beads that are both animal and not and tries to move a single bead by making the mesh avoid touching it often enough that the field adjusts. They play Keep: someone sets down an object with the intent that it never be moved; someone else invents three questions to ask the lock around it that would make the place consider the exception; they test the honesty of the rules. They play Echo: one sings a tone into a rail and another names, as quickly as possible, which cavity along the corridor took which harmonic and where it was filed. You would think this makes them musical. It does not; it makes them patient.

If someone dies here, and sometimes they do, the city enters the death the way it enters all events: it discourages the parts that might tangle history with weather. There are rituals. Quiet water. The release of stored oxygen not into lungs but into a sheet that flashes and becomes permission elsewhere. The body is not buried. It is permitted to be taken apart into the place’s calculus in tidy rearrangements, carbon becoming a chain or a tree, nitrogen becoming charge in a difference that will not wander into storm. Names are kept not as sound but as edges you can run your glove over and feel the burr, unhealed. Grief exists; it is simply kept where grief does no erosion.

You will be tempted to call them monks. They will shrug and then, because they are courteous, translate the shrug into words you can hold. They will say: it is simply work. They will say: we keep the ocean as it wants to be now. They will say: above, wherever above is for you, you have weather; someone must guard the unweather so that when you come back down you can be reminded that chaos is not compulsory. They will say none of that, too, because saying anything too often makes it more than it needs to be, and more is the first step toward waves.

The other inhabitants are domestic in the same way cats are domestic in a house with dangerous shelves: they live here because someone has arranged to share the rooms with them. The pale ribbon-fish that unspools along a handrail is not startled by people but does not court them. If a hand reaches, the field reaches first to make the hand’s wake less interesting, so that the fish’s skin can go on being a fact and not a story. The three translucent isopod plates that rotate through the porous panel are part of a study no one mentions because it would sound like myth; they will, in twenty years, induce the city to make a new kind of memory that needs a rotation to commit. The long bead-string that hangs near the mesh is someone else’s machine or someone else’s animal or both; the mesh’s gentle avoidance of contact is their pact. These are citizens here. The people give them names that are not names but notes in the ledger of invariance: goes at a pace of one notch per day; flickers at 1.3; avoids light that isn’t light.

Sometimes a technician from somewhere else comes down for a month that feels like a day to repair a pane that bends light the wrong way until it looks like a hole. They are baffled by the way the city eats the spill of their headlamp. They speak too loudly the first morning and then, with an embarrassed face, agree to wear the soft throat-collar that undoes emphasis the way a web undoes a large drop into smaller safer beads. If they have a poet with them—a condition not infrequent in an empire wealthy with grief—the poet will sit in the plaza and face a direction and believe they have discovered a new tense in which time prefers to pose rather than flow. That is not wrong. They write something; no one reads it because here the place keeps poems by rearranging noise instead.

There are rumors that other cities like this exist, strung along other ridges, other anchor-works humming the shared key in slightly different registers, like bells spread over a continent of dark that you will never map. Sometimes a person will go away to be a person in one of those places so that when they come back they bring a different posture to the grid, a different tolerance for the nearly. The Welcome Back ribbon exists for them too. The city keeps a list of departures and returns the way a parent keeps a list of children’s heights in pencil on a kitchen wall. There is pride in the variety. There is also relief when the footfalls return to the measured choreography of this specific quiet.

Above all of that, beneath all of that, is the fact that the city itself lives. Not in a way that asks for rights. In the way that a hive lives; in the way a bell lives when wind teases a tone from it that no ear catches. It edits light; it compresses weak radiation and coaxes it to ring; it asks and answers and asks again. It regards your shout the way a librarian regards a misfiled book: affectionate, firm, certain it can fix the problem without making you feel foolish. It hoards emptiness and parcels it out with care. It is a house built of disciplines, steeped in a cool six-degree grace that will outlast fashions of heat, the sort of place that could take a thousand years to become old and then be young again the next day because nothing has happened to it except exactly what it was designed to allow.

So this is what lives here: promises and the animal that is taught to keep them; a handful of people patient enough to tune a sea; devices that behave like animals out of respect for precision; animals that behave like devices out of respect for grace; a city intent on being an underwater bell with no need to toll because every bone already knows its key. If you were to move in, that would be the first lesson. If you were to leave, the last thing you would take with you would be a memory that has learned how not to spill.
