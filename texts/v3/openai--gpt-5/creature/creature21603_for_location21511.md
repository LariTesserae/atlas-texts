They are not citizens so much as a tense, perpetual togetherness. To call them trees, vines, mosses, algae, fungi is to list organs of a single mood. The inhabitants are agreements that learned how to root in pressure, sugar, and stone and then chose never to be anything else.

The Choirers are the first to be seen—the buttress-rooted columns that have taken the cliff’s voice as scripture. Their roots are not blind. They are stiffened tympana, ciliated with hairs that taste amplitude the way a tongue tastes salt. They find the filaments of lesser trembling and invade those, laying down struts where the hammering cannot snap them, arcing out where a node gives them give. What you read as flying buttresses are their fingers, triangulated into the wall by a lifetime of listening. They do not put weight where the note would punish it. Their boles rise and lean and flare in obedience to periods measured in heartbeats of stone.

They hold terraces the way lungs hold air: elastic, always a little fuller than comfort. Their crowns do not reach for sky so much as for the long horizontals where light, mist, and tone are in right relation. On each limb, leaves as big as doors lay themselves flat to become floors, then stitch at the margins so they can carry a film of water like glass. They are flattened harp strings set to a silence narrow enough to cross. On the underside of these leaves, hairs thicken into velvet. They are not for warmth. They are for sound, absorbing just enough of a troublesome overtone to make a corridor bearable. You walk there because they have made the sound step aside.

Bridgewrights grow out from the Choirers and learn austerity. Each liana is rope and avenue, braided from dozens of smaller cords, tense as drawn bow. They pick their spans the way a spider picks hers, except the geometry is acoustic. They never cross where the roar has a jag; they never set foot in a place that will rise into a killing overtone at noon. From a distance they look generous, a hundred lanes flung out across vacancy. Come close and you see redundance built into every throw: if one line is cut, three take its loads on the same quiet filament. There is no collapse here, only deflection.

The Cup-Bearers pave the avenues and also make them. Bromeliads, urn-ferns, wide-throated orchids with their lips turned toward the thunder—every cup is a hot cistern, clear to the bottom and so still under the roar you feel you are looking at the reverse of sound. They are not decorations; they are public works. Each catches mineral and stray sugars, each prepares a broth, and from that broth the city crochets new edge. A thousand cups spilling a little all the time is what fattens the honeyed ledges. The Cup-Bearers are also mills. Their throats are rimed in hairs that comb the airborne pollen dusted loose by percussion; their stamps of bloom are calendars keyed to the cliff’s daily overtures. At the moment the long vowel of the main fall drops into a certain cavity, a dozen flowers open their gills and exhale powder. Two minutes later the same chord returns and a thousand more answer. It is less sex than metronomy. Fertility is a rhythm kept.

Epiphytes are the tile on every surface, but they are not freeloaders. Mosses here have learned the order of the day the way children learn the order of bells. They lean and then stiffen as the note passes, they wick and then close their pores in unison; across a terrace you can watch a slight darkening roll like a shutter as they all drink at once. They and the slick emerald algae under them are the city’s larders. Sugars sponge into their bodies and then out of them into the root-ropes that cross under your feet, into the many-into-one phloem that keeps every district in budget. They green the steps and make the steps grip. Each patch is a hand extended to catch a falling piece of city and return it.

The Licheners and Lithics are not seen unless you kneel. They are the ink in the rock, the endolithic threads and films that colonize each seep and oxidize what the stone gives. In places they set down lamina after lime-green lamina until the wall itself becomes a page of their writing, bulging into rim and bowl. They are slow, but they are architects. You can tell their neighborhoods by the scalloped lips of pools, by a sheen like breath on glass where their exudates ease water into sheets. The very rules of dripstone are theirs to quicken or delay. Sometimes they thicken a step faster than it can be gnawed; sometimes they lay a sticky silence over a rill so it runs sideways for the length of a room before it remembers how to fall. They do not know sound, but they co-author its chambers.

Fungi are the Quiet-Keepers. They do not build bridges; they pad the footsteps. Their hyphae web the undersides of leaves, seam the corduroy floors of root, pack the galleries of shade into pillow and felt. They are acousticians by accident. Chitin tents and velvet fans swallow hiss, flatten slap, and damp the tiny rattles that would complicate the city’s chord. In the hush-pockets—those illegal wells where the thunder refuses to enter—the fungi flare into lacquer and soft horn, glazing walls into baffles. Spores release not by the wind but by busking: they wait for the one tremble that shakes them loose down corridors built to favor that tremble. Then, a sigh of dust and a colonization of the next shaded rib.

What swims are not animals but arrangements of light and glass. Diatoms write tiny cathedrals in silica and tile every cup with stained panes. They are librarians of photons, shifting the direction of glare into meals for moss, moving the harshness of noon into a diffuse gold that penetrates to the lower galleries. Filaments of green flagellates ornament the quieter cisterns. They pulse, but at a scale that does not count against the place’s vow not to thrash. In the caves, where the heat is an eggy whisper and the water goes still as waiting, there are glassy skins you can peel back with your gaze—microbial varnishes that catch the tannins the city releases and arrange them into umbers. They are patience with a taste for iron.

There are Flowers, yes, but calling them that misses their temperament. They are valves, their petals elastic diaphragms that beat in and out with pressure. The lip is seamed not with pigment so much as with resin and grit. In the hours when the chord rises and the convection lifts, they glisten with a frost of caught pollen, not raided from a bee’s legs but plucked from the air like fog from a net. Their nectar is thin. It is a bribe to no one. It is more likely an antifreeze, a solvent, a way to keep the lip from clogging with its own success. When it is time to close, they do it the way the city does everything else: all at once, in a measure you could set a watch to.

Fruit are a soft technology. Where you would dream of wings, these have sails made for water and windless air. They droop for months like bladders, filling with gases warmed until their skins push taut. Then a note arrives that has arrived every day since their species learned to count. At that crest they let go. Each one rides a skin of water no thicker than an eyelash across the face of a cliff. They tack by nothing you can see, trimming themselves along gradients of heat and humidity that memory has taught them. Some lodge on a mossy bank and sink their little hooks; others skate onward into mist and become part of the long silt of the unseen floor below. The city does not grieve them. That is not how it loves what belongs to it.

Everywhere, under all of this, are the Arboreals’ wires—the shared vascular ropes that make the metropolis a single blood. Sugar and mineral and heat move along them in pulses that draw perfect loops if you could see them plotted across decades. There is no hunger here, only reprioritization. A limb that is failing returns its stored sugar to the pool; elsewhere, a young bridge that has found an honest quiet thickens overnight. When a column rotates a degree to meet a migrated coolness, it does not do so alone. Whole arcs of greenery drop a fraction, whole lanes lighten, as circulation reroutes. It is as unceremonious and as absolute as breath.

Sensation is not confined to leaves. Root-tips taste tin and sulphur and silica and use those tastes to decide where to put city next. Hair cells along their flanks send calcium waves like murmur through a crowd at the arrival of a new overtone. In some districts you can find rows of hollow stalks like halted reeds, their insides slick and lined. Their job is to sing back. They catch a piece of the amphitheater’s cry and return it, faint and pinned to a different place, creating a path where brute volume would otherwise batter sap apart. The architecture is a negotiation conducted in feedback, the live matter retuning the conditions that tune it.

What does reproduction mean when you have chosen to be a budget more than a body? It means budding neighborhoods. It means a district of white tufa palaces, once modest, recruiting veils of moss and then vines and then a young Choirer raising up a rib stronger than expected because the note there is kind. It means small rebellions too—an outgrowth that tries a new quiet and keeps it, a cord that grows slack and is allowed to rot into velvet. There are schedules for falling. The Bridgewrights do not snap for drama. They soften their cores over months, signal their intention along phloem, and when they do finally drop, the drop is caught. Down below, what would be called litter anywhere else is a bank that oozes itself back into someone else’s span.

In the caves, life changes its diet. Where light does not reach, heat and chemistry stand in. The stone damp breathes carbon and sulfur, and organisms with no need for sun build paper-thin terraces out of calcium and patience. They are pale, but not sick. Their walls gleam with films that would be poison if the space were smaller. The wells of hush—illicit rooms carved by some happenstance of curve and angle where the pressure will not go—are cradles. Shoots germinate there in humorless little parliaments, straight-backed and undecorated, until they are strong enough to endure percussion. The city skirts those wells with deference. It is one of the few superstitions it keeps.

There are absences that live here as actively as presences. There are no eyes that flick at the edge of leaves, no clicking of feet, no warm bodies making their own drafts. The closest thing to a face is the sudden surprise of a petal thrown wide. It is not loneliness. The city has concentrated society into conduction and whisper, into the give and take of sugar and slurry, into a solution of selves in a solvent of sound. If some strand of you insists on citizens, then they are these—tones breeding terraces, terraces grooming tones, Cup-Bearers and Bridgewrights and Quiet-Keepers and Choirers, and all of them, at scale, a single inhabitant stretched over stone.

There are occasional retunings. A ledge fails—not with a scream, but with a hush, as if a hand were put over a mouth. The chord shifts down a comma. You can feel a recalibration go through the boles like a shiver. Buds close. Others open. A set of steps thicken their lips to take over circulation from a rill that has wandered away. In a month no evidence remains but the memory in sap flow, a slightly different loop, a rosette with one petal bent.

If you stay long enough to acquire the local habit of counting distance in pitch instead of span, you understand that what lives here is not so much many somethings as one habit expressed endlessly. It is a patient appetite for equilibrium. It is growth that agrees to be revised by air. It is rock tutoring root and root tutoring rock until each is legible in the other’s language. It is an organism that found a good chord and, because it was good, kept it, because keeping it was easier than inventing a new sky.

Who lives here? The resident is a chorus laid over stone, a web of green and glass and lichen that has agreed with physics on the shape of forever. It speaks in pressure. It eats light and drips. It uses heat to keep its pages wet. It writes steps and bridges into the cliff face and reads them back each morning at the same line break. You could say it has perfected itself. More exactly: it has perfected its listening, and from that, everything else.
