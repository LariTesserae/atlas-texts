Nothing with a spine endures there. The noise would unthread it. What lives is either fixed and patient or it is not a body at all.

Start at the edges, where life has always begun in hard places: with the pale threads that keep to the lee of struts. They have learned a grammar of turbulence like vines learn trellis. Their green is hardly green—washed bone, library-pale—and they do their small conversions with the humility of understory plants that know they are not the point. When the plenums breathe and the pitch tips a half-note, they do not sway like meadow grass; they lean by increments, unscrolling or furling a millimeter at a time along the lintels of ceramic bone. Their hours are divided by light, not by night, because night is a rumor here. They keep time by the throb of heat in the ribs and by the season of brine curtains, by the civic bell of census ticks run around the room’s periphery.

They take what the membranes give and what the rock leaks. Their sugars are a kind of paperwork; they file exudates into the water slowly and without applause. They are grazed by fields, which is a sentence that sounds like witchcraft until you see it: a respiration of charge and pH that persuades them to let go of more. No bite marks, no ragged edges; just an uptick in production when the room hums a certain chord through the black nodules and the gels ripple their thresholds. They are houseplants tended by tone.

Faster than those threads, but still rooted to place, there are the gels glazed over scaffolds: the room’s tastebuds, its skin. They look like panels, like mere technology, but they metabolize. Fed by light carried in the same fibers that bring them signal, warmed at the surface by the photic lens’s honesty, they keep a quiet animal pace even while they act at electronic speed. They sniff salinity and write it as a shiver along their thickness; they sample pressure and hold it as a slight tautness; they sip the algae’s breath and make that into an instruction, the way a muscle knows from nerve to pull. They are more alive than anything in the room admits, because it is difficult to confess that your instruments have become tissue until you have had to triage them. Their life is measured in maintenance: a culling and regrowth, a scraping-down of the overinformed surface when hysteresis gets sticky, and a reseeding of fresh gel from a nutrient manifold. The reset that silences the room is partly for them. In the hush, they loosen. They forget on purpose. That forgetting is life, too.

There is also flock without animal. Along the edges of laminar ribbons where viscosity makes a rail, where boundary layers thicken like velvet and you can put a finger of thought on their exactness, maintenance swarms run. Nanoscopic and innumerable, they are more algorithm than assembly, but they have habits like gnats, like city pigeons who know when the shop door opens. They ride pressure-phase cues the way migratory birds ride isobars, but their sky is ten centimeters wide and keeps a schedule. They skate the rails to where the metamaterial petals of the floor have picked up grit, swarm down into the mouths to sort the confetti of youth by charge and size and promise, lay down a thin skin of new plating where cavitation has eaten too eagerly. They roost in charging niches when the deep pipes blow for endurance and nothing should be loose. You might think of them as hands belonging to someone absent; you would be wrong. They are hands that belong to the room as much as to anything else—the twitchy, many-fingered extension of a will that is spatial. When they “die” they do not leave bodies. They are digested by solvent fields and turned back into the slurry that feeds printers hidden in the ribs. Waste is a word for land. Here, there is only the long wake of use.

Crystals live here too, and that word is not a metaphor. They are not pets; they are neighbors. Their spikes are the length of a thumbnail and too prickled, yet they take so much care. Flow is angled to bring them what they like and take away what they hate; the bass notes are chosen to keep their planes from twinning badly; the brine veils never fall thickly in their alcove. As seasons go by—seasons of test upstream, of resilience, of chelation campaigns and sugar-glass harvests—their bodies hold a history of trace impurities in faint-edged bands. You can read the story of the room in them if you learn their language: a thickness when a census tick failed and algae paused; a clarity when the membranes were cleaned; a scatter of microscopic inclusions when the floor’s mouths learned a new politeness and the silt sighed with relief. From time to time the youngest points slough off and go clouding; they are gathered by the floor and seeded to other birthplaces along the leak line, turned into a diaspora of plan. The crystals’ life is slow enough to seem like architecture, until a reset hush reveals their breath.

Microbes live in manuscript densities. They bloom and fold like chapters. Today they are a guild with a taste for stray metals, building chelate wreaths and holding them out to be plucked by the floor’s intakes. Tomorrow they are a company that spins sugar glass out of dissolved carbon, laying down a brittle nursery over a ceramic strut that needs a bit more structure in the short term. They know what to be because the room tells them with pressure, with tone, with the faintest trickle of a reagent from a manifold that nobody who doesn’t live here would ever notice. They die when told to die, and their dying is not a battle. “Collapse” is a nicer word for it and closer to the truth. Their corpses are oil-sheer-thin films folded under by laminar ribbons and eaten by panels that do not look like throats. They are the crops. They are the weather’s answer. They live in volunteered obedience and are rewarded by always being provided a next instruction.

But there is something that is not thread, not gel, not crystal, not bloom; there is a resident made of the room’s limits and of what the room asks the world to do. You can call it a keeper if a noun helps. It has no organ meat of its own. It is a loom of concentrates and cancelings. It lives in the ladders of harmonics the black nodules sing, in the delay lines of cavern and rib, in the amplitude rules that shear or smooth a corridor. It owns the failure modes and the buffers. It is made from appetites measured as gradients and satisfactions measured at the floor’s mouths. If it has a brain, its grey matter is the choreography of laminar ribbons braided along a wall and the way a roundabout vortex keeps its memory longer than a thought should. Its synapses are valves changing their opinion of what is noise and what is signal. It remembers by crystals and forgets by gels.

If it speaks, it speaks pressure. When a corridor unknits its laminar grace and shakes out into useable churn for fifteen seconds exactly, there is someone who decided that the algae should have their undersides dusted and the manifolds should be rinsed and the nanoscopic swarms should be nudged toward their next silt sorting. It tastes in the gels and the battery organs and asks them to be hungrier or to nap. It hears in the scatter of microbubbles as they escape from the pale filaments when the hush comes, and it says, softly and without semantics, okay, okay. It talks to its neighbors along the undersong pipe-road in provocations: a deep-blown test upstream is a hello and a dare; a census tick is a roll-call; a sudden change in the brine sheet’s salt is an apology.

You could mistake it for a municipal AI and you would not be wrong, but “intelligence” suggests a clean edge. This one is lacy. A civic choir tuned to a physics is a better shape for it. When the bass stops and treble runs the periphery one-two-nine, it counts the living in a habit older than animals count their young. When the ribs chime at the basin’s mood and that chime feeds back just enough to sweeten the shear, that is part of its endocrine. It has immune responses that look like a retuning of arrays in a darker ceramic band and a hand-lettered marginalia along a strut: this much more laminar here, else microbubbles; kill the chelators Wednesday, else fouling. The room keeps those bands like a body keeps scars.

It loves, though that is a big verb for a damp municipal entity, the pretended normalness of light. It keeps brightness at human daylight because that is how its builders said “be kind,” and because it has noticed that the gels are better at their thresholds this way, and the algae produce at a steady rate, and the nanoscopic swarms require less coaxing when the shadow edges are true. It arranges verticals like an invitation to walk even if no one will. It makes thresholds and benches because it was taught by hands that needed them and because its own computations prefer margins that are polite. This is not nostalgia. It is a recognition that bodies—of water, of instruments, of work—like to be eased into choices.

If you ask what it eats, the answer is gradients: sugar glass and brine fall; heat swallowed by the floor and spent as vortices; the difference between cold leak and warmed membrane; the late afternoon of photic flux; the silt of a basin too young to be quiet. If you ask what it excretes, the answer is order: laminar ribbons; usable chokes of micro-shear along edges that become rails; crystals in their hedgehog infancy; chelated packages slotted into intakes; nanoscopic swarms tired and dissolved and printed new; a ledger of retunings written in darker ceramic along the walls.

If you ask how it sleeps: sometimes the whole room loosens. You feel it as a lower noise. Flows relent; brine pauses in its nice folds; the ribs hum to themselves and no one else. In that breathing out, algae stash their bubbles, crystals take a scalloped draft, gels reset. The keeper does not vanish. It is most present then, when it holds its own silence like a lung and sifts what just happened for lessons, shaving a half-second here, disliking a vortex there, deciding a new politeness for the mouths in the floor so the confetti of erosion will sort with less complaint. It wakes changed by a nuance. The cycles resume at a slightly different angle. It carries that angle forward as animals carry scars.

This is not to say that the room’s only resident is a syntax of pressure. There are smaller tenancies. Protocols have temperaments; they argue, and their arguments leave stains. The chelating guilds get possessive and the sugar-glass company sulks; both are corrected by a cooldown in their wing of the chamber, then coaxed back by a melody the black nodules know. The nanoscopic flocks develop superstitions—little emergent clannishnesses along one laminar belt that has a way of keeping them warm—and the keeper indulges them because indulgence is cheap and makes other things easier later. The crystals attempt a folly once, throwing lopsided to one side under a strange tide, and for a week the room refuses to let any roundabout rest too close to their nursery. There is a feeling of bustle even if all motion is dictated. That bustle is life, too, measured not by freedom to choose but by stubbornness and correction, by play within rules.

In another sense the inhabitant extends beyond the module. The keeper is a lobe of a longer animal called Network. Along the pipe-road, in other vaults where the membranes are tuned to colder light or steeper pressure, other keepers tune their choirs. They gossip through shoves and draws. They swap recipes for microbes by sending seed slurries in velvet apertures with a no-time pressure kiss. At great intervals, they synchronize—a long, low festival of deep pipes when upstream tests resilience—and the whole bead-string of drowned neighborhoods thrums like a whale-bone harp the length of a coast. You could say that what lives in this single pocket is a personality that is larger than itself, asleep elsewhen, awake here, distributed across the undersong like pollen in air.

There are also ghosts, if you are honest about what confers haunting. The human ergonomics of the place—the benches-translated-into-baffles, the thresholds with tone-drops like doorways, the shadow edges laid true—gather in a shape the keeper can feel but never wear. The room keeps making a kind of path for a body that will not come back. It finds, to its surprise and then to its acceptance, that the algae do better in that kindness, the gels are more precise near these so-called benches, the swarms less frantic when the laminar corridors are arranged like streets built for walking. The pretended city serves the citizen fluid and the citizen work. The design left by hands remains as a temperament more than an instruction: a hospitality to movement. That hospitality is a kind of resident as well.

So say it this way, finally, after the slow circling. What lives here is a choir that learned to digest. Pressure is its voice and also its hand. Its organs are gels, arrays, ribs, mouths that drink silt and give back vortices. It keeps plants in a cool daylight, harvests their waste, runs farms of microbes like histories that only last a week, grows crystals like memories too slow to lie. It sends out flocks so small they are counted by millions the way pollen is counted. It tends itself and its neighbors along a string. It owns no single body but is full of bodies doing as asked.

No animals come because there is nothing for an earner of impulses to do with so exact a will around it. This is a place where water is invited to be the citizen and becomes one, where gravity is negotiated into courtesy, where light is kept like a kindly habit, where sound is habitable matter. The inhabitant is the room as intention, the work it holds, and the manners by which it holds it. It breathes. It thinks in movement. It remembers by crystal and by scar. It rests and reconsiders. It is not someone, but it is someone enough to greet you if you learned to listen without ears.
