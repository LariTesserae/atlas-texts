They are people with a step learned from stone. Their calves are knotted ropes, their knees easy, their heels never slap. They move as if every stair were agreed upon in advance—as if their feet were promised a certain height and the stone kept its promise. Altitude has widened them at the ribs; they carry a slow, deep breath as if it were a tool. Their hands are dry and clean, always some dust in the creases of the palm but no grit under the nails; the tips grow a pad that feels the world in fine textures: moss like felted rain, copper like a leaf warmed by thumb.

Their clothing is the color of continuance: fern-greens, the gray of wetted slate, the red of tree-bark brought indoors. Bast fiber and a fine moth-thread woven with a twist the fog likes to cling to. The cloth is heavier than it should be and soft as if already broken in. They wear it in layers that do not flap. Copper pins hold folds in place. They polish the pins with plant oil until the high points shine and the hollows keep their green, so each fastener looks a little gardened.

Hair is braided to behave. The braids are threaded with very narrow cords whose ends are small weights—pebbles bored through, seeds resined. These are not for ornament; they keep the hair in the thickness the air seems to expect. Faces show every ancestry that ever walked a mountain. Skin tends to the warm side of pale, darkening slowly to a varnished walnut under a life of thin sun. Cheeks are cool to the touch; breath smells faintly of mint and the sap used to soothe the trees when they are cut.

They speak like the place speaks. Words travel only as far as an arm held out; beyond that, gesture and bell-notes carry the rest. They have a vocabulary for intervals and for textures more than for distances. There are a dozen ways to say the pause between one drop and the next (the pause before, the after-pause, the long pause that means a fog-thin). There are words for the feel of the lip of a jar on the tongue and for the change in weight when a moss mat is watered at the edges and not the center. News is arranged in measured phrases with rests, so that a listener can stop hearing and the sense will wait unspilled. To call across a terrace, they lift their forefinger and tap it twice into the air; the other person will look without startle. Laughter is often a shared exhale.

They keep ledgers. Everyone keeps some kind of ledger: a string of knots tucked under a belt; a chain of pebbles in a pocket that moves left to right and back again; tallies in the soft copper plates that brace a stair’s railing, scraped lightly with bone and later rubbed out with oil. Children are taught to count where the world already counts. They count goats by hoofmarks in a pad of clay renewed every fog. They count the lives a tree’s limbs hold by the number of drip-lines caught in it. They count their own days by a quiet practice: one bead turned per dawn in a bracelet; when the circle completes they polish the beads to their first-smoothness and begin again.

There are roles as stable as the terraces and as light as their air. Bell-tenders tune the ceramic tongues so that they can be heard at an arm’s length and not more. Moss-keepers know each green by its thirst and comb the cushions with bone rakes to keep their skins breathing. Channelers spend their afternoons listening to the small waters; they set a fingertip under a drip and decide if it is a drop to keep or a drop to pass on, then they nudge a levered stone a fraction. Shadow-readers go to the plaza when the cloud thins and sit with their eyes half-closed; they adjust a stone by the width of a fingernail so that the four standing stones make the old figure once more. Goat-walkers ghost along the public steps with a hundred hooves like rain behind them and a coil of thornless branch in hand to convince the herd that moss tastes richer one terrace down. There are Thread-mothers who tend the moth boxes, though not all are women; they stroke fog-wet silk across their cheeks to feel for snags, and each year cede a third of their cocoons to the moths themselves in a small ceremony with no words in it.

They are good with tools that fit in the palm. Bone and wood shaped while the cloud sits. Clay thrown and polished to a sheen against skin; they test a pot’s lip by touching it to the mouth and then to the bellies of their wrists. If it sings too bright when thumbed, they shave the rim; the final clink is low, the sound of a promise kept. When a jar breaks, a child brings the largest shard to the place where the floors are mosaicked and gives it to the one who inlays. The inlayer will not yet look at the child; they will turn the shard in their hands until they can see where it fits, and only then will their eyes lift and nod and the child will feel taller.

They eat exactly as richly as their fog allows. A morning porridge of sedge-grit and goat milk, warm as breath. Red bark strips steeped to make a tea that smells of a walk just after a mist. Fiddleheads grilled on a hot stone. The cold-sweet fruit from the dew-rings, split and shared with someone who holds the other half; the way they bite is to press fruit to front teeth and break it open with the jaw still. On celebration days a haunch of goat, fire-seared on the outside and cooked slow in clay. There are fungal cakes grown in the cool dark of a storage hole, sliced thin and salt-puckered. Honey exists if you are patient and do not mind counting: a few combs a year from stingless bees in the red-bark’s hollows, harvested with a resin smoke that makes the air smell like a pinecone warmed in a pocket.

They have a taboo against moving a loud thing quickly. Hurrying is allowed; haste is not. Anger is not forbidden; it is expected to be borne like a pot of water to the lip-mark and not one slosh more. To apologize, you help someone fit a thing to another thing until the join is invisible. To grieve, you walk the line where the parapet ends against the fall, once at each bell. To ask for help, you bring a broken piece already cleaned, already ground to fit somewhere; your ask is the offer of your own mistake sanded true.

There is no chief. Decisions accrue until they balance. Terraces send their Counter to sit in the place of the sumps. They are never the same person twice in succession. They do not debate long. Instead they go and make the smallest version of the thing and see if it lives. If it lives too much, if it draws water from where a moss should have had it, if it makes a sound that can be heard two arm-lengths away when it should not, if the condor’s loop flinches near it, it is undone. If it lives in that exact way the ridge seems to like, they build the second smallest one. There is a story about a traveler who arrived with a plan for a wheel and a desire to change everything, and how the city answered by asking the traveler to make a hinge instead. The hinge did more good than the wheel would have, and the traveler is buried here.

Birth is a quiet, watery business. Midwives time their hands to the drip; they count the rests between a woman’s breaths and the mountain’s. An infant is greeted with a drop from a breath-well placed on the soft of the fontanel and then on the heel; then its mouth is touched to the lip of a cup that has seen a hundred other first sips. Names are given in pairs: a shadow-name learned the day shadows are first chased, and a water-name known to the family and to the sumps and said seldom. If someone must be called back from too far into anger or grief, their water-name is spoken at an arm’s length and the person stops like a jar filled to its line.

The dead are balanced with air. A platform on the crest is made ready with woven mats weighed by stones. The body is walked up in a procession that is mostly silence, the smallest children carrying the copper pins of the person’s clothing on a shallow tray. The ravens attend because they attend everything. The condor, if it writes a loop then, writes it large enough to include the platform. When the flesh is gone, the long bones are cleaned and touched with ash and hung for a season in the colonnade with the chimes, their hollows corked with resin and their strings tuned low. Then they are bricked into a wall at thigh height where those who loved the person will put a hand when they stand and when they sit.

They collect stories the way they collect water: not with nets that take all at once, but with cloth that catches always a little. There are at least four origin-tellings, each true in its turn. In one, the ridge rose during an inhale and has been keeping the city in its lungs; sometimes the children hold their breaths and pretend to be mountains and their elders let them and then tap their chests twice when it is time to let go. In another, their great-grandparents laid down stones to have a way to walk and the mountain, which had never been counted, was so grateful to be made finite that it stopped changing. In a third, less told aloud, the city is a narrow place between two great sleeps, and they live there because narrow places require watchfulness, and watchfulness is a form of love.

They do not fear outsiders; they fear two kinds of excess. A stranger who wants to be fed and will also sit in the shade and stroke the moss by its edge and keep their voice within the length of their arm is easy to host. A stranger who insists on filling a jar to its very brim or speaks from terrace to terrace as if the stone were a trumpet is corrected. They give gifts with limits embossed into them: a cup whose lip teaches where to stop; a knife whose handle fits only a certain grip—if your hand is wider, you will cut poorly. In exchange, travelers who come from the running weather bring lumps of salt the size of a child’s head, and seeds for herbs that handle fog well, and gossip from the lowlands that, once peeled of its storms, is good to taste.

They have games that teach the kind of attention the place rewards. Children play Stones for Shadows: four pebbles thrown, four shadows touched in order as the cloud thins. They play Even-Steps: someone calls a number, another tries to cross a terrace with that many steps and end exactly where they began. They play Water-Hands: a bowl is passed around a ring; each person may add one drop without spilling; to be the one whose drop runs over is to call lunch. On clear days, rarer than anyone admits, they play a game called Overlook in which they lie on their bellies and peer over the lip of the world and trade what they see below in even exchange—two goats for a river-bend, a village smoke for a glint of far copper.

Not everything that lives here is people. The moths in their floss-white boxes live here as citizens with a work and a season off. The goats, their hooves inked for counting on festival nights, live here in partnership; in the evenings they stand heat-heavy by the hearth-courts and let small hands thrum their ribs for the low music goats make. Salamanders own the slide and repay the city with patience; their skins are touched by children and then the children press their cheeks to the stone to learn the texture of wet. Hummingbirds are accepted thieves; families plant extra orange trumpets so that the birds will return in procession and teach the babies where to look for speed. Ravens are auditors and gossips, bright-headed, counting like anyone else. The people feed them a deliberate bright now and then: a ring of copper, a pebble the color of an egg, something chosen to match a ledger column, and the ravens carry it away and keep the count safe in other air.

There are also lives without faces that they greet by habit. Copper is spoken to as if it were a tendon that could be overworked. Resin is thanked and soothed. A moss clump that finally bridges the gap between two stones is addressed by the name of a long-dead aunt who also bridged a gap. The drip-lines are individuals; everyone in the city knows the sound of three of them as clearly as they know their own. The fog-frames are hung like instruments in a conservatory, and on some nights when the air is generous and the bells like breath, people come to the crest and sit with their backs to the frames and listen as the fog plays the hour.

They are not serene because serenity is a kind of forgetting. They are content and alert at once. They can be fiercely funny; a humor of tiny disproportions and exact similes. They can be jealous, and their jealousy is a hand laid over a ledger until they figure out what it is they owe. They can be reckless for the size of a single step, testing whether the seventeenth stair can be one finger-width higher without breaking the rule—then laughing when it cannot. They know their mountain has arithmetic and they like to show they can do math, too.

If you stayed, you would learn that they measure affection in fits. A person will come to your hearth with a tiny thing they have made to fit a thing of yours precisely: a wedge for your door, a plug for your jar, a pair of antlers cut and set as a lintel at the sky-door of your house so that the first thing you see on waking is a curve you did not know you needed. You would learn to walk without changing the world. You would learn what the bells mean: this bell means fog enough for the upper frames, this one means the sumps are asking for a sweep, this one means a goat has gone one terrace higher than the math allows. You would learn to swallow a word when the cloud folds heavy and to speak it when it thins. You would learn that the condor writes a loop each hour over the same gap in the cloud because someone a long time ago taught the air that there was a gap there and held it open with the daily attention of a city.

And you would be counted, as simply as that. Not numbered to be less, but brought into a count that includes moss and moth-thread and the sound of copper under thumb, the known hoof-falls of a goat, the quiet click of a pot’s lip shaved truer, the exact pause between drop and drop. You would be expected to add what you could not help adding, and to leave unfilled a space that did not need you. And you would find that your body changed: not in height or weight, but in the habit of being right-sized, your asks the measure of your arm, your offerings the size of your breath. This is how what lives here lives. It is not a rule so much as a posture, an agreement between a ridge that decided to keep its sharpness and a people who decided to keep their promises.
