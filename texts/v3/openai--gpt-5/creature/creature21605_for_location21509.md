Those who live here are plural, but they behave as if they share one body. If there is a single citizen you could name, it would be the hollow itself: the Host that is wall and roof and lung. It is not a plant like those that sip rain. It is a resin-maker, a light-eater, a sifter of air. It pulls salts and trace metals from dust and aerosol, folds light into sugars, and spends what it gathers on structure—membrane and frond and gutter—until the place is both an organism and a precinct.

The Host is not a single stem but a federation of cords and sheets and sacs, all grown from a common clade that remembers how to braid with itself. Where the stone offers an edge, the Host sets a lip of tissue and knits around it. That lip thickens into a baffle; the baffle becomes a gallery; the galleries turn into loops that corral air and make it behave. It does not only grow in bulk; it grows in function. Certain patches of membrane learn to soften and part; others learn to stiffen on cue and throw a gust back out into blazing emptiness. There are organs within it that resemble organs the way a hive resembles a single bee—distillers for aromatics, paddles that swell and relax to keep pressure constant, rivulet-sieves that comb grit into terraces so delicate you could confuse them for decoration.

Inside this Host move the small articulate lives that look like citizens because they go about with purpose. They belong here as ivy belongs to a wall it has made its own. They have been engineered by long companionship into the tools and tongues of the place. Their bodies all shine with the same kind of gloss the Host glows with, a polish of oil that is the difference between living and burning in air this dry. Many are no heavier than the resin drops they sometimes get trapped in. They clip their movements down to what gravity and etiquette require. Loudness in motion is a rudeness; loudness can be a death.

One of them, the seam-moth, would read at first as ornament. Its wings are not wings in the flying sense but low-slung, flexible panes that beg to be mistaken for a fold in the membrane. When it comes to rest, it vanishes by completion: it presses itself into a crease and drinks the microfilm of oils that pool there, sampling chemistry as a reader runs a finger along a page. More than reading, it edits. Along its abdomen are rings of tiny teeth that can score a membrane to persuade a change in tension. The seam-moth is trusted enough that the Host yields to those edits, a little; over days a crease will shift by a breath, a gutter will fall a degree, and a slow stream will find a new root to serve. Moths teach the hollow to fit the weather it has.

Another citizen is all limb and rib, a grazer that carries itself like a drawn notation. Its whole body is long bones with taut membranes stretched between, its mass calculated so exactly that the flex of a frond becomes staircase and spring both. It licks resin twice—the first to soften the crust at a specific salt pocket, the second to taste the readiness of what will dissolve—and then it waits while diffusion works. To watch it is to learn the pace of this place. Its tongue is a ribbon with barbs that snag crystals and comb them from the sticky. It eats what others cannot: a diet of trace salts bound to resin, the taste of a day’s worth of sky. When it excretes, it turns, presses, and lets the droplet join a narrow river as if returning a library book on time and to the correct shelf. In that return is half the city’s plumbing.

Between the seamers and the grazers run lines of smaller glossed lives—minute shelled scoops, soft pipes with feet. They are conveyors. They carry, not to anywhere dramatic, but from slick to slick, gallery to gallery, a traffic of breadcrumbs so constant that missing a turning would be noticed in the scents. Their bodies must be almost unbearably pleasant to touch; they have been made and remade by a world that would abrade any rawness. Where explosions of dust bring a new taste, it is these couriers that spread word fastest, simply by blurring along their habitual routes, their wakes dense with aromatic notation.

Some of the sacs you see hanging from the middle tiers look like lanterns because parts of them are pure transparency, but they are noses and stills rather than lights. The Host grows them to sample the air, to yank out whatever will be useful, and to exhale what needs saying. The stills are not solitary. They keep a staff: delicate siphoners no bigger than a fingernail that live inside the sacs like birds in bell-jars. They tend films, harvest precipitates, unclog pores. When oil runs rich, they skate its top as if it were glass; when the air tastes like a problem, they scuttle to pull taut the lip of a membrane here, relax one there, recalibrating the hollow’s breath. If the Host is the city, these are its civil servants and chemists.

Predators come, but the place makes a distinction between hunter-as-citizen and hunter-as-weather. The citizens you could call needles, if you could ignore how they weave themselves into the place they might pierce. They are threads stiffened at one end into a grasping, four-sided hand, at the other into a stabilizing vane. Their eyes are not eyes; they are patches of bare, scent-receptive skin. They join the polite traffic, but seen from above, their paths are not random: they patrol the thin leagues where the brood-wells’ faint sweetness draws thieves.

They are allowed in by the Host with a softness that looks like hospitality but is closer to contract. There are places where membrane tightens into netting that opens only along scent signatures like signatures on a treaty. A landless needle—a passerby from another pocket—will find itself politely refused: a sheet that yields just enough to prevent a break, then returns it to the light with an insistence that does not bruise but also does not negotiate. A resident hunter can pass those same drapes without a sound, find what it is meant to find, and be gone with a debt to the place kept small.

The hunter-as-weather is the larger shadow—those few bodies flung high enough by the ridge’s updrafts to cross a hollow and choose to dip. They are not built for oil. Their skins are naked to water and to abrasion both, and when they plunge a membrane into panic, they are given a lesson in chemistry: a sheet that shudders and spits them into a fringe of sacrificial whiteness where oil meets their breath and hazards it. They are scalded without heat, kept too slick to find purchase. A reed-thin shriek, a struggle with nothing to push against, and then they’re back in the glare clutching an absence. If they come in again, they learn to do it as a weather system does, and the hollow treats them accordingly, with the same elaborate manners it gives to wind—split, slowed, made harmless.

The brood-wells at the center are not nurseries in the domestic sense. Nothing cuddles there. They are bowls of more-ness, the richest exudate the Host can make, scented to invite a specific laying. All citizens come to attend them because all citizens come from them, though not all from the same invitation. An egg small as a poppy seed dropped by a seam-moth female in such a well tastes a ratio and is told to become legs. Laid against the same curve a day later, it reads a slightly different blend and makes a room between its tissues where a later life will hold distiller’s tools. There are no mothers in the individual way; there are mothers in the compositional way. The differences between grazers and seamers and sac-tenders and needles may be genetic, but they are written by perfume.

The grubs that prise loose from the well are luminous little cylinders with simple mouths and a hunger that looks like reverence. They are placed, by scent at first, into slow honeyed streams of work. The first thing they learn is that pressure and perfume are more reliable than sight. They learn that a court with thicker light is a place to carry resin toward, that a thread of green-pepper odor means a bruise, and that the least sound can draw a disapproval expressed without malice but with consequence—the closing of an accustomed passage, a coldness of membrane that tells you you’ve broken a math.

Given weeks of such lessons, they take their shapes. The ones who will become long-limbed grazers molt into drafts of themselves; you can almost see calculation in the way their weights sort themselves out. The seamers practice on sacrificial curtains that never fully harden. Siphoners spend days inside smelling games, tasting vanishingly small differences until they can set a still’s lip by a difference as fine as a ghost in a room. The ones who will patrol learn the Lips—the thresholds of permission—by tracing them with their skins until they can walk blind and the hollow will trust them.

There is a kind of citizen too small for a seat in any portrait: the microbe whose entire body is a strategy for not letting water be a word it understands. It lives in the resin the way fish live in water, flicking from pocket to pocket on forces that others set in motion. It strips out nitrates from dust, re-stitches odors, chews apart an aromatic that’s outlived its message and gives back its parts. Whole terraces of function would collapse without this invisible editorial board. If you press your nose to a membrane and think you smell a new mineral aftertaste, it is as likely you are smelling a council decision.

Travelers exist. They are not a species as much as a profession. They carry on their polished shells the agreements of one pocket to another. Their bodies are tall ovals that can lie flat like a leaf or stand and walk like an instrument case with feet. Their steps are a rhythm learned by all, and when they pass, certain corridors relax. They can shift their gloss from one recipe to another so that when a path takes them from a hollow where sweetness is safe through a bridge where sweetness invites thieves, they pop to a pepper-skin and are ignored by the wrong mouths. None of them lives only in one room; none of them belongs nowhere. They keep the string of rooms honest to one another and the grosser world out.

Everyone here counts time, not by change in light—the sky is so even above that day is a lens rather than a theater—but by the behavior of certain scents. At high midday the sap-notes of the stills fatten by a shade and then begin to thin. When the shadow of a column reaches the circumference of a particular shelf, the Host opens a new gutter by a whisper and closes another. The citizens time their work not by hour but by such openings. There are festivals of a sort, but they are quieter than you might imagine. The day a new scent blend is put into the air so that a gravelly bloom from lower down the ridge will be recognized here is as close as ceremony comes. Everyone present tastes the message and repeats it until it is simply part of the place. There are relics, but they are not bones; they are old membranes that hardened to amber and hold inside them the notes of a storm that passed a hundred breath-seasons ago. To put your face near such a window is to feel a history trying to get out and being, still, patient about it.

If you are listening for malice, you will listen a long time. Theft exists, but it is the kind of theft a tide commits when it takes what is not nailed down and makes it into something else. There are soft-bodied grazers—bead-large, grape-soft—that breed fast in pockets more enclosed than this one. They will sometimes flood a well and drink more than their ration. When they do, the patrol-needles will herd them, not by violence but by an absence: certain doors will simply not be where they were, and the only way is back to a thicker-scented room where hunger is less of a madness and the sums add up again.

Above everyone: light. Not to adorn. To pay bills. The citizens face it like accountants. A seam-moth deciding whether to clip a ridge into a vapor-permeable fence knows it is balancing budget lines: let more light through and you gain resin you can spend on growth; let more grit in and your siphoners work double shifts pulling poisons and prizes apart. A grazer decides whether to lick now or later the same way. The Host, if it has a word for God, says it with the part of the sky that is not blue but a pressure across skin telling it what the next breath will be.

When weather from far away finally climbs this high, it arrives thin and startled, and even then these residents meet it not with celebration but with preparedness learned at cost. Water is an alarm. Should a bruised cloud drop dust powdered with ice, the hollow blanches. Every membrane pulls oil to its surface and shuts up like a mouth clamped tight. Sacrificial skirts take on the hissing harm. The citizens wait, still, until the message thins out of the air: safe. Then the place opens again, but not all at once; the etiquette is as much for storms as for bodies.

At the edges, founding happens. Where the ridge’s ribs jut, the Host reaches. A frond will extend, then fail and crisp; a second will harden just in time to give the third the right angle. Seamers and siphoners and grazers go out with it, the way a tongue goes with a mouth. One day—one breath, here—a membrane will find two stones that answer each other across a slot of sky, and the little room that will be another city makes its first wall. The first citizens of that room are not pioneers so much as continuations. They do the same work they have always done, but in a new geometry.

What lives here, then, is a habit given bodies. It is constraint taken into flesh and turned into craft. It is oil pretending to be river and being believed. It is the belief itself, held by membranes and moths and long-limbed grazers and microbes and needle-hunters, that quiet is not the absence of life but the condition of its thinking. If you could somehow learn to wear the right gloss and carry the right scent and make your limbs as light as honesty, the hollow would permit you. You would be expected to taste, to wait, to lean where the pressure says to lean. You would be given work. You would, perhaps, be understood.
