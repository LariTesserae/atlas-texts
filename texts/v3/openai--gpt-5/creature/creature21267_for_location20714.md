What lives here begins too small to touch and ends in bodies that edit the weather. You know them first by the way the dark sounds around them, then by what the air brings to the back of your throat.

The first nation is the film—skins of living that lick stone for ion and trace. Their kingdoms are texture and pH. Bacteria feather the sweat on the ceiling into slicks that catch salt, then slacken when the tide lifts their breath. Algae make lawns in trickles and write thin green banners off drips. On them, and through them, bloom the gloved hands of moss, the felted plush that grips edges—every nap full of spores and sugar. All this is smell before it’s sight: a tannic-green tea tinting the closeness, a mushroom-iron aftertaste when the shelves pulse and open.

Fungi hold almost every citizenship above the scale of thinness. Shelves come in brass sections—a rim for catching fall, a gill-shadowed underworld where air is slowed to eddy. Cups breathe bruise-glow when startled, a damp ultraviolet sigh more guessed than seen. Clubs and antlers insist up, up, up, as if height were a verb you could say without light. They’ve learned this world’s law—sound is weather—better than anything. There are mats that tighten at low note and release at high, mats that wring themselves when centuries of air choose solstice, mats that go from wood-hard to silk-rot when a community of beetles hums the right pitch against a rib of bone. Spores here are not free. They are grazed in clouds. They are herded by wind-languages.

On that pasture come the keepers. Beetles, yes, in gardening guilds. They find a nodular outcrop and train it with a pendulum stolen from a dead thing: a rib, a stalactite spicule. In drifts of night they stroke it with soft legs until the tone lines up with a permission in the fungus, and then the whole gray field opens like a sleepy mile of mouths. They eat and carry and seed. They pass down the hoop’s note—what to call from the mineral—by tapping it into the chitin of their own bodies, a callus-song they teach their hatchlings by pressing carapace to carapace until the cuticle remembers.

Crickets are the weather’s metronomes. The floor-crickets are knee-priests, solemn, long as a hand and built like stained-glass windows of tendon and plate. They tune the room by rubbing femur to scrape until the walls admit they are empty enough to listen. Ceiling crickets, pearled and pale, hang like punctuation, beating their cerci with a tin-rain that threads every other sound. They are more than noise. Their bodies are clocks that wake on specific pressure falls; whole parishes of them rise and fall to the twice-daily tide, calling the inhale and the exhale so precisely that everything else entrains to their pulse.

Millipedes are the trains, running in lanes so worn they feel like braided rope. Some are twine-thin; some are arm-thick, the color of putty, with legs like a line of needles stitched along a sausage. They are traffic more than animal—the sound of them is a powered brush over a drumhead. They go everywhere the drip does, squeegeeing green to gray so the next stratum of film can come in behind. Their passage writes the day’s story in ribbed gloss and fresh matte, a palimpsest you can read with your bare soles.

In the lower pockets live the mathematicians of hollow things—pale crabs, their hepatopancreas ghost-pale through exoskeleton, clicking their claws not for threat but to inlay a room with the rhythm that will make it speak back. They pick up perfect shells—paper-thin dead snails, pocket gourd husks, the long calcareous trumpets of some extinct worm—and drum themselves into courtship. When the bore presses, the whole flat cropland of them goes to thunder, their ground a kitted orchestra. In that tumult, mates find mates by timbre alone. They migrate sideways with the salt-edge twice daily, their eyes useless, their lives a wheeled geometry of pressure gradients and tone. Some tribes farm the algae sheets, some farm the shell middens themselves—polishing, thinning, polishing—to pull a sweeter ring from the same dead spiral.

There are swallow-kin here—birds that lost sky and gained muscle memory. Blind, yes, but with shoulders that read updrafts like braille. They do not build. They heap themselves and spit and whatever moss they can carry into pockets and become nests by remaining. Their bodies make the shape, and their children learn the hollow of refuge as a curve under the breastbone long before they learn any wing. The air is their nerve; they will fly a corridor so tight it is not a corridor but a flute, and they will know when a quake has re-cut a note by the way their flapping goes grainy at the wingtips. Their diet is noise-fed too: midge-storms over wet fungus, crickets if they can catch them quick as a cough, moth-bats when luck bites the other way.

Bats themselves have gone to church and knife and wind. Some are largesse: linen-winged, dog-broad, skimming through with a silence so greasy you feel it on your skin. Their faces are levers. They pour their own voices into organ pipes until the stone agrees to map itself in echo. Others are midge-fast, each one the flit of a thought, harvesting spore fog the way swallows take gnats. One lineage got clever with weight. They carry pebbles in their cheeks and let a chain of them fall over black water: plip-plip-plip—counting the depth by the time between notes, marking whatever comes close enough to nose as a place to remember. The right-sized stones pass from mouth to mouth like pearls with provenance. For this they maintain caches at pool-edges, piles sorted not by color but by voice. Old stories in that wing-folk speak of the day a pool ate the wrong throat because the cairn had been handled and the pattern of drops lied. No one touched that pitch again.

Fish go as rumor does. Their bodies are opal if you had eyes to see; you don’t need them. You know them in a riffle that sharpens against a shin. Blind, with scaleless faces rubbed down to an honesty, they frisk in the quiet hours and vanish when the distant wallop of a bore starts walking its way down the tunnels. Some species can read the eel-rope feints and some cannot, and blood under water is the only color in the place.

And yes, there are eels: ropes left to rot until they ripple. They climb the wet in S-shaped patience and hang under lips of rock, a slackness that waits for breakage. Their bodies talk to each other in skin flickers you cannot see, a code for split-thrust now, bite here. When they collect they make a garland, and when they strike they make a circle around a foot or a fish or a rat thing and close.

There are things that learned that silence is a weapon. The quill-haired hunter is one. It is the size of a child and the shape of a backed-out haircut, shoulderless and all long spine. Its quills aren’t quills; they are strips of horn laid in ranks that rasp air into a ragged curtain of whispers when it breathes wrong. That whisper eats the edge off surrounding soundwaves. Everything living here listens; everything depends on those edges to know where teeth are and where cliff is. A place with its edges rubbed out is death. The quill thing strolls with a cloud of wrongness around it. Isopods freeze. Crickets throw themselves off their holes to break the shape of the wave. Boots—it does not know boots—step into a smoothed-out pit and go there quickly and forget to yell. You learn the hack once if you live: a paper lattice of vine behind your calves that chirrs when the whisper comes, giving the way back a little audible life. The hunter hates lattice. It sings too high for it to shave.

On the margins where the rock sighs open to a lighter world, there is a mammal that climbs. It is not a bat and not a primate, though your bones want to call it something you’ve met. It is jointed in the hands the way a spider is jointed in the leg, the long phalanges stiff with tendon so that it can hook a liana and ring it like a bell with its own weight. Its forearms are drumheads. Its chest is a hollow tuned to the pitch of its mother. Eyes it has, and they are poorer than its knees. The knees are whiskered with keratinic fans that register draft like touch. It goes up at dusk to a place where light leaks in thin as false milk, and it tastes flowers it will never see. It brings home pollen on its face and in its fur, and that pollen is food and plaster and the binder for those neat little shell-cairns that hold their tone longer under wet.

It is one animal, one version. There are others of it. They make a people by refusing to be single. They do not build—cannot; the cave eats built work and licks it down to idea. Instead they persist as methods. They are bright at the ear and thick at the chest. Their whole throats are instruments. The cartilage rings are looser than yours; they can pull the neck wide and turn themselves into a flute. They live in moving arrangements of safety: a handful of slings knotted into vines and hung in a lee above regular bores; three or four nearer-rooms tuned by draped skins to a family’s preferred echo; a dozen caches of pebbles and shells and cords strung with teeth. Ownership is timbre; if a shell sings a particular E when struck, and that E has been walked back to a birth note sung into gypsum whiskers on a certain day, it is understood to belong.

They count with echo more than with finger. A messenger will clap and listen and know whether the way ahead has fattened with collapse or thinned with new gape. They mark their memory with call-and-return, teaching their children the gallery delays as if reciting lineage. Walls return the sound of header bones stuffed in fissures—it is not decorative, but a way to anchor the room to an agreed pitch. A story is a route. A name is a chord you can hum into stone and feel the room remember you.

Farming looks unlike the word. There is a patch of bracket fungus long coaxed into complacent hotbeds with a certain beat on a certain rib. There’s a seam of gypsum hair that, when stroked with a wetted thumb, sweetens the air enough to wake spores from three more chambers down. There are horn-flutes made of bat dead, out of which a pair of old ones can shake a rain from the ceiling when the pressure’s at that edge where the chamber will either sweat or not. Their agriculture is inducement. They know which frequencies dehisce which cups, which rattles bring out which beetles to groom the mats, which soft mouth-notes keep the ceiling drips regular so the algae flat stays soft and edible and doesn’t go glass-hard. They read the crickets for long-term. When the knee-priests shift their antiphosis by a fraction, the people start looking for new shelter because a quake-storm is in the offing.

Predation can find them; they answer by making absence into a tool of their own. A line of them walking in a bore period doesn’t walk. They hang from the roof by vine-loops known to be younger than last week and they swing. They do not carry torches even if they could make them; light would be rude here. They carry the problem between their teeth and their chest and let the world argue with it until it gives up what’s true. On their ankles are lattices as fine as whale scars. If an eel loops, the lattices snag and make an inland wave that reaches up the shin and into the pelvis. Then a leg flick at a practised beat and the eel slips time and misses.

Trade is rhythm. The bats own depth-measuring stone piles at pools and will let a person mouth in for a check if that person can sing the roost’s lineage back to them without losing the beat in the boing of the edge stones. The crabs keep shell-middens; you may not touch unless you can thicken the air with your chest to match their drumming ground’s resonance, and even then you leave the thin shells; you take the chips. Beetles are hired and paid with rooms. If you can open a gallery to their correct fungus and let the wind draft across their wing-cases just so, they will plant the exact velvet you like and keep it flat with a hundred patient feet. There are written things: a heap of pebbles sorted by tone; a smear of spit and pollen painted on a bone and stuck in a crack at chin height, that bone humming when a bore is on its way so that anyone who licks it can tell. It is writing you taste.

Young are named at gypsum. The whiskers grow in little lint-balls in certain seams, each a tuft that weeps when warm breath kisses it. A mother holds her child there and sings. The seam softly duplicates. That echo is part of the child now. They test foods the same day. A bit of algal flag. A midge. The salt edge of a pool. When their stomachs retch at the chalk-taste of true-new collapse, everyone nods: this is one who will go down to retrieve stone and bring back age.

The dead are not buried. There is nowhere to hold a hole. When someone stops they are dismantled to method. Skin is dried and cut into straps. Bone is hung for tune. Teeth thread a cord. Hair goes to thicken the knee-lattices. The voice is carried in that last name, which is struck and hummed and shared until it disappears into the room and the room knows them. Grief is a wrecking of one’s own resonance. There is a cave-practice of finding a seam that hates low notes and keening into it until it shrinks, until the very fungus given to the dead shrivels and pops, and then the bellow-follow when the rest of the wall opens again and breath comes back.

Across all of this moves tide. Twice daily a bell that hasn’t been cast rings anyway, a slow cool push and then salt’s dry aftertaste. The bats feel it in folded membranes. The birds tilt against it with breast feathers, then cheat into it and rise. The crabs know the exact moment sideways begins. The people’s chests shift themselves to a smaller tuning and then a larger without thinking. When ocean storms roll above, this place rains hard. Drops drop like thrown stones. Everything opens and eats, then hardens and waits. When quake-storms rove the corridors a week long, new rooms appear sudden as breath in the throat. The edge of the world goes humid; the laws change a fine degree. The folk roll their slings and go and retune their caches and leave taps on walls—three-fast, two-long, no return—don’t come here for a while.

Predators keep shape in absence. Sometimes the floor is scattered with isopods like confetti until, all at once, it is not, and the air feels shaved and your teeth hum a warning. Sometimes a river-tunnel is alive with blind fish under your knees and then your ankle registers a rope that moves like a thought you didn’t have. Sometimes there is a whisper so wrong that the walls don’t know how to reply to it and everything that depends on wall-answers runs. Survival here is a knack for not insisting on sight when the dark has other laws.

Along the margins, the daylight wages a little war. Lianas thicken down like knuckled thumbs. Out the climbers go with claws and poor eyes, up and back with pollen coats and sour flower sugar on the breath. They wet it and make a glue that holds a shell to a bone long enough to write. They taste wind from the other world and bring down its weather by telling it to their chests over and over until something in the limestone thinks it remembers and sweats accordingly.

You can walk this place as a catalog if you like—arthropods fattening films, bats fattening bats, algae in green, fungus in white, crabs in their percussion orchards, fish threading silence, eels pretending to be rope, birds held aloft by a habit of air and spit. But that is not how it holds itself. What lives here are the ways sound learns bodies. The way a vine invests in a quiver so that a person’s shoulder-blow can wake a draft and make a fan open like a prayer flag. The way a millipede lane becomes history because the ribs on it gloss old and the new ones scumble and you can hear the difference with bare feet. The way the youngest stalactite hangs like a wolf’s tooth, broken this year, the cave not yet in love with it enough to soften it—and there, on that sharp, a ring of skin where something learned it was too bright to forget.

It has not been long since flatness failed. The world is fresh break. The edges still cut clean if you ask them to. The life has rushed into the cracks like water and laced itself there with claws and cuffs and air-maps and that inexplicable willingness of bodies to cooperate with echo. If you insist on calling some of them people, they won’t mind; they will not make monuments for you. They will make more method, tune another rib to another fungus so the pores open and the beetles come and the room eats and then tightens and then waits. They will mark their days in pressure and return-difference, in the boing of a pool-edge stone, in the hiss of shells withdrawing as you step near.

Who lives here? The whole place does, and it uses everyone. Each thing is an instrument that has found a frequency it can hold until the room answers back. The air is busy with this. The dark is not empty, but thick, and not unfriendly, but definite, and it asks you to listen before it tells you what it is.
