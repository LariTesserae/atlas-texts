What lives here has teeth only in the way a shore has them: a fine serration meant for water, not for biting. What lives here leans rather than steps, listens rather than looks. It is a citizenry of holdfast and blade, of film and fan, living in the grammar of falling water until their bodies have taken it for syntax.

The tape-forests are the first to meet you, if “meet” is the word for being read by touch in a place that has extinguished the idea of face. They rise from pockets of sediment like a great harness left to soak. Each long, ink-green strap is notched along its edge, not for defense—their world has nothing that comes with appetite—but to change how the current rakes them, to multiply the little pressure-differences that open their pores and wake their chemistry. They chafe against one another in a continuous patience, and that chafe is their warmth, their work, their winter occupation. They prefer the places where the bowl has taught the water to go in one direction for a long time. They prefer a note that does not waver. They orient to that, lean into it, and stay.

Between their thickets the glass courts lie down. Glassweed does not do shade; it makes surface. On naked terraces and open shoulders it unfurls in pale, many-laminated fans that are as close to invisible as the day will allow. They are the eyes of the place in the rare hours when the rain thins. When lightning diffuses milk-blue through the bulk above, every sheet catches it, writes it in silver, and gives it away again to the gloam. This is how glassweed eats: it is a hand for a brief pulse of clarity and then a pocket when the pulse goes, cupping eddies of slow water against its faces where vanishing light can double back on itself. It draws its body with calcite the way other plants pull breadth from air; over years it makes its own thin plates, chalk-transparent, until a court is a splay of panes grown out of hunger and sound.

Everything that is vertical is furred with the frost-colored periphyton. It is not a single thing but a quilt of guilds that know spray and shade. On ledges and under ledges, in the blown skirts of the rim-falls and in the lee of dolmens, they drift upward and cement down in beds of lace. This pelt is how the cliff breathes; it is where the weather leaves its nitrates and the thunder’s chemistry is turned to sap. It is the anchor-point for the rumor of the air in a lake that has forgotten sky. Open a pinch of it and a hundred little trades make themselves known: sugars passing to stone in exchange for grip; calcium bought with a day’s worth of wet wind; faint acid paid in tiny drafts to loosen a smoother future. When ice forms on their faces in long, delicate plates, they keep working underneath. It is a country of service and mending.

Under roofs of clay and stone, where the storm’s breath is captured, reefs build that have only ever known the sound of thunder from the inside. Sponge-plants pad these bells’ margins with velvet, more texture than organism, punctuated by the antlered growth of calcareous algae that look like quiet corals and are anything but quiet inside. They listen harder than anything else here. The bells themselves shudder like lungs when falls strike them slant; the reefers take that shudder and turn it into timing. When a drought within the rain lets light fall longer, they open to it with an extravagance learned across a hundred lean years; when it goes they continue, metered by the slow thub of the world’s wall of water. Fault-lines of crystal push from their tissues like blossoms, not decoration but the record of long steadiness. Each air-pocket is a city; each city hums in a different key; across centuries the architecture thickens to make the key more itself.

In the great dark of the drowned amphitheater, the chalkwriters keep their school. They grow like fog made domestic over cracks where glacial breath percolates out—air older than any hill nearby, exhaling without heat, heavy with the minerals of ancient storms. The white mats furl and unfurl in that faint current, scribbling themselves into existence in a script that holds. They lay down plumes of alabaster on the old lava and the old granite, and in those white drifts the long rain remembers when it was snow and when the bowl was not yet a bowl. They eat with what patience demands: a slow pulling apart of salts, a tasting of what little light runs this deep, a supplement from the bowl’s own mechanized life—the slight friction where forever is made into sound.

There are the strike-lights, too, planted in calmer bays that behave like waiting rooms for weather, their stalks charged with a secret that unveils only when the above-world speaks in fire. Each thunder-roll propagates down the length of their poles as beads of sudden green. The beads hurry like coins chased across a table, then vanish. Why light if there is no eye? Because in this world light is a tool with many edges. It opens valves. It cleaves molecules that are loath to cleave. It starts chains of chemistry at a distance and brings kin into the same hour. In seasons when lightning is the only clock that keeps time across the whole bowl, the strike-lights keep a gossip active that even the tape-forests heed, adjusting sap and pores to meet the pulses.

Not everything here is flat to your hand. There are knots in all this patience. Buckleweed grows rope. It twines as if it had been told the story of torque by something that could move, then went back to stillness to dream it in plant. Each strand is a list of reasons to hold on: a spiral of mineral, of cellulose, of subtle glue, tight within tight. Old workers once read this in their hands and trusted it. They bound stones together where the water felt like a thought that could be persuaded, and the lashings are still there, petrified now by chemistry and time. The buckleweed itself still dwells where eddies and angles make its body an answer. It throws spooly bouquets when days go pale and the air-bells breathe more often; its seed-stalks hook to the slow places without malice, nothing hunts here, only gravity.

Seeds are important, but seed is not the word the residents use among themselves. They do not have words. They have orientation. They have weight and blade and barb; they have solubility, sensibility to viscosity, a memory of notes passed from parent to offspring not as message but as temper. The pods swell with a draft of those rare steepening lights, then let go. Their coiled embryos curl like springs under the slow rain, edge-weighted so that they sail downward along a precise arc, always toward a place that sings familiar. Places that sing simple are the densest—where flow is a solved problem, where fronds have leaned so long the stone is polished to friendship. There the young take with ease. Places that sing a little different every year wait longer, puzzling over themselves, growing quietly until another family finds the way to answer.

If there is a people here beyond these plants, beyond the bell-reekers and the chalkwrights, it lives in the interval between water and stone, in the tuned hum that the old dolmens and pinched half-weirs maintain. Call them the leaners. Across the bowl, in groves you can measure and in patches you can only suspect, their bodies line up to pressure like a million ears around a drum. They share no brain and yet they cohere, following a logic that is not aim but consent. A standing wave lifted from a picked V turns to food in a hundred stems; half a chain of dolmen harmonics steadies a whole neighborhood’s growth; a pillar with finger-holes makes a wake that an entire curtain of fronds learns and leans into across three changes of season. It is this—this way of agreeing with the rules of a place so completely that the rules begin to remember you—that convinces a visitor that life here is not only in bodies, but in the decisions a body transmits to the world by standing where it stands.

Nothing takes flight. The only quickness is atmosphere. What you would call nerves are laid down here in phosphate threads through silt—white, fine, easy to bruise, healing across months. The mats under loose stones that look like winter fire are more than housekeeping. They are the books these waters keep. Layers of dust and fall and thunder-ash sift down, are stitched into place by algal lace, glazed by slow sugars until a thin page is made. Turn back a century and everything is the same, slightly. Turn back a thousand and the tune is lower, the traffic of falls different, the thunder’s recipe altered; the writing records it and keeps such records from walking away.

Out on the lip, where the water grows frazil skins and the air smartens into knives, leafless fronds hold themselves like deer antlers, rime on rime. They do not break, or if they do they have prepared to be that brittle. Their bodies are made of compromise: hard enough to host crystals, soft enough to let those crystals melt without tearing. Lightning sweeps and the ice lets go; it reforms; the rim-fronds spend an entire life as a conversation with this one act. Their neighbors are walls of flow that exist only as long as a freeze and unfreeze takes. The rim-fronds take those changes into their calendars and crowd their short season with purpose.

Everywhere, the green trades with stone and makes weather into habit. It pays careful attention to the pressure of a drizzle against a ledge. It learns the difference between the hum that means a slab is creeping toward a tilt and the hum that means it is not this winter. It senses lightning when it is still far away and raises tiny bulbs along its lengths—bubble-lanterns that shine a cold green when massed water speaks in roughness nearby. Do they signal? They need no others to see them. They are simply good chemistry set to trip with violence, a way to breathe harder when water would make breathing weak.

The builders are gone. This is itself a kind of life here—the memory of hands in a place that has none. The half-weir in the narrow throat, the ladder of peg-holes rubbed pearl by long fronds, the ring of stones that hums when the bowl has filled its lips to spilling—these are tools left to do their work alone. Stones tuned to keep a pitch have been fed so much weather that their voices have become the bowl’s. The very absence of their setters has taught the lives remaining to expect, to take advantage, to grow down into the grammar of those arrangements. A plank that opens and closes against nothing under a certain turbulence is a metronome you can root to, if you are a plant that knows how to hear in its cuticle.

No face turns to you. That is the strangest consolation. You do not startle anyone. You do not enter a chain of eating. You press past a million small labors and none of them pause. They incorporate your wake and continue. They are uninterrupted, and so their lives are led to conclusion, each in their own curve, in a way that the running world rarely allows. When the brief drought comes inside the rain and light steepens into the water, all of this seeds and sugars without drama. An entire grove throws a fresh green at once, a surge like a gasp; the bells fill with scent for a few hours—the only time smell is a resident here; the basin is briefly a bakery of stone warmed by sun. It is not a holiday, no fireworks, no migration, just the right side of a cycle getting the chance to show itself and taking it.

Down at the floor, in a plane of silt so dark it might be a ceiling, there is a rectangle kept clean by a tremor you cannot hear but you can see in the little puffs that refuse to settle. It is a door frame without a door. The slit that makes it is too narrow for anything but a hair, but it scours, minute after minute, enough to keep opportunists from setting a foot. One day the pressure will slacken. The green that waits in the walls around that absence will move in. Nothing here hurries toward its future; it merely stays poised for it.

So what lives here? The basin does, voiced into a body by its plants. The long ribbons and the pale fans and the frost-fur, the reefers under bells and the chalkwriters in the cold, and all the cords and bulbs and knives and laces that make of weather a way of staying. The tuned stones that are now more chorus than tool. The tuned water that hums against what hums back. The highness itself, which is not emptiness at all but a sea that has learned the trick of remaining high. This place’s people are a patience that listens its way into form, and the place has grown so permanent around them that you can mistake permanence for personhood and not be wrong. They are not a flock, not a herd, not a nation—unless you count a choir that never stands down as a country. They occupy, and in occupying they sing.
