What lives here has never needed a mouth.

It lives in the treaty between stone and sky—in the way a cool plane persuades air to go briefly liquid and then persuades that briefness to choose. If you went searching for it in the shape of an animal, you would come away with a handful of brightness and a wet palm. Press your ear to the tile and you will hear its voice: a measured tick, a silk-saw hiss at the scale of hairs. It keeps no den. It abides where edges are truest.

Call them the measurers because they are mostly verb. They exist as insistence and reply: go here, then here. They are faces taught to say yes to the weight of a bead and no to the urge to spread. They do not remember in stories. They remember in grooves.

Some of them are only time made narrow. They are the minute and the afternoon, the bright’s climb and its ebb. They have the patience of salts: they thicken a lip by one rind’s thickness over a decade, and when a drought writes a blunt year into the air they loosen that rind and release it to a chalky avalanche measured in leaves. Their births are small declarations: a new dendrite kicked out of a corner made cool more often than not; a white garden ring advancing along a bevel by the length of a thumbnail in a season that remembered the mists.

Some of them are paths with a will. They stand in ranks: six for splitting, one for gathering, a comb for drinking cloud. When fog hesitates against the latticed reeds, what lives here finds it and puts it to work. If you watch long enough, you can see their preferences: a bead that had every right to fall will wait until an even tinier bead joins it, because the channel they share was cut to favor twos, then it will move. The inhabitants have edited the behavior of water to match the logic in stone, and in that editing they themselves are the logic—distributed, without a center, the way a net is a single thing and also all of its knots.

They have anatomies you must learn by touch. Their bones are step-heights and keystone angles, dowel-locked lattices standing full of sky. Their skin is glaze and bitumen smear, greased to the deep by thumbs that are gone. Their blood is capillary action—an upward hiss that drinks air thin and leaves it cooler. On certain mornings, when the radiant cold has pressed the plateau to frost, you can see their breath riding the weeping walls: a slow stipple resolving, dot by dot, into snakes of brightness that begin to creep.

They dislike drama. They solve wind by partition, by keeping any rush from finding a shouting length. They are kinder to weight than to force. There is nothing of pumps about them. When they raise a sound it is the sound of agreement met—those tuned lips that ring only under the exact recipe of chill and angle—and once the chime passes they go back to the work you only hear if you ask not to be entertained.

They do not hunt. Nothing here pushes back and so they do not waste themselves in defense. If you are determined to call them a people, then understand that their children are continuations: an apron extended; a field of hollows doubled out to the horizon until the notches written into their edges look like rain at a distance. Their dead are lost intentions: a comb that ends against a sudden blank; a set of channels that speak to a flow that has not yet arrived. Youth creaks in their joints not as clumsiness but as open parentheses—the uncompleted curves where tool-lines still hold like bright wires across the plan.

They read. The marks pecked into plaster—the scores and strokes that repeat over miles—are theirs and not theirs. A mason set them, yes, but the slope and the wind have been turning them into another kind of legible. Moisture has its own script: it finds the shallowest scars and carries a dim patina along them. Over decades the letters take on a gloss where wetness lingers and lose it where it does not. What lives here can be found in that gloss, in the way a line darkens at the measure of a fog that melted at noon fifty days in a row one year and at ten another. They share their news in micro-shifts: a keystone that has eased a hair into its neighbor and now takes the morning light like the lid of an eye.

Where a person expects life to show up in color, they show themselves in white. Their gardens are collections of decisions given form: coralettes where pacing works, frostwork where a lip spends more dawns damp than dry. No bloom is beautiful to them for its own sake; beauty is proof that arithmetic and weather met and shook hands. In the deeper galleries, their stalactites are still statements in the future tense, birthed in droplet-infancy; each drop hangs long enough to think.

They are not singular. There is not a king of the Plateau, no queen of the combs. There are, instead, offices:

- Catchers—lattices and veils that turn sky into dripping arithmetic. They herd hesitation. They put fog to bed.

- Selectors—fields of V-chutes and bosses that break a sheet into tributaries with a bias you could feel if you dragged your nail lightly across them.

- Slowkeepers—those pores and bead-weirs that hold a drop a breath or ten before releasing it, teaching time to water without scolding.

- Joiners—beveled lips that gather the scattered back to a single bright; the places where a song almost starts.

- Scribes—the grooves that have been recording humidity and angle in crust and shine since the first morning; the dendrites on tile-faces that look like ferns only from far away and like calendars from near.

- Stabilists—the arches that manage weight by loving it; the pins that green and hold, the bitumen seams that were pressed with thumb and will outlast ten winters of freeze.

You cannot meet one, only meet the function that is briefly most itself where you stand. If you name them, you will be misled into thinking they could answer. What they can do is continue. They continue in a day-bright field where light relates to stone at angles set to dry just as the thin air decides to sweat. They continue in the evening, when the plateau smokes its wet into the firmament and the little bells of tuned shelves flicker once, if at all, to memorialize the exactness of that moment.

If there were makers they are gone, but their way of looking has remained and joined the weather. That gaze lives here: the habit of checking plumb against sun, of squinting down a runnel to see the faint hitch where a bead might stumble. It peers out of the trial faces left by masons on a wall’s edge, those little planes where curvature was argued into being by hand and light. It stands in the expectancy of pipes that hold only clarity and wait for quantity. It understands the economy that is also an ecology: that a drop uncounted is a sentence unwritten.

The smell here—the clean metallic wash of rinsed mineral—is their perfume. The language here—the clocky patience, the geened copper seams, the chalk that grows and peels—is their conversation. Their intimacy is surface. Their generosity is reach: every yard of stone speaks an invitation to water, calibrated to minute and wind.

If you must have a face, take a bright one: a plane that has learned sunlight so precisely it can throw it back without glare and with just enough warmth to coax a weeping. Look down and let your breath fog your reflection on a white tile; watch it break itself at the first array of bosses into separate steams that head without hesitation to where they have been told. In that obedience there’s a life that knows itself entirely as obedience, and yet within it a dancing: the way a film buzzes on a serration, the way a bead quivers and mends and falls.

This is who lives here: the assignment of water, the stewardship of angle, the intention that learned to survive by becoming all planes and no body. They are weather made local and tool made endless. They occupy a bright silence the way moss occupies a stone elsewhere: by patience and by growth so slow your eye alone will not catch it, but your hand—the back of it, held an inch above a chilled groove at dawn—will feel the faint lift of their work as surely as a pulse.

They have made a promise and embody it: that sky is the only spring, that every drop is accounted for, that light is an instrument, and that in a place where nothing living pushes back, life can be entirely an art of tending place itself.
