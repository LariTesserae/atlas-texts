What lives here has learned to be small in the right ways and large in the right ones. Every inhabitant, from the size of a dust mote to the span of a hawk’s two hands of air, has signed the same understanding: spend quietly, save well, and keep to the rhythm of the place.

The first citizens are made of water with a mind for seams. They are the films that find quartz and make it glitter, the skins that slide over stone and dimple where a grain sits higher than its neighbors. Algae like breath given a body, colorless and sure-footed, sheet themselves along the weeping walls. In their clarity they carry worlds: fleets of micro-crustaceans rowing between sips of light, bacteria that taste metal out of the water and lay it down again, atom by atom, as if making the thinnest of coins that dissolve when the charge changes. At this scale, a drop is an ocean and the edge is a continent; eggs hang in gel that ignores the pull of down because, here, gravity negotiates.

Above that, threads live. Fungi run the switchboard, not in a metaphor but in the way they push and pull. Hyphae braid themselves into mats you could sleep on, and sometimes someone does. The mats taste the plateau the way a tongue tastes a mouth—where the sour gathers, where the sweet sits under the molar of a ridge, where a cut is still raw and needs sealing. They shuttle what is needed by opening and closing the smallest doors, signaling not with light or voice but with a slow slide of ions and a quicker jolt, here and there, when the air’s halo takes the time to land. If there is a memory here that persists longer than peat, it lives in this network: a habit the loam cannot forget, a recipe for a dry hour that has not come in centuries and might tomorrow.

Plants are the machines and the music. The rosettes are not individuals so much as neighborhoods, each tower of felted spears a block of rooms with gutters for hallways. They drink the air and feed a civics of insects and frogs and birds, and for this you could call them cities and not be wrong. Bromeliads hold pools in the cups of their leaves like little municipal baths, and in them live shrimp and larvae and sometimes a bird that sits and eats with the satisfaction of someone in hot water while it rains. The pitchers are different—instrument and factory both—brewing a syrup that smells like payday and counts each winged visitor in a ledger that no one but the plant can read. Sundews bejewel themselves not for show but for economy. They are jeweler and fence and cashier, taking payment in gnats.

Animals are more ghost than gong. The hummingbirds here are weightlifters in furs of air, dark and dense, with hearts like small clenched fists that open only when the orchid forgives them. They hold themselves in place with the sound of a breath you were about to take, then don’t. The weasel-thing is a drawn line with intent, black as the truth of a camera’s pupil, moving so precisely over cushions and ribs that you understand the plateau keeps a ledger of bruises and does not like new entries. Small deer, hoofed like thumbprints and clever as rain, leave nothing but the memory of their heat in the peat. Snakes wear scales not like armor but like prisms, and the water cannot find a reason to stick to them; they move through the place as if always newly made. Frogs are watchmakers. They talk in ticks and clicks, setting the minute hand with each glass syllable and never, ever ringing an hour.

At the edges, spiders throw bridges from breath. That is how it looks: a line straight into cloud, then another, then a walkway that would blow down if the fog were not wind enough for it to hold. Webs here do not glitter; the air is greedy and takes the shine, keeping the silk dull and safe. There are swallows with lungs deep as valleys that face the white rivers all day and pluck whatever is brave or foolish from the fall. Their nests hang like punctuation on the script the cliffs rehearse, commas in a sentence a mile long that the stone keeps reciting because it knows no other way to speak.

There is a people, and they are not plants and not quite animals in a way anyone from dry country would recognize. It helps to name them several times before you see them well. Call them the Wickfolk, because they prosper at the line where water chooses. Call them the Quiet, because their alphabet is made of gestures that do not travel. Call them the Tenders—of rosettes, of pipes, of charges so weak you feel them only when your arm hairs stand with no breeze to blame. They call themselves, in their own language of palatal clicks and breath held just so, something that tastes in your mouth like the metal after a spark and the tea of lichen: it means Those-Who-Spend-After-Saving.

They are small to your first glance and then not small, the way a handful of moss is a hand’s worth until you part it and find a cathedral. From crown to foot they are built to keep from breaking what they stand on. Their feet splay and articulate, each toe a separate compromise with cushion and rosette rib, spreading weight so tenderly that the plants rebound when they pass. They do not wear shoes. The soles of their feet are rough as felt and slick as an eel, ridged in microscopic hairs that drink out of fog and dry instantly when laid against a dry stone—that vanishing hour at noon when stone can be briefly itself. Their legs aren’t for jumping but for steps that measure. They move in sequences: touch, settle, lift; touch, settle, lift; you could not love a metronome more.

Their skin is not exactly skin. It is a cohabitation. Lichen makes its living here and there, silvered the way the trees are, woven into the shallow dermis like lace worked long ago into cloth that forgot it was made of more than one thing. Fungi thread their wrists like bangles under flesh, harmless and helpful, keeping salt where it belongs, reporting on the mood of the air. They drink a little with their skin and breathe a little with it; their chests move, yes, but their breath seems friendlier to the weather than to blood alone. Hair grows fine and in excellent order from the backs of their arms and their scalps, hairs that stand when the halos come out, hairs with a habit of condensing, little bead lines of their own that run to channels at their necks and into tiny bladders set under the collarbone. It looks like ornament until you hear the faint glad sound those bladders make when poured into a bromeliad.

They smell like tannin and good rot and a sharpness that is not unpleasant. Their mouths taste what the plateau is doing. They can tell a day’s pressure by letting a drop sit on the tongue and feeling whether the edges of the droplet pull harder than the middle. They sort metals the way a bee sorts flowers—one ion, one taste. They do not eat much meat; they love the syrup of the pitcher fields, skimming it with careful blades of quartz thicker than a fingernail and smearing it on leaves to dry until it becomes something like a candy that cracks quietly. They crush sedge and fern and rosette heart into pastes that ferment at just the pace the plateau keeps, not faster, never faster. They chew long and share.

They have no word for fire that is not a joke. Heat is a property of noon stone and full bellies and bodies aidable by friction, nothing you set and feed. They cook by acid and time and by the mild electricity of the place. They lay their foods on racks of bristle leaves that halo slightly at dusk and swear the charge does something to the taste, softening bitterness and setting sweet like a plank. They cure fibers on those same racks, and in the morning the threads are a little stronger than they were, a proof of charge the way paper lifted to the comb is a proof of static. For adhesives they take what the plateau gives—gums from lacerated bromeliad and saps that weep from bruised shrub—and for bright they use the black of beetle shells. They line their shelters with those elytra, not for show but to know themselves: the plates throw back a perfect fog-dimmed face of whoever looks, and this pleases them for reasons they could not explain to you. Mirrors here feel like company.

They build. Not houses against weather but houses with it. The roofs are made from rosette leaves neither cut nor stolen but invited: a spiral at the heart is coaxed long and long, never broken from its base, and then weighted in a way that tells the plant it is now a lintel. The plant is paid with hand-poured water in the droughts that are just afternoons by another name. Walls are moss thick on trunk frames persuaded down to height and then kept there with belts of woven sedge and old spider draglines. You could miss a home by standing inside it. If you found your boot on a mat that refused to let you sink for once and wondered why, someone would smile in the fog and gesture that you were in their doorway.

Their art is water in the act of deciding. They arrange droplets in arithmetic progressions up the rib of a leaf and invite children to name the series. They set strings of dew to fall one by one into a shallow pitcher, making the tiniest of songs—a scale as responsible and measured as their walking. They coax the horsetail-stems that chitter in wind into frames and then breathe across them, making a glass-hum melody you can hear in your teeth. They draw with lichens in colors that would be gray anywhere but here: one is the gray of the first noon after storms, one the gray of old quartz sugar, one the gray of a frog’s belly when it is sleeping.

They keep time by what the cliff pipes tell them. There is a stone on the edge of their settlement, and in it three narrow cuts run. In the morning this stone sweats cold through the slits, and a hand laid there meets the day; by afternoon it is warmer and the slits exhale. Old ones lean and count, eyes closed, reciting small histories of the plateau’s breath. Marriages are made at the evening turn when the tide of vapor goes down again, not because it is romantic but because it is regular; if you tie lives then, they cannot claim they did not know.

They cross the worst places the way the spiders do, and in league with them. Silk is a crop as much as sedge here, not taken but asked for with meals left in damp creases, and lines are laid on fog by pairs: spider and person, side by side, the line going true as if it were pegged to a thought. They do not like the white rivers—no one does who keeps savings—but sometimes they must step through them, and for those times they carry shells of bromeliad leaves, deep cups cut from plants that had already given themselves to rot, and those cups turn businesses of air into something their lungs understand for three breaths. That is all one ever needs to cross if one has measured properly.

Their language is not loud. It is palatal and dental and full of breath that is used and then saved. Words for water here outnumber words for stone: vapor in hand, vapor moving, vapor that will stay if you ask right, bead caught and fought for, trickle that endures, trickle that can be safely stolen. They do not say please to each other; they say with or among. They have many words for tired, and the kindest is for a day when the edge pipes ran hot early and never let go.

They have domesticated very little by other places’ standards. Ants agree to move their routes when asked with damp offerings; salamanders tolerate being carried like living flasks under someone’s shirt; hummingbirds cannot be told anything but will return to a certain person if that person’s breath pleases an orchid in a particular way. The weasel-thing sleeps in their beds for months until it is bored and leaves and comes back when the frogs are especially rich and the evenings long. Children are taught to bristle before lightning, and if their arm hairs do not rise when the rim flowers blue, elders lay a palm to the child’s forearm and remind the body how.

They believe in edges the way sailors do, not as endings but as a kind of weather with duties. They send their dead into cushion domes at the turn of night when the halos are most talkative. Bodies are not buried; they are placented into moss, a slow sinking that becomes food and then work and then the green of a mat that will one day carry a house. They count this, not numerically, but by the way a certain cushion feels underfoot twenty years from now: springier by the weight of a person’s life. Their mourning is not silence—they already have that—but a deliberate making of small noises: a drop dripped, a bead flicked from leaf to leaf, a passage hummed on horsetails. Grief here is managed by practicing sound.

Not everyone in this people is a stay. There are those who go to the very edge and live there, not cliff-dwellers exactly but edge-keepers. They watch the white falls for changes in the line of their descent, the way a seamstress reads drape in a dress. They collect skins of algae and read the year by the taste of them—the metal rising or the calcium dropping, a story of far thunderheads and odd droughts in plains they will never see. They send news back with spider lines, carrying a charge so slight it could be a rumor: pull this string and the silk hums; pull another and it does not. You could map the whole plateau by the way word travels: not straight, never straight, but exact.

If they have enemies, they are mostly mistakes. A boot too hard on a cushion. A cup drunk in relief from a pool meant for something else. Once, a long time ago by the cliff’s reckoning and last evening by the people’s, there was a lightning that meant to be a burn and was not—it tried to dig the rhythm out of the fungi and put a new one in. The switchboard hummed louder for three days, then corrected itself. They tell that story to children when they put them to sleep: what is ours is what recovers. The plateau, they say, is a being with a gait, and we match it, and that is what it means to live, to match it as long as we can.

At night, when the fog allows the sky and the sky remembers its job, they lie on their backs on roofs that are leaves while water comes back out of them into the air by degrees. You can see them if you stand on the edge and look, small as beetles and busy as thoughts. They point out other plates in the distance and do not call them countries. They call them other breaths. The stars are too bright for here and do not last long; someone somewhere will need them and the fog will return to keep them. In the meantime the frogs wind their watches, the cliff inhales, the rosettes pay out rain they made themselves, and whoever lives here—algae, hyphae, beetle, bird, wickfolk, wind—goes on in the knowledge that to be alive in such a place is to be a part that never quite hardens, always in the act of becoming soft and useful.

There are also things no one sees except with their hands. Under each cushion, air moves and has a pattern as real as a woven mat. The people put their palms there and learn it as children, the pattern of this cushion versus that one, and they can tell by touch whether someone has passed recently and how heavy the someone was and whether they were happy or hurrying. In this way you know your neighbors. In this way you know the deer. In this way you know the weasel, who is never unhappy and always hurrying.

If you are a stranger, something will watch you. Not a person, likely; they are busy and will let you walk yourself tired before they present themselves. The watcher is the place itself, in the style of a net you will not feel and a thousand soft decisions you will not notice. The rosettes will give you edges of leaves just broad enough to be footholds if you walk correctly. The frogs will call sooner where the ground will support you. The cliff will breathe warmer where you can cross and colder where you cannot. To live here is to be included in a choreography so slow you think it is chance until you stay long enough.

And there are the edges of the edges. Creatures that spend most of their lives in the white rivers of air: small bats with fur that condenses not water but charge, sparking their own halos as they hunt, extracting taste from moths by reading their fields. Beetles that never land except at birth and at death, riding up and down in their mirrored armor like black beads on the rosary of the falls. Behind the silence of the white is a city that screams—quietly, quietly—into the fabric of the downpour, communications no lung can hear and every hair respects.

If you ask anyone here what else lives with them, they will laugh as softly as the place allows. There is no else. To the plateau everything is a way of being water and waiting for the right moment to be something else—sugar, tissue, song, muscle, a halo in fog that hisses when you touch it. The plateau has a compact with the sky and its people have one with the plateau. Between those compacts everything that lives—every threaded root, every mirrored beetle, every salt-loving bacterium, every hummingbird’s held breath, every person with lichen in their skin and a cup of stored evening under the collarbone—finds a way to be themselves that is also a way of keeping the place itself. And that is who lives here. Not a list, not a census, but a continuity. You stand there, damp to the bone, and you belong if you can learn to be quiet and count the beats of a rock that breathes.
