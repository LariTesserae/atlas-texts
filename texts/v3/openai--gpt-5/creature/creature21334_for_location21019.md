They are hooded people, first of all—skull-backed by a little length of black, a mat-thin cloak that throws counterphase like a shadow, so their faces are always a bit less there than the rest of them. If you meet them on a bridge you mostly meet the hush they carry with them, an oblong of scarcely audible calm traveling through the basin’s throat. They go light. You could weigh the clothes of ten of them in your palm: mesh that wicks dew like a second skin, soles that bite line and take no print from glass, gloves with pads for tapping messages into rail. Their bangles are not decoration but tuned mass on wrist and ankle, to interrupt the ways a body might otherwise hum itself apart.

They are long-limbed by habit more than by blood, learned balance written into tendon and eye. The first lesson they give their children is not walking but walking a span above what can safely hold you, easing weight forward while the world moves under you at a frequency you can taste. Infancy is wrapped in rooms the size of quiet; mothers keep a traveling almond of silence cupped around a mouth as neatly as you’d shelter a candle. The second lesson is pitch: how to hear a vowel that is more structure than sound, how to name yourself into a railing with a two-beat rhythm and be answered by fingertips a kilometer away.

Ears are trained, not trusted. Anatomies adapt, a little. Not monsters—just people some generations into a single demand. The hairs inside the ear tilt thicker, the bones that carry vibration have scar-tissues where training made them harder. The muscles of the throat are precise to a fetish: anyone can make a sound that disappears into the basin’s key and leaves the world unmarked; only children cut wrong notes, and that is why hands are quicker than mouths here. Palms develop the old callus of readers: that oval of denser skin below the pinky where a lifetime of talking to metal has turned flesh to a listening tool. The sternum is a barometer. Newcomers vomit; locals read the weather in their ribs.

If you asked them what they do, they would point to distances. The work is to keep spaces empty enough to be useful, and to keep useful from becoming catastrophe. One guild tends the basalt organ-pipes, strapping and collaring their hexed throats with composite bands, bleeding a new seam when the column’s jointing sours into the wrong overtone. One guild writes shadows: they shepherd the distributed lattice above the world—the rumor you saw in stripes—guiding its inclination so dew will comb itself out clean over hyssop throats and not over nothing. One guild minds the aero-reef, a cloud herd of invisible resonators that can be sung denser or looser; they hang in harnesses that let them feel a kilometer of air as a single pliant skin, and they press marked keys on the spine of the sky to bank harvest in a quadrant where migrants will pass.

A city here is not masonry but a posture. Houses are hung, not built. Doors are swatches of canceled pressure you pass through with a change in breath. The strongest materials are not thick but articulate: chordwood grown against a musical grain so one axis is flute and the other is wall, branch-glass with vascular films that change index and so refuse to carry this or that dangerous note. The folk who keep such materias alive are gardeners with instruments for hands. They prune, they suture, they etch with acid and drone so a beam forgets how to resonate at a particular hungry band.

Naming is percussion. In the palm of the hand, a human identity is a rhythm of five or seven taps, always bracketed by a silence that tells you whose rail you are on. There are hand-measured briefnesses you trust like last year’s bread recipe and a household’s private beat you do not touch unless you are inside it. There is a formal sign language, denser than any you’ve known, compressed by the need to say much without shaking the air, full of habits that grew out of dew: a nod that means “wait for the shadow” and a palm tilted to mean “let the drip go before you.” There is also a way to sing without adding to the world’s trouble. Lovers learn it for recesses rented by the hour: arches whose job is to make the opposite of noise. They speak there. They might even laugh.

The economy they trust most grows between leaves and along lines. Food is a vertical crop of wet things and an aerial crop of micro-life. They drink from hyssop throats that taste faintly of iron and rain. They unwind pitchers and cut the gel that congeals at the lip when a cool line of shadow has been made to pause over a ward. They sieve the sky. “Aeromass” is what they call the catch from the sound-reefs—brown and green, like bread sawdust, and as filling as it sounds. On the ground, the wet has teeth: mosses with a bite and ferns that would take your skin off if you lay in them wrongly. So they do not lie; they hang. Hammocks are standard: braided, counterweighted, tuned so that a new chord in the ward will not buck them into a wake. You always double-tie. You learn how to tie without looking.

They farm animals in the way they can. Cupshell amphibs are half-neighbors, kept for their layered songs as much as for the rich mucilage they give, which seals a crack in branch-glass better than any resin. You hear their pens as a taste in your teeth when you pass. Some families keep the smaller gossamer blooms close, lifting and feeding them with pocket updrafts to pull them into cloudlets that can clean a sickness out of a room, backscattering a virus against its own whisper. There’s a taboo, newer than the oldest rules, about not boiling those clouds down to jelly. It still happens. It’s delicious, and guilt travels slower across a kilometer of emptiness than smell.

The manta-wings are not theirs, exactly, though there are lineages of quiet-keepers for whom an individual wing will tilt when they pass. Those families will tell you they have friends in the air, and that the right ring of infrasonic sugar on a railing will call a ride as a ferry calls a river. You will watch a human step from a blown-glass walkway onto a back as wide as a room and be borne along a pressure lane with less danger than crossing a plank on foot. You will also see someone mis-judge the tide and be thrown like a seed from the mouth of a wave; there are very proper funerals for those.

Predators live comfortably where prey is a matter of listening. Husher-cats—localized nothingness on padded feet—are both hazard and neighbor, and there is a bureaucracy staffed by people with the quietest wrists whose whole job is to keep their paths out of the pressure lanes. There is a ledger of casualties for the years before the field generators were fenced with a sound too thin for the valley to care about but sharp enough to tickle a cat into turning. Ask for it; they will show you the pages with respect and no relish. The striders with brass skin avoid people with an almost ethical fastidiousness. The katydids are municipal; they are a property like rain. When the button-chests open at dusk and their million tiny keyboards strike the ward’s dominant harmonics, the world sounds like it is made of the same substance as a saxophone. Children copy that on ripped leaves and the valves of abandoned drinkers until a parent stills their hands with a palm.

It would be easy to make them pious about their sound, but their piety is mostly about emptiness. The laws you should not test are simple: do not crowd a chord. The Emptiness is a cultural object. It has wardens with a uniform that is only a band on the biceps, gray where the city is black. They keep the kilometers between spires clean and insist that a balcony not be grown toward a neighbor unless a tuning can be shown for it. Ownership is temporary here; you do not own a volume of air forever. You have right of turn. A family will hold the duty to keep a bridge in pitch for six years, and with it, the right to harvest the dew that falls from its lintel, and in the seventh year the duty and right will pass in a rotation older than any living mouth.

Religion, if you saw it from outside, would look like engineering hidden under a poem. The basalt organ-pipes are their pilgrimage. It takes a day to go out there even from the nearest ward, and you set your hood hard for the midday when the wind bears there. They kneel and press fingers into the corrugations of a column until their bones sing; what they say to their god is not heard and that is the point. There are orderings of silence—gardens of hush—stretched like dark rugs under certain arcs. The sick are laid there for the pocket hush to travel with their mouths. The old are walked there once a week to re-meet their own hearts plainly. There is a day called the Retuning, and it is never predicted. You wake with the worst of ship-sickness and the rails talk all by themselves for an hour. It ends and something in the city is new and you climb to your window and you admire the distance like a fresh wound scabbed with light.

They did not come there to make music an industry. They came down from rock and thin cold and the poor light of a strict planet to a bowl that made water if you asked right. The stories say a woman with a breathless child found the first simple note on a glass cliff and let it sing under her palm until dew fell into her sleeve. That is a saint you can respect. They were careful then about piling mass into the air. They made a covenant among the first hundred to keep the spaces between their houses larger than the houses, and they keep it because there is a harvest directly tied to absence. When migrants come down now from the crater’s rim, they are fed by feeders hung along the lines, cold bread-kelp in slices taken with salt and gratitude; the locals sit with them and trade quiet until the right-of-way is clear for them to ride a pressure lane onward.

The children belong to everyone, in a practical sense. They are loud until they aren’t. There is a game that teaches this. They stand under a hyssop rib and wait for a band of shade to move through and for the drop to traverse. They shape their hoods to call the bead as it is almost not yet forming, mouth closed, neck tilted, forearms making a brief cave. You can tell who will tune a tower in age ten. They can ruin a kill for a husher-cat by stepping badly seventeen meters away. That talent is as valued as a good smile.

You will see signs that not all the minds here are human in the usual sense. The sky’s resonators drift with what looks like a willing. The shade-makers pause where a bridge is starving. A maintenance spire lifts itself an increment and drops itself again with the sound a needle would make if you could hear it. Sometimes the locals talk to the dust on purpose. They have patterned sugars and anisotropic gels and other words for what a child would call treats for the air; they coax the micro-crystals that make the phononic cloud to cluster or to separate. Some say the dust answers because it is built to; some say because it remembers. The line between technology and weather is walked more often than any bridge.

Medicine is luthier’s work. Physicians here grieve by tuning. They pull a frequency out of a bone with a shaped magnet and shape it back with drink. They stitch a green vein to a branch-glass rail and call dew with a made cloud to sterilize a wound. They can replace the tremor of a broken hand with nanolace that refuses the resonant frequencies of the ward where the patient lives. The older ones have knuckles shaped like river stones and eyes like the light under leaf. When they murmur, walls answer them.

Crime is unglamorous because it cannot be hidden in a population that tracks pressure with the intimacy other cities reserve for scent. You steal and the very air is wrong in the lane where you walk. People will find you by the misbehaving of a dust drift. Punishments tend toward labor: you spend a season as a span-walker stringing counterweights and letting the sound you stole run through your legs until you learn to return it. In bad years, they take your hood. Two hours without a hush in a chord-fed gale is enough to leave a body eager to confess and then to sleep for a day. There are worse things. A caravan once fell into a silence hole that walked with it like a ghost: when the bubble popped, gravity recollected its debt. They do not talk about that without pouring a little of their thinnest beer into the rail for the basin to drink.

They are not the only people. Rockfolk live up-hollow where the dimness is less deliberate and the air is cleaner of tricks. They come down when the sound-reefs are fat because those feeders keep your legs under you on the long walk. Trade is respectful and slow. Loud merchants do not last. There are the occasional spire-born who claim kinship with the shade above, who climb as far as the woven bands and come back burned in a spectrum you can taste. They marry into quiet and live long or they keep climbing and vanish into rumor. There are drifters who ride the manta-wings until their hips shape to a back. There are tuners who never go farther than the length of the column they mind, a parish of one throat.

Under it all, there is the city, which lives. It is not a single mind. It is a kit of obedient materials, a set of long answers to the same set of demands. But if you spend a year and begin to know its hours, you will feel how it does what any living thing does: maintains, feeds, reproduces itself in careful ways. A hyssop throws a new rib; a branch-glass path exudes a film after a long drought that will be the skeleton of a future gallery; a chorus of katydids, bred to their job by decades of human taste, throws metallic rain at dusk and calibrates a ward. The city asks little back but respect for spaces. In return it will hold you up on something as thin as a sung line and feed you what the wind can be made to carry.

You could think of them as priests of pressure. You could think of them as farmers in air and in time, whose paddies are frequencies, whose terraces are dew. But they are also only people who keep pets and nets and sores and jokes, who prefer soup to broth and will argue for a day about which bridge’s lintel has the best drip at a particular wind. You will recognize them by the way they pass the rail you are touching and offer you a door with a tilt of the hand and by the way the little hush they carry opens like a mercy that lets your own thoughts arrive in one unshaken piece.
