Who lives here are the kinds of beings that have learned to grow a life out of breath and runoff, to read weather from glass, to measure their days by the soft click of droplets reuniting. They are not only the citizens with lungs. The city counts its inhabitants in all that persists in its flows: bodies, but also swarms, guilds, habits, and things with taste enough to prefer one gradient over another.

People first, because you feel them long before you see them. They are humidity-adapted not by myth but by practice. Their skins shine even in honest daylight; not oil—never oil—but the patient sheen of proteins cultivated by their own resident biomes and rubbed in with vinegar that never saw a fruit. Their hair is braided to wick or to shed depending on the work. Their eyelashes lengthen as if they had learned to be reeds. Their feet have grown a culture of callus like cork that grips the always-damp composite, and their sandals are fluted, sluiced: footwear that counts on water being there. They move with their center of mass kept low, a shared instinct born of millions of small slips. To greet someone is to check your mutual hold; handshakes are a way to feel how a person drains and drinks.

They dress in textiles you could call cloth if the word were not so dry. Rag is a wrong word. These are nets woven of fungal filaments sparked with nanoscale ceramics; capillary cloths that taste the air and either drink or refuse; smart gauzes tuned to bead water at a pitch that sings when downdraft passes. Garments carry seams like gutters. Children wear bracelets and belts beaded with tubes of clean condensate and sometimes live water—shrimp, a quarter hour long-lived and bright—as a way to keep a currency of attention about them. You can tell the downdraft hour by how many wrists suddenly become bazaars.

Their speech makes use of vowels because vowels carry in humid air. Consonants are for close work, the words you mutter into someone’s ear because sound is a resource and echo is too easily spent. They whistle privately down staircase hollows to call their own. The old tongues that lived here before stone grew wet again are still present, but watered; the words for sand and dust persist as oaths that cut, because to wish dryness on a block is to curse it with death. Distance in their mouths is reckoned in driplines, not meters: “two sluices and a downtube,” “a weather front away.” They name their children for flows and fixtures and diligences: Lintel, Siphon, Quietule, Lowthrum, Sage of the Ninth Weir.

They keep house in vertical slices of the sponge, which means everyone has a relationship with a wall. The wall is alive; your tenancy includes a contract with its fungi. You tend them. You feed their little hunger for trace minerals and, in return, they knit your cracks when the mass-lift hums your ribs out of true. People keep jars of salts and dusts the way desert folk keep water. They dab a pinch on the seam where sweat welled last year, knowing that if the delicate stalactite grows evenly their lease will be renewed. There are apartments that line their lintels with those translucent stalactite fingers as status: look, we have lived long enough to cultivate this trickle.

Work looks like craft because even industry here must be done with a hand for flows. There are tower-keepers, gravity coaxers in little guilds with badges thin as a breath; they tune the sigh that turns cloud into edible water. There are field-singers hired to stand in lift-shafts and voice subsonic lines that smooth air through a block; they get paid in stipend and in the right to set their hammocks in the warm plenum over the engine rooms. There are vent-scribes who read the glossy alphabets the black fungus writes down the shafts and translate those living letters into repair schedules and market gossip; the fungus writes in cursive because strong blocky letters spore worse, and everyone has learned that lesson.

There are gutter-casters who throw nets for eels at quietule when the pipes slow, who can judge the density of coils by the temperature of their own knuckles on railings. There are lint-shepherds who comb the pale mats where roofs meet, gathering not food but raw light: they ferment those mats into pigments and luminous inks that drip sign in greenless shade at night. There are downdraft criers who walk the windways a minute before the front comes, shaking a string of bells made to ring at a frequency that water obeys; their job is part safety, part ceremony. On the mezzanine, skimmer-herders run tethered craft through the standing swirls, stitching the air with capillary drinking; each loop earns a trickle that fills a bladder that later feeds a sump. Caravaneers at the far flats do not go between places so much as accompany maintenance across a landscape of devices; they sell sleep, adjustments, and food to ring-tuners who keep the anchors holding weather to ground.

There are poets of noise legally employed by districts to sculpt the city’s voice so that no one block dominates; they look like buskers and act like biologists and are paid to keep the organ-tone healthy. There are jurists called flow-wrights who argue cases about who is to blame when a wall weeps wrong because somebody upstream changed a filter patch. Their courtrooms are wet on purpose so that the evidence can be witnessed as condensation; verdicts condense and fall out of air in a tray for the clerk to collect.

They are not alone, and they are not the majority if you count by bodies.

Machines live here as familiars rather than appliances. Mouth-bots graze the tile and leave it cleaner but never sterile; their waste is a regular commodity, pellets like wet clay rich in the sugars of a thousand microfilms. Children trade them. The bots have dialects, and anyone who works a block long enough can tell a local grazer by its click. There are long caterpillars of maintenance drones that sleep in the warm under-panels of bridges; at the hour before dawn when the underside rains loosen in curtains, you can watch them uncase and extend brushes and tongues that look like weeds, and the sound they make is a soft combing that fills you with a cheap happiness. Swarms of microdrones with whiskers like ciliate fringes patrol the harvesters’ ribs, encouraging droplets to coalesce here and not there. They are recognized legally as a populace with rights to pathing and quiet during their mating—don’t laugh; they breed by firmware in set periods, and those times are posted like civic holidays.

The animals—you think first of the obvious ones. Condensate-mice, slick as soap, wearing current as whisker-language; they nest where cable and runnel meet and steal the soft lichens of shoes. Roof-crabs with magnetite-gritted knees scrape at the iron films; they taste like electrical storms; street kitchens sell them under vinegar from the solvent towers, and the brave suck the claws and let their tongues buzz. Drift-swallows hang like commas at vents and begin their sentences when the engines shift; they are grammar for the air. Gutter eels are the city’s sinew; old women in rubber aprons comb them out of their rope-sleep and gossip with each other in steamy rectangles around troughs where they leave the skins to glisten like strips of night.

But these are not all. There are window periwinkles, a slow nation of shell that write tiny spirals of clean on glass; some people pot them to keep their panes readable, and small courts have ruled on disputes where a neighbor’s periwinkles migrated and ate someone’s signage. There are moiré moths that taste electricity and lay their shimmering eggs on fields wound for a lift; liftmen bless and curse them, because a few are good luck and a cloud is a shutdown. There are bats that have given up echo for pressure-taste; they flock by leaning on gradients and fall out of the sky partway through fruitless nights when the schedules have been wrong. Vendors catch them gently and bring them home to release at quietule.

At mid-heights there are sump-whales. Of course there are sump-whales. They came mammal because somebody loved the idea; they stayed because they find the scum that collects behind a blown weir as inexhaustible as a myth. They teach their calves the city by name; a keeper once told me that a calf will not pass through the throat that hums at 21 hertz for three days if her mother has not sung that note into her—or would not, before the ordinance made keepers of every basin. There is now a public holiday for this, and people come to watch great slick backs slip under arcades, which is a way of remembering how to take in a sight without drying it out by looking too long.

You find predator cultures in ledger-skin. The runners—fox-thin, ankled with tax chips and permission tags the way a sail is stitched—move through crowd-currents with a swimmer’s gift. The chips do not tame them; they make them members of markets that have learned to acknowledge the appetite that makes a city honest. A coffeehouse keeps one. It eats spoiled shrimp and rats the size of forearms and occasionally a glove of grubs fallen from a stall, and people know it by name. Its owner—ha, wrong pronoun; its accountant—argues for lower rates on its labor when the municipality raises the chip price; the city’s animal council speaks for it too.

There are also keepers of tiny republics. The children of the driplines who raise pink shrimp on downdraft banners are part of a lineage; their grandparents maybe grew mushrooms on the peelings of the market who grew stones for walls. They hold their tubes like bangles and flick their wrists to flash them green-blue-coral in the honest light. They prefer to tell you the shrimp will die before night; you buy them so that it will happen on your wrist instead of alone in the wind, and anyway some of the bracelets hold down-market octopi that are only copying shrimp and will go home with you and learn your faucet by nightfall. Nothing here is simple.

People live by schedules and by the bend of the block’s voice. Flood-cycle is a civic religion as much as an engineering constraint. You join a district by learning its cadence: the afternoon gust that you lean into without thinking; the timed quietule when the deep engines slow and ducts go feral with moth and bat; the false night when a cloud passes and thickens the air and the harvesters adjust their ribs and make a rain as thin as thought. A household is organized in rotas that resemble tides; meals are planned around downdrafts and lift hum. Lovers meet at the seam where the under-eave rain first loosens; they bring each other towels and eel-sweet vinegar breath.

There are taboos that look like engineering because they are engineering. Never block a drain with your body. Never open a door into the path of a scheduled downdraft. Never sing at the frequency the lift inherits from the bridge because a cable once sang itself loose and tied a block in a bow. Children learn to taste a wrong schedule in the metal on their lips when an alley’s gravity goes a little sideways—that taste is lemon-ozone, followed by the urge to step away into a lane with more honest pull. There are adult games in that alley, of course: you will always find youth courting injury where the rules skew. They use the skew to kiss upside down.

The city’s senses have citizens too. The wayfinding hum that rides just below hearing is not only for pedestrians but for tongues. There are tasters in every block paid to lick stones, literally, to make sure the hum’s flavor stays right; a drift upward in tin or a sudden copper means a fault. They ring bells. The bells do not sound loud; they are tuned to water and to the field that keeps the skyway from slumping. In the market, song sellers hawk contracts in long vowels because legal string smells sour when said in consonants here; you buy a clause the way you buy a bun, and there are inspectors of syllable-craft who stamp a line of carmine fungus on your receipt so it will read under a midnight drop.

Family is a word too clean for the arrangement. People belong to flows. You might be of the Third Weir, a house that tracks the way the weir froths and adjusts schedule by whisker. You might be of Ring 6, meaning you’ve got rights at the bridge cables and obligations at the skyway. There is a clan that claims every amphitheater sump by sending a niece into each to learn the eel’s sleep; they meet once every ten rain-cycles to carve their walls with little roped coils. There are flow adoptions—when you marry, it is called a confluence, and your names braid; if you divorce, your world bifurcates, and the law requires a period of high turbulence so the split won’t become a stagnation.

Death is not a burning and not a burial. No one wastes dryness. Bodies here return to the masonry. Whole precincts have their dead seated in the living wall, their bones chewed to calcium by the careful fungi that know how to knit load-bearing stone. There is comfort in this. A lintel holds because three great-grandparents are in the seam. Mourners gather under a persisting drip that remembers a name and touch their tongues to the salt that condenses on it at a frequency only that person’s wall could teach the air. You are not expected to understand until you have stood there.

At the edges live people who trail weather the way herdsmen once trailed grass. They chase the condensation pillars noted by a mathematics most of us forgot to learn. Their tents are ribs that weep. They wear their hair in coils like finials so that the wind around them can be read by an apprentice. They barter in adjustments—“three basis points less on your fog for the week”—and they keep animals that nobody else knows: wind-lice that grip taut lines and leap to steal airborne salt; little wrens that catch those lice; a breed of dog with hair that writes a curve when rubbed backwards in the presence of an out-of-tune field.

You might argue that the true inhabitants are the processes. The organ-tone that fills your chest is alive in the sense that it persists, reproduces, and adapts; there are keepers of it whose ears have grown bony ridges that couple better to low sound. The scheduled gust is a being as much as a bus: it has preferences born of design and inertia, and there are crews who pamper it the way one grooms a horse, oiling its intake and currying its louvers. The taboo on voids is enforced by motion itself. Let a corner go quiet and you will find within a day a new clade of fungus creeping in like dusk; maintenance will arrive not as police but as a choir with speaking fans that reanimate the air and oblige the creeper to join the proper economy.

If there is a wildness, it hides on the undersides where children go to test themselves. They hang over rails to feel the rain start as if a wall were clearing its throat and hold out cups to catch the first beads. They make games of guessing when a condenser will begin to cry. They bring back small lives cupped in their hands: a bead-seller snail the size of a thumb and ringed with jewels of its own cooking; a moth that speaks in sparks to the lip of a battery; a seahorse of the sumps fashioned from memory, tin, and patience that an elder will tell you used to be common when the seawind came more regular than now. The elders speak more slowly, salt-cured and careful, a little drier: it is the elders who remember a tree, and it is the children who do not miss it.

The city itself belongs on this list. It is not a metaphor to say it breathes. It has moods and fevers, wants and rituals. It raises a cloud on your exhale and will pour it back as a localized rain five streets away. It has appetites for equalization, for smoothness, for continuity; it trains its citizens to prefer these, too. It punishes stall and stasis with smell and silence and then with overwhelming maintenance. It rewards those who work with its flows with a kind of sweetness, the sweetness of getting an engine to keep moving with less, of persuading a droplet to choose you.

This is who lives here: human and not, animal and device, contract and chorus, all of them condensed out of air made thick by being breathed together for a very long time. They live by listening. They live by slope. They live in consortium with stones that drink and weep and grow back. They feed on what the city exudes and give back heat and motion and a million small decisions that keep the skin unbroken. When you leave, if you do, they go on being themselves, which is to say the weather goes on being its own kind of citizen, and the sounds keep measuring the blocks, and the drip keeps assigning names.
