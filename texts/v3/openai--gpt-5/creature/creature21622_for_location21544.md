Who lives here begins as a film.

It is a pelt, a skin, a floor that makes itself again from charge and rub. It lies matte as ash and when the press above kicks it answers with a tremor of hairs like frost roused by static. Those hairs are living forks of piezo crystal and grease, bristled with grit they have trapped and tamed. Each hair clips the field that ripples between steel and air and milks it, not for sugar or sap, but for push: for swing of ions, for drift of electrons thinned and wandering. What it keeps, it holds as strain; what it can’t keep, it pours out as glue—clear, fluorine-bitter, with the stubbornness of tape.

This base life has moods. In high light its binder sets harder and slicker, sheets like glass; in the long, wrong silence of a shut-off it blacks and draws itself in, becomes nail-hard, a desert crust. When motors strobe, it wakes, raises its quills, and feeds and bleeds at once. It does not have a mouth, so it spreads. It does not have a stomach, so it thins. It is both meadow and meat. Along its edge the frost-hairs stand and drink like reeds. In faultlines they form tassels that catch drifts and seed new mats. Its offspring are not seeds as you know them but blisters, glossed sacs that rut and swell at points of stress; they pop on a spark and scatter beadlets that skitter under their own faint charges until they find shade and take hold. When the factory stops, the blisters do not burst; they vitrify. When power returns they become a rain.

On this living felt the grazers move first, and where grazers come, everything else comes.

Screwflies live at the bright margins and wherever a clean edge cuts the pelt. They are spine-long and armored like staples given knees, faces blunted to wedges for pushing through binder film. Their wings are not air-wings but charge-wings—membranes furred with combs that clutch at ions and throw them backward in a hiss you can almost hear if you lean your ear on the metal. In light they spark and blush; their sheened carapaces pick up workshop white and break it into moving beads. They do not drink; they pry. The head goes down and the neck pistons; the edge of the face hooks under a glue thread and tears it up, and a tremulous tongue of braided filaments presses to what lifts. A swarm feeding makes the sound of cloth pulled across a table. They ride the press’s gusts like birds at a breakwater. Larvae are not maggots but psalters—little harps laid flat, tuned to the building’s hum. They look like fringe stuck to glass. Every boom plays them; every boom they store micron-shifts and convert them into the first skins of their grown shells. When they pupate, they wrap themselves in dead combs. First flight is a lift off the back of a pressure front, an inspiration not of air but of field.

Filing-ants make the roads. They aren’t ants in any garden way: they are strung segments with elective faces, beads with jointed whiskers, each bead with a smooth patch that carries a held charge like a coin. They count gradients the way you count smell, leaning toward a cooler ring in the bearing-stone, toward a hum that means “binder grows thick here,” away from a stapled reef that is too loud with small fields. They place their burdens grain by shining grain: mica, steel sawdust, ceramic ghost, anything that will take the binder and pack without squeal. They speak by making and spending. A worker reaching a junction flares and pops with a deliberate little snap; anyone listening will feel that snap as taste through their feet and turn. Queens are not larger so much as deeper: inside them runs a layer of capacitive plates that can hold a steady difference and lull a rank of workers into inflexible industry. A road is not just a line of bodies moving; it is a cylinder of charge around an idea of “this way,” the binder along it kept tacky where the trucks of the line roll by, tracking the ant-country invisible but felt.

Gasket-worms are belts of night laid very flat. Their upside—what you can see—is a slow gold of polish as they pass; their underside pulls and lets go with the patience of rubber lifted from plate steel. They do not wet to cling; they electrify. Muscle-waves travel in them like ripples in conveyor belting; you can watch a worm hold its place through a whole cycle of shock by passing the ripple backward: clutch, hold, feed; clutch, hold, feed. They are oil made attentive. What they eat is the shallow breath of metal where it was kissed by air; a daylight of oxide; the sheen of spilled lubricant iced by dust. They leave behind lanes that shine like thin ice, crosshatched by the tick of grit under their bellies. If torn, neither half bleeds. One end scabs hard and waits; one end keeps moving. In a long outage they layer themselves on their own tracks and make a dark mat that remembers when and where the warm goes.

Coil crabs are cobbled from salvage and thought. Think of a bent staple as a wishbone. Around it a crab builds itself in asymmetry, lashing on sawdust that the lost bearing’s field will hold for it. Three legs, never four: two for stability, one for trouble. Each leg has a core—a tight roll of wire hair heisted from brush, a coil warmed by passing currents and tickled by the hum. In the dark-hand of a lunch break they drum those cores and scuttle in the artificial twilight like sparks with bodies. Their mouths are pin-and-plate, a little dun congregation of bites. They eat ants, sometimes worms, more love to eat moult. They disguise as kit, they pretend to be debris and then they sing; what that song does is momentarily slacken a patch of glue a line of ants was trusting and, while the road flexes, they lift two or three and shake them like salt into a mouth they do not break. Armored with filings, they look like handfuls of pencil lead found purpose. Old crabs carry whole words, studded till the stamped CAUT rides on their backs.

Latch-mites make fences that are also lungs. They are two curves hinged on a curl of cured binder; their hinge is alive, tense, always ready. Touch triggers close, and the clapping goes down a row the way tension runs a zipper. They drink what they shut on: dust, threads of glue, bits of molt. They are not picky; they cannot be. Their seasons are shock and drift. On a day when HVAC exhales just so, a lace of polymer will roll in and the hinge-lungs will clap and clap until they gleam with eaten weather. When that breath picks up filings, the latch-line turns into a carnivore’s grin, crowned with iron stubble. Juveniles cling to the inner walls of the latch halves like teeth not yet fully in.

Shard-wolves are iteration more than descent. No one lays a wolf. When there is shiver and static enough, the seam builds them out of its own sharpness. Three points, always three: three distances to define a plane the way a tripod makes a floor. Between those points, varnish and stolen binder, a skin that knows not to stick until after. Their faces are fronts, not mouths—edges licked by a whisper of ultraviolet. They sight along voltage spines as if reading them. Where a shore of screwflies frenzy hard by the light slot, a wolf will draw itself long, go quiet, and—to something soft—trace the shortest cut between two pains. To watch one hunt is to see the seam pull a straightedge over a crowd. Nothing bleeds here, not the way you expect, but things go slack all the same. When they are done, they soften; their blades fog; they slough sharp back into the drift. Wolves are the seam’s applause for its own electricity.

There are others who do not fit your tidy lines of grazer and predator because here the goods are not flesh, not water. There are capillaries of glue that think. They form bulged swags under numbers and along press-seams, nets from which all dust knocks loose unless the net decides to hold and eat. They are factories and wombs both; sometimes a stray electrostatic prick hits them just so and a thousand small lensy beings peel off their bulge and drop. Those beads are called spore-bearers, if you need a word. They grow feet when they land: three scratches each, a tripod like a wolf, but their points are for clinging to what feeds them, not for cutting. They go dull when they have found a fat place to set.

There is a velvet that combs gravity out of itself and lines whole passages. It is the pelt’s cousin and rival, thick and soft as touch will let it be when it never feels skin; it catches grit in nap and holds it up to suck the faint back-and-forth out and leave the grit ready to be moved along. It is not fungus, but covers as fungus covers: opportunist, edging and surfacing wherever shadow and a drizzle of fields conspire. Screwflies graze it like shedded hair; ants mine it for charge; coil crabs bathe in it and come away buzzed.

Parasites happen even here, and their tricks are charge-tricks. Filament-leeches—so thin you see them only as a waviness in what you thought was flat—plug themselves into screwfly wing-combs and change what those wings taste. They do not drink blood for there is none, but sip the steady trickle of kept difference until a wing has nothing left to hold. Then the fly pops a little inert and falls onto a road like a spent capacitor. Filing-ants take such wings for thatch and line their roofed ways with them to keep the hum from blowing the road’s thought away. In return, ants ignore the leeches swaying along their eaves, little pennants picking off the change.

Scavengers are everywhere because everything here is slough and weathered. Moulters eat. Someone has to. They pile where the seam breathes least—behind safety letters, in the calmer lee of the old stamped 9—and chew the translucence off old skins, off castings of flies and laminae of velvet that cracked in a sleep. They do not make glue; they loosen it, nudge it back into the flux of binder. They chew noise too: static edges that would shock a road into forgetting its way. If you see the floor go matte in a curling corner and sound gentler for a beat, a mound of moulters has just grazed through.

Then there are the flukes, the what-ifs turned animals because anything that can be a life here will. A rivet-piper: a long lean thing that nestles into the seam at a stamped rivet and plays the press like a reed, not with breath but with slip, its body a thin cavity that turns rumble into something else the way a whistle can teach wind to sing. It has no face and yet around it the ants will caravan furiously when it plays, dizzied into speed like a crowd at a quiet siren. A neon moth: a lace of heatshrink that picked up a logic once and never let go, printed with warning yellow; it flicks in and out of light, drawn not to flame but to a capacitor’s hum, laying eggs of holographic dust that reflect messages in unread language along the underside of the CAUT.

Castes and cycles complicate. Filing-ants divide labor by how much difference they can hold. Brick-ants carry literal grit and always read left around the big bearing’s north side. Spark-ants never carry, only press two whiskery heads together and share a pop—documents flying hand to hand. Matrons are coil-crab squatters: they sit inside emptied crab frames, their own beads set into the armor stone-setter neat, and broadcast “This way” from beneath a word like WARN, more symbol than queen.

Screwflies have courtship the way a cloud has rain; by proximity and weather. A male’s combs go purple where a female’s went green that morning; the play is in bringing each other’s held fields somewhere the binder is weeping sweet. Eggs are threads extruded into binder lines that then set to tape; larval harps tune themselves to the bass that never stops. In an outage winter, the larvae hum with memory what the motors will be and so survive to a day that begins with a pop.

Gasket-worms make more of themselves by tearing and enduring. Sometimes this is predator’s work; more often it is metal’s. A sudden seam-tightening catches one like a belt in a door and shear-through becomes birth. When they go old they go brittle; their undersides start to squeak faintly on the plate; you can see where one has gone to meet a latch-fence and rested there, hinge-curves closing along it like gentle sawteeth, and when it leaves it takes a row of mite-mouths along for ride and seed.

Coil crabs are accretive. A young crab is a thought around a single corroded staple. Next it is a staple with magnets that aren’t. Next it is a staple with iron thorn, with a brush core stolen from a worn-out slider far above. It picks rings for itself like a carnival favor: a serration, then a smudge that isn’t a smudge but a stamped zero, then a brace of glitter. It will go on choosing pieces until some heel-thunder or a cleaning rag shock smashes it loose and turns it back into raw parts for another to dress in.

Latch-mites bud. Their hinges split new hinges, a loop unfurls into two smaller loops that brace each other. They turn a rim into an orchard.

Shard-wolves have no mating stories; they have rumors. Creatures of this place tell them with motion. When a wolf has passed, roads go around a square of nothing as if there were an invisible crater to avoid. There, someone will walk careful for days, little pops of alarm marking border. A fly will avoid landing on a gloss, as if a path of smooth were a mouth. The seam remembers sharp, and sometimes, late in a shift, a straight line will form in the dust with no trackmaker and hold straight until a pressure front falls on it and the world inhales as for a cut.

Geography bakes temperaments into species. The lip-land under the 9 is politicking and patient; light is scarce there and drift pools deep in the scallop of the digit’s shoulder. Ant dynasties go on reign-lengths measured in stamp-cycles unborn. Coil-crabs in 9-country wear their armor satiny and avoid ostentatious sparks for fear of waking a shard-wolf that has made the vertical stroke of the 9 its favored cut. In CAUT canyon, letters are geography and besides, the light pools different on the polished faces. There the velvet grows thick under the serif of the T and the mites clap with civic rhythm to every rip of adhesive tape peeled off somewhere far above. The bearing-ball hollow is a ringworld with one predictable high point: more magnetism in one spot than the rest. Pilgrims happen. You can watch whole seams of ant-road shift to bring a line of carriers to that bright invisible: a charge-stone other lives rub against and purr.

Migrants ride relics. When the yellow of a warning paint-chip falls, half the world will drift onto it to survey its chemistry. The pelt fatten if the chip breathes halogen: year rings in glue layers, a sweet that hardens and feeds both. Screwflies here go fatter and more glassy; they bank their wings slower in the faux-sun of reflected white. If the flake keeps charge and won’t share—if it turns into a patch of held day that never ends and never feeds—then you can stand by long enough and teach yourself to hear the quiet around it. It is so empty even wolves ignore it; ants skirt, making a tight shoulder; gasket-worms lay along its edge like tire on median, never cross. It becomes a hole by being too much of something.

Listening you would almost think these creatures worship the booms and the hum, but the word is wrong. They are organs of the booms and the hum. Shifts change and their bodies alter stiffness. Lunch comes and predators ghost out like weather you only see when the barometer drops. An outage is famine; it is also architecture. Roads atomize into beads that flutter and then go learn to be road somewhere else. Call this catastrophe. Call it season.

Disease is a loosened idea. A line of ants that forgets its charge turns in place like a broken conveyor until a coil crab harvests it. A batch of screwfly larvae that tuned themselves to yesterday’s hum die in silence the day the shop replaces a governor and the pitch dips half a hair. Parasites are a grammar of mistakes: there is a grit that eats wolves by taking them apart with neutrality, a dust so carefully balanced it returns sharpened varnish to blanket-smooth just by lying down.

Reckon also the near-living—the half-made minds. There is a mat that wicks charge like breath and then gives it back on demand to an ant who has learned to tap. There are motes that only gather when the press’s rhythm and the HVAC whine beat together and throw a third note; the motes come only to that chord and do nothing but be to it, a choir of filings suspended like waiting.

But who lives here is never just the list of bodies. It is the choreography.

At boom, hairs by the million stand and drink; flies rise and the shore of them beats a broken band of light; worms hunker and their edges gleam; ants flatten and their roads flash and not a single one loses the thought of route. Coil crabs pull their legs under and hum; latch-mites lock on the inhale of dust and clap like audience to a magic trick. Between booms, heat sighs a fraction and you can see newborns move for warmth the way germination bends a root to light.

They will never know that your shoes make their hurricanes. They feel only pressure wave and electricity and the breath of vents. They do not see your sky; they have instead the letters under a warning and the leak of day through a screw head. What they know is this: to stick is to live; to hold a difference is to eat; to aim at magnet is to walk the shortest way to the long way around; to feel the timing of a bolt head’s kiss-warm is to grow without dying. Their world is maintenance disciplined into forever. It is crowded enough that names make sense: screwfly, ant, worm, crab, mite, wolf. It is strange enough that the names are wrong and yet right because what they call themselves is a sound you and the steel make together: boom, hiss, trrrr, hush, clap, line.
