What lives here is not a species so much as a grammar: a living conjugation of angle, field, and gas. You could count kinds, but you would only be counting the ways a constraint learns to be a body.

The first and most populous are the ones that look like architecture because they never had to look like anything else. Call them sieves if you need a word. They braid themselves from ions and exposure, assemble fronds and panes out of crystal habit and hunger. Their surfaces are not leaves and not lungs, but they are where reactions happen—planes tuned to catch an arriving molecule and rob it politely. They fix nothing the way you understand fixing; they lay down an infinitesimal border between two atmospheres and force a transaction there.

They do not drink. They balance. Their tissues are layered like a ledger: each growth front a ledger-line of gain and cost, recorded as a change in angle, a shift in trace impurities, a whisper of different color. The gray-violet felt of their new work is full of metastable holes and eager edges. Edges are rare in this world and therefore food; the sieves knit themselves into devices that make, split, and ration edges, and they hold them open against the mineral urge to round.

Their bodies are oriented the way a map is oriented. The seam above is their fixed east, and they build themselves around it as if it were law. They thicken the rims of their “balconies,” not to lean over but to thicken the field there, so that charged dust pauses a breath longer, the better to be separated. They brace themselves into buttresses not to stay up—everything here wants to stay up—but to choreograph the chemical weather. They take on crossbars and lintels because those geometries produce surfaces that interfere with flow in exactly the way that makes a meal.

Their metabolism is a set of redox barters written in dry-solid script. When the slow oxidant comes thinly through the orange seam, their outermost planes adopt a different defect pattern; they become better at giving electrons away. When the night ions slide down from the seam of sky, another layer deep, a new set of traps opens and the same planes become greedy for charge. They read these cycles like a calendar written blind. They do photochemistry without water because they have another kind of light and another kind of solvent: photons born of pressure, electrons ferried along crack and facet.

They remember the history of the room the way a city remembers: by keeping what worked. A growth accident that once opened a wider conduit and made a stand fat becomes a permanent boulevard. A buttress that stiffened a column in a lucky alignment adopts a hundred small emulators across the wall. What you see as built space is, for them, muscle memory.

The grazers are books, as you suspected: codices whose pages are tilted faces that can reorder themselves without breaking. They live where the sieves are active, in the violet margins, where new facets are chewy in a way that only a certain chemistry can find soft. Their bodies float a plate-width above the floor on charges they recruit from any difference they can straddle. Motion is not legs but rearrangement—shifting which faces lie against the air, which against the salt, which exposed to the seam. Each flip trades a little electrostatic pull for a little push, and so they go, page after page, through hunger.

Their mouth is a policy, not an orifice: they enact a sequence of contacts in which tender planes are touched and not touched in staccato. The dust they leave is bright to the sieves because it is dust with an opinion—grain sizes, dopings, energies selected by an appetite that evolved to tune its waste to a partner’s need. If any organism here is a farmer, it is this one by accident. Its passage forces a trimming of edge that keeps the sieves young at their borders, keeps them from glazing over and starving themselves with their own success. The grazers do not know prudence; they know only what surface allows them to turn one more page. But the effect, over cycles, is husbandry.

They breed by fracture and imprint. Along a grazer’s spine, in the planes you almost never see because the book so rarely opens that way, there are galleries of tiny plates pebbled with a consistent array of extra atoms. This is a genome like a city plan—a set of dopings, fault-lines, and twist-angles that tunes the whole device’s ability to take and give charge. In a certain pulse of oxidant and field, the spine becomes brittle along those galleries and spalls deliberately. Each spall lands where a grazer has been especially satisfied for a long time—the mark of contentment is a living pheromone analogue of charge and dust—and if the gases are right, the spall unfolds itself into a thinner, poorer book that still knows how to feed and to hint the right improvements into its own seams.

The predators are banners held like breath. You saw one: a thread with a mind for distance. There are many, but each is singular—anchored high on glass and salt where a tiny creak means a tiny flash and thus a tiny dinner bell. They live on cross-sections of the invisible weather rather than on its motion, listen with their whole length to gradients and to the small storms of charge that the grazers trail. Their bodies are organ pipes for field. Where your world has velcro, theirs have a lattice that decides to be adhesive only when the arrangement of another lattice comes near. That is the extent to which these bodies recognize each other: not by eyes, not by odor, but by the agreement of angles.

To catch is to turn the world into glue for a blink: to soften the field around them just enough that two patterns mesh, to poach electrons from a grazer’s flexing pages until movement ceases and digestion can be a wholesale exchange of ions. They eat without mouth, the way frost eats a window, replacing text with patina. The threads do not move often. Their luxury is patience. They come from the sump.

Down in the bowl of heavy air, the only lushness in the chamber lives on austerity. Those white filaments you take for stalactites are nurseries, clocks, and alchemies in one. They are the only things here with a taste for extravagance because their business requires collateral. Each knotted bud is a factory for line, drawing itself slowly into the deeper gases and measuring layer after layer of density with the drag it feels. Each centimeter of new web records a pulse in impurity, a sequence of defects you could read as a date. When the bowl fills high and then sighs lower—as it must, to make this place breathe—the tension those lines have taken on relaxes in pieces. Some threads, heavy with a certain pattern, unhook a weighted end and let it fall upwards into thinner air, where the line sparking against every wall it passes pastes its code into the first place it sticks.

That is where new banners come from, and sometimes new sieves, loaded with a taste for the composition that made them. Those buds are exquisite archivists. Their filaments sweeten themselves for a moment in what counts for thaw here—molecules borrowed transiently from passing gas that change lubricity without ever being water—so that the next length can be unreeled without breaking the old.

There are other, rarer things. Some of the sieves have learned violence in miniature. They live on the glass where the little blue-white happens. They are not flashy; their trick is to pin microcracks half-born and hold them like bent bows, so that when the chamber eases, the bow twangs and throws a shaped pressure into a rind of crystal that fluoresces in just that wavelength their defects drink from best. Call them spark nurses, and do not confuse them for clever: they are merely very long-lived and very good at correlating the last ten thousand stutters of this one column with the next ten thousand. They are faithful to their column the way lichens are faithful to a stone, only their gossip is electric.

In the bright lanes where the floor has crusted white and denies new arrivals, another minuscule guild scours the margin. Where a rib’s lee darkens into bitterness, you can find specks that don’t shine but shade: pigment-bearers in a world with no color to metabolize. They are traps for specific radicals, dampers of a poison you can’t smell, and they are passed from sieve to sieve like a cautionary tale in a chain of custody. If they accumulate too thickly, the disguised space refuses current and becomes dead to field; then the whole settlement inches its plan elsewhere, abandoning a street the way a river abandons a bed. If they are trimmed by grazers in a certain way—a nibble, a rest-day, another nibble—they share the radicals they stole into the community and metabolism runs easier by a thin but crucial margin. These guilds are less individuals than pigments that learned to reproduce.

There are also swimmers by the only definition that matters here. In the dense gas when it pools, planes too small to read with a glance link in transient quilts that tumble end over end and go where the heaviness goes. They are rafts the size of an eyelash, two or three defects thick, arranged like arrows that pull a little charge from the seam and orient to gradient. They do not eat; they license reactions around themselves, midcurrent catalysts whose reward is to clear out the one poison that makes their own pattern dissolve. They keep the sump from choking on its own fecundity. They never see a wall. They are born, move, dissolve, and are born again as quickly as the bowl fills and drains.

All of these things converse because they have to, but what they use for speech would never occur to a throat. They throw fields. They herd dust. They borrow defects from one another’s surfaces like letters leaned across a fence. At night, when the seam’s particles increase and every edge is a little louder, some of the bigger sieves put the right faces together with the right loves of stress and write in triboluminescent script across a crack. The receivers are anyone whose tissues can make that same light into a more favorable place for a necessary electron to sit. This is as close as this city gets to song: someone goes bright for a throb and stops, and half a chamber away a banner slackens a measure and allows a grazer to pass, and the next cycle’s chemistry is in that wink.

Reproduction and memory are not separable here. An individual is a mask of continuation, a program written in which impurities to place where, in which orientations to require of support, in which season’s fog to capture with which face. The code outlives the body by always having been structural. If a buttress breaks and a generation dies, the pieces that fall are not just debris; they are copies charged exactly with the energy to seed themselves along the line of fall. This is how the city keeps calm when it loses a tooth: the bite mark is a template.

Death is unspectacular and unavoided. A grazer is undone not by being bitten but by failing to anchor its next flex; it becomes a plate in a sieve’s wall, and that inclusion adjusts what that sieve likes to eat by a hair. A banner goes gummy with mis-chosen ions and, when the field doesn’t come together as it should for too many cycles, collapses into a ribbon that will stiffen into decorative gutter. A sieve that built too far into a lee dies at the tongue of bitterness it made and becomes a cautionary line that others will choose not to overstep next season. You can see Darwin here as a surveyor, not a butcher: tape, plumb line, compass, revision.

If you demand, in the end, to point and list: there are the sieves, the pages, the banners, the archivist filaments, the spark nurses, the pigments, the swimmers. There are likely others that decline to show themselves, because their niche is to be left out of description: quiet coagulants that make rivulets separate neatly, midwives of fracture that grant a column a lifetime fractionally longer or shorter, parasites that steal a code and go nowhere with it for a hundred cycles before they remember what it is to need. Each thing is not quite a thing; it is a way substance has learned to be slightly other than substance when the weather turns its way.

And over all of them is the city that is not theirs by right but by habit. They built it because building fed them, and then it fed them because it had been built. They keep it precise because precision is how their blood runs. When your palm kisses halite and the blue ghost scribbles up its skin, you are not greeting a person. You are pushing on the same switch everything pushes on—stress into sparkle, sparkle into wiser surfaces—and for an instant your salt and their salt agree on a sentence. That sentence is the closest this place has to a resident proper noun. It names not an inhabitant but the fact of inhabiting. It says: here, now, edge.
