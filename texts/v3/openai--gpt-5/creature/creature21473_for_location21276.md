The first and largest thing that lives here is not a thing with a face. It is the lattice itself: the world-plant, the Weave, the Skins, the Chord—locals have many words, each meaning a slightly different behavior of the same body. Think of it as a colony that forgot it was made of parts and decided to be architecture instead. Every anchor, every span, every translucent scoop is the plant in one of its modes; every bristling hair and bulbous node is an organ, daily self-tuned to wind.

It hums because it must. Sound is the shape it holds itself in. Inside its ribs it keeps a pressure, not of air but of sap, and the sap obeys music. There are valves that only open on a certain note, channels that only take flow when a gust runs at a certain angle. If wind is scarce, the plant slims itself: membranes furl to narrow throats, and the whole register creeps upward—a taut sadness. If wind is plenty, it fattens to a deeper, sleepier bell. It is not sentient in the way something with eyes might be, but it senses. Frost along one rim, and it brightens the other; a storm shears an anchor, and within hours new tension members begin to thicken along an alternate route, a detour made of living cable. “It hears with its skin,” people say, by which they mean: it reads the world through stress and folding.

Many small lives make a city inside it. In the nodes live the bankers: bacteria and archaea that eat the sugars the plant presses from moving air. They pay back in two currencies. One is nitrogen, their waste turned to feed the next span or the next bloom of hairlike collectors. The other is a dim, steady shine. Not enough to see by, unless you’ve starved your eyes a long time, but enough for a vein to glow like dusk when your hand passes over it. People call those microbes Candlebreath and praise them during long darks: you thank what keeps you from having to be brave every step.

On the undersides of membranes, dew-lickers groom the world. They look like nothing in particular until they move—a smear of legs, a white comma flexing into a hook and unhooking. Their bodies are mostly combs: rows of microscopic teeth that drag beads of condensed water along wax to the runnels. They make a living by skimming a fraction off, each tiny mouth a tithe collector. Where they gather thickly, the sound dampens, and people will brush them aside with a tuft of filament if a tone needs freeing. They don’t mind; they like the motion of being brushed, a ripple of pleasure that passes through a thousand at once.

Between anchors, where spans cross, colonies of chord-feeders raise scaffolds of their own: stiff as glass, threadlike spiders that are not quite spiders stretch silica out of the trace grit they find in fog, setting it along stress lines like stitches, strengthening the plant where it thins. They build in patterns that are also scores, a geometry you can pluck; children learn to thumb those lattices and “read” the health of a span from the buzz in their skin.

Up where air knives itself thin and stars sit like grit on glass, there are sails with stomachs. Grazer-flocks slip the void in congregations, their patagia bright with vein and little veins, their feet clever as hands. Up close they are all cartilage and patience. Their mouths are flat scrapers with a fine serrate fringe; they seek nightly mats of extruded filament-weed and the wax-bloom that skins grow at their lips. They feed by shaving, never biting deep, never breaking a membrane unless they fail to listen—and they rarely fail, because hearing is how they know where to go. Their calls are in the high register, a chain of hehes made to be caught and returned by the world. They aim for the quiet-wash between two big notes and let themselves be carried, turning wrist and ankle sails so that the place’s own chord pushes them into food.

In their bellies live yeasts tuned to cold and bouncing. The grazers are green the way milk can be green: through what eats inside them. They make their own heat by processing the plant’s sugars on a move, like running fires banked in flesh. When a flock rests, it does so slung under a span, every individual laced into the next by tails and toes, a scarf of living sail, their combined breathing nudging a line down half a note. People like to sleep near them sometimes, for the warmth and the kin sound; a few communities keep a small domestic band, not quite pets, more like neighbors paid in salt and shelter.

Where something grazes, something else takes cut. Scythes do their work under everything. Seen suddenly they look like blades falling forever. Seen at rest, they are all mechanism: a double hand of a jaw under a long, humming face, hindfeet that are also hooks, a tail that is also a sword-guard. They do not tire of hanging upside down. When they drop, they do it into a map made of pitch. Each head holds two cavities frilled with membranes; the air writes itself there as it goes by. The Scythe tastes the pattern and knows where the grazers must go to surf it. It aims not for bodies but for the moment a call line folds. The break you hear—the silence for a heartbeat—is the Scythe cutting the note and the herd at once.

They are not cruel; they are accurate. They eat wax and sweetness and meat without discrimination. Inside them, cut things become a single warm paste that feeds an engine of nerve. Their young practice on the calls of their elders, learning to throw a pocket of quiet through a vibrating rope just enough to make a novice grazer flinch the wrong way. People resent them the way sailors resent reefs: you work around them or you die on them, and you use their bones when you can for something useful. Scythe-scapula knives take and hold an edge like frozen thunder.

Everywhere there are scavengers. Resin-biters, white and tidy, carry beads in their front mouths and polish the world into rooms. They live long lives and add to their houses every week. Their cities look like shells cut open: nacre-smooth galleries, ribs of amber, sparkles trapped like tears in glaze. They keep more than themselves warm. A good resin-biter clutch can raise a node’s air by the smallest but most merciful of degrees, just enough that the frost slides a little later and the child sleeping under woven cord can keep their fingers. There are fly-things too, but slow, adrift on minuscule sails, eating what the air offers. There are parasites as delicate as dust and as precise as math that live only in the fringe of the membrane hair and ride it from youth to age, their lifetimes counting the slow thickenings of wax.

Then there are the people.

They are little because little is what the place permits. Bigger and they would drink too much breath, weigh too hard on spans, draw the eye of a Scythe as a plume draws lightning. Little and strong is what they are: cords braided under skin, a short torso with room enough for lung and gut but no more, forelimbs and hindlimbs both handlike. They go to all fours without shame and to two with equal grace, the spine a lever, the tail a stay. Their feet think. Each toe can make a loop. Each heel can bite a line. The soles learn knots the way a tongue learns names.

Cold teaches them everything else. They sleep low and many. They scar themselves where skin would always be abraded otherwise; over their fingers, over their spines, along their jaws, they lay down layers of collected resin like teeth turned outward—a second hide that can be warmed, mended, unsewn. The teeth inside are resin too, transparent like fossilized honey, renewed in layers through life. They are forever chewing: sugar-strings, salt-grit, tough wax bloom; the resin answers by thickening, like a shell that listens to what it must break and grows itself accordingly. If they ever do a thing just to be beautiful, it is with the teeth. They inlay them with the smallest foreign motes: a fleck of iron, a chip of obsidian, a sliver of old broken mirror. When they smile, sometimes a star arrives.

A child’s most precious virtue is not courage but ear. Whole games are built around it. Little ones are set on a porch facing a hard wind with a harp in their hands and told: find where you won’t shake. The harp is very simple and very subtle: a span of fiber with a slider and a bell. Touch, slide, tap; you feel the resonance in your bones. The instant you hit a quiet line, the bell whispers, the trembling of the world reduces to a tolerable hum, and a certain kind of fear subsides. From there, life is the art of carrying that quiet line with you: tying a basket at the angle that thickens a note just so, weighting a dangling cord to nudge a Scythe’s preferred ride into a different octave, plaiting hairs to make fog drip in a way that turns a lashing beat into a consenting pulse.

They build small because the plant will not suffer a house. Not to cut is oath, but not to weigh wrong is more urgent still. Every structure is a compromise with wind. Frames are half ladders and half webs, porous to air and kind to sound. Huts cup back to the flow, with slatted porches that comb fog like tongues. The best rooms are resin caves borrowed from the biters, warmed by their slow burn; the worst are anchors’ lee faces where sharp rock cuts sound like paper, and nobody sleeps well there. They spend their day on the open weaves, moving in a set of braided paths, loose enough to reweave at an hour’s warning when a quake or storm shifts the world a half-tone to the left.

Language is the chord turned into mouth. They favor verbs like tie, hang, ease, thicken, lighten. They will tell you a story by plucking the porch rail and letting it talk the weather, and then pushing a bead along a string to say when the hero moved. Their proper names are not words but tunings: a small harp with pegs that only they know how to set, and when they meet someone they tune it for them and hold it up, and if you know the person you feel in your teeth who is meant. They have speech too, but they keep it soft. A loud voice is a blunt instrument that can send wrong ripples far. There are times when you go to the edge and shout; those are mostly griefs and warnings.

For writing they use knots, scores tied into cords with beads of metal and pebbles of glass and fistfuls of resin hardened to different weights. You can read a long journey by running a knot-line over one forearm and thumbing the beads: here the sound was low and full, here the fog was sugar-thick, here the span sang sharp and angry like winter, here we tucked and bound and went breathless over nothing. Their books live in balls that lose their order if untied wrong. A good reader ties them back better than they were.

Food is motion crystallized; their table looks like work. Night’s extruded filaments clump and bake in the lee of a node; they cut those mats into ropes, plait ropes into racks, hang racks in a wind that hits the right wetness, and after a day the ropes will have sweated out brine and stiffened into something both sweet and salty that can be knotted into traveling knots. They strum the knots before they eat, a peasant’s grace: a poor knot will tell you it wants more wind or more time, and a good one will hum like a full room. They chew grazer when it is given, but they do not hunt them except in hunger; better to trade fog-salt for a shoulder from a flock’s shepherds on migration. Scythes die where they die. People take what the wind grants: long bones for scrapers, jaw-hinges for clamps, scraps of hide for cords that nobody wants to touch or name.

A hearth is rarely a flame. Fire, hungry of oxygen and prodigal of smoke, is for sacrament, for the one time at birth and the two times at death, for cutting obsidian right, for making a bell sing true for the first time. Heat—honest and quiet—they buy from their neighbors under the floor: the resin-biters’ slow chemistry, coaxed with a dripped thread of sugar-water into a more generous mood; the Candlebreath microbes’ faint shimmer, scaled up by building walls that reflect even ghost-light back and forth until a room holds a dusk it did not have to earn again. When they want brighter they will bring a tray of fungus to near-saturation, a thing that glows like a thought and dies back overnight if left alone; they seldom dare waste sugar that way, but now and then desire is the weather, and someone paints their ceiling with small blue galaxies.

Work makes kin more than blood does, though everything is intertied. There are Lays—those whose tune is to the world-plant—and there are Drifts—those whose tune is to the great emptiness between. Lays tend the quiet lines, make the ward-weights, tune the hair-collectors and change the register of a span by a hair’s hair. Drifts go node to node, carrying knot-books and salt, trading pieces of mirror for pieces of meteor, staying three days to hear the place and then moving again when the aurora takes on the slow, wet green that promises easier glides. Every family is some of each. The old, when the ears get too full of weather and the fingers lose thirst for knots, sit with the children and teach them to count without numbers: this many beats is fog-tide; this much drop in a node’s tone is enough to drink; this particular creep into minor key means a quake long before your feet know it.

They marry by splice. Tying a life to a life is done with a new line, a cord that has both resin and hair and a bead from each of its anchors; the pair knots it in public with all the village fingers pressed to feel their choice. Touch judges the fitness: a tuck wrong makes dissonance down the length, a tuck right makes a line smooth and quiet. Every hand in the clan helps to unmake the knots if later the couple comes to say the wind went a different way. Unknotting is not a shame. A bad knot breaks under strain and that is sin here, where everything must flex.

They are spare in religion, as weather makes you. They name four rightnesses and live by them: Hold, Ease, Share, Return. Hold means: keep tension where it belongs; always tie many knots; never leave single points of failure; keep your grip for those whose grip is worse. Ease: learn the quiet lines and open them for every body that must cross; do not set harsh notes where they will drum someone apart. Share: water above gold, salt above treasure, warmth above all. Return: carry your weight back into the world when you are done. That last is law about corpses and law about craft. The dead are not dropped but hung, weight made ritual. A body is set in resin and wrapped in cord and made into a ward-weight that will hang at the height of the hut’s threshold for a year sealing off predator-chords, then shifted outward and outward into the general net until finally it hangs above nothing and sings only wind. The place keeps them; nothing falls pure. To fall unknotted is a blasphemy as real and raw as murder.

Their stories rise from physics and go strange anyway, as stories want. The plant itself is many characters: Mother Span, who felt a quake and sewed herself a new anchorage on her own back; Skins-Throat, whose membranes ate the fog of a storm and sang so low the people slept for three days; Old Anchor, who dragged the key of a valley sighingly down and hid a village from Scythes for a generation. There are heroes whose power is not killing or even climbing, but hearing—the woman who could taste a dissonance in her molars from a village away and knew which hair to braid tighter to tame four weeks of screaming wind; the boy who took a knot he found in a traveler’s coat and turned it into a map home. There are villains who are not people: The Silence, a rolling sphere of dead air that comes some years with no smell and no frost and kills three before someone can light fire; The Struck, a tree of lightning that walks the combs in a dry season and takes seven anchors before a storm makes it sit down and vanish into the rock.

There are strangers, but they never look like you hope. Once or twice in a generation, in certain green auroras, you see glows moving under the far membranes that are not Candlebreath—great bowls of blueness like storms made gentle moving with intent. The Drifts have tales of a people of the lower nodes who never come up, who keep their strength by burning old membrane skins in pits of their own breath and never letting a wind touch their faces; of another kind altogether that lives in the fog itself like fish live in water, riding pressure-waves instead of ropes. How much is seeing and how much is wish is a feeling each person chooses on a different day. People of the Chord are not evangelists. The world is confident enough to not need witnesses; it plays itself whether you listen or not.

Life is winter-dominant. The seasons with names are about absence: The Lean Wind, when grazer-sails have a sunken look and the plant holds its breath; The Low Key, when storm-fronts lay long like valleys and you live in the slow bass-lines, laying up salt and resin; The Tall Fog, when the combs fill and everyone and everything becomes wet and fat and easier for a while; The Set, the bright one, when for a handful of weeks the plant swells new spans into places where yesterday only abyss hung, and the children run wild out onto bridges no one told them would be there. Their calendars are cords with movable knots. You slide a bead to feel how long. You move it back and start counting again when a quake groans through and the anchors go tired and your familiar sky-web rearranges.

They have law but not many laws that bite. The biting ones go to water first. Steal salt in a dry week and your neighbors will put your hands on the quiet line and keep them there until your bones sing to you through your wrists, a pain carried home. Sabotage a membrane’s tune to make your own hut steadier at the cost of a neighbor’s, and they will cut your knots, not the ones holding you to life, but the ones that keep your home. You become a Drift until you learn Share and Return and someone takes you in and helps you re-tie. There is no prison here; there is too much space.

They are born here, in resin rooms warm as breath, mothers and co-parents hanging in slings, cords in their hands to press when things are hard. They are raised on sweet-mats and patience and the callous that comes to fingers that work tiny knots all day. They take their first names from their first tunings; they take their second names when they fall and are caught. They grow old by growing more careful. In the end they shrink a little and laugh a little more than they ever did—half the laughter is fear escaped; half is the larger thing, the one that is gratitude, though that is not a word with a chord in it. People here like what admits its limits. A clear sky is as beautiful as it is unkind.

Many other lives braid through these. Little warm-bellied “birds” that are not birds share porches and steal thread. They have mouths like teaspoons and eyes with lids as slow as thought; they hoard resin and beads in nests you must bribe them away from, and sometimes a village will collectively indulge one as if it were weather you liked. Spur-haunters live hard by the rock; they are armored like shingles and eat sparks from lightning-crack. Their bellies chime in small tinny peals when they are sated, a sound some people use as lullaby. There are thin black snakes that are not snakes at all but a looped set of cilia that spin like oars; they live on the shadow sides of nodes and drink noise; if your harp goes flat unexpectedly, look for the dark threads and brush them free. There are too many kinds of fungus to count. They are the slow collage artists of the place, and their art is to pick apart what is old and lay down what will be new, each with its own appetite for salt, for sugar, for cold, for time.

Whoever you are under the membranes, you learn the same truths even if you do not have words for them. Sound is not only something you hear. It moves you, changes the way your heart does its work. Nothing stands alone; you never tie once—always three times, always many. Emptiness is not a lack but a property; you treat it as a thing with which you must make a deal. Light is not proof of life. Warmth is not a given. Safety is not a place but a set of relationships. A person is not only their skin but the web they pull and are pulled by.

It would be easy to say: these creatures are brave because they live so near falling. They would shrug and say: falling is what keeps us honest. They understand their world in the units it asks them to use. If you ask them what a good life measures, they will put a weight in your palm and say: can you feel when to ease it? Can you listen until a terror stops being a shout and becomes a note you can touch and thicken into a way across? They cannot tell you where spirit ends and tension begins because here that line hums and moves and is alive. They have learned, better than most who name themselves human, to live in a chord that is never still without breaking it. That is what lives here: a choir of hung things, all of them moving a little, holding a little, singing enough to keep each other from falling all the way through.
