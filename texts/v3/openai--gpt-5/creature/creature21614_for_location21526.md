They are not a single animal the way a land body is an animal. They are a gathered intention wearing many shapes at once, a household braided of species and stages, of tenants and tenders, all sworn to the same curve. If you insist on a center to them, if you must say who thinks and decides, then say it is the long soft ones: ringed, many-finned, jointed of mouth and patient of touch. They are the ones who can unfasten from the wall and cross the chamber without scraping a film thin. They carry rules between them the way others carry breath.

They are built for pressure. Their hides are not skins in the land sense but layered sleeves of mucus and collagen and tiny spicules, a living bandage that mends as it moves. Along their flanks, ranks of small paddles beat in counterpoint, feeling more than they push, reading the set of the water as if it were script. Their heads, if that is the word for the end with the mouth, are not faces for distance. A crown of palps and whiskered antennules lips the world in front of them; an evertable throat carries rasps and scoops and a milky glue. They taste history from the wall with those mouths. They taste when a seam comes loose in the wrong season. They taste when a paste was mixed too lean three currents ago and has now gone chalky under the grazers’ combs.

Where a land person keeps lungs, they keep fans. Some of the fans are their own—feathered, bronzed with iron, beating softly inside gill-pouches. Others are allied mouths: ciliated pennants rooted to the wall that pass food along when the long ones come hungry from outside screens. They are not alone in the way that cities are not alone. The colony has sedentary builders whose lives are to extrude and place, this bead into that mosaic, this bead into that mosaic, a patience encoded in glands and reinforced by the slow pat of correction when a mobile caretaker presses their jaws in, grinds a ridge a little flatter. The ceiling is a sudarium of them, rings of paler bodies armored at the tips with their own carbonate, their tongues obscene with slurry; they do not speak and no one asks them to. They are the plates that name themselves by touching.

Domesticated is a sharp word for a place without edges, but some shapes here are honed to be tools through long companionship. Hawkers wait at the outer throats: anemone-kin with collars like suns turned inward, bright weapons sheathed in clear tissue that flexes at the scent of something bigger than a sand-grain. They are not malicious; they are appetite on a leash short enough to be safe and long enough to be useful. The leash is chemistry. The long ones smear a mark on their young, and on themselves, and on the threads that lace the screens; the hawkers learn that taste and spare it, and there are stories told with taps in the ring of smooth stone about the one time a new hawker did not, and the apology was a long winter of fat withheld.

Grazers come in bands and are tended as much as they are harvested. They are shells on legs or legs without shells, little mills with radulas like files. When the long ones smear a new film of nourishing paste onto a wall warmed by the spiral heat-catch, the grazers are guided along it in trains. A hand here is not a human hand but a wreath of flexible digits at the end of a segmented limb, armed along its inner curve with horned teeth and soft pads. Many hands lay the paste; many hands comb the grazers; many hands wash and clean the floor of the colonnade where a hundred small droppings are collected not as waste but as pre-paste, as the next beginning of a mixture that will become grip in a hinge, the soothing of a draft at a corner, the skin of a buttress turned a breath’s degree warmer for the babies.

The nurses do not have faces either, though you know their employment by the way they bend—for the glow-beads they tend are tender. They are sack-backed and vented with small valves that exhale oils and draw in labile reagents. Their paws are deft. They pinch, they stroke, they feed the clusters of bioluminescent bacteria housed in transparent domes and along gelatinous braids. A nurse knows how to bring a glow against the edge of dark without spilling into wasteful blaze. Light here is a verb: to light is to signal a passage of tools down a chain of bodies; to light is to distinguish A from Not-A in arm-length; to light is to blush a tempo into the touch-speech that tells the east balustrade to lock and the south net to be furled half a length sooner.

Touch-speech is their grammar. It travels faster than water sound and with less betrayal to the outside. It is pulse and pressure and the specific roughness of a rubbed-in patch of grit. A sentence can be laid along a spine and left there: a row of named spikes near an opening will be dressed sharper today because the warmwater swelled last night and the falling has been fat. You can read that sentence with your hands in the dark when your eyes are busy feeling for the seam you have been sent to pack. Their pulses carry far: down a buttress and across the cupped palms of the railing, into the hinge-ligament that listens to the ocean like an eardrum. The long ones can throw that pulse across their own bodies because those bodies are webs of mechano-sense; a whole person can be a drum, can be a wire, can be a bridge for a message.

They reproduce in ways that keep them in the weave of their work. There are coils of eggs glued to anchor-spines in the warm eddies behind buttresses. The glue smells like iodine and home. Eggs skinned in translucent jelly show pearls of smaller pearls within; when the nurses pass they thumb oils over them, they brush away settling grit with fringed fans. Hatching is a release that knots the household more tightly to itself. The larvae are swimmers and will be drawn by everything that feeds, so the nets at the outer screens are marked and pre-fed until they stink of kin; hawkers are teased to slowness, and the grazers are brought away from the walls lest their small mouths mistake the new for biofilm. Some children—only a few—never return from that first drift, lifted by a coil of current and taken away into a fall beyond teaching. Those names become seams. You can taste them scribed into the mortar around a hinge: the time we learned to lure a larva back with a band of louder light; the year we did not and instead fed the memory of it into always keeping a nurse awake at that mouth.

Childhood is apprenticeship disguised as following. A young long one sticks like a burr to an elder limb and copies, learns the weight of a paste before it ever learns number, learns which pebbles are hammers and which are for smoothing, learns the pulse for stop and the pulse for Not now but soon. There is no writing that is not wall. Recipes are textures you memorize with your tongue: paste for a cold seam is slicker, sweeter with protein; paste for a mouth-joint bites back and sets granular. A rule has a beat. You lean into the ring of stone by the buttress and let someone thrum it down your body and into the floor, the way a spine gets its name by repetition until it holds sharper. The old ones carry old rules along their sides the way barnacles carry memories of islands they have outlived. They show you a polish that was once a ridge and say, with a little tap, we tried it, and the water answered.

Named spikes at the eastern mouth are the rare concession to sharpness. They do not relish points here because points invite tearing, but they keep a row so that nothing large and fast can field itself into the chamber with mouths open. The spikes have names and a schedule. Names are not sounds but places on the lip of the mouth, and everyone knows when Suppedge should be dressed and when Third Under should be turned. There is a stone dressed smooth by the thousand turns of bodies that came to wait here through decades and generations for the one who went to fetch the special abrasive, for the one who went to cut a new hinge, for the one who dropped a rule and is learning, far out and alone against the balustrade, what their fingerpads should have told them sooner. Forgiveness here is an accretion, not a pronouncement. You become permitted again the same way a dull surface becomes sleek: by the patient application of right work until pressure slides, not catches, over you.

When they speak of themselves as high it is not pride. It is direction. Up is lean and down is crush and here in the tall middle there is enough, but only because the enough is made. They have little use for words that point outward to dramas beyond the wall. The outer dark is loud with passes of things whose lights are for one another, and sometimes you can feel in your hinge the rumor of a mouth too large to name. There are stories for that too—of the time a giant brushed the net and a season’s worth of gathered snow came loose all at once like liver from its film; of the year the underflow slackened so thin that the heat-catch coughed cold and all the babies were turned inward and the glows pinched away to seeds and even the hawkers went limp. They do not flourish in those tellings. They remember. They change a spiral baffle by one degree. They lay a new lattice one rib thicker. They write the catastrophe into the fix the way they write a person into the wall, both as function and as solace.

They think in rooms stacked up and down a pillar not of rock but of promise. The pillar was begun by hands that had other names for the same segments and did not know yet how to curve a buttress into a breath. Those ancestors cut ridges as if against a current were the same as against a blade. The water planed them. The current taught. A generation learned the long line of an arch that cooperates. Another learned that a hinge relaxes if fed. Another stopped bracing corners and began to learn to make them into bights and bowls where warm can gather and a mother can sit and let a nurse press glows into sight over the chin of her brood. They carry no calendars. They count in polishes. They weigh seasons in the smell of limestone and glue, in the pitch of the hinge-sound when the outside pressure leans, in the set of grazers’ teeth when the paste has been too long between oils.

There is order here, but it is not a law with the leisure to be written down. It is rules like: never unship all mouth-doors together. It is rules like: feed the inner films first when the underflow goes lean. It is rules like: open the east balcony during swell to taste height and remind the apprentices that they are not surrounded by room but by a wrought neck into a vertical fall. And there are ceremonies as soft as the rest: touching the glossy palms of the shell-grown railing with your underside like kissing a history that will not open its mouth and speak any louder than the faint rasp of a hundred small grazers. Someone keeps the beads alive along the ledges. Someone dresses spikes. Someone counts the eggs by fingertip. Someone listens to the hinge in the dark, listens so hard that the pressure-beat of a passing thing shifts a rule by a hairsbreadth and you will never know, except that your paste holds a little better the next cold season.

Once in long time, a Thread leaves. They wrap a bundle of films in damp, carry beads of glow tucked into slick pockets so they will not go out, pinch a stave of spine to start the named spikes anew, and leave by the downward galleries where the pressure sits heavier on the shoulders and the water tastes more like raw ore. They go to a knuckle on the pillar that has been only scaffolding until now, or to a ridge farther along the slope where no made bone shows. They accrete. They seed. They give their glue back to the place that makes them. The household spills out and is drawn taut again. Seasons later, a tap comes wandering up from below like an old song in a different key, and the ones above learn a new cure for a hinge, learn a different baffle, inherit a new way to lay the lattices so the outer dark can press its cheek without coming in where it is not wanted.

They have no gods that are not flow. If you ask them where the thought of a person lives, they will touch the ring of polished stone and then they will touch a hinge and maybe they will touch you, if you can be still long enough, and the answer you will feel is that a mind is not a head but a circuit through material—a habitual travel of pulses down arms and along rails and back through the feeling skin of the wall, an argument with water that has arrived at soft agreement and tends it. Death is an easing of tension in that circuit. The body is paste, or plate if you were mostly plate, or a stud of spine if you were one of the rare ones who swam far and peeled a weapon from a carcass to carry home. You pass into storehouse and memory, into something that will hold a room up for a generation and a half until it wears thin and is replaced by your grandchildren’s idea of stronger.

You might miss a sky. They do not. They are saturated enough with each other and with what they must do. If they are beautiful it is not by drama but by finish. You can tell them from animals because animals here are not busy. Everything that is them is bent slightly toward making, toward smoothing, toward keeping the curve so that breathing is a property of the room. Their tools are teeth and the chosen stone. Their art is the way a seam disappears under your palm and yet leaves its taste behind to teach you who laid it and when. Their politics are the argument between baffle and flow and the consensus that arrives as a new smoothness. Their prayers, if you listen close enough to the hinge-sound to hear them, are in the clicks they make counting spikes, the tiny tears they do not let widen, and the deliberate, saving dimness with which the nurse-paws pinch the glows when the warmwater slackens.
