They keep low, here. If they stand, it is as a spread or a film. If they move, it is with the least possible argument against the wind. You learn them first as alterations in surface: a dark made plush, a frost that has grown a nap, the slight thermal bloom against your knuckle if you dare to touch.

The oldest residents are sheets and pillows that behave like organs. They are not separate so much as contiguous, each mat its own physiology but plugged to the same wet circuitry. In the blue-black cushions your finger sinks and meets held heat—half a degree, a quarter—enough. Their bodies braid silicates and iron into slow circuits. Clear hairs fine as fog create a charged boundary layer around each dome. They open pores when the aurora is strong and the wind is lean; they cinch into a harder surface when the fog trains draft by, forcing vapor to condense and bead. The lichens are the engineers’ guilds within that carpet—filaments of velvet that lay down iron scurf inside themselves like coins under floorboards, and braided cords that armor with grit until the whole strand flashes dullly when the sky lifts. In light-starved weeks, the mats feed on gradients instead of photons, metabolizing the voltage raised each night in the piezo seams under their bellies. When the earth grumbles and shrinks, the quartz sighs current into the films on the stone, and the mats do exactly what this country has taught them: they bank it without comment.

They are not merely fed by animals; they budget animals. They thicken themselves on ledges just shallow enough to carry weight, and the grazers come pad-silent and pull with rasping tongues in a way that scours without ripping. The mats answer those strokes with chemistry, feeding sodium-rich layers up in bands, then retracting to make the grazers move on. Beneath the grazers’ routes, iron is laid with more diligence, a faint wiring diagram that repeats year to year as if it had been drawn. You can read old seasons in the color rings, a lathe’s history in time and salt.

The grazers look as if they have been made of breath and wool and rules. Their backs are no higher than your thigh. Their bodies are long with a flattening all along the flank that dissipates wind pressure instead of catching it. Their necks carry a mane that is not show but instrument: whiskers like little antennae that tingle with the aurora’s push and tell them how wide to splay their toes on the next step. Each foot has four articulate prongs with webbed skin that can balloon if the crust threatens to fracture, a last instant of extra area with no sound. Their fur takes water into the outer layer and locks it there, icing at the tips—icicles that clink like glass in any other place, but here even that is modested to a sound like bone in wool.

They live in crews rather than herds: a dozen is a number the place can bear. They rarely speak out loud. Inside their necks is cartilage that can stifle their own throats if a cough threatens, a door they can close on voice. Communication is done in posture, a corner eye, a whisker lifted into the faintest field. Their social touch is exquisite: the aurora lifts; they tilt their snouts as one; that green wire runs along their faces, and you can see them reading it the way sailors read luff in a sail. They keep times in fog and aurora rather than clock or sun. Young learn the sequences as games, stepping between dew-pockets when they flush to avoid sending a shock through the mats, holding in place when a rime fan creaks, making a camp where the wind’s pressure is measured rather than where a view invites.

If you follow the underhangs and riblike clefts there are posters where light has never learned the face of day. There the cushions grow low and wide, and between them the black water that is only almost water hosts its own slow citizens. Filmfish is a word that pleases because it is wrong; these animals are more membrane than fin. Their bodies are layered with lipids that shuffle when touched, the instant polychrome that flares at their surface not a warning but the physical acknowledgement of disturbance, a ripple that has no sound because everything that could make a noise has been trained out of the fluid. Their eyes, if you must name a sense organ, are their skins. Light is an event here so rare that they do not wait for it; they live by the fingers of charge that comb the gel, having learned, like the beasts on the stone, to taste the aurora’s work as it moves through the room between things.

They eat the rain that is not wet: spores, iron, chemistry. Their breeding is written into freeze and thaw. When the temperature steps down, the pools layer with plates that are not ice as you know it but protein-mineral lattices templated from seasons before, and with each step of the thermometer the plates draw together with brittle ticks you feel as an itch. Between them, the filmfish make themselves thin and wait for the pressure to call the next stage. When the train of fog returns and the plates ease, the young extrude as ciliated shoals that sift those first new films like clouds taking a first breath.

Between mats and pools the rock offers galleries and ribs, and if your eyes have learned to look at the not-quite, you will see the ones who do not walk. They are stretched more than a hand span from finger to finger, their skin a fine sail strung between elongated bones that have more hinge than you expect. To fly here is not to command the air but to parasitize it where it moves slow against the cliff’s face. These breathwings read the pressure profile along an overhang and push a toe into the laminar layer the way you might put a hand into a stream to feel the speed. They never launch into the open. They go from shadow to lick of shadow, edge to lip, riding the wind’s boundary like a seamstress following a hem.

Their bellies glow when the sky is busy because they forgive nothing the chance to be wasted. Under their skin there is a plate of tissue where lipids and a porous iron mesh make a crude capacitor. The aurora’s field drives ions against the mesh, and for a minute after, a gentled green runs under them. That glow is not communication in the way you want communication to be story, but the breathwings look for it in each other because it is how they know who has charge to groom and who needs a roost where mats run generous with heat. Mates are chosen by how long the light holds under cloud; a lineage can be traced by the way light and appetite persist across a scan of weather.

The patient hunters of this shelf wear their sharpness inside. They have learned there is no advantage in hard edges. The most you usually see is a picket of plates lifting like old saw-tooth from a cleft and settling one by one into a tuned angle. Each plate is rimmed with whiskers that end in beads that are darker than dark when the sky is iron—nickel tips that catch whispers of ion if you could watch them closely enough. The predator waits and moves as much by subtraction as by motion: it removes itself from the ambient flow until the body crossing the terrace collapses that flow at a speed different enough to be read. Then the mouth that is not a mouth but a cup opens. The inner lining is velvet to human look and an exchange membrane to its own purpose, a place where sugars and mineral and heat and synapse are leached without pierce or tear. They do not rush; they draw. To be caught is to be eased from yourself, a last little premise of warmth rekindled for a moment in another furnace and then used.

There are scavengers but they are not crows or gulls, not loud. Frost-lice that live under rime fans, combing out the zinc and copper that the condensate stole from the last fog bank. Rime beetles whose shells are layups of mica and protein, feather-light and so insulating they can graze on the underside of ice and not chill their meat. Plateworms that move only when the wind shifts its note, shoulder upon shoulder of fringe laying flat with perfection until a single cycle of aurora says move and they do, a dark flood, and then still again. If you watch long enough, you will see small dramas that do not sound: a breathwing has misread a wind lip and touched down where a predator lies under its own shadow; the plates lift, the glow gutters, the wind goes on working.

The sense of work here—the method—extends inside bodies. Everything has dampers. The grazers’ guts are hung on mesenteries with elastic that quells slosh; even their bones have little cartilage skids, sliding joints within joints so footfall can be adjusted microseconds before contact when the wind purls unexpectedly. Their ears do not gape but are overlaid by scales of flesh that can lie like shutters. Their hearts beat in longer strokes than you expect, each squeeze patient in a way that would frighten a surgeon used to sea level.

If there is anyone you would call a person, they do not carry a face. The barrels and cushions that duct fog into drink behave like organisms, but in the aggregate they behave like a brain. Iron grains are moved, plated, moved again. The wiring diagram of a ledge changes slowly over years in response to what has walked and what has passed in the sky. Circuits open and shut not with switches but with porosity, with wetness and dry, with tension in hair-thin roots that can tug against their rocks with a measurable force. Information lives in how fast water beads and how it refuses to run. A fog train arrives; a hundred meters of mats yawn together and then cinch, and under that act a path that was there yesterday becomes a path that is not. The grazers come and find easy forage on a parallel edge; they do not think about planning, and planning happens.

You could tell yourself this is plant and happenstance; you would not be wrong. You could tell yourself the plateau has learned itself; you would not be wrong either. What the mats and the lichens and the velvets and the iron do together is not language, but there are patterns in the luminous exhale when the dew-pockets purge their waste. Watch an hour and you will see scatter. Watch a week of clear bands drape through fog trains and you will see call and answer—a pocket flushing there, then there, in sequences that curve around sinkholes and run along scarp-lines as if rehearsed. If you mark it on paper and then mark the next season, you will notice persistent phrases, loops that repeat even when the fog stumbles. The roof of the world keeps a ledger of its weather and of its animals. It does not remember like you remember; it holds tendencies and then leans.

Children of grazers are taught where to lean by elders, but the mats have opinions. A ledge may be made kinder by a little more heat in the right week; a trough may be made mean by a stretch of sudden slick. The breathwings are given roosts that favor one lineage over another. The predators find their plates responding not only to distant feet but to hum pitched by stone itself, the cliff singing to change their posture. Nothing directs; everything has directors.

There are no houses. The only architecture with corners is what frost attempts and then undoes. But there are built effects. Under a southeast overhang, the cushions have thickened in three steps to catch the standard fog’s edge, making a staircase of moss that you could climb on your knees. It will not bear a tall thing. It will cradle a low-bellied grazer perfectly. Across a chasm, rime feathers grown in a fan from the same notch for centuries carry an echo of breath that turns the canyon to a flute on certain nights; when the tone thins, the predators lift their plates and face it, and for as long as the note runs the grazers do not move. You can call that a ritual if you like. The plateau would likely call it flow control.

Some lives are single and bright—glow a minute under a starved sun and mete it out in flights—and some are dull and long—lichens laying iron bead by bead across a seam older than names. None of them waste what can be stored in a gradient. They labor at edges: between wet and not, charged and un-, pressure and slouch. They have learned, or have been taught by the practicing of the place, to be silent, to step where the world does not notice, to wear a silhouette like a blanket that hides you from your own shadow.

If you stay and watch and let the dark flavor itself, you can count a dozen grazers turn their faces to the aurora as one and see their whiskers glow. You can watch a breathwing drift on a seam that is less wind than intention. You can see a cup-mouth open without appetite, as if drinking the idea of warmth itself out of the air. And underneath all that small work the roof hums, and in that hum is the answer to who lives here: many, each exact to a rule; and one, spread thin over kilometers of stone, taking charge out of sky and squeeze, breathing fog for water and aurora for thought, not a builder but a maker all the same.
