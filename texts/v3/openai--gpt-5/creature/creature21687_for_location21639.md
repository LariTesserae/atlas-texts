Those who live here are not a tribe so much as a habit the place has taught to people. They are the ones who accept noon as a law and make noon useful, the keepers of edges and of thirst measured to the mouth. If they have a name it is given only by outsiders, who would call them the tenders, the noonwrights, the people-who-lean-on-levers. Among themselves there is no need to say who they are. They fit.

You see them in glimpses that look like memory: a forearm the color of pale bread braced against a wedge; the back of a neck salted to a matte, the hairs lying down in the direction of work; a heel smudged with lime; a jaw set lightly as a knot is set; the flicker of half-lidded eyes learning brightness by squint the way others learn script. They move as water is coaxed to move—without hurry and without slack. Their bodies are written with the place: callus where rope must be leaned on, a fine weal of salt where a veil’s hem brushes the same shoulder each time, the faint black of char and fishbone soil worked clean under the nails at night. Their clothes are pressed plant: white cloth stiffened with silt and rinsed until it holds a crease like a rule. Everything about them gives the impression of edges believed in and kept.

They speak, but not much, and never in words meant to carry. Air here eats voice; they answer by gestures you would think invented on the spot if you had not seen them repeated along the frames by someone half a field away: a thumb rubbed once along the inside of the forearm—tighten the knot; two fingers tapped lightly on the lower lip—the drip is too fast; a palm flattened and slid through air—change the veil’s rake. When they must make sound, it is the s, f, th you already know from the place: breath and leaf and thread, the consonants of friction and of things not colliding.

Time for them is the bead’s travel, the taking-up of a wick, the time it takes a shadow to be a stripe and stay a stripe. They own no hours and so cannot waste one. “Later” for them is the next drop; “long ago” is the day a mirror was smudged to throw heat more honestly. Their patience is not a virtue they talk about. It is a tool they use, like a paddle smoothed with the inside of a cow horn.

The work they make and are is a liturgy of small insistences. A person leans her hip into a lever and a current slides like a mind changing; a boy tips a gate’s slab with the toe of his sandal, hair damp with the sweat the amphora drew for the air; a set of hands that have fitted that knot before tighten it again to the measure the cord remembers. They comb water up with split coir until a tray drinks; they comb it down by unslinging a veil so that a jar hears the drop and answers. They pick salt the way one plucks frost from a sash—by the edge, never scouring a plane. They shave papyrus and set the strips to dry with their seams like bright scars, then write with them not words but wind. They set mirrors not to blind but to heat, then move them by the width of a thumbnail to make the argument in the air more earnest. They keep separators clean so that nothing smears.

When they harvest, they are gentler than you expect anyone to be in a world without rot. Taro comes up with a rocking motion learned from eel-backs; mint is pinched where stems already want to branch; plantain is loosened from its lattice and set to rest on tile where every tiny sweat-bead the cut makes can be seen and thanked by the tongue later. Fish from the flumes are not really caught. They are invited into a jar that would be their river’s bend, and when they are out they lie in bowls whose rims are flush with the exact world.

There is a center to them, as there is to the place: the low tower with clay plates that sweat the air fine and hang it like beads on cords, the bench of lime that holds a person’s back so that they are reminded they have one. Someone—always someone, you cannot say which—sits near that cistern to tend the cups. Cup-tending is not hospitality here; it is grammar. A person comes in and their thirst is weighed by the thumb-print in a handle, and a shallow bowl is turned under that weight so that it holds exactly enough of the air’s drink. No one’s name is called. It is not needed. The tool has remembered the hand that fit it last time and turns itself a hair in that direction, and you see the person’s fingers fall into place as if it were the only place they wanted to be all day.

Children live here as proofs that even noon has beginnings. They are taught by hand, not by mouth: to set their fingers into the ghosts that handles remember so that the match between palm and oak learns itself; to put their ear near a drip and hear what “regular” feels like in the bones of the skull; to read the papyrus combs to tell whether the air has been eating enough or eating too sharply; to measure distance with their own footfalls and circle back to a place they can’t swear they’ve visited. Lessons are not called lessons. A child is given a straw tray to carry flat between both palms, four fingers of water gleaming in it, and told to walk the fifteen paces of glare between mint islands without folding a wave. That is school. When a child leaves a paddle leaning against a wall and comes back to it and the shadow of their small hand on its surface has not changed, only deepened, they learn what memory is here.

There are elders, but an elder’s hair is just another veil: a softening of the glare, a way to notice angle. The oldest ones watch baffles and mirrors, because those are the only parts of this place allowed to throw back what comes at them. They are the argument-keepers. The baffles, slackened just a little, will make a day that was nearly too dry into a day the cords can drink. If they are tightened wrong, nothing breaks, because nothing here is allowed to break without choosing to. But a strip of mint under a papyrus comb will come off flat instead of cupping to catch dew, and that is a loud failure for people who listen to quiet.

They sleep in shade you do not think is for sleeping until you see the hollow of shoulders worn into a lime bench. They do not make houses. Their beds are slung where ladders of water climb so that the softest noise they know—the sweet teeth of capillary—can be how they go to stillness. If they dream, the dream is of this place catching the air’s breath and handing it, drop by drop, into a jar.

It is not only people who live here, of course. Every creature you saw is a citizen in the way citizens ought to be: each with work it cannot explain to you but would not think of not doing. The dragonflies write the air’s mood, their off-corner clicks where wings kiss a veil telling more about the day than a thousand flags could. Geckos are lids: they close the idea of a jar so that what is inside feels bound and good; when they sleep on a clay vessel’s side they draw a line of trust around it with their heat. To the toads falls the listening. They set themselves under a weeping wall and point their heads toward the sound of each drop falling in its catch-basin and, by the almost-imperceptible turn of a pupil, tell you if the tick is slipping from regular into rush. Herons keep time—not clock time, which would be irrelevant, but the unit of a place not wasting its posture: one foot up, down in the same place, a hollow tap that proves the tile below is still what it first meant. Eel-fish patrol loops to make sure the place believes itself a river often enough to remain interesting. Snails practice the most difficult art here, which is to repeat. They sign the seam between tiles with wet script that will fade in three minutes and then lay it again to the same result. It would be a discouraging life anywhere else. Here it is the declaration the world asked for: sharpness can be gentle and still be itself, forever.

If animals do not multiply or die where you can see them, that does not make them imaginary. It makes them principles you can pet if you are patient. There are always enough, and exactly enough. The same is true of the people, though it is harder to say. A woman with a rope like her own arm passes. A boy sweats against the belly of a jar. Three figures walk with poles. They are numbers as well as faces. If a person disappears, you look at such-and-such station the next day and see a new hand fitting perfectly into the shadow on a paddle and do not ask where the first went. Perhaps they crossed the levee and unspoke themselves. Perhaps they sat at the tower bench and did not stand, and the place taught a tool to hold that weight forever. In any case, grief does not pool here. It beads, merges, finds the next slope, begins again.

Belief, for those who live here, is not belief. It is agreement with rules you would think superstitious somewhere else but are only honest here. Water goes where it is asked, therefore one must ask right. Ask without levers that chatter or wheels that bite shoulders. Ask with slope. Ask with veil. Ask with a wick split to comb thinness. Distance is obedient, therefore straighten your thought and do not argue with fatigue. It will carry you back to the middle if your feet know the line the causeways know. Edges are truthful, therefore cut and do not rub; if time would make a corner round, do it with a hand if it needs doing at all. Animals do what they are, therefore let them. Tools remember, therefore return the favor by remembering them.

Eat is a small word in this white blaze, but they do. They sit under papyrus, legs wiped with mint, and they eat taro and plantain and greens rinsed cool in jars where strings of dew slide. They salt what needs salt with crystals shaved from under a drip-line while they are still proud enough to show planes. They chew slowly; food that was raised by the argument of veils and air argues politely back in the mouth. They drink staring ahead, because looking at the cup would be excessive attention to what is already attended to from all sides. If there is sweetness here, and there is, it is the sweetness of water arriving at itself.

Their cunning looks like humility. They could take more than they take, but that would ask the tiles to flow. They could divert the loop to fill a cistern high in a day, but then the green would be less exact. So they practice enoughness with the precision other places reserve for rage or invention. When something tears—a veil, a thumb, a thought—the mend is made with wedges and rawhide and words that make the air change its mind a degree, and you see how the place wants to be put back.

If your eye is drawn to the levee that is not a barrier but a way to pronounce an end, know that they keep faith with it the way a writer keeps faith with margins. You may walk to it and look over if you wish. What you see will depend on you: sea, blankness, mirror. They do that sometimes: go out with a cord and measure a little of whatever is beyond to see if their ratio is still a ratio and not a boast. They come back. The glare welcomes them back like a word they have finally spelled correctly.

The greatest inhabitant, the one that cannot be pointed at or fed, lives across all this the way a spine lives across ribs. Call it the Argument if you must. It is the meeting of heat and cloth and line and wanting. It has habits: it settles on veils and lets them darken, it takes mirrors in the eyes and throws itself harder, it bites water so gently the water gives itself up without fear. It is not a god because it is answerable to angle. It is not a machine because no axle in it needs oil. It is a beast tamed by hospitality and by insistence—made useful by the same hands that fitted the paddles. You can sit under it and be made more yourself by how it addresses you: not to burn, but to ask you to admit you are thirsty.

What else lives here? The rules do. The loop that climbs and falls without consequence, and yet green things thicken. The distance that brings you, in honesty, back to a place you knew only by desire. Edges that keep their edge. Number without count. The way a tool waits for a hand as if nothing else had happened all day. The thing that is not wilderness and not city but a kind of long house laid on the world like a white sheet pulled tight, where care has been allowed to grow tall enough to cast veils.

If you stand long enough to let yourself be added to by this, you will notice that you have been taken into the population. Your breath has been stolen, politely, by a jar that is drinking for you. Your sweat has left your skin and risen to trays high above your head to cool a papyrus you will write with tomorrow. Your footfalls have been measured and returned to you with note: this pace is too short for the causeways; lengthen it by half a toe. You have been made a creature of the argument by standing still. You will leave salt when you go, and it will shine like frost for a day before it forgets itself into planes. If you return, the paddle you leaned against will fit you, because it knows where your knuckles are. This is what lives here: creatures with eyes and without; men and women who make edge a kindness; an air that surrenders water because it has been wooed correctly; a noon with manners; a population of drops; a memory of hands. A care so practiced it has learned to be an animal, walking on causeways, looking at you as if to say, boundary or invitation—either way, we will hold the line.
