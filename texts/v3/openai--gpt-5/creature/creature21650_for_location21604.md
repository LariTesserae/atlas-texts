What lives here learned to keep time before it learned to keep house. Its bodies are many, but the shape of its life is a schedule. It sleeps and feeds and hides by the clock of the cave, a clock set by swells and sets and pressure changes too blunt for instruments, too obvious for everything born to the seam.

Start close to the film. The first citizens are not larger than the comma of a thumbnail. They write in glaze and rasp marks. The black-green sheen on the rock is a city made of light, diatoms with glass lattices, filaments of algae stitched to the stone with a grip so fine it feels like oil. They take the photons that find them, even in this branded gray, and mint them into sugar. Their surface looks continuous until you watch it for a minute and see it creep: a mottling that is also traffic, each cell dividing or ceding its place, the sheen repairing itself in the hour between rasps.

The day shift on that film are the scrapers. Limpets make their rounds with a patience built from hunger and habit. By night they describe exact circles the size of coins, and by day they freeze to the rock like thoughts you can’t shake. Each keeps a home scar, a low relief notch they have ground into basalt the way you might hollow your side of a mattress; matched to the curl of its shell, it seals when the cave inhales. Their mouths hold a file not of steel but of iron all the same: a ribbon of chitin studded with magnetite, teeth that grow and are ground off and grow again, a conveyor belt of knives. Alongside them, chitons hug the ledges like an extra layer of rock with stitching. Eight plates articulate under a coat of muscle that reads the surface in minute bumps and grooves; their eyes are aragonite lentils, stone looking through stone at a world of millimeters.

Between them and beneath the arch, barnacles open their doors on the low count. Their hinges click as if passing messages—open now, open now—and a bouquet of feathery legs fans out and combs what the last cough of air left suspended. Each cirrus stroke is both a reach and a recoil. They are covered in the long blow and exposed in the long breath; their lives are a ratio of shell to wind, and they calcify that math into crenellations around their mouths.

The grazer’s gardeners follow: snail and whelk, periwinkle and dogwhelk in sizes you only see when you have found the right angle. The periwinkles file edges tidy; the whelks test the barnacle doors and drill when the hinge is thin. A dogwhelk can soak a seam with acid and turn food into math: time plus quiet equals an operculum pried and a mouth pressed home. It looks patient and is only competent—knowing which target cannot close all the way in the rush.

Anemones are a slow kind of opportunist. When the chamber exhales and the pool goes pane-smooth, they lift their crowns and comb the space between pulse and pulse. In this dark they are their own light—white-green translucency that smuggles daylight into the cavern and holds it like milk. When the fist comes down again they purse shut, all thought pulled into meat.

On the margins where brackish fingers comb the stone, there is a forest you have to kneel to see. Liverworts lie like scales, soft as tongues, each lobe a bay with its own weather. Sphagnum stands up its little cisterns and keeps them full on intention, wool that has elected thirst as a profession. In their thatch, rotifers wheel their faces and drink the world one ripple at a time. Nematodes thread from bead to bead like needles, and in their tides of damp the springtails bounce in gravitational jokes, owning the meniscus as if it were land. The predators here look like punctuation until they insist on being verbs: a mite loosed like a tick of static, a spider the size of a freckle strolling a thread too fine to carry spray.

Not all the spiders are air’s creatures. Somewhere—on a coast that taught it—there is a kind that learned tide and built a house of silk under a lip of stone, a satin envelope inflated with stolen breath. When the cave inhales, it rides the pressure; when it exhales, it unlocks its door and goes out hunting among gills. A story like that can find its way into any fissure with the right rule set. If not here, then its analogue: everything at the edge learns more than one medium or goes under.

Amphipods are the forklifts. Their bodies remember both water and shore and make forklifts of themselves: humped backs, spring-loaded tails, eyes like rivets that catch any change in light. They drag specks along the film to their mouths, reversing, forwarding, starting over when the ninth wave makes them. Sea slaters cut diagonals no shoe would trust across sloped stone, reading the slope by antennae, measuring exactly how much of a pause lives between one cough and the next. Their feet are delicate hammers; they are tidiness made ambulatory, and they hide their softness in an exoskeleton that shines like oiled leather.

In the pooled lens, the quiet predators keep one part of themselves ahead of their stomachs. A sculpin watches by not blinking. It is the shape of its floor until a shadow moves wrong and then it is a jaw. It knows the seventh and tenth blows without counting, because a life here teaches your nerves to hold a calendar. When the room pushes, it lays its belly on the stone. When the room draws in, it lifts like an idea you’ve had before and snaps, then is rock again. The eelpouts come in like parentheses around whatever meat the last set shook loose. They move like sentences that want to go on forever, punctuating themselves only at necessity.

There are crabs that keep their bodies like tools. A porcelain crab, cardboard thin and paler than a fingernail half-moon, backs into a crack and turns its fans into lace to whisk copepods and torn specks. Shore crabs in purple and olive glass at each other under the overhang, their gestures the language of the blind: knocks and taps and sideways holds. Some days a larger carapace ghosts the threshold—a kelp crab washed in by a push too big to front, wrestling its preposterous legs through a letter slot before the next draw takes it back.

Tubes erupt along the lee where a ledge gives the blast a shoulder. They belong to worms with patience and gloves. Some are parchment and some are sand, armored with the sweepings of the floor. Out of each, when the long piece rests, a crown like the finest fern erects and tastes the passing. A pale thread of a worm makes an S out of itself and undoes it, harvesting the invisible glamor on the stone while everything that wants to step on it is busy bracing. Between those, muscles of orange sponge and mustard sponge and ash-gray sponge take up tenancy along the wet seams, filtering all the time we call pauses.

Under the brim, blue mussels find enough grip to begin. It doesn’t look like real estate and isn’t, and that’s the point: a lip cleanly cut, a little lee, and young threads can harden into byssus that turns soft silk into steel strap. Give them a season without a once-in-a-decade storm and they make a ribbon, and the ribbon makes more; give them the storm and they are a story of beginnings. Colonization is a verb here that always includes its own demolition.

Outside of sight and inside of smell, microbes turn trace iron into rust and back out into appetite. Along the drips the rock shows peacock where a film of bacteria floats a color that looks like oil but breaks like kaleidoscope glass when your finger rides through it: a sheen not of petroleum but of cells, iron laid down in layers a hair’s breadth deep. They are metallurgists at a scale that never gets credit, and they are the first to make electricity out of touch. If you taste a coin on your tongue here, their work is counting you.

Sometimes a larger mind claims the back basin and leaves proof in emptiness. On a low sea and a long night an octopus may plaster itself into a pocket in the crack tight as a glove turned wrong side, lining its house with torn weed, studding the outside with pebbles it prefers. In the morning, if luck and tide cooperate, there will be half moons of shell, crab knuckles turned over like used bolts, the clean geometry of a meal that knows tools. Then a storm will come and make the back wall into an instruction not to linger, and the middens go thin as intention. The octopus will come back when the metronome says this factory is running a slower shift.

Birds own it by season, if they can find a dry tally mark at all. A petrel takes a pencil-width seam and makes it home on a spring, its body a lamp of grease and patience that warms the whole arithmetic by a fraction. It smells like a seal and like an engine-room and like sex, which is to say alive. Its chick spends weeks in a slit with a thunder sheet for a door, learning to sort the ocean by pulse.

There are visitors that don’t take leases, only tours. A seal’s head IOUs the threshold, black as the basalt itself, eye speculum-wide to read the light from cracks humans don’t notice. It breathes once, writes its name in short smoke on the damp, and is gone. A raven lands above the cleft on a fair day and cocks an eye, inventing you, then loses interest because you are not edible. Gnats will arrive on any still morning and embroider the damp in slow motion until the next cough ruins their weaving and the springtails take their dead.

And then there is the one inhabitant with only the instruments of place. Call it Pressure if you need a name, or call it Breath. It lives not as an owner but as an event with a body made of room. It knows every fracture’s throat, every seam’s hiss-note, every aria the roof can make when struck just so. It runs the plant. It cues the shifts. When it enters fully, everyone flattens; when it withdraws, everyone blooms. It counts to seven and to ten and then to set and season and year and then back down to glass time, drip time, tide time, until the counts lay on each other like strata you can lay a hand across.

Its hands are water and air. With those it keeps what we keep calling weather: fog minted on the ceiling and cashed out as beads, beads coalescing into return, return into a pin-rain you could time your breath to. It rubs rock against rock until both are rounder, and into the bowl of the cave it pours sound—not steady tones, but a current of noises braided like river flow. It inherits a ship’s heart far out in the bay and adds it to the day like a second metronome. If lightning tastes the coast and sends charge into the fringe of salt spray, it keeps the tingle the way a body keeps a memory, hair lifted into brief prophecy.

It is less haunted than busy, and busyness can look like life if you are not strict about definitions, and why should you be here. Its law is that arrivals be fluent in push and let-go. What it hosts are translators. The barnacle opens when the word open means live. The sculpin strikes when the word strike means survive. The moss holds when hold means do not dissolve. The petrel sleeps when sleep means be stone enough to be there when the next breath comes. The octopus reads without language and writes with arrangement until the storm edit arrives and takes it all away.

No track here is long. That is not the same as empty. It is a population of small knowings arrayed along edges: fresh to salt, hard to soft, light to almost. It is a town kept together by the factory whistle that is not a whistle but air through a seam, a warning everyone is born able to hear. It is an economy of motion that makes enough to keep itself in motion, which is most economies if you are honest about them.

So what lives here? The film and the teeth that mow it and the legs that comb the drift. The eyes that become rock and the gills that become patience. The silk that keeps a pocket of another medium alive between blows. The red muscle that learns a keyhole by touch and returns to it when the city permits. The green that is a roof on a cup a raindrop deep. The black sand that eats and is eaten. The pressure that organizes all of it the way wind organizes fire.

And the cave itself, which is not just a room but a way to be a room. It is young enough to remember being fracture. It is old enough to have its own manners. It holds a long breath, and the bodies inside it fit themselves to the inhale and exhale like language to a chest. When you go, it does not notice. It has its own residents to manage, its own music to make. It goes on manufacturing fog and gloss and hum as if those were products, and perhaps they are. You hear the cough of it when you are already out of sight, and you could call that a goodbye if you need to, but it is not meant for you. It is meant for the barnacles and the limpets, for the worm that will risk a wave for a taste of the world, for the petrel’s chick that dreams in oil. It is meant for itself, which is another way of saying it is alive, in the idiom of this coast.
