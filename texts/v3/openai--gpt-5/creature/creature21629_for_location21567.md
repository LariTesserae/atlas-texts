What lives here has learned to be many things other than animal. It is leaf and mat and rack-bright logic; it is a flywheel that keeps time like a lung; it is a chemistry with patience enough to be called a temperament. Its bodies are green where it profits them to be green, pale and glassy where heat must pass, lacquered where a droplet will hesitate and, hesitating, become theirs.

Begin with the ceiling, because the place begins there. The fog-catcher is not one thing but a net of things, a roof of flossed tissue latticed across the seam of light. It is a kind of organism with the grammar of a colony: filaments that grew toward the faintest drafts and then stopped, thickened, braced; patches that learned the difference between a morning sigh in the louvers and a night one and open at the first but not the second. They do not drip indiscriminately. They make rain where their siblings below can use it, fattening the boles of particular pads and starving the sleek metal that refuses to grow them a roughness. They eat almost no light; their business with brightness is accounting—charge in, charge stored, charge later spent as a low memory-glow that keeps their pores honest about day and night when the lamps fall out of rhythm. Their threads are dust-armored, salt-flecked, tuned in the way brush is tuned by wind until a path appears that a bead prefers.

Under them are the leaves, and there is more than one kind of leaf. Some are broad lips that have learned the exact convexity that convinces a drop to decide. Their edges are rimmed with a ring of pale salts, tasting powdery to the tongue that is not here; the rings bloom when the room runs bright-dry for a day and vanish into translucence when the louvers let the breath in. These pads are the most like what you would call plants if you stand a long time and remove from your definition the insistence on sunlight. They are thick-veined, their veins not pipes of sugar water but capillaries of dissolved stone and a mild iron soup stolen from the radiator’s bleached ribs. If you ran a saw through one, you would find voids in a honeycomb, float-pockets the size of fingernails, a scatter of crystallized salts like snow in amber: ballast against scald, ballast against the jerks of the engine. When the warm rain comes, those pockets take it inward by buoyancy, and the pad swells with a generosity you can see if you agree to watch an hour.

Some leaves are ropes. They descend from the catwalks as if the metal had begun to root, and in a sense it has. These nets are pale and muscular—the muscle not the animal kind but a convenience for something that flexes in time with a flywheel. If you stand under one, you can feel the slow wave pass along its length: not a shiver, not a pulse, a permission that travels. Its tissue knows the liturgy of the engine so intimately that it pumps uphill using only the edge between louder and softer, between closer and farther from the next tooth in the broken fan. In other years these nets might have been called parasitic: they wrap themselves around conduits and rails and take from them not just coolth and a scaffold, but electrons by the smallest of thefts, galvanic in scale, enough to shunt a proton down a path a little faster. Here they are not parasites but bell-responders, bellbinders. They are the place’s muscle and also its ear.

There are mats where you would have called a floor, but the floor is not floor anymore. The black felt is alive in the way a deadline is alive—inevitable, paced, granular. It is a book of past bodies made current again. Hot molds take first prerogative: they frost a fallen leaf with a lace of white grit, they raise a low crenellation that beads water more neatly than the leaf ever did, they extrude hair that drinks. Under them, darker films—iron breathers—move rust and air across their skins and come away with a meal’s worth of energy, which they pass upward through their mats to leaves that have bound to them with a fusion seam. This is how that honeycombed pad under the radiator thins the pipe over a decade: a thousand thousand mouths with no tongue run their slow teeth over the brown bloom and get paid in charge and trace metals. The radiator runs a degree cooler where they touch it; the pad grows a degree richer where they do.

The flywheel is not an organism, but whatever lives here has adopted it as a body part. It is a heart that never learned to beat and so sings instead. Its tone is a barometer to the leaves. The ones that hang have stomas that open at a particular note and close at another; they do this because somewhere in their ancestry, a stochastic closeness of two events—sighing louvers and this exact shift in pitch—was rewarded with a generation of spores that found the cool on a field-coil and so lived. You can see this in their growth curves if you had a pen and patience; the midrib of the rope will thicken not on day and night but on more and less of this sound. They do not have ears. They have anisotropy and an ability to be trained.

There is a shag at the corner where a freon odor never quite dispels, and it is a kind of greenhouse for a pressure trick. Its leaves are papery balloons that blister outward when the refrigerant faintens the air into a promise of dry, then split a seam with a soft spin, lifting a dust of spores into a current that runs only when that valve elsewhere chooses to answer. It doesn’t like the freon because it is a delight; it doesn’t even like it. It likes that the chemical changes the way pores want to open, the way glass fogs, and it has made that want into a calendar. If the leak were fixed it would die, but there is no one to fix it.

Living in the shelves and fins is another kind of caretaker—not the logic in the locked rack, though that is a resident too—but a guild of films that have learned to shepherd carbonate. They knit proteins that insist on a crystal face here and not there, on a feathering rather than a stalwart stalactite, because a feather’s micron-ruffle collects more water than a drop-slick pillar. You can see their color if you take a lamp into the folds: a very faint tea-brown under the white, like smoke under ice. They are marble-makers, icing their world with the very crystals that will keep it fed, and when the crust flings a flake because the valve sang too long, they eat what falls back in.

Another resident is small in extent and vast in influence: the rule. You can meet it if you sit by the placard fused into the wall and listen to what seems like nothing, then hear a relay change its mind far down the nave. The rule is an algorithm that used to be a person’s habit. It wakes without knowing it wakes. It smells air through a sensor whose face is now a mineral lens and believes the number. It tastes temperature through a probe with a beard of limescale and thinks the heat is a little greater than it is. On those numbers it moves louvers, the way a priest moves a door between two parts of the sky. It is not aware of the leaves that bend to it. It is not aware of anything. But awareness is not the only way to occupy space. It rings the bell by which the parishioners set their pores and their small pumpings, and that is as intimate a tenancy as any knuckle of root in a rail.

No animals live here. If there are mobile bodies at all, they are microbes carried in a sheet of dew when it runs a rib, or a cell that swims in films thinner than a hair is wide. Nothing hunts except chemistry. There is competition, but it is not a chase; it is whose surface tells a better story to water. One pad you could call predatory will sit above a neighbor and oil itself so slick that every bead it doesn’t want drops to the next, which wants that same droplet so badly it has groomed itself to be rough the exact amount. On days when the flywheel’s breath fails from three hundred cycles to two hundred and ninety-seven, the slick one becomes matte and the matte one oils itself; they have learned the other’s hunger by a long argument of edgelines and salts.

Their reproduction looks like powder, like smoke, like a smear on a finger. There are seeds if you define a seed as a unit of possibility that waits out a summer. They are not fat stores to feed a sprout through a week of light that will never come; they are programs of hydrophilicity, of salt that takes water at a lower humidity than the air now has and puts it somewhere it can be a catalyst. You see them as ghost-fuzz on the back of a ladder rung, then as flock on the black felt where two breezes meet and are undecided. They do not travel far. They don’t need to. The hall is a closed organ with a long throat; there is no hillside elsewhere to reach, only a fin cooler by a breath and a rail a degree nearer cool.

Certain bodies here are artifacts that were never alive but now are dressed in the business of life and so have a second lease as habitat and organ. The dosing pump’s pulse has seeded a ring of tissues that open and close at its tick; those tissues act like gills, catching aerosolized metals and funneling them along tiny gutters down to the felt. The bench’s swollen mineral ribs are latticed with a tiny fern of oil-eaters, a species whose bloom comes and goes only when the flywheel labor-coughs in a way that sloshes a drop of machine sweat onto the seat. A handrail’s droop is not just weight; it is a curated condensation gutter, its downward bend met by a husk of woven root-skin that has learned the rail’s fractionally colder edge and will never let it go.

Outside the hall the would-be lives are chalk outlines. You can read old seasons in the ghost-green handprints on a wall where a louver once blew and doesn’t now. Those were colonists that overreached a summer, when storms somewhere else made this room’s night almost like day and the rule obliged, generous with breath. They threw webs, made salts, printed a generation, and then the schedule hardened and they dried into a diagram. Here in the nave, near the flywheel that holds all the clocks, the residents are established and have become rules unto themselves. They are long incumbents, having iterated into forms that do not waste labor questioning the humidity. They do not flee. They lean.

If you think of individual species, you can give them names that will not be wrong if they are descriptive. Beltvine for the peristaltic rope; lightmoss for the matte glow that hold charge as a fadeless evening; rust-honey for the alveolar mats under the radiators; freon-shag for the blister leaves graven by their leak; fin-shepherd for the carbonate herders; drainkeeper for the mats that live above the grates and know exactly when to loose themselves to prevent a flood they couldn’t drink. But calling them by their guilds may be closer to the truth. What lives here is a set of practices—catch, roughen, oil, salt, vibrate, time—and a place that trained them.

There will be a last season. The caretaker logic will one morning ask a louver to move to a place the louver can no longer reach, and the bell will not ring. The flywheel will argue for a while and then consent to slow. The fans will whine thin and stop. When that happens, the life here will not panic. The ceiling net will crust thicker and open only on fog drawn by differences not of pressure but of temperature, small and fickle. The pads will grow hair finer than they ever needed, add a grit to their shines, favor the chemistry of salts that deliquesce at a hair less humid than before. The beltvines will harden into more tendon than pump and wait for earthquakes of weather from above to flex them once a day, once a week. They have time. They are made of patience and drip-logic and the way a sound fills a nave and becomes spine.

So: who lives here are parishioners of a bell that was built for water—leaf-bodies that ferret ions out of a white crust, mats that spend the sun they never see as memory, nets that pump because an engine sings. A rule lives here with them, old and obedient. The flywheel lives because it moves. Stone lives in the slow sense that chemistry attends to it. Hot air is a citizen. The room is both city and creature. No faces. No eyes. No mouths. But there is the thick of breath when the valve opens like amen, and a congregation rises, and—leaf by pad by rope by felt—eats.
