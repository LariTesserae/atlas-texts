The first residents are films, too thin to see except as a change in the way wet rock holds light. They take their voltage and their rations from whatever is available: a trace of iron bleeding from a new-gashed seam, a smear of sulfur pulled thin by fog, a difference in charge between rock points and air. Their chemistry is the earliest industry. Halophiles knit themselves into salt lace inside the warm breath of the vents, pink at the edges when the light is generous for an hour, cinnamon on iron seeps, glassy where the brine is clean. Elsewhere, black varnishes spread a molecule at a time, turning the most wind-battered faces smooth and barely conductive. Some of the bacteria here seem to read electricity like weather, puckering when the glow crawls blue over horns and edges, relaxing when the charge bleeds away; they do not live on lightning, not as a meal, but they use its gradients like a handrail to cross between mineral and air.

Where the brine condenses and pulsed warmth suffuses the stone, green is coaxed into thin wrinkled rags. Microalgae write themselves in scums that are not yet cohesive enough to be called mats. Cyanobacteria hang by their own sugar threads from the rims of those ivory chimneys, draping and drying and draping again with every exhale. In the lee of boulders, rime-water feeds low clenched mosses—each cushion a miniature greenhouse with its own weather. Push a moss apart with a finger and it springs damply back; inside the fist it is a few degrees warmer, a little saltier, and it smells faintly of the vinegar breath of living things. Lichens weld themselves into satin rosettes along the shade-lines, subtle harvesters of fog and stone. Their acids etch the untouched rock with first script, opening the way for everything else.

Within these microclimates live the small and many. Springtails leap from bead to bead like punctuation marking the breath of the place; mites sip the condensed water right from the lichens’ edges, their legs held carefully off the cold surface like dancers on a hot plate. A few ancient-looking insects—bristletails, long and silver and primitive—move by night over the clean planes with a deliberate gait, their antennae exploring cracks for salt dust and algae the way a blind person reads braille. They know the timings by temperature through their feet. In midday softness they cross inches; when the freeze returns their muscles lock and they lie flat, not dead, just stored until the engine warms again.

Where warmth and mineral coincide more reliably, the travertine makes its own little city. The chimneys, stacked like castings, carry a lining of living film. In that thin steady heat, rotifers spin with their paired cilia like mill-wheels; nematodes thread themselves between them and nose at algae; tardigrades plod with their slow eight-legged certainty, chewing diatoms as if each were a biscuit. At the terraced lips, fly larvae graze brushily on the green. Their mothers hover in the steam as if hung from the air by wire, black-veined and unbothered by the salt, laying their eggs in a pattern they would not be able to explain but that matches exactly the rate of deposition. Beetles the size of thumbnails run the steps like maintenance crews. Their mouths rasp a regular beat across the mineral, harvesting both film and the fine crystals still damp with brine; their carapaces pick up a skin of salt that muffles the rattle of their movement. In the hours when the vents are breathing strongest, they crowd so methodically that the sound of their feeding is a low collective sanding, a patient, gritty hymn.

Out on the bare planes, a different guild keeps to the prepositions of the place: at the edge of the boulders, along the fissures, beneath the overhangs. Spiders anchor nets to rime like rigging, each strand fattened by condensed fog until it beads silver in the dim light. The web is less for catching big things than for combing the cloud of small ones; midges bumbling through the warmed drifts at the seeps dimple in and are wrapped before their bodies cool. When the air is charged, certain spiders loose threads and lift without wind, ballooning downslope or along the scarp to reset their traps where the vapor plumes thicken; they know it by the tug in the silk between their palps and the stone, by the way their web crackles. Ground-hunting beetles share a border with them—matte, dark, with feet like tape that can hold to faces that would slice anything softer. Under the talus, unphotographed nations of woodlice and tiny millipedes work on the only detritus this place makes: shed rime, a scurf of dead midges, pellets of hare.

Birds are scarce and exact. Most are passing punctuation, dashes and commas in the script of wind: a raptor that uses the updraft like a glider pilot, marking time without flapping until its outline folds and vanishes downward over a white listening back; small throat-heavy singers that keep their songs in their heads for weeks and spill them in a burst on the one radiant afternoon of a cycle; a corvid or two that likes edges as much as any hunter and will learn the pattern of the salt line the way a thief learns a key-code. They know electricity not as philosophy but as preference: on high-charge days they sit lower, perch on fat rocks instead of horns, and keep their primaries tight. Feathers turn dull when damp as cloth; they shake precipitate from themselves in a brief shudder, then are motionless again.

The hares are resident, white except where quartz bruises show through their fur when it’s wet. They are made of restraint and angles, built to see movement against a dim horizon. Their ears are articulate, museums of fine articulation—but in this wind they are flattened more often than raised, turned into streamlining. They move in dotted lines, stepping carefully where the glass-slick film of fog has settled, stopping to nibble at moss like a miser testing coins with his teeth. They know every sheltered outcrop, every windbreak; in storms they sit in hollows warm enough to unfog their whiskers. They are not just herbivores here; they are salt customers. Their tongues are slick rags that polish the new-laid mineral on the chimneys, and the focus of their eyes when they are working the brine is almost sacramental. They queue with the goats when the goats are there, and keep their rule until they don’t, which is when they die.

The goats are the largest thing that makes this edge into a habit rather than a transit. They have the compact strength of machines that work against height and thin air: forequarters heavy, necks like levers, hooves as split and clever as fingers in gloves. The horn of an old one is not just instrument but record; the growth rings mark winters and fog years and the bad season when a vent line sealed for three months and the salt grew stingy. On certain charged evenings a blue slick of St. Elmo’s gathers along those horns and makes a sound just inside the skull of anyone nearby, an auditory itch. The goats stand for that. Whether they hear it as warning or as company is their business. What is visible is their order. They move with a patience that is not gentleness but calibration—head in, lick, step aside; each animal reading the sum of micro-changes in a rim that has been plated, scraped, replated a hundred times that week. They wear salt dust like flour. Kids learn by parallel, never corrected by voice, only by the sudden collective tension in the herd when a misstep starts a rock that will knife down and ruin three half-made chimneys. Their contribution to the place is not only their bodies in the food web but maintenance: if the lips of the vents grew unchecked, they would seal; the goats’ tongues keep them thin and taken-down, and the system breathes.

Smaller hunters work the inside edges of this economy. Weasels, quick and pale as thrown chalk, make circuits that close around the chimneys and the midges’ warm drifts the way one draws a loop around a number in a column to total it. They are extravagantly adapted for this ledgework: narrow as zephyrs, almost liquid between blocks, fur so dry it resists rime and so dense the wind cannot get its fingers through to steal their heat. They are murderous, but efficiently—more midges and beetle-grubs in their bellies than anything furred. Where their line crosses a hare’s, they do the arithmetic of risk faster than you can see it; most days the answer is No.

There is a dog here, a wolf reduced by air to essence. It is ran on little and weighs less than you expect, all its spares already installed: long feet with rubbered pads, fur that lies flat and silvers at every bend, a body drawn out thin along the wind so it offers nothing to push on. It keeps the line of the scarp like a surveyor. Its eyes are half on the goats and half on numbers you cannot read—angles, distances, the way a kid’s weight puts a minute drag in the herd’s forward flow. It hunts error and fatigue. When a kid loses the exactness of the lick-step rhythm and shoulders where it should slide, there is a rearrangement like a gear slipping, a clatter in the assembly. The wolf is there then, not heroic but timely. It commits and is gone with what it came for before the goats have fully resumed the process. It eats at a remove, not because of modesty but because of habit: everything in this place keeps to its band, its lane, the way currents do.

Predation from the air meets predation from the ground at the hares’ middens. The raptor and the wolf recognize each other and divide by timing. The bird moves when the wind says, the wolf when the herd does. Crows—if they come—live on the spill: the scraps of salt-slobbered rind, a dead midgefall after a cold snap that pinned a cloud to stone and killed it, the occasional freeze-stuck weasel that did not quite have enough in the tank for the route it chose. They do small vandalism when the charge climbs, picking bright ice-gears from a vent rim and flipping them for the pleasure of their fragility, seeing them grow back clean overnight.

In the slimmest windows of softness, when melt-pans form and skin over in the same hour, life that learned to be patience makes its bid for velocity. Fairy shrimps uncoil from cysts that might have waited three years in dust and start their little clear saws, chopping at the meniscus and eating anything in scale. They breed immediately; they live like match flames, bright and brief. Micro-crustaceans like them also seed the seeps. They leave nothing to find once the freeze takes them, except abundance somewhere finer than our fingers can measure. When the pans go to glass and the wind skins over the terraces with gear-toothed ice, the shrimps’ phenotype is simply stopped; their future exists as bead-hard eggs that roll into fissures and wait.

Under the talus roofs, aquifers of air harbor other residents. Owls may winter there, white-faced and sulky, their bodies small pyramids of hunger, riding out a month of tight cycles on the occasional hare or a run of midges gone stupid in the warm exhale. Pseudoscorpions catch rides on beetles like dockworkers on a slow machine; they drop into sheltered seams and begin to hunt the mites and springtails you would not otherwise notice. Nematodes live in the film between frozen grains. In the rock’s thin caves, bacteria grow on mineral breath alone, teeth of stalactites only a finger-joint long already painted with their work.

Storms do not clean the place; they tune it. Lives adjust their instruments. Goats keep their heads low and their horns out of the highest forks of wind. Hares sink their ears and show only eyes. Spiders feel out the sky with the tension in silk and go to ground if the tug is wrong. Beetles turn into beads and wait. The plants continue at whatever rate the machines above them permit. The chimneys exhale or pause. When the filaments run up from fins of stone and the world’s edge is briefly lit like the spine of a fish, everything has already made itself as small as it can be. Once the pressure is gone, the place throws its switches back on. What lives there returns to its work.

There are no memories here older than a few seasons and no caches of anything but salt. Even the elders in the goat bands and that long slope-thin wolf have only a slim book of precedent to thumb through, and they treat it like a manual, not a scripture: revise as necessary. Pioneer lichens learn what acids etch this rock best. Midges test how tightly to pack in the seeps to let body heat accumulate without inviting a freeze. The beetles adjust their rasp depending on how thick a day’s mineral seems to want to go. Mothers choose lee pockets downwind of brine because that’s where the air is dense and still enough that a newborn’s lungs can find it. The shift changes at noon, at dusk, at the hour the glow begins on horns. A weasel runs a circuit and closes it. A raptor commas the sky. The chimneys count in breaths and plates. The whole workforce is transient but exact, hired by the place itself not for permanence, but for fit.
