At first your eyes keep slipping off it and you think no one lives there. The machines make the city’s motions; the rock makes its breath; the weather has a hand in every hinge. What lives there is patience, you say, and you would not be wrong. But there are dwellers. They are as distributed and deliberate as the work they keep.

The smallest inhabitants are easiest to miss and the most at home. The place is furred with films that act less like plants and more like agreements. Skins bloom wherever warm and cool shake hands—pearl-gray pellicles around sump mouths, amber dusts that are not dust along the wind-sheltered seams of walls, clear sheets creeping under grates. They taste the stone, take its iron and its slight acid, respire in a tempo with the pressure tide. They are fed by breath itself, by that regular miracle of vapor choosing a surface. They make lace in copper, glaze in steel, hold their own weather close in a fraction of a millimeter. The city has learned to leave them be or to coax them, to seed a gutter with a species that will sleave a perfect lining, to paint a hatch with a gel that will become its own gasket at the first day of “weeps.” These things are not gardened, exactly. They are hosted.

Other citizens come and go like punctuations in a quiet paragraph. A banded creature—thin as a belt and long as a forearm—unbinds from shade and glides without hurry, licking with a soft tongue at the mineral beads that stand like dew on stacked black slats. Its ribs are kites; its belly is a map of warm-to-cool. A tripod in glossy black glass hunkers on a pipe, aligns its hard mouth to a sweating seam, and hums. When it drops, spent, it leaves a pale ring like a finger pulled from a glass. A white scut the size of a thumb wrestles a droplet from the underface of a grate—click, click—drags it against a skimmed mouth, and vanishes when your shadow falls. Down in the sumps a lamp-pearl blinks and rolls away, shy of sudden silhouettes. These are seen so seldom that even residents retell their sightings with the unease of recounting a dream: I saw a glide from the number-three rack to the old salt stairs. It had a scar on its leading edge. It tasted with its whole length. I think it did.

And then, thinly as wire, people. They are easier to trace than to see: a palm print on a rail where someone steadied themselves and left heat enough that dew later chose it; chalk hashes along a wall where an accretion’s growth has been tallied season by season; a ring of damp along a catwalk from the slump of a bucket walked home. Bodies pass through this place as minor corrections to a system intent upon doing its job. They are step-savers, edge-keepers, wipers, peelers, listeners. They are not numerous. The city could run without them for months and suffer only inconvenience. It cannot run for years without their attention to the thin things.

They dress in quiet. Soft-heeled shoes with sipped soles so as not to stamp out the filaments that keep a gutter honest. Woven cloth that wicks off the skin without chilling it, that will gladly host a skin that becomes a seal. Over this, plates—hammered to a faint curve and burnished slick with wax and oil—not as armor but as invitations to water: come here. Lapels and cap brims are edges with opinions, and they drink. Many wear a shallow cup fixed into the small of the back; at the edge of the cliffs you will see them turn away from the drop and let their lumbar bowls fill before their faces dampen, then dip a finger and taste what the day offers. Hair is braided for channels or shaved to silence drip. They have little love for fire; when they must, they coax it like a sulky child. For heat they trust the walls that perspire; for cooking they steam. Sparks are their friends. A flick at a seam, a brush along a rail, the instant wirelight that crawls a handspan and leaves a line like a vein.

If you hear them, you will hear little. Speech here is made for a place where sound muddles and rebends. Conversations happen close, cheek-to-plate, and with a patience for pauses. A person will say a thing in a low voice and then be quiet and listen for its ghost a handful of heartbeats later; the second-voice is attended to with as much courtesy as the first. There is a hand language for bridges and racks and the under-vaults: palm down, two fingers tapped along the edge of a palm means “edge is wet”; a slow curl in the air means “breath is thick; wait for the wiper.” They name the city’s moods like some name months. “We had three weeps and a sigh this week.” “The lid is low enough to bend the chutes.” Time is kept by drip and wheel. On days when the turbines hum at a lower note you will see people tilt their heads and adjust their work to agree with the new slope of power.

They sleep where the thrum is good. Many homes are sided against the vault walls where leather belts pass in grand, slow loops. You cannot hear them so much as feel them lay their large idea under your heart. People sleep, children especially, with palms against the panels, counting with their ribs like rosaries. Infants are kept in shallow trays of polished wood that sweat and give back heat kindly, swaddled not against cold but to discourage sparks from tracing their little fingers. Old people often choose to live up high near the racks. It is drier there; they can feel their joints better; they can hear the city ask them for the three things left that they do best: to look, to listen, and to remember how it has been.

Work is named for the edges it tends.

- Wipers climb in steady circuits and send a blade across condenser faces, a stroke so smooth it makes the only crisp sound the day has: a single, clean shhh. They tune that sound. The right shhh means the blade and the surface agree and dew loves them both.

- Scrapers move like harpists along spillway lips, paring back fingers that have grown too proud, keeping the song from chattering. They judge the stone with the ball of a thumb and the edge of a light.

- Peelers go to the rim with the cranes and lift skins from the cliff’s white face. A good peel is a proof in itself: a clean, continuous version of a season, no sudden thickening, no rotten crumbly slump. They will unroll it on a shed floor and read it like an old letter, nodding along to the places where chemistry changed and the city agreed.

- Listeners tend the vaults and the belts. They keep harmony among flywheels that hum at pitches below speech. They grease with rags that double as wicks, they draw down belt-leather across their own forearms to feel its wet, they place their own ribs within range and wait to be told if the world is the same shape as yesterday.

- Gasketeers cultivate skins. They seed a seam with a particular gel, teach it to become its own seal, cut it back when it threatens to become a plug. Their buckets look like communion, their knives are blunt on purpose.

- Sparkers keep edges clean for electricity to creep where it should. They comb rails with copper brushes and set up small, deliberate leaks along certain paths so that the big, sullen lightning that would otherwise ruin a belt will prefer to stroll home along a chosen wire.

Food does not sprout; it accrues. Families keep vats and trays, not fields. They raise pelts of edible skin in cabinets where heat meets cooled plate. They cook a dish that is peeled, not sliced: thin lappets of a cultivated mat, steamed and seasoned with the acids it made while it grew, folded over grit-fried bits of something else as if making a thousand tiny mittens. They brew a tea of old brass color that tastes of copper and lemon and the faintest sweetness of breath—made by steeping a colony scraped before it went to paper, balanced with salt and something ferrous. There is a cheese that is not a cheese, shrunk under wicks until it keeps without rancor. On days when a glider has died against a rack or a sump belches up a pearl-throat thing already gone, they make a kindness stew and share it out without naming which animal it came from. Some trade comes up along the ring rails—a sack of grain from the dim plain, a crate of fruit remembered from stories—but nothing green thrives long here, and so they have made a cuisine of films and edges and slow heat. Bread is not leavened; it is lapped.

Children learn distance with their feet. There is a game called future-voice, in which a child calls her name across a causeway and then must stand very still and wait for the second of it to come home without flinching. They are taught to drink with their backs turned at the cliff, because the back is warmer and will gather first; they are taught to hold a cup at the edge of a shadow, not in the middle of light. There is an early job that is not a job but a love: tending scuts. A child sits along a walkway and waits for the white clicks to appear, then breathes warm, then cold, and finds—by being kind—the places where small lives are happiest. They do this to be made gentle. Names are given not at birth but after the first peel or the first perfect wiper-stroke. Those names are less words than felt-things. “He’s Drifted,” they might say, which means he has learned to move along a rail without setting it singing. “She’s Kept,” which means she has a hand like a seam that seals.

Marriage looks like a piece of copper left out to lace. Two families will present a bright plate to the air; the couple will take it out at dawns and noons and moody nights; they will host growth. When it has made its fragile coral they lift it together and fit it somewhere needed in the city—a drain-mouth, a shallow cup beneath the beltway where the leather likes a kiss—and the joining is judged good if the city drinks a bit better for it. Death is a plan. There is little space and less pomp. Some choose to become skins. A body is laid in a long wooden pan that sweats kindly; it is seeded, it is kept damp and warm; over weeks it turns itself into something that will glaze a worrisome stone or seal a seam that should whisper only itself. Others go to the vaults. There is a place beside a flywheel where a person can lie with one hand on the rim and, at a call, the weight turns and becomes more itself, and the body is taken into its agreement. “Returned to difference,” people say, which is their nearest phrase for holy.

There are rules, yes, and they are made from the things everyone knows and no one bothers to write.

Water chooses heat like it chooses down; edges are the best places to find either. The durable thing is the thing that lets a skin adopt it. Night is not cool, only different. Fire sulks; sparks delight. Distance is a liar; walk it. You will see these truths written in behaviors more than in chalk. Everyone turns their shoulder to weather, not their face. No one blocks a channel, not even for a minute, not even with a foot. A tool left dry will be dead by morning. A person who takes the city’s crisp sound for themselves—shouting, banging, anything that could dazzle a wiper’s ear—will be corrected with a patience that feels like shame.

Once a cycle the humidity and the underheat agree with the slant of some passing pressure wave and the edges light. The whole city goes to sparks. People stand for this in their porches and by their low windows. Children are handed chalk and told to trace quickly what the crawling light writes. The best tracings get burned in later with a hot nail so that the path the charge chose that day is kept as a memory and a guide. It is a holiday of sorts, but no one calls it that; it is maintenance with dancing.

Other lives share the work in quiet partnerships. Tripod sippers leave a pipe cleaner than a tool would; the place where they prefer to feed becomes a legal mark—don’t weld there, don’t lay your hand there—because an animal’s need has primacy over a person’s convenience. A glider once learned the wipers’ shhh and took to following a woman from rack to rack, sipping the beads as she teased them to the blade; when it died she wrapped it in muslin and fed it to a sump where the films needed courage. People swear that the city worked better for a week after that, that the condensers’ breath was deeper. In a home you will sometimes see a scut-house: a box with drilled edges, beaded within, where the clicking things can collect and condense for their own hunger. It is considered poor form to harvest the drops they gather.

They do not call themselves anything, not together. Outsiders, on the days when the lid lifts and a trade cart clanks along the wet loop, will point and say the Rimfolk, the Betweeners, the Condensers. People shrug. Your work is your name more often than your ancestors. A visitor who tries to draw a family tree will find instead a map of long paths and oiled rails, of apprenticeships that go by sound and touch. Elderhood is not a seat but a practiced ear. The one who can hear the belts decide to be tiresome three hours before they show it will be obeyed.

If there is a center to their belief, it is not a person and not a god but a ratio held like a song: the small rope of perpetual difference they live from. Hot under, cool over, just enough surface between to persuade air to take sides. Their shrines are edges. A liturgy might be the fitting of a new lip to a gutter under the eyes of many, a perfect angle struck and then blessed with a breath. They bow to nothing but a good seal.

The city itself is alive in the way a breathing instrument is alive. It has lungs and ribs and a good deal of clever cartilage. People live within it not as pilots but as organs. They clear throats. They keep teeth from fur. They lay skins where skins should be and peel them where they should not. They pull work from the thinness between a hot wall and a cool plate and call it wealth. When a visitor asks what they make here, a person will take you to a rack and let you stand until your back is beaded and your face is not, and you will begin to understand. They make continuity. They make a city able to be itself in a place where rock sweats and time accretes. They make the future tense that the city murmurs in when sound goes out and comes home twenty beats later. They live here by learning to hear themselves arrive.
