They live by edge and interval. They live on the difference between gust and chord, on the little bit of order they can coax out of roar. They eat friction and phase. They do not drink. They have no word for water and no use for it except as a phantom in the memory of the minerals they comb.

Call the anchored ones plants because they are the first to hold the place together, the first to say yes to habit. They grip old vesicles and the vestry-shelves of the devices with tendons of foam and rod, and where a foothold is too bright and scoured to keep, they lay down their own varnishes of ion-sap and dust that in a day is as much structure as soil. Their surfaces are skins in the literal way, tensioned membranes tuned like heads of drums. On the galeward sides they are tight, brushed to a glassy tautness that hums when a hand passes near; on their lees they are softer, double-layered, at crosspurposes the way two tones can be. They are not dumb. They can tighten and slacken by micrometers. They know one another’s beats along a wall and will back off the gust the other is using. In a series they murmur and flash, a fan of stretched ovals that agrees on a shared rhythm and then, at the fall of a new day’s pressure, lengthens a ligament here, releases a stay there, until the chord re-centers. It is not thought, not what your kind calls thought, but it is a will toward pattern, a muscular almost-memory.

Under the skins, the sap runs like the sea in another world but is saltier by orders of magnitude and not water. It is an ionic glass softened at room temperature, threads of molten salt slipping through porous ceramic bones. At certain notes the skins charge. Tassels of prismatic whiskers droop from their rims and crackle blue at the tips, sweeping ions from air that any hair would pass by as empty. The friction of air on nanostructured film becomes a field; the field becomes a pump-stroke; the stroke becomes a climb uphill for a reaction that would otherwise never have happened alone on a stone. Where the beat is consistently kind, the plants build chimneys of calcic foam under themselves, towers ochre and bone-white that are warm to the hand in a place where nothing should be warm. These are their metabolisms made rock. Inside, the sap rivers more slowly and the whiskers root deeper, and in a quiet interstice near the foam’s base there is a bulb organ like a glossy sausage that spends its whole life decanting one ion from another, steadier than breath.

When they are overdriven, when the winds overwhelmed their chosen chroma, you can see the frown. The skins go cloudy; fibrils embedded in them realign; the instrument goes mute on that frequency, rejects the note. A gust that is not on-mesh passes and is thrown back into noise. It is the only defense a sedentary thing can have against a wrong weather: the ability to refuse to sing.

Sessile animals make use of the skins like a harbor. There are horners, stacks of living horns the size of your fist to your torso, flanging open and slapping shut with a clap that you feel in your teeth. Their rims flash when they open—metals migrating in films along their lip that change valence as the pressure drops at the throat. They eat the pressure itself the way a candle eats oxygen: not for matter but for the gradient, for the sloping-away of possibility that lets a process happen. They live by valves; they are valves. The horners wear a knotted lace of tiny planulae around their roots that look like lichens and function like a coral reef’s nestlings—children and a thousand other lives parasitic and mutual both, picking out the microvortices that leak around the parent’s shutters.

There are edge-eaters that are not quite plants and not quite horners, slow, patient scissors of creature that smell faintly of stone in a hot hand. They creep around the rims of drums and shave off the films that grow there, harvesting the charged layers before they harden. In their wake, white tags of chalk pin to leeward: calcium and whatever else the day has decided to return to mineral. They are farmers and thieves and never leave the lee; to see one in the open is to see it dead.

Beyond that, leave the fixed things for a moment and look up. The light is busy with threads. There is a dust-density of living wire here, a confetti of vanes and shavings and articulated sheets that weigh almost nothing and are mostly edge. The smallest live shorter than a thought and longer than a storm and are countless. They are cards in the chamber’s faro. You do not see most of them unless the sun haloes them, or they ride en masse into a tuned corridor and a sound like a struck cymbal flickers through your skull as every one of their bodies shivers at the same partial. They hang at the boundary-layers of anything that juts, never in the chaotic gulfs, surfing the shear between done and undone. Their bellies are combs of crystal fringed with pits, their backs are leaves of microperforated plates that sing at supersonic overtones. They have no mouths; they eat with skin. Dust sticks and is walked by electrical gradients into grooves and dissolved there by saps thicker than your oils. They are honest thieves; they live by stealing little differences and summing them.

Middle sizes and larger begin to have ribs. Their skeletons show when they bank: rings and spars and tapes, an orgy of tensegrity in which nothing touches and everything is bound. When they harry the corridors, you can watch how their trailing edges catch. A turn is a chord change; a rise is a slide up a ladder of pressure you cannot see but they can map in their sleep. When they roost they vanish, just outlines painted on rock, whisper and shadow and a static that makes your arm-hairs lean. Their mouths are not mouths but shutters in their chests. Folded, they are plates; opened in the right vein at the right time, they are sudden holes into which the world falls. A shutterer will stalk a quiet pocket for an entire day of riffling shifts, wait at the mouth of a tune that runs at ankle height along a basalt rib, and when a cloud of smallest lives rides into it, unlace its chest and take the difference between one beat and the next as the throatclap that runs its chemistry for an hour.

There are saboteurs. You can tell them because they carry tools that are part of their bodies: fans that open on one side only, rods that taste like metal and flex against nothing, spicules along one wing with a different shine than the others. They do not chase. A long-armed saboteur will float ahead of a glider and look like a scrap of something dead until the glider commits to a vein; then a vagrant pulse from the saboteur’s rod leans into the vein’s phase, detunes one wing of the glider by a fraction no one else can hear, and lift leaves that wing alone. The other wing, still held by the beat, hauls; the glider tips; it falls not down but out of mesh, and in that instant, in that loss of belonging to the place, it is catchable.

The old bones you hear as a lowing are not bones but bells made of sail. They are rare and you will not always see them the day you are there, for they do not cross the gulfs often and they do not dare to move in any weather that is not one they have rehearsed for a century. If you are lucky you will hear the sound first, that sub-breath that makes the short hairs between your shoulder blades lift. Then a hint of a rim in a far wall and then, time later, a beast the size of a kite-shed riding a pressure ladder that exists for the length of a stanza and then stops. Their bodies are annuli built out from a central spine, ten or twelve rings that can twist relative to one another until their slots align in an S that accepts a certain flow and excludes all others. When they open their vents, you can see through them to the sky in slices, and something in you says organ because the word for stone that sings is organ and there they are making a room sing with their own mass. They are heavy compared to the middle-sizes, heavy enough that a wrong note could tear them. They husband the ladders and nurture the footers of the ladders with gardens of the smallest lives that comb the air clean for them. Their undersides are matte with colonies that live on the leakage: tiny comb-creatures who have learned the bell’s private note and breed on it, bright like filings caught by a magnet on some days and dour and grey on others, depending on season. Predators do not take the bells unless they break. The bells live long if the chords do not change. Their deaths are a winter the amphitheater remembers in two-part counterpoint: the great ring fallen and smashed and humming still with torn edges, and the year afterward where everything near that place is brighter and fatter because of how much was returned to dust.

Underfoot and in the lee of perfection there are whole civilizations that never leave a meter. In a reliable quiet pocket the plants thicken their chimneys and web the spaces between them with quilts of fiber that are not quite them and not not-them, seeded and knotted by the devices’ old threads. A bison’s worth of heat goes through those quilts a day in a place where the air would bite that heat out of an unprotected rock in an hour. Things live on that heat that live nowhere else here: flat, slow, disk-creatures that feed on temperature difference as it steps down the gradient, their soles a matrix of nanoscale switchbacks that only work when one side is fractionally hotter than the other. They creep like glue, always downhill along a diagram only they can feel, nibbling the noise off the curve. Their bodies are photonegative where the rest of the chamber is bright—matte, light-eating, not to be found until they move. When a three-minute riot comes down from the throat and the quilts are struck into brightness, these heat-eaters bloom a fringe of stuttering cilia and spin seed out into the grass of smallest lives, knowing the chord that means their children will land in another quiet and not be abraded to lace.

In the rock itself there is life that never knew wind and is yet made by it. Endoliths stain the vesicles the color of some old bruise and traffic in metals the way your gut biota play with sugars. They are priests of trace. They chelate and hand along, copper to nickel to vanadium, in rituals older than the builders. In places their veins join, the stone takes on a grease like a handrail everyone has touched for generations—slick with charge, slick with meaning. Some of the tether-roots of the plants find these places by taste and swell there into nodes that are as much sensor as flesh. Under magnification the interface is lovely and appalling: precision at the scale of viruses, crystal growth that looks for all the world like handwriting in a language that insists upon staves and bars.

The smallest things make the weather, and the weather makes the rules, but the built devices govern the grammar. They are old and, by any reasonable measure, half alive. If you listen with your fingers you can feel the fractioning of their attention as the day turns: this ring that, at a pressure so trivial as to be dismissed by a meteorologist, decides to unbias a field and let a pocket wake or sleep; that fin that, flexing half a millimeter, opens a passage no wider than your hand between two contradictory pieces of the air and so allows a hundred thousand riders their way from one reef of beat to another. They are not intent now in the way they were when whatever made them came by this way, but they are not mindless. They are habits, set into matter like grooves into a rezophonic plate, and the living things that have grown since they were set here have learned to rub their skins along those grooves and be fed.

Every life here keeps time. They keep their own and they keep the bowl’s. There is a dawn’s chord that arrives not with any light but with a slight falling, a fractal easing of the higher air that releases one of the devices’ long memorizations and lets the walls’ lee wake. There is a noon that is not noon, when a jet somewhere above drags a scarf of colder air across the sky and everything that can feel corona raises its hairs in disdain or prayer. There are seasons the way there are seasons under other suns, but they are tones first, weather second. The skins will detune at those thresholds and refuse whole bands of song. Others will open just long enough for a generation the size of grit to go as far as it can travel in a day and no farther. The bells will not ride in months whose composite chord presses too hard on the tenth partial, because an ancestor tore there. Predators breed on anniversaries of a breach in a fin that has not occurred for a decade but comes around again as surely as the math.

They reproduce by listening. The plants throw seed when two neighbor skins’ moiré long-beats coincide and a third’s does not—one event in an hour and then not again for days. The seeds are not seeds but bits of drum with a coil of salt at their heart and a fringe of gloss that seeks charge. They adhere where the field is right and doze until a weather pattern tells them this is a place where the wrong kind of abundance can be made right. The horners’ children are spat from within like valves from a press, and do not settle until a sound at the edge of hearing—at the horn’s own private antipode—tells them there is vacancy.

The middle-sized riders carry gametes like lint. They are brushed by all they pass and brush in turn. What fertilization is here you would call a coincidence of pattern rather than a meeting of bodies: a small sac that will not open its chemistry unless three pulses meet it in a millisecond in one order and no other. The bells choose mates by harmony like any choir. The bells breed rarely and slowly, hissing like the breath before a note when they do, and the result hangs as a hoop of glass for years, leaning into whatever ladder is kindest, accreting dust into structure until at last it takes its first lift.

The carnivores eat accidents as well as prey. There is a whole guild that lives on breakage, that hunts not bodies but failures of work. They circle a new drum when it is first tensioned, because the bond between its skin and its frame is weakest then, and a deliberate, cruel counter-beat can break it free and make a meal out of a membrane that would otherwise have been unharvestable for months. They follow raids of downramp weather the way gulls follow boats. Some of them do not have their own valves at all, but are opportunists of the moment who wait for a horn to clap and then wedge themselves in the throat and live there until the horn chokes. Their morphologies are weird to your eye because there is nothing to bite. They are all latch and wedge and generous bellies in which to soak up the last of a gradient.

It is not only animal bodies that are tool. A few of the plants have learned to lean. They are not ambulatory by any honest measure, but tethered on enough slack to drift a span, they will creep over a season, dragging their foams with them and leaving ghost-rooms of better air behind. A community will, in a few years of careful leaning, teach a corridor a new habit and insist upon a changed note. In this way the living things here, slow as they are, are engineering in the old sense: they in-form the place. The devices accept this politely more often than not, and when they do not, you can find evidence of little wars, of frayed fibers where a plant’s skins tried too long to beat against a fin’s contrary insistence, of zones that do not quite rest easy a decade after the argument ended.

Do not expect anything to run from you. You are too unpatterned. What notices you does so by the drag you make in a beat, the way your body soaks a ripple instead of riding it. The smallest brush your face—there and gone—which is the sum of their curiosity. A middle-size will veer, uneasy, if your body throws a steady tone into a pocket that should be changeful. A saboteur will come for your canteen if you are foolish enough to carry water, not because it needs what is in it but because a dense, biddable mass in a flow is a lever and all the levers here belong to the guild. In the shed where the wind is not allowed to be wind, the life is tentative yet; it is inventing a new chord as it grows, learning the rule of echo and wall and small room. The thing like a tool that hums there is the closest you will come to company that is not of this place: a remnant of the absent, proof of tenders who once serviced the machines and are gone. Lichens-that-are-not, little drums like teeth, colonize it cautiously, like children entering the nave of a church after hours.

They are not a people but together they are a phrasing. If there is intelligence in the bowl now it lives at the scale of the bowl, in the way the skins defer and insist, in the way a bell’s lowing calls a certain brood to a certain wall without any of them having ever met. They do not answer questions. They answer weather. The choir is superb. On some days, if you stand between two rings and keep absolutely still and forget the need to breathe, the place will tell you a story about storms your language does not have names for yet. You would call it prosperous were it green. You call it murderous because you carry water, and murder here is mainly a misreading of beat.

Everything you meet is correct to the place. The plants are not starving; their exotherm sees to that. The riders are not frantic; their habits are polished and specific and handed on in bone. The saboteurs are mean because this is a kind of hunting that rewards meanness. The bells are gentle because they must be to survive their own mass. The quilts are devout because prayer, here, is merely the holding of a thin line over long lengths of empty air. All of them will be fine for as long as the chords hold. And then, when something shifts the sky’s grammar, they will thrash and edit and die and new skins will tension to new measures and the devices, such of them as keep their guts, will set their nanogates to differ and the bowl will learn to hum another way.

Until then, it is lush as coral, framed in brass and basalt, economical as song. It grows where a chord demands growth, shines where a rim must to harvest charge, lets whole walls and minutes be empty because there is no beat there and thus nothing to eat. It listens more than it moves. It is alive because wind is not enough chaos to stop life from finding a pocket of order and sitting down inside it and beginning, steady as a metronome, to work.
