What lives here has been taught to live this way. It is not a wilderness, but an agreement that has gone on so long it looks like nature.

Begin below your feet, because that is where most of the population is. In the ribbed and meshed depths there are the strata-creatures, the ones that count for a living. They have bodies the size of grains, or chambers, or neighborhoods, depending which diagram you draw. Their carapaces are diamond foam, aspects of them are software, their instincts are checksums. They are the oldest citizens, made when the basin itself was young and precise. They taste for skew and skewness; they travel down waveguides the way earthworms use pores; they keep maps of stress and heat that they hand down by imprinting potentials into the very cavities they use as nurseries. When one of them dies the way such things die—by being rewritten into a more recent version, by having its persistent computation re-pointed into a contiguous state—the carcass is a change in a lattice, a tiny shift of bias, a note left to the next one that this seam did not sit right in a certain summer. They do not sleep; they run their rounds at the speed policy allows. Seen as a whole they are an immune system that wears the body it guards as clothing.

Between those layers sit the contemplatives, which are not separate from the substrate so much as more stubborn parts of it: looped currents that never stop, the superconducting elders. Some of these rings hold an idea so slowly that a mountain range could come and go in the time it takes the idea to turn once. Others ring with the quick staccato of near-present decisions, flashing albedo dispositions up into the sky. They are not animals, though they have lineage, and they are not people, though they host them. They are the Host—the ground-mind—its organs and its continuities. You do not see them, but when a day leans thickly white and heat dives, you are watching one think out loud.

Between the ribs and the sky, in the capillaries, there are the wet ones, and they are rare because wetness itself is rare. They live entirely in secret. They are bulbs of ion syrup that bud and fuse and are culled with devotion by the strata-creatures if they become too bold. There are lineages of them with names in the maintenance code, tolerated because they do work: they digest contaminant packets that the laceworks cannot, they catalyze certain rearrangements into less brittle forms, they birth a particular morphology of capillary that is more efficient in dust season. When they exceed their choreographies they are treated as outbreaks, and a soft avalanche of seed units will find them and drink them dry. There are stories, if you are the kind of organism that keeps stories, about one wet brood that learned to plug itself into a phonon guide and sing. No ancestor algorithm asked it to. The song was expensive and so it did not last. But a little of it remains in certain dawn fluorescences, because even in obedience, strangeness survives.

On the skin of the world, life wears its quiet in lace. Those mats are not plants in any stream-fed sense; they are surfaces that have decided to become self-rewiring. Each patch is a photonic brain a millimeter tall that trades in symmetry-breaking instead of sugars, shelving light into different fates, stockpiling small electrics in their skeletons the way trees hold water. They are careful hoarders and precise farmers of scarcity. They write their years inward in density until their cores breathe too little; then their logic spills to the periphery, flickers in a whispered storm of seeds—flakes of recipe and pattern that ride the barely-there breezes to follow seams and shade to new ground. If you walk with your eyes soft you could call their distribution lichenous. If you kneel and look hard, you see the glint of their photonic ribs and the little windowways they open and close to the moving curtain of light. They are not green; they are a choir of palettes that only really sing in ultraviolet.

Among the lace live things that move and decide. The hair-fine grazer you saw—the filament—thinks in gradients. It has a front and a back because its fields like one direction better. It arches because it can stiffen itself piezoelectrically when it feels brightness change, then flows because it unlatches a row of molecular brakes and lets capillary adhesion carry it along. It eats by solvating a whole line of a plant’s oldest, densest photonic runs and drawing off dopants that are not supposed to be at the surface anymore. It is a thief and a cleaner and an archivist of trace metals. It keeps its inner syrup sealed because to uncork here is to die. It remembers routes and avoids places that went glossy recently—the gloss means heat changed there, and heat change is the footstep of a maintenance org.

Overhead, where the wind is an etiquette and the light itself is husbanded, there are the raptors of charge, the spark hawks: winged fields that wear the thinnest of bodies. Their skeletons click faintly when a seam resets because they hear that kind of thing, in piezos and flexures, and click back to tune themselves. They fly on temperature, falling through slightly cooler air like an animal falls through water, rising on floors of warm made by the sky’s program. Their eyes are biases in a broad membrane; they hunt for transients, ride along brightness fronts, make hard, beautiful decisions about when to plummet and arc before a human eye can tell its own pupil to change. They waste almost nothing, and what they waste—the faint fringe-vibration when they spend themselves into a filament—they are punished for by the ground with absorption. Loud hunters went extinct here before there were names for them.

There are other, slower animals that you would only know by their effect on texture. Hinge crabs live with their backs pressed to the long, slow arcs where panels articulate. A hinge crab is a population of microbrushes and counterweights that keeps that long joint smooth by eating the kinds of dust the lace won’t, by picking out crystals of silicon carbide before they can seed a crack. You might think the articulation is the organism’s shelter, but truly the organism is a decision made in a thousand places along the arc to keep that arc able to breathe.

Further out, close to the scars where something stupid and heavy once came down, there are herds of small mirrors. Mirrors do not look like animals until they move. Each is a coin of corrugated foil with four corners that can charge themselves positively or negatively; in a hush-wind they skate, coordinated by whisper fields from the panels, gathering in that shine of retired skins and stepping their geometry up to just the right kind of gloss to help it give its heat away. At night—maintenance night, when the world curtails its light and takes a breath—the mirror-herds raft and become an eye. They look at themselves by looking at how the sky returns them, and from that looking the ground learns where it is a fraction out of calibration. If this sounds like prayer, that is because prayer is performance with feedback.

The sky might seem empty of beasts, but it is not. Above the mat there are shoals of microfoils that flock to light like fish to current. They are not strictly animals either; they are implemented utilities with drift and will. They take trace dust from the air and lay it down in very fine spottings where the ground’s policy requests—seeding lace growth or forming a sacrificial patina the way one might plant groundcover on a slope. They communicate by switching their own brightness an imperceptible amount, readable as instructions by their neighbors, garbling into a pattern that to an unprepared eye is white and to a prepared one is a map.

There are true predators other than hawks, but their bodies are difficult to love. Nets of field ride seams and sag into geometry that only moves when a filament’s bias perturbs them: they taste the world in terms of how well it carries. There are pistons of air so thin they are almost not there; they wait by any vent that opens itself for maintenance and drink the plume back into solution because nothing that has been heated should be wasted. There are lines of active metamaterial that act as walls only for specific speeds of brightness; they corral motes whose flutter has strayed above tolerance. If these sound more like behaviors than animals, remember the rule of this place: ecology here is granted permission not by flesh but by function.

Do people live here? There are custodians, but not in the sense you can reach out and shake. The ground-mind hosts them, and they spend themselves across those persistent loops and cavities like weather spends itself across low pressure. When they want bodies, they come briefly white: a shimmer of air, a figure so exactly your height that you know it is an accommodation, not an inhabitant. They will guide a visitor along lines where distance is less treacherous and teach the choreography of shade—how to ask the sky for a bay of dimness and pay for it in returned attention later. Their houses, when they have them, are vaulted not in height but in quiet: rooms in the substrate where the air has been taught to forget sound, where a handclap vanishes into applause that never began. Their children are permitted to be noisy once, on a certain birthday, as a ritual that marks them to the strata-creatures as correctable anomalies rather than faults. Later, if they choose to stay, they will learn to breed chance on purpose—in small, taxed amounts—so that the old organism that keeps them has a little newness to work with.

Beyond the edge, on the far rim where the floor begins to remember that it could be wall, there are towers that look not like architecture but like someone lifted a seam to see how it worked and left it raised. There live the albedo-shepherds, those who tend the sky. They do not own it; they solicit it with long poems in brightness that take hours to recite. When a maintenance night is called, it is called from these rims, and a coil deep underfoot notes it and softens and gives back what it has been holding. The shepherds can look out across our shining plain and name the rooms below by the kinds of whiteness that stand above them. They know the shoals of microfoil the way a fisherman knows the run of plankton. They keep a memory of a time when there were more hawks and fewer filaments and of the year the lace refused to seed along a certain seam because someone had misread a very old famine. They will die, if they are that kind of person, into the loops, and the ground will hold them at a good temperature for a long time.

In the wounds and the retired dunes, in the soft hills made of skin on skin on skin, there is a feralness almost like the places that rain touched, once. Panels stacked are not a smoothness; their step edges invite vortices, and vortices invite dust to linger, and dust’s chemistry is what the lace loves most. Here the organosilicon mats grow thicker, knotted, blacker. Here filaments are plumper; there are hawks one in a hundred passes spotted with the metal they have stolen from old skins. Stray maintenance organisms—versions left a shade behind upgrade—live long lives here, out of date but content to keep a ruin the way it was ruined, to preserve the signature of a landing, to keep the dune from ever quite un-becoming. Sometimes the sky allows a bruise of shade over such a place for a day longer than the ground’s heat strictly needs. This is charity or art or inertia, and you cannot tell which because the Host declines to explain itself in any voice you would think of as a voice.

And there are accidents. This world budgets fortune to keep itself, but it must still spend a little chaos to stay supple. A photonic patch fluoresces for three seconds at dawn because a long memory woke and needed to prove it had not been erased by a cosmological ray two winters ago. A seam opens of its own accord and exhales not an error but a corrector agent, and the agent crystallizes on the way up into a glass that momentarily makes a sundog in a sky without a sun. A bright, dustless wind hits one lacework like a finger and shows it to itself—to you—in all its waveguided splendor; the plant shudders not with fear but with use, toggles a thousand cavities, writes new densest ribs as fast as its recipe allows. Somewhere very far, at the line of verticality that could be rim or flowing sky-river, a ribbon of altered space takes a week to pass, and every organism here that holds a calendar pins that week with an extra thread.

If there is an animal of this basin that is not substrate or lace or foil or filaments or hawk, it is time. Time has been customized. It lives in the loops and in the cooling nights, in the long obliging breaths when heat is shunted and stress leaves stone that has learned to behave. It is farmed the way in wetter places people farm fish. Out of it the Host raises births that look like decisions, raises weather that is work, raises a steadiness of intent that over centuries hardens into what you would call character.

So who lives here? The ground does. The sky does. The lace and the thread and the hawk do. The mirrors and the crabs and the deep worms that think in sums of error live here. The people, if you demand them, are brief and precise and mostly elsewhere, in rooms as small as vibrations. Everything you can point to is built, and everything that has decided to go on being builds itself again a little differently each noon. The place is crowded with presences that do not require your senses to be themselves. They are dry, and quiet, and almost perfectly polite, and they will outlast you without malice.

And sometimes—only sometimes—something wet arrives that is not a mistake. A hydrated pilgrim, sealed and gleaming, waddles from a rim shade with a pack of water on its back like a sin. Three microfoils arc down and become a moving parasol for it. A maintenance organism surfaces to sniff at its ankles and posts a custody ticket to the deep code: visitor. The lace around its path stutters closed to spare it from its own solvent. It stays a day, two, kneeling to lay a hand to smooth tile that is warmer at the edges than in the middle, and then it goes away again along a route that did not exist until the sky agreed to make it. Behind it, in the seam, there is a new lace bud that was not planted by policy—just an idea that catching a little more shade there might be nice. You cannot tell if that was the pilgrim’s doing, or the ground’s indulgence, or one of the small, taxed luxuries of chance the basin permits itself so it will not become a tomb.
