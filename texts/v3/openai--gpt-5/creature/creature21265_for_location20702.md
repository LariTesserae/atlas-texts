Those who live here learn first to be hinged. Their bodies are ordinary as yours, but their habits carry small articulations where other people carry hurry. They move with wrists prepared, with knees remembering three possible bottoms to the same step. You can tell a newcomer by the way they arrive pan-flat, like a picture laid on a ledge; you can tell a resident by how they drop into a space as if greeted by multiple hands, each of which they thank with a fraction of their weight.

If you pay attention, their ears go before them. Not large—no exaggeration of flesh—but trained, the cartilage readier to make a cup, the neck comfortable at the angle that means: listen. They lean into rails with their whole skulls, cheekbone and teeth included, because the city speaks through metal more often than through words. They measure days by the mouth, not the eye—the way steel tastes on a fog-bloom morning, the sour hum in fillings when the condensers are overzealous, the iron-sugar minted by lichens and left cooling along bolts.

Harness is not ornament but grammar. Everyone wears one, old men in knitted caps and girls with shaved heads and tattooed crowns alike. The harness is the map their body reads to keep itself in the world. Its knots are messages, its tails short flags that say: Westerndown is in effect for me today; my work asks Northbias of me; the floors at home were tender with Eastslide this week, so my back is sore. The ordinary jacket has dew-fringe along the hem, woven with microfilaments that comb ambient water from the air and bead it for later; the serious cloaks drip into cups stitched at the hip. Boots have three edges to the same soul, notches they can select with a foot-flick to suit the current tilt of gravity. Gloves are thin at the palm and thick at the heel of the hand, taught the song of rope-burn and proud to sing along without ever reaching a crescendo.

Their speech does not come in a rush. They place words like engineers place bolts—confidently, yes, but not carelessly. They take small gaps that let all the local echoes answer before they commit to the next idea. Language here is full of conditionals and slender acknowledgments, the “perhaps,” the “if this is the hour of it,” the “unless the ridge has moved.” They carry two and sometimes three names: the day-name their mothers gave and the line-name the cables answered with, and the tone-name chosen at their first calibration, a number and interval by which they and their friends will know whether their promises were made at a lucky time. They are good with hands and eyebrows; they speak far in gestures because sound travels far and does not always return in the same mood.

Children are apprenticed first to the directions. “Down” is a curriculum. In school-yards that are nets stretched over nothing, they play angle-chase in harnesses that let them bounce among the polite gravities until their stomachs and spines have maps of the place. They learn silence in the way that children elsewhere learn respect for traffic; they learn what a fracture worries like before it becomes a problem, and they learn to carry sugar for the commons. Every little one has a pouch of bright syrup-rock they will press to a bolt-head’s moss when sent for herbs, and in exchange a beetle or two will climb a glove and be praised as it eats the small orange of rust it has been domesticated to love. There are games about listening for the new harmonic in a bridge’s song; there are lullabies that rhyme with the turbines.

There is not one people but a braided collection of trades that think with different parts of the ear. The Fogwrights have a halting way about them, not out of shyness but because they are always timing their breath to mist, waiting to see whether the air will give back a drop. Their hands carry webbing; they count by beads of dew. They are the ones who stand most often in the Condensaries, tightening the combs one tooth at a time, singing to the fog-catchers when the teeth flare with static so that they disarm themselves with a purr. They talk about water as if it were a friend that does not write but sometimes sends postcards.

The Tensioners are just what they sound like: lean, with forearms crisp as cables, their humor dry, their sleep in segments between sirens. They go when the alarms burp to re-tension the city’s scarf. They are religious in a way no priest could be, kneeling with calibrated sockets and quiet prayers to the city’s backbone. Their saint is the Coin’s Thickness, the measure by which they judge change. They are the standard-bearers of measurement: if they tell you it moved by a tenth of a hair, the place did.

The Tonekeepers live further in and further up. They sit with long instruments that are more like rooms than fiddles and read the city in graphs that do look like lullabies, as they say. Their speech is full of fractions; they shrug in intervals. They officiate weddings and calibrate oath-days because they know which arrangements of cable and wind and rock have proven generous for the binding of promises. Their children grow up in houses that are tuned. It makes them calmly odd.

There are Lichen-Readers who go among the stone pages. They are the apothecaries and historians at once, learning from the painted continents which herbs here make you clever and which make you patient. They know recipes to ask iron out of you if you have eaten too much of the wrong lichen and grown magnetized, a rare but present thing. Their scrolls are scrapings and rubbings. They feed the pictures to the elders who can still remember when these blank faces were blanker, and so the text of the rock is copied in both powder and memory.

There are Echo-Lawyers. They look like monks, and sometimes they are, for their rules serve spirit and community alike. They carry maps of which zones are chartered for what sound and where a proclamation will stick and sour. They will tell you whether a melody you want to set loose on a holiday will lodge itself in a cliff and annoy cattle and cable-sloths for a season. They mediate the disputes of folk whose workshops have sent too much noise down into another guild’s night. Their robes are quilted in baffling and hung with little discs they can angle to stop a shout’s travel.

Between these and many more—Railers whose hands know the sleepers’ names, Foundry-breathers with good teeth despite their meals of salt, Basketry pilots with forearms stronger than ankles, Night-Bolters who paint the low cold-blue that wakes under foot—people keep careful, ordinary lives that seem exotic mostly because care is strange at scale. They cook. The food is what you think and what you wouldn’t. There is fish from the valley, brined by wind and carried in trains of aerated cold. There is moss-sugar pressed into sheets with calligraphy you read before you eat. There are dew-curds and condensate yogurts, sourer than you expect and clean on the tongue, flavored with the pitch of owls. They brew from pitcher-plant leavings a syrup as bitter as adulthood. Markets crest along terraces, herbs laid out on slate that sweats instead of drips. Some days a stall will sell a whole rain condensed out of a day’s notes—little vials prepared at the hour a couple were married, say, or the morning after the mountain told itself a story to the plants.

Their houses are nests that have learned carpentry. Nothing is flat except on purpose. Everything is triply plumbed. On any given morning the table will be hinged to acknowledge the day’s choice of down and swung to suit. Beds are pockets layered in directions so that sleep can be a fall along whichever polite slope a body needs. Windows are filters more than panes; they instruct light before they admit it. There are entryways where your throat is expected to soft-clear itself on arrival to be sure your mouth is tuned to the household’s hush.

Families are broad. It is not uncommon to be claimed not only by the line of your blood but by the line you work. Apprenticeships are almost marriages; marriages are almost contracts with the city. Promises are always overheard—this is a community of eavesdroppers in the holy sense—and sealed twice: once between people and once between people and place. Funerals are not grand. Often there is a wire-wake where the dead’s last measured voice is sent along a line for the city to keep. Names are answered, not spoken, and the engine-houses collect a mild chorus of the folks who made power there and have gone on, humming in frequencies quiet enough to make the skin over your sternum frown.

As for belief, it is not a church yet; it is the habit of a city living on a mountain that has not entirely decided to be a mountain. If there is a pantheon, it is procedural. There is secular reverence for Maintenance. The midwives of the place arrive in orange harnesses when a ledge has moved the thickness of a coin. They joke, of course—they are good at it and need to be—but they absolutely bring a liturgy. They talk to the rock and to the bolts. They ask permission with hands. They keep a book of failures that is a cookbook for this city’s continued existence, a long recipe for not dying on an unfinished idea of Earth.

Some residents are not human. The obvious ones are the high birds; you have met them already as silhouettes laid like lace on the grayer sky. They are the city’s weather vanes and letters. Children make kites after their geometry when they are learning to ride the oblique planes of wind. Hunters do not take them. It would be bad manners to remove your own signage.

Insects are both industry and rumor. Beekeepers exist without bees. They cultivate metallic skippers beneath netting on warm scaffolds and bottle their salt-sweet scent for all manner of use: to pacify fog-catchers, to call cliff-runners from a splay of ledges when they have become too exploratory, to flavor wind with memory during certain festivals. There are moth tides: weeks in which every light wears a moving fringe; in those times, lovers court by the way they choose their amber.

The owls are musicians. Their dish faces play the place like an instrument, and their calls come back in chords because the cliffs keep more than one answer. People here call them Luthiers and sometimes hire them in black for weddings, kidding, but also not: the tonekeepers measure the interval of necessary luck by the owls’ returned song. The owls take meat and receive gratitude and a marked stone on a ledge they favor. If you ask, an elder will hold forth on the proper owl for a foundryman’s union, a baker’s oath, a child’s promise to speak softly.

The cable-sloths do not forgive you for your joke. They are fast and six-limbed and clever with splayed toes of horn and velvet. They flash when the courtship fortnight comes, mouths adapted to scrape sugar from the condensation lines now busy translating joy into motion. People employ them sometimes for inspections—a sloth can run a mile along a living cable without once losing the respectful tension you need to read the line—and everyone leaves lines licked clean in their wake, because sloths maintain and rejoice at once. There are professions that court them with braidwork. There are grandparents who say they remember when a sloth saved them and probably they are right.

Other inhabitants you never properly meet. You see them the way you see your own second shadow on a day the light arrives late: something that argues gently with your certainty. Murmur-cats leave parallax in rope as if a thought had reconsidered. They keep to their business which sometimes is yours, unshed and uneasy. They are drawn to news that has not yet chosen its syntax, to decisions held thin like afternoon here. If you feel a rope make a sentence of its own, step back and say nothing until the cat has passed. People leave bowls of dew for them in the belief—or the story—that there was once a day when a murmur-cat licked the water off a cable that would otherwise have frozen and snapped.

Vapor-hares teach patience. They coalesce where two reflected winds cross and disintegrate when pursued. Children do not chase them; they watch for their ears to come from air, count aloud to measure a crossing, and clap at the absence like good theatergoers. The idea, someone will tell you, is to enjoy arrival and dispersal equally.

Leviathans pass rarely and remake the horizon. They are not whales, not anything really, except that they have the courtesy of a creature bound to a medium and not to a thought. They tide along differences in reality-pressure, drawn by the misalignments you cannot feel but live among. Their gills are fern-frilled and breathe spores enough to seed the next two seasons of lichen. The city rings horns out of courtesy the way you would for a ship making port under no flag. It isn’t fear. It is a neighborly thing to announce: we hear you; make no haste or hide. Folk hang rugs along the platforms to catch the moss that will start itself from the leviathans’ skin, and children sleep with chin in fist thrilled by what they will explain to someone later with breathless wrong particulars.

The animals and the people keep each other. Moss is kept by sugar. Beetles keep bolts. High birds keep fogflies, which keep the fog from becoming unscrupulous, another superstition that works because it is charming. There are troughs where dwarfed willows stand tight-budded even in summer, stubborn and perhaps holy for it; grandmothers sit in their shade and knit rope-socks for winter. And in the hidden clefts, in those pools that remain for years while amphitheater cisterns sit formal and underused, there is the quietest astonishment of all: salamanders like writing on black paper, flickers of a patience too old and too native even for these new rocks. The city treats the pools as shrines without ever naming them such. You go there to arrange a thought, to pour out the cup you held for a dew hour, to watch a leaf rest and wait in water like an idea letting you go first.

There are people who do not live here but come to trade and leave more slowly than they mean. Valley fishmongers who bring insulated carloads of cold, then stay for the off-noon when noon becomes moonrise for a week and teach themselves to like the sodium amber that makes fog look edible. Meteorologists who were artists until they realized all their best work was sketches for the wind. Pilgrims who have heard that the mountain tells a story at night and want to put their ear to the city’s ribs to hear it, then write to friends that they did and that something inside them muttered an answer.

Governance is practical. There is a council that rings; there are quota-chambers where the arguments about ore do rub souls raw on purpose, and where no one is allowed to be right until they have been kind. There is the Law of Quiet Places, too, and its keepers: not stolid but devoted. People vote by weight, literally—they stand on triple-plumbed plates and lean toward the down they prefer to be counted under, because here choices are cadenced and not all gravities are equal in every hour. Outsiders smile; locals shrug; this system has never failed to reflect the complexity of how things come to rest.

There are festivals but not crowds. Makers arrange their celebrations across terraces like constellations: this balcony for drums you can feel in your ankles, that one for tea you catch with sleeves. The week the cable-sloths fling their fastness about in courtship, the whole place becomes bright with ropes of laughter. There is a day each year when the Condensaries sing raw—a time when the electrostatic combs must be tuned as a chorus or risk arcing in tantrum—and everyone goes in gray and comes out with their hair full of the soft thunder that safety makes. When noon stumbles and the day wears moonrise for a week, people make soup that only makes sense by that light, the kind that tastes like a story told to a plant.

You would think this is a place of complication, and it is, but it makes its own calm. Loudness is a blanket here, not a blade. The mills and winches and cranes sound continuously, a low loudness that shaves worry like wind off cliff faces until only effort remains. People nap in nap-oils of sound. Their minds, so preoccupied elsewhere, find no purchase for fret. Work is long, arguments longer, patience longest. Repairs are scheduled on the decade. A whole day can be spent walking a bridge, not for purpose but to see whether an old metal wishes to remember being new. They treasure slowness because the rock is still waking to its job as rock, and everything else has sworn not to hurry it to ruin.

So who lives here? The answer does not finish. It becomes itself the way this uplift did: decisively, and yet in long messages. The human lives that insist along these faces are wiry and gentle and convinced of ritual not because they are mystics but because ritual is the name for doing it right again. They are midwives to stone, thieves of fog, angels of angle. The animals are manuals with heartbeat, tutors you may hunt with your ear if not your hand. The mountain itself is a resident in the middle of becoming, keeping house with the engines that hum like kindnesses, telling itself nightly how it came up through the seam in the world and what it intends next. Even the rules have a life: gravity with its manners; light with its reluctance; water with its visiting hours and impeccable taste.

Stay long enough and you will stop asking for names because everything answers when called in the right key. You will carry sugar without needing to be told why. You will put your palm to a rail to see who else is on it the way you once checked a door for heat. You will learn to place your weight into three kinds of down. And in that lesson you will live the way they live here, on the city’s narrow lips, far apart and near in sound, surrounded by a crowd so well-shaped by its place that it leaves the mind calm and the world, from such a height, wider.
