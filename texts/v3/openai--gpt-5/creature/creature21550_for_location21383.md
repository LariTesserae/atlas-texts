Those who live here have trained themselves to be the continuation rather than the interruption. You could pick them out of other towns by how they approach a surface: palms first, heel second, a weight that spreads itself rather than tests. They wear the same finishing the plain prefers, a softness of edge that invites the eye to move on without snagging. Their coats are matte and ribbed, not for ornament but for exchange—fins and folds that offer heat and ask it back according to the schedule on which the whole city keeps its faith. Their boots are wide, with layered soles that speak to the ground in vowels, never in stops.

They are not pale so much as unlit. Their faces hold color but keep it turned inward; you catch it when they laugh and a warmth rises to the cheekbones, and then it recedes. The iris has adjusted to a world that saves its light for instruments: pupils give themselves up freely to hooded glow and rewrite themselves at once when the hood goes down. They do not squint. They own their eyes like another tool that can be set aside. The senses they trust most are contained where the air has the least to do—fingers, soles, the long bones of the forearm. Children learn to put a temple to the rail and hum before they learn to shout, because shouting here is theater and the air cannot be bothered.

Clothes remember what the hand forgets. Cuffs are faced with thin skins that tell indifferently whether the thing touched is one degree higher than it ought to be; collars offer a small cool against the throat to advise breath to pace itself. There’s a third strap on the back of each glove, not for tightening but for balance, the small cant that will lay a wrench down instead of letting it fall. Metal is treated like a volatile. It is always encased, always dulled; it speaks when asked and is otherwise silent. Even their cups have learned manners—ceramic that returns a thumbprint in a way you can read, that lets a small cluck satisfy in place of a ring.

They are not many, and they prefer to be less. The city is clusters because they are. A cluster is not a family and not a guild and sometimes both; it is a set of heat-appointments, a ledger of pipes and people that considers who can afford to steep and who must shiver and for how long. New clusters are rare. Old ones change by attrition so soft that no one can say when the change began. When engineers arrive from elsewhere—thick-air places with weather—they are shaved of their edges under the file and assigned a throat-instrument and told how the hum goes. They keep their vowels for the stone and save their consonants for one another.

They count as priests the people you would elsewhere call machinists. The leveling-guild has its files and its long, slow rites: the rounding of the corner of a door shipped in with an attitude; the blessing of a bolt head so that its torque will not go to the wrong place; the smoothing of a blade that will serve a purpose in secret under a hood. Their taboos are practical and no less devotional for that. Brightness in the open is apologized for. Sparks are called sins and forgiven only when a petition has been recorded in the ledger that walks the city like a calendar. The person who tends the petitions is an accountant and an abbot. She sleeps in a room where the temperature does not vary by half a degree, because her own warmth must not suggest to the pipe she sleeps above that its appointment has altered.

They eat by recirculation, the way the whole city lives. Water is a saint they keep indoors. It travels in small processions—sealed, accounted, named in quiet ways by the people who care for it. They brew tea like a prayer: a measured leak of heat, a steam you must never show to the air. They drink it under hoods and you recognize a festival in how many cups you hear cluck in an hour. Food is ordinary where it must be and careful where it can be. There are vats not exactly in the ground and not exactly out of it, a kind of stomach the city wears where yeasts teach imported starch the local patience. Fat is kept in cold rings and eaten slowly in the same day. Knives are mostly ceramic because steel here feels hot to the hand at a wrong time and complains. They bake thin bread on stone that keeps the day and the night equally; to clean the stone they use a pumice that remembers the last hand that used it and keeps the next from going too fast.

Children are trained by games that would seem unkind to anyone who expects weather to teach its own lessons. One game is the Blind Fin: a child is led along a row of radiator teeth with a thumb on each, and must call the number when they feel the one tooth that is a fraction high or low. Another is the Quiet Drop: small weights passed from hand to hand and placed on shelves that will tilt if an elbow touches the wall too sharply. They learn the humming of the rails, the tensions in the pipes, the taste of aurora on the tongue; they carry a set of codes in their wrists and throats that will tell the next person along the line if a thing is the way it ought to be. They play seeking games with glass crickets that will not move for a week and then will, and the joy in finding one awake is not in possession but in having been in the right place at the right time to say you witnessed punctuation.

They are not without joy. They have it in places that reward slowness—in the way a fluorocarbon lake ceases to breathe at the exact minute planned for and then resumes, in the glide of a trolley that rides a rail without conversation, in the joke of catching a rare moth in the corner of an eye and choosing not to call it to anyone else. They have music, but you would feel it more readily than hear it: wide-latticed things that hang in the halls and convert the breathing of the rooms into the smallest possible dancing at the edge of perception. Their songs are hummed along rails at night when someone wants company across distance; you can put two fingers to the side of your neck and hear the answer ring your bones like a bell without striking them.

They have names that belong to the plain. There are women called Cant and men called Slow, a child named Half-Degree who will one day work the valve that says so, a line of people all called Fold because that is what their coat hems do and what their grandmothers were good at. They do not mind that these names are jokes; the humor is a way of saying that the weight of keeping the rules is shared and expected, as in any old city where the gods are manners and the devils are carelessness. They make a ritual of apologies. A person who must use a torch will go from door to door and hum the intention into the rails so that those who share the stone will feel the bright coming and make peace with it.

They keep a calendar that is not about days. Their weeks are the cycle of the belt that brings ore; it takes this many hours to strip a ridge and that many to steep the slag. Their months are the frequency at which the faint auroras taste like tin and then less; their years are the wintering and summering of the heat in strata you would not bother to name elsewhere. Once in a while they will mark a true event—the tilt of a new set of fins above the east works, a pump retired and another recommissioned, a line of teeth that achieves a uniform red when seen through the goggles that find that color meaningful. On such days they will wear insignia you might miss: a pin of black ceramic in the shape of the thing the day celebrates, visible if you share their instruments and almost not at all otherwise.

Below the cliff they don’t go. That has to do with sensation. To live at the edge and never cross it is not prudishness but tact; the strata down there own their heat in their way and would not be kindly to someone who makes appointments with the sky. Even the haulers that ride the rails to the lip and lean their noses over do so as one extends a hand to a sleeping dog one has never met. Rumor says there are people who have made a city in the thicker airs, and there is commerce in the sense of an exchange of gradients—things that can only be made low for things that can only be grown deep—but the exchange is rarely face to face. Packages come up on platforms that float on differences, and the platforms are spoken to by the rails as they arrive and depart. A person who travels down returns polished with different habits and is quickly visited by the leveling-guild, if only to file away whatever bright has stuck to them.

Machines here live and are treated as such. They are not named the way animals in other places are named but kept in the ledger with the same care and that is another kind of naming. Their moods are tracked. The fan that seems to prefer waking at the half minute after the scheduled one is known for it and indulged within limits. The belt that carries ore leans to the right when the air pressure falls along the lip, and the person in the watch-house who counts its loads leans with it without thinking, the way you lean when a child stumbles and you cannot cross the room in time. There is affection in how a valve is tapped with two knuckles after it does a hard thing and a thank-you in the little chin-lift some of them give the thermometer stake when they pass it. The city thrums and they answer; if you stand still long enough you can hear the conversation without understanding the grammar.

Other living things keep their presence by smallness and the right kind of cunning. The paper-thin serpent at the radiator fin is only the most visible. There are also pearl-motes that gather at the skin of the fluorocarbon lakes on rare days when a surface charge is out of tune and drink it like fog—then settle back into the black without rippling it. Toward the edge lives a flat-bodied caterpillar you will never see moving because its calendar is the tilt of the world, but if you mark it with a pinprick of ceramic ink and come back in a year you will find it a finger’s width away, still as a year is long. There are bullet-black moths that manage hospitality with the exhaust and discipline themselves to never arrive twice in a season; a keeper of radiators will stand with a net and a hooded light and permit one to land once and then darken the light and let it be punctuation elsewhere. Even the chloride blossoms have a practice you could mistake for breath; on certain nights they will collect the faintest frost of atmospheric condensate and then give it back to the long cold in a sigh too delicate for the air to carry.

When a visitor arrives, the inhabitants are gentle as if with animals that spook at everything. They invite the visitor to sit on a low bench that spreads weight. They give them a cup that will not ring if it is set down uncarefully. They speak as one does on a sleeping child’s pillow. They teach them the emergency signs—how to tell the stone you are falling without moving air, how to tell a fin you are sorry without touching it. They tell them the rules without calling them rules: how the place likes to be treated, what it remembers poorly, what it holds close, what it lets drift. If the visitor says it is quiet here, they nod and say yes, it is a place that minds its own business. If the visitor asks where the music is, they gesture at the rails and say it is already playing.

They dream, as everyone does. They dream in difference. A person here will wake and feel that a thing is one degree wrong and go to see, and perhaps nothing is wrong at all and it was only their own body shifting in sleep from one set point to another; this is ordinary. Children dream in red because that is the color their instruments give for okay; old people dream in flat lines etched on black and wake satisfied. Lovers hum to each other through the bedframe rather than the open air and taste tin together when the aurora lifts its sheets. The leveling-guild dreams commonly in corners that will not be rounded, and wakes to sweet relief at the sight of a file on a peg. The person at the watch-house sometimes dreams in loads and sometimes in empties and sometimes in a measure you might call patience.

This place is finished with drama and keeps to maintenance, and the people who live in it are finished with drama and keep one another. They know that keeping is a verb that asks for the gentlest possible hold, and they have given themselves to it without the smallness of martyrdom. If there is a story they tell themselves at the end of a day, it is not a tale of conquest but of continuation: we did what was appointed; the heat went where it was meant to; the stone felt no shock; the fins tasted the long cold and were satisfied; the moth was allowed its comma. They will tell you there are worse prides than that, and if you have learned to stand with them until the almost-sound of the city lines up with your pulse, you will have enough of their language to agree.
