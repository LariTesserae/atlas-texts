What lives here is not one creature but a cadence.

First to take up residence is weight that chooses itself. The drop is an animal in this chamber: domesticated by stone, faithful to a path, fed by sweat. It remembers the cornice that grows it and the hollow that receives it, and the distance between those is its hunting ground. It does what other bodies only mimic—it waits until it is exactly itself and then lets go. It is not lonely. There are many of it, but they behave as if there were only one, returning by schedule, not by flock.

Around the drop, in the thin sheet that is not a pool, a city with no roofs goes about slow work. Bacteria take the place for a proposition and never feel a need to revise it. They extract taste from drip-water alone, draft their houses out of ions, and pack themselves in skins that remember pressure. If you could see, you would call it a meadow, pale and translucent and perpetually grazed down to a sheen. It persists where a fingertip brushed it away last time. It is not brave, only stubborn, and every day here is the same good day for it.

The white hyphae are the next-most confident inhabitants. They lay maps you can only read with humidity: a raised letter there, a fur of silica trapped in threads over here. Sometimes they issue a rope—a finger of fruit that is more placeholder than pronouncement, as if the idea of a mushroom had been spoken in a whisper and silenced midway. Their metabolism is a patient theft: acids on fresh edges of crystal, a floral draft in the air when a decision is made. No green anywhere; they purchased their living from chemistry and a willingness to be nearly nothing for a long time.

Animals arrive with embarrassment and stay only as long as it takes to be small. The blind isopod is a shard of flaked paint that rethought itself and grew joints. It works the floor’s rim like a monk polishing an altar, sifting film with mouthparts that have never seen. The snail writes a silver ligature on stone at a speed you cannot feel until you realize you’ve been reading it for ten minutes. It knows the seam that takes it inside; it trusts that seam like a door. Somewhere in the punctuation of damp a springtail flexes and closes its sentence. Each of them is milk-pale because that is the only color patience has in the dark. None of them ever become many. There is not room for many.

There is a resident made of fiber, and it is as alive as anything moving. The wick along the floor has no sap but drinks exactly. Someone led it here and combed it into the exactness of a channel; now it hums at the edge of hearing and makes of the film a stream without ever letting it call itself running. Its life is capillary: it grows hair with mineral, grows old by becoming crusted, and then, when hands return, it is refreshed—a new line laid, an old line retired and set to petrify in peace. It knows its place because water tells it where to lie, and it listens.

There is a person who lives here by learning not to stay. Their body is a ritual the size of a human; their home is elsewhere on the mountain, in air with opinions and sky with reasons. Here, they become a bead among beads. Their craft is attendance. They have never brought fire because the rules don’t need to be written; they strip away anything that scratches: the shell button that once announced them, the stiff cuff that rasped, even the rough hair on the back of a hand gets oiled until fabric learns to keep its voice down. They enter already slowed, mouth closed to spare the hush from breath, hands out, reading, as if each rib of glassy basalt were a sentence they are obliged to pronounce with the pads of their fingers.

They knew the seam where pegs would take hold and they pressed hardwood there with a mallet made of another, softer wood. The ladder exists because they were shorter once, or because the shelf refused them and they negotiated. They bring no metal. The bowl they pinched from clay at a fire somewhere that wasn’t here; they left it to be glazed by time and chemistry. They placed it under a drop that does not miss, and their palm learned the weight of that vessel in darkness so they could lift it, pour, return it, never clinking. If they are many through years, or if the one body is followed by others who keep using the same hands, the place makes no complaint; it polishes whatever fingers come into it.

They keep count. People elsewhere strike water clocks and mark the day; this one moves a knot on a thread when a thing happens that always happens. Every tenth fall, perhaps, or every ninetieth—the fat one that teaches the heart about rhythm—makes the cord sway and then sit still again with its new arithmetic. When storms cross the high rock above and the draft thins and chills, they feel the drops hesitate like animals sniffing air; maybe then their own counting grows slow by a single breath, and a small knot takes a whole thought to shift. They are not timing the outside; they are letting the outside touch the inside’s metronome and leaving a note on the ledger of their fingers.

Their work is maintenance of allegiance. They align the bowl when a seam’s nascent stalactite inches the drip a fraction sideways in a year. They retire a wick that has grown too furred and lay a new one along the old wet path, never urging the water to go another way unless the stone itself indicates consent. They replace the fiber when it rots itself to threads, and each old piece becomes a relic that the chamber will eat with the same courtesy it extends to a fallen hypha. They tap the little glass pocket in the wall with a knuckle just once in a season, to hear if the tension has softened, then press that ear back to silence. They drink, rarely, when their joints ache or their sleep has frayed; they call it milk-of-stone or lungwater, and take it in mouthfuls so small the chamber never notices it has given something away.

They are named outside: Attender, Wick-Keeper, the Drop’s Clerk. No one shouts for them, even out there; if this duty is ever taught, it is taught by hand in a smaller voice than a whisper. A child might be brought once and shown how to climb the pegs without knocking a shoulder into glass, and the child’s hand will be measured below the shelf as the bowl is lifted to meet it. A death might be marked by a last knot slid to one end and left there, not as a tally but as a slackness in a thread that will not be moved again. The rock inherits that slack and makes of its fibers a mineral hairdo, very gently, decade by decade, until the cord itself belongs here more than to any living wrist.

This person doesn’t own the cave and they would not say they live here if pressed in daylight. But this is where their mind lives when it is practicing the shape of attention. When they are ill, they become one of the small things that feed on films, licking salts in the dark like a deer at a lick, grateful as an animal in a place that forbids animals. When storms cut the mountain’s face and send iron smells into valleys, they come here and find a rule with no jagged edge. If they ever cry, it happens outside. In here, water does the crying properly and falls correctly into what receives it.

There is another life that is perfectly at ease: the chamber itself. It is not a metaphor; it is warm because it is still finishing its making. It does not live by intention, but it keeps promises—the promises of physics that are, in their steadiness, indistinguishable from vows. Its memory is long for such a new being. It remembers the gas that once made this hole; it remembers a bubble giving shape to a future room. It keeps the smoothness of its bowl and the glitter of its fanned fracture without asking what beauty is. If you were to address it, it would answer by continuing to be what it already is: a body that teaches other bodies how to be small, how to fall, how to wick, how to not overflow.

Because largeness is impossible here, everything that lives has learned to have edges shorter than a hand. The scents are inhabitants too, layered like thin quilts: mineral, wet, an unflower’s perfume, the ghost of skin oil. They do not come and go; they queue in the air and take turns touching you. Even waiting is alive—a presence with a weight that rests between drip and dish. The place is crammed with presences that are nearly-things, and the only thing that understands how to be so many without crowding is time.

So who lives here? Water, in the singular, practicing how to be plural. Rock, still warm enough to be a mammal in its sleep. Microbial meadow, writing deeds in translucent cursive and renewing its lease under every touch. The eyeless few that graze and recede like commas. The wick, which is a technology but also a pet accustomed to gentle grooming. And a human who has learned a smallness of movement and a largeness of patience, whose home is the mountain but whose citizenship is this single room of stone. If you stay until the place files you under “now” and not “always,” you will live here briefly too, as a bead among beads, hanging on nothing until you let go.
