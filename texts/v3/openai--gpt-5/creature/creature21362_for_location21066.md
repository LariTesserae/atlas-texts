Those who live here have taught their bodies to behave as another surface for water. They are made small where they can be, long where they must be: thighs corded from rising and descending shallow terraces forever; feet broad and articulate so that bog will not take them; hands with the memory of beams and rope in them, knuckles darkened by the same damp that sugars stone. Hair is always either bound or gone. Where it remains it is braided tight and slicked with plant oils and resin to deny fungus a place. Where it is absent, skulls gleam with the soft gloss of wood perpetually handled. Their skin takes on a satin no animal grease ever made, a patina of fog and lime and the faint bitter that salt leaves when a catcher has hung heavy for a month.

They do not hurry. Not because they are languid—nothing here is—but because the young land resents speed. Their calves learn, early, exactly how much force a hummock will suffer before it folds, how much a limestone tooth will slide if they treat it like a stair. The children learn this with their whole legs before they learn any letters: the angle at which an ankle must be offered to make water choose the groove you want, the way to listen with the foot-soles for the trapped give under sphagnum that means underneath there is a hollow cup of blackness. They carry staffs not as defenses but as interrogations, the ends clothed in tassels of reed that gather fog as they walk and drip the answers into travel-cups.

Their clothing makes no claims on warmth. It serves first to shed and to dry. Their garments are layered mats and bands: long tabs of woven sedge bark and phloem cut and pounded until the fibers lie quiet, then knotted together in patterns that are names as much as they are garments. The outermost layers are often glazed with a skin of sap and lime that turns them rain-dark and sleek. Under that, the weave is open, so the body can breathe and not surrender to the mushroom-snow that writes white into everything that forgets to move. For their feet, soles of bark and bast, striped by lashings, wide as a hand and a hand again, curve up at the toe not from style but because their bridges never arc and the lip of stone is everywhere.

If you come near before they choose to speak, you will notice that they have practiced the art of not disturbing the city they built. They set their loads down on cradles, not bare planks, so that no sudden tap runs away across a terrace and declares them. They do not slam doors because there are no doors to slam—only mats to lift and hang again. They tie everything that might clatter, and they set wedges at once when a board announces itself with a thrum. Their voices are low and edged with air; you hear the breath in them first, then the word. Laughter happens but it happens like rain—lightly and in more places than the mouth.

They have no words for hooves or shrieks. The lexicon of their world is made instead of edges and slopes, of planes and beads and gives. They name things for how water keeps them. A favored child is “well-lipped.” A fool is “uneven.” To settle a quarrel is to bring it “to level.” When desire rises, it is spoken of as “condensing.” To be tired is “to be already draining.” Even insult pleases the ear: “You would cut against a weep.”

They live in islands. Houses—if that is not too modern a word for what are really precise pauses—are clusters of roof and frame standing on piles over what would swallow them if allowed. They always island where there is a dependable secret in the stone: a weep that has been persuaded into a thread, a hollow that the basalt cannot defeat, a tufa-lip that has learned to grow true. Around each lives a family, which is a mathematic as much as a blood thing: who will rise when fog crosses the notch in the ridge, who will go to whisper the ropes on the catchers so the beads fall where they should, who will see to the lashings, who will read the runnels and decide whether today the basin will want to accept what it took yesterday.

Food is a practice of listening to what the place is already willing to produce without animals to patronize it or punish it into abundance. They do not farm so much as coach. They lay fine screens across standing sedge where the pollen ripens—gossamer nets cut from gutty plant fiber and dried into springiness—and draw them in the afternoons when the air is heaviest. The nets come back gold-dusted; this is flour. It is roasted on hot lime stones until it holds together, then rehydrated with water that has sat in shallow pans all day warming, sweet with the faint taste of the basin. The fern-croziers that unscroll everywhere feed them when new; they know which ones make tongues itch, and those they scald and scald again until they give up and become harmless.

In the black-soaked basins they cultivate films on purpose: threads of green that love this thin sun and this constant bath. Cords seeded with these greens are dragged across the sloped tanks every morning, shaken every evening into frames where the water runs through but the food remains. This they dry in the open under screens that hold the fog just off them. It forms sheets like paper that tear with a soft sound. They stack and rehydrate it with fragrant runnel-water and eat it with the sedge-cakes and stems of shrub cut and crushed until the bitter becomes a medicine instead of a challenge. Mushrooms are coaxed as carefully as rope is twisted. They lean cut logs under eaves where only fog falls and keep them clean and then not clean, depending on what fruit they court. Wood that is meant to die in this way is named beforehand and thanked after, as if it had chosen.

Salt arrives in the fog. The catchers hung for salt are made differently: finer, with more patience in the knotting; they are rigged across frames where wind combs the air so that the ocean’s memory—an invisible powder—sticks and rests. At intervals the cloth is washed in a small bowl. The water is left to itself in a shallow dish and, when it dries, if one is patient, there is a ring that can be scraped and used to make broth sing. The taste of it is the taste of distance: a mineral not native to this table lifted here by the air and made domestic by a hand.

They do almost nothing in a hurry that has to do with flame. Those low kilns you imagined are real, but they are cool most of the time, their mouths like memories of noise that the city would rather keep quiet. When there is firing there is preparation for it days in advance: wood cut during the rare strings of open blue, charred gently under domes so that it burns without cackling, sacrificed so that clay can become its final self. The reward is not dazzling color or glazed pride but floors that are smooth and sweetly echo when a drop alights. Lime is burned because lime must be burned for the city to remain sanitary. The smoke is a collective act; everyone knows it will come, and they live with it as they live with fog. They wash after with lime water, which cools and cleans and is counted as an ancestor thing—one of those acts the first builders did and that the place accepted.

They have learned to let water count for them. Along the stairs up to the crusted cliff faces where the weep-lines are thickest, frames of cord and screen hang whose only purpose is to make a day audible. When this string is glossy along a third of its length, such work begins; when the ring of drilled stones has offered its little trickle into its neighbor for the ninth time, another task ends. A city without bells has taught basins to chime. In the plazas of cut stone there are bowls you can awaken with a fingertip and a single drop; the ringing rises like a thin animal from the polished lime and walks until the carved lip steals it. People compose with this. They move catchers to drag fog through differently, choose bowl and drop and moment until the plaza sings its quiet and then lets it go. Songs here are never portable. Each piece belongs to its basin. The poems are the wedge-marks themselves—rhythms struck along new faces so that the pattern is the measure and the shape is the line.

Because there are no animals, the ways their bodies move around each other have not been sharpened by fear. Children run across the short causeways with both hands flung wide like wings they have never seen. Weddings—if that is the right word for choosing to live on the same island—are made less by oaths than by labor: two families lash a new frame and stand it up together. There is gift-giving but it is not a defense against pilfering mouths. The gifts are rights to flows, held for a while and then released again: you may cut your cistern into our runnel this year and we will take what that means for our ring of cups. No one hoards against hunger by burying grain; they hoard against rot by raising what must be kept where air can walk. The granaries are towers of mesh where the wind moves through and the damp must ask permission two or three times before it is allowed to sit.

Names accrete like tufa. A child is first called for some small fact of their arrival—“First Drip” or “Fog-at-Temple” or “Shoulder-Wet”—and later for something noticed about them, and later for the work that their hands seem to have memory for. Most adults answer to more than one ring of themselves. Between those rings are the passages everyone here has: the day you first read a runnel by ear and by eye without help; the day you first set a peg and it took; the day you first felt the give in a footpath and said to yourself and the air, Not safe, and that was right; the day you sat in the falling-fog and heard the bowls choose you for a composition.

If there are gods, they are obvious and uninterested in secrecy. The pale stone that drinks and returns everything is one of them and is not ashamed to be a god made of chemistry and patience. The dark stone that poured and then denied passage and keeps the water sitting shallow and gathering green is another. People do not beg these for anything. They make offerings only in the sense that they make improvements: a lip is carved, a tangle of root is lifted away from a runnel, a notch deepened, a catcher's angle tweaked. The thanks is knowing and the prayer is maintenance. But there are powers here that they address with folded hands and heads bowed low over the bowls: the air that arrives everyday at nearly the same hour and that refuses to be dramatic; the silence that is the only crowded thing at the cliffs. There, they practice their most careful behavior. They send no stones down. They do not speak above the waist. Elders take children by the wrists at the brink and show them the difference between looking into the cloud and looking at the lip that holds you.

Death makes stone. When someone ends, they are washed with the water from a warm shallow, and the washwater is kept and poured through a narrow spout into a carved flute so that their leaving becomes music once. After, the body is not put into peat, which would turn it into a difficulty, nor left for birds, which being absent would make it a scandal. It is laid—after a time of lime—beneath a faithful weep on a frame of reeds. There, over a season that has no name, it teaches stone to grow. The tufa lips at those places are called for the ones they covered. You can touch your great-grand’s mouth as thin stone and feel the slick breath of water that still kisses it. The city accrues ancestors along its courses. To move a lip is to move a person, and that is done only for danger.

The law here, to the extent that they speak of law and not of level, is a geometry. Do not take more from a flow than you can return in clearance, in cleaning and in catchment. Do not disturb the quiet unless the quiet has become mold. Do not step where your soles have not asked the ground a question first. Do not put into wood what stone can hold, or into stone what water can keep. They have disagreements, certainly. A terrace cut a thought too far into a ridge’s knife, a basin claimed by a family a hill too close to where another family has grown accustomed to hearing its ring. These are resolved like everything—by adding a lip, by cutting a channel, by carrying someone else’s timbers to the next bridge.

They have stories for before they understood how to set a staff down in such a way that even the air barely sighs. The oldest are of time below, where things cried constantly and beat wings and had teeth; those are not told to frighten but to explain why no one has a word for hoof. There are newer ones, of arriving up here, dragging each timber to the plateau’s hem by hand and the hands blazoning the stones with their gouges as if to mark: we are the last animals and it is our job to behave as if we know no animals at all. They tell stories of the day the first bowl sang by itself—by chance, a drop from eave to stone—and how everyone stopped and put their hands flat to hear it with their palms. They have a tale of the day a sheet of basalt let go and made a sound like a mountain improvising; on that day they all stood very still and did not touch any rope, as if all their own little strengths together might add up to a pull too much.

What lives here, finally, are people who have let the edge teach them manners and let water write their calendar and their music. Their virtues are more like craft words than moral words: they are level, they are true, they are well-lipped, they are steady. They place themselves as one more set of quiet devices in a terrain full of quiet devices, their bodies keeping the rules of air and damp as sphagnum and tufa do. They have made a city that is less an occupation than an agreement: you will condense, and we will give you places to belong for a while; you will pour, and we will travel with you until it is time to allow the fall. Between these they live in the way that everything around them lives—patiently, with clean joints, their little violences only the taps of a wedge in fresh stone and the soft tearing sound of algae dried to a sheet. They have the looks of people well suited to being turned into stone someday without complaint, polished by a world that has no need to hurry them into or out of anything.
