They do have a name for themselves, but it is not a sound. It is a gesture—wrist turned, fingers curved to cup an invisible bead, the other hand laid flat and steady beneath. If you had to say it, you might call them the Held, or the Keepers of the Lean, but they would only smile with their eyes at the approximation and show you again: this is us, the droplet and the hand; this is how we choose to live.

They are small to any other world’s reckoning, not so small as mites and not so large as frogs—a span you could measure against a moss stem and find their shoulder near its thickness. A person among them stands the length of two knuckles, maybe three: tall enough to carry a coil of thread and short enough to walk under an unfurled fern without bowing. Their weight is a fraction of a bead that might drown them; they know this, the way a sailor knows water.

Bodies are pale with the place’s brightness and the disciplines of it. Skin tends to pearl rather than shine—chalked in ritual or out of habit, rubbed with crushed shell until it remembers the touch of white. Hair grows fine and close as down and often braided to lie flat, then dusted with lichen powder so it will not remember to fly. Their eyes are large without being showy, irises greyed to drink the day; the pupils change slowly, never dart, and the lids fold heavy and deliberate, trained from infancy to blink without waking a drop. Their ears are modest and set back; it is not hearing they trust so much as the small reports that travel through bone. Put one of their palms on a taut line and watch the face change: eyes half-close, mouth softens, head tilts to listen through the tendons of the hand. They read their city by touch: by the faint tremor of a thread somewhere six houses away; by the softened give of moss on the morning you should not climb; by the slight tug that means a bead has started to walk and will be at the waiting-hook in ten counts of breath.

Breath is counted. Children are taught it as a first arithmetic: this is the pace that does not make the droplets shiver; this is how you warm the air with your mouth and not with force. The first game is to set a seed on the palm and blow so evenly that it neither lifts nor skids. Later, when language comes, it rides not voice but gesture and the thinnest of whistles made with reed and lip, a moth-sound that is more for rhythm than for meaning. Meaning is in the hands. There is a whole grammar for what cannot be said loud: love named by a circle of thumbs pressed to brow, grief by an untying, anger by a palm held flat and withdrawn slowly, with care.

They dress like the light behaves here: pale, layered, reflective. Woven nettle and sedge felt to softness; wraps of bleached cloth; bands of chalked cord; sometimes a small pectoral of snail coil, polished to throw brightness under the chin. Shoes are wrappings dictated by the day’s dew—tighter when the beads are big and ready to fall, looser when the nets drink for them. They wear work on their sleeves, literally: tiny tassels, each tuft a record of a net mended or a bridge retied, a private calendar of the lines they have made hold.

They do not waste heat. Inside a garment, on the flank or under the shoulder strap, you may find a clay tube where something ferments slowly, exhaling a breath that warms the wearer and smells like bread learned to be patient. Young ones share wraps; old ones are given the best of the warm places without argument. To be stingy with heat is shameful. To lend it without asking is a virtue they count.

Their hands are not rough. Calluses here are not badges. You cannot be rough and keep a world on its surface. Pads of fingers are broad and delicate, nails trimmed to planes and polished with fish scale, an edge fine enough to slip under a thread without catching. Tools are almost an extension of the skin: bone needles that fall asleep in the hand and dream the stitch by themselves; reed clamps with pressings worn to the shape of a thumb; shells cut down to spoons that barely weigh their own shine.

If there is art, it hides in use. The best nets are beautiful because they do what they must perfectly. The loveliest windows are dew-lens chains that guide a circle of light through three rooms to a lap where a baby’s mouth waits for warmth. That moment is a ritual. Midwives here are a quiet nobility. Their kit is polished to a vocabulary: lenses, hoops, threads pre-waxed with slug slime so the beads move as if soothed, a felt square for the new one to lie on. They hold the hoop to the cool day until a disk of tempered brightness sits on the lip; then they offer it to warm a lip deep-blue from birth air and watch the color return, slow and even, as if the day is being taught to love the child by degrees.

Children are taught slow and precision before they are taught speed. Their games are hammers muffled in wool that tap tiny beats to music only the fingers hear; bead-walking with a thread, coaxing a droplet along a mapped path; building towers of white shards—shell, chalk, scraped bone—that must catch the day and give it back without spilling a single bead placed upon them. Naming comes when a child learns first to hold a line steady while someone else works on it. Their true names are handnames, and not everyone knows them. The public names are pale, gentle things like Sedge or Lean or Chalk or Small North.

They say the city is a long conversation between elders and moss. The elders are not only the old. They are anyone who can look at a stem and know where to wrap so that a net can sag without snapping; anyone who can tell from the way dew leans on a Tuesday that the Thursday after the next will be a brown-spore day and everything that entails. Some days the elders sit at the communal bower of droplet-cups and breathe together until their breath is a cloud that summons water. It is not magic. It is simply knowing the scale of you and the shape of what you want and asking in the right way.

There is governance, but you would not see it if you expect halls. It happens in knots and in the assignment of whiteness. White is the bank. Pieces of shell and chalk and bone are counted and lent, distributed to paths that need to gleam and rooms that must be bright for work. The law around white is strict and tender: you return it polished or you return labor in place of its missing brilliance. To waste white is to thin the day. Punishment is work that teaches balance: tending a line for a week until you move as it moves; carrying frozen beads in winter from the field to the elders’ bowls without breaking a chain; sitting watch beneath a new bridge and reading, with your palm on the foundational thread, the grammar of give.

Three crimes exist in the stories: cutting a live stem, shouting, and choosing yourself over a droplet when both are in reach. That last one makes them go quiet even in sign. They do not forgive it easily, but they do not banish. They assign the hardest tenderness: you become a keeper of slugs, you feed them paste and read their slow and absolute moves until the part of you that was in a hurry remembers the older clock.

Everyone works, and none of it is busywork. There are netwrights, of course: the ones who string condensation webs with spider-silk coaxed politely from abandoned frames and hair bartered from the outer margins where sometimes a shred of fur snags on thorns and can be gathered like a blessing. There are meniscists—the dew-readers—who carry their hoops and walk softly from morning to midday and back, taking the day’s measure by watching where beads cling fat and where they thin. There are bleachers and shelliners who sit with their trays and rub and rub until surfaces learn to do the day’s work. There are fern-wardens who chart the curled fists of fronds and decide when they can be asked to shade a path. There are fungus-keepers with their basins of tray-grown hyphae, cleaning away mites and encouraging fruiting with breath and damp cloth and the coarse lullaby of a reed-sound, because the fungus seems to like it.

Food is brightness mostly: greens that taste of stone made kind; thin berries that blush and do not burst but give a whisper of sour-sweet when pressed on the palate; cloudberry seeds spilt on flatbread made from crushed stems and sours fermented under wool. They herd springtails the way another city might herd goats. A soft brush, a suggestion of salt underfoot, a fence made of hair with the faintest of shine on it to say here and not there. They eat them by pinch and spoonful—protein without blood, a harvest without smell. Amphibian eggs are a feast won by gentlest theft: watch for a day when a frog is not looking, ease hands under the gel and lift a clutch into a chalked jar where it continues to be itself until it is not theirs any longer. Slugs are not food. Slugs are petitioners. A slug at your door is asking to be fed, and you do it because they are even slower than you have learned to be and because the way they move reminds you how hard the earth holds things when it can.

Spiders are tolerated like neighbors with difficult virtues. If a spider keeps its lines tidy and does not trip the city’s nets, it is allowed to anchor here and there, and sometimes a thread runs from city to spider web and back, a loan of geometry across a border. If a spider is sloppy, there is a way to persuade it to move: you sit beneath its work and you play the line with a reed until the spider decides the song is wrong and leaves to make some other music elsewhere.

Predation is avoided by being a tempo the larger mouths do not register. Frogs pad through the rafters of green and are gods without intention; the people pause and lean until the green hand is past. Waders erase the sky in a passing and give it back unmarked; there is a note in the law that says stories of the wader must be told only with breath, never with reed, because the reed makes a shape the larger world can hear.

Parts of them are mathematicians, but their math is all slope and threshold and hold. They measure in angles you can feel between finger and thumb: how inclined must a thread be so that a droplet will move in ninety of your heartbeats and not eighty-nine; what is the sag of a bridge under three bodies and the breeze between noon and noon; how much chalk must a room eat to glow. They keep these measures not in books but in cloth: bands woven with codes of tension you can read by pulling.

They flirt like people who cannot afford to be sudden. A glance is not much here. A promise is a hand placed on the other’s wrist while both palms rest on the same line, learning how their weights add. Lovers learn to breathe each other’s pace, to share a wrap, to warm each other’s noses before they go to sleep so that frost will not prickle hairs and make them sneeze. When they love, they do not say it with mouths. They bring white from their own store and add it to the other’s path so that the morning is brighter and the person walks in safety without even knowing why.

If there is a ritual for seeing the other world, they pretend there is not. They say it is a rumor that you can look through a big bead and see outsides so enormous you fall out of yourself. They say this rumor is useful because it makes children behave. They say so with a smile you might not catch if you do not live at this scale. They do have days—the fog-fat ones—when the bravest set a great droplet like an eye on a stick and move it across the city to teach. Children are allowed to look until they remember someone’s hand on theirs. Then they must look away. No one says what they saw. The meniscists clean the lens and tip it back into the waiting net and clap very softly in their way, which is to press the tips of all ten fingers together and then let them fall on the nearest beam.

They die as quietly as they live. No pyres. No holes. The city cannot spare flame or deep cuts. A dead one is wrapped in white they have polished in life and taken to a place where moss drinks steadily. There the body is given not to ground as a below but to green as a different kind of above. A line is tied from the shroud to a living stem—a last small kindness of shared hold—and left until nothing tugs. Sometimes a strand of hair is kept and woven into a musician’s reed, and for a while the city breathes a little of that person out into the day when it plays.

They have memory for storms that are not storms. A day when a child screamed in laughter and a whole quarter slid in a sudden shining and did not come back from under the water. The law grew teeth from that. On feast-days when smoke is allowed they tell the story slow, with reeds that bow to the safe hush and hands that remember. Then smoke goes thin, because the city has counted what it can spare, and the mushroom stew sours in a way everyone pretends to like, and they dance a dance of leaning—partners moving as if beads along a thread toward each other and out again, never quite touching, practicing the almost that keeps the world.

Some travel, but travel here means crawling the granite shoulder, finding a hair, folkline-thick and slick with old weather, and following it to where a lost rib might be found or a well of salt where once a wader shook itself. Those who do this have a different walk even when they come home: a little of the outer weight in them, a little of the larger cough in their chest. They sit farther from nets, not because they are careless but because they cannot quite fit back into the strictures of the held. Everyone forgives them. Everyone takes from them the new white, the new bone, the coarse stories of horizons that are as frightening as they are useless. There is great kindness in not needing everything you know.

You would like to know how long they live. That depends on whether you count by dew clocks or by winters. By dew clocks—a day to traverse the ring from east to west—they live a great many. By winters, fewer. In their words, a person owns maybe three times a child’s patience, and by the time the third patience is worn to fiber, you set down your reed and your white and your nets and you sit with your hands on the line and you listen until you cannot tell whether the hum is you or the city. It is an honor among them to be mistaken that way.

What else is there to say that is not already present in their methods? They are what the place requires, and they have made the place require them in turn: skin turned to the day, hands learned to the line, breath disciplined, heat shared, white banked, mercy kept as a principle of physics and of living. They live tucked into a hollowness, inside light that behaves because they help it behave, beside water that stays where they negotiate with it to stay. Their splendor is not spectacle. It is continuity. They wake to dew that seems as if it had never left; they put on brightness they made themselves; they step into a day that is barely different from the last and is thus entirely their own. And in that margin, in the bead that leans and does not fall because a hand is there, they make a world.
