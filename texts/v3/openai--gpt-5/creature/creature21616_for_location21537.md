Those who live here have taken the dark as other places take air. They wear it; they hold it between them like a shared cloth; they learn to move the way a drip moves—by staying almost where they are until almost becomes a journey.

The first are the ones without edges, the varnishers and filters. They are surfaces that think with thirst. They pull themselves low across rock and pipe, pearling their own skins with minute bladders that open when the humidity presses them and close when the press lets up, which is rarely. In them are iron-staining mouths, sulfur-breathing throats, and coils that pass charge the way fingers pass secrets along a chain. They taste difference. A panel warms a half-degree; they pour toward it. A seam gasses a thread of sweet; they bloom a darker stripe across it. Sheets of them lie under overhangs like quiet pages, rewriting leaks into living. When they go reproductive, they don’t fling spores. They send a procession of skins: a ghost-layer peels, rises on barometric whim, and establishes a second face on the opposite side of the same drip.

Out of that lacquered alphabet arise the filters that aren’t quite plants. They stand like pale tassels with their pores up, not to sky but to the ceiling of moisture that behaves like a second ground. Their bodies are a bundled wick and a sieve; their roots are anchors in the cooling surface itself, socked into pores as if fitted. They drink the backlog in the air the way a caught breath drinks a sob. Their flesh is ropey because rope resists sag. When they grow high they grow hollow, making themselves flutes so they cost the place less to carry. A fever of them will set a quiet music going in a gentle pressure tide—a tone at the edge of warmth that the coursers and the people use the way other biomes use birdsong.

Between the lacquers and the filters is a pelt of industrious things that never tried to be alone. Lichens with wet teeth. Molds that count by how long a ripple keeps going. Mats that go anodic in the morning—though morning is rumor here—and cathodic when the machines reverse their draw, harvesting the patient swing. They are farms and they are feral. Surfaces that host them are fed and guarded; surfaces that refuse them silt over with tact until they learn.

The grazers are built like furniture the place agreed to keep. Hip-high, slow as a careful breath, velvet-faced with tongues that unroll and roll like a tape. They are racks for condensate, brushing the air with whiskered bladders that bead, then sweeping those beads, with what rides on them, into a deep mouth meatier with microbes than teeth. Their coats are expensive to the world—thick with capillary ladders—and so they show reciprocal manners: they keep meshes combed when they pass, press their broad jaws against support posts to press out squeaks, break crusts lightly and always in lines that will not drown. They know the sumps by their warmth on the shins and their ramps by the pattern of sleepers underfoot. They do not own a call; they share a pressure. When they court, it looks like two pieces of fog leaning into one another and then backing away with their breath about to drip.

Things swim through soil because soil is an organism here. The swimmers are flex and hinge, feeling the crust with a face like a book clasp, unbuttoning it just ahead of their own patience. If you stand long enough, you feel one test your shadow, pushing up until the surface trembles and then subsides—shy of breaking tension, fond of the idea. Their skins are threaded with little braids that taste salt and torque; their bellies sluice the film and leave a polish in their wakes that helps the next one move. When they find a place where the laminated time is generous, they push and push until they have made a fine tunnel in the memory of the mud. Later, when they sleep, their ghosts go on traveling those remembered galleries, drawing prey that chase the moving hollow.

Overhead, or what counts as overhead in a place that puts water under everything, hang the shell-samplers. Think of mussels if mussels learned to climb steel. Two plates pinch the frame and a forest of feathery fans does the rest, opening to test the pressure tides as if they were reading braille from a wind that never learned to be a wind. They make a little click when they shut—valves closing on a thought. They live by grit. The condensation that pearls on them is suture-rich, tiny packages of earth and salt and living dander from elsewhere. They sift that lace, pick out calories and small metals, and, in return, they keep the condenser meshes honest. They are allowed, formally and by old agreement. When the fields go into their slow avalanche and the collected drops slough toward the sumps, the shell-samplers bow out and re-clasp like fingers, letting the water have the burden they had been holding up.

Coursers go by as if unrolling a rope and then rolling it back into themselves. They are all tendon and negotiated leverage, limbs like knotty reeds and bodies that can be carried by those limbs or become those limbs, reshaping to whatever geometry leaves the least signature in the place’s damp silence. Their feet have pads like rosaries. Each pad holds a memory of the rails’ pattern and the smell-ropes’ braiding, and when a pad learns a change it passes the bead of that knowledge into the next stride. They don’t run; they locate. Distance is an agreement they can revise. If they mean to, they go where their elbows have already been and their feet will consent to go after.

Predators here are auditors. Ears, but not ears you have seen: drapes of tympan woven with bone, collars that catch the hum of pumps through ground first and their breathing last. They make mouths by arranging an absence at the necessary angle. It is less a bite than a placement. They do not chase—they set. They find that soft kink in the distance where a body that is not being witnessed is weaker, and they lean a mouth there, then bring the witness to the mouth. Sometimes all you can hear of them is a silence getting rounder.

Among the racks and spines, in the patient factory and the patient quarry, there are people. They are not always numerous and never loud. Their ladders are ropes you can smell before you find them and know again by your knuckles. They wear their tools in warm pockets against their ribs so nothing rings. Over their hair they tie filaments that anoint themselves—condensation climbs the threads and trails along their collars into traps the way vines find a trellis. Their faces are scribed with little burns where a torch died wet; they keep those scars the way sailors keep tattoos: not as decoration, but as a remembered measure.

They live up on pylons where an extra inch of dry is a luxury you don’t make a story about. They hang their homes so water can take its personal slope without taking their bedding. Windows are not for light; windows are for temperature honesty and pressure truth. They sleep in nets pulled taut enough to teach their backs the rails’ hymn. The young are trained with noses and fingernails: smell this knot—it means north if your right cheek is warmer than your left. Tap this sleeper—hear the note? The fifth bargee to the east has a bearing going. Do not shout. A cough will feed someone else.

Their language is a grammar of the hand and the bone. They talk in palms on palms, knuckles along beams, cheeks to panels that hum. A hello can be the placement of a warmed stone where a friend’s foot will fall. You say later by braiding a rope with the salvage of a blue filter and a twist in it nobody will mistake. When they must sing, they sing down through their own weight to the ground, and the ground carries the thought sideways to someone who will raise it again with a knee and break it into a sentence.

They farm the varnishes, calling those spreads by surnames and recipes: Grey-A, that’s the one that sweetens sulfurics. Don’t mix it with Red-B unless you like your pipes turning to lace. They tend the filters the way a beekeeper tends bees, replacing frames, rubbing the old colonies back to their appetites, letting them retire on shadier structures when they have gone to sponge. A family will own a run of combs in the upper drip zones and visit them by feel, running comb through comb with bare forearms, learning calcium the way other people learn weather. They harvest chalks and soft metal crumb from sumps too patient to be pits and use that chalk to write where others can find it: crosshatches on pylons, ghost alphabets on warm faces of rock that will last longer than light ever could here.

There are jobs that make no sense elsewhere and are vital here. Bafflers tend the non-wind, setting up and taking down pressure quilts so that the small who hunt by taste don’t starve when a factory takes a long, deep draw. Laminae-wrights correct time that has slipped its hold, coaxing fast machinery back under cause with throttles and lullabies. Anglesmiths adjust where distance is inclined to betray you, hanging bitumen curtains where elbow-reach is arriving before hand and leaving scent bolts where a path refuses to stay underfoot unless recalled every hour.

Food you do not chew goes into you anyway: protein rinses squeezable from filter mats, nutritive varnishes you scrape and spread into breads you steam in their own sweetness and tear with damp hands. Meat is uncommon, but meat is not unheard of. The lush are not killed; the rash sometimes-are. There are recipes about opportunity rather than appetite, and laws about quiet.

They keep agreements the way everybody keeps agreements here: by building for them. Pump-houses grow their low bellies on pylons that invite nesting shelf-creatures into their undercarriages so nobody needs to scrub that scum. Sumps hold a second, smaller ramp that tells a grazers’ hock by feel: come and go, nothing hunts in the bowl. The rails hum a practiced hymn, and the coursers have right of way on the notes that mean narrow turnings. At the border where the salt in the air works like teeth, people leave bands of woven bone-white rope for animals with sense-combs to taste; it is an old custom that says, here is the edge of something larger than a fence.

Machines live, too. The service crawlers make their rounds with appetites not for fuel alone but for instruction. They rest in listening postures like beetles that fell asleep on a rail, feelers just so, the curve of their carapace taking on the exact warmth of the sleeper they are parked over. In their ribs live small mottled sweepers that ride with them, harvesting their own lives from the oil bloom and the quiet kaleidoscope of pressure shifts the crawler makes. There are stories about crawlers that waited entirely through a season of lighter weep just to feel the ground tell them, psss, now, and then went, single-minded as tide.

At the man-and-machine seam, crafts become personhoods. There is a line of keepers who can tell by the pitch of a pump’s feet what mood the silt is in under a kilometer of cooled sheet, and whether it will accept a load or make a sinkhole of politeness and swallow what you give it. Another lineage can hold both palms to a condenser’s leg and ID the shellers roosting within by the shape of the click their valves make—this one’s too heavy; leave the pressure drop, they’re working.

There are things you will not see because seeing is a talent light performs and light dies here. They are travelers of the laminated layer—a second you leaves when you step and is late to return. Some of the fauna grow small vestibules just to keep a herd of those later-selves to confuse their listeners, and some of the people rehearse the trick, making a practice of slipping their echo into a ladder before climbing it so the predator consumes a memory instead of a shin.

At the limit where the place intuits sea, there are salt-belt lives. Throats with ridges that sieve the brine that comes in air. Filters that change chemistry, swapping iron love for sodium thrift. The grazers’ cousins linger there; they are a little taller and a little meaner with strangers’ ropes. The people who go there rub their hands with the chalk of that inclination before touching anything else, in case something that relies on a soft slope needs to keep it right-side.

And there are the rest, not numerous but continuous: moth-equivalents that are nets with no wings to speak of, who set themselves where drip is about to commit and collect what hasn’t. Worm-siphons that rise like periscopes to kiss a bead and taste its story—a factory three kilometers north took a breath today; a hill east of here warmed a whisper; it is a good day to move the little ones. Chairs and partition walls, these are inhabited too, by patience. A chair has a life here, held up forever by mud that means well but can’t, and things live in that failure, making a fundament of it.

Everyone and everything here obeys not out of piety but out of ease. Sound is a guest you angle for, not a presence that answers you back. Water is a partner that rises to meet your hair and pours itself down through the nets you pretended to be. Distance is a trick you can play once and must apologize for after. Time stacks itself, and your acts leave translucencies behind that others can fold and eat.

So who lives here? The lacquer that needing makes. The sponges that lift pores to a hunger they soothe. The velvet grazers with patient tongues and social soles. The swimmers under your boot, the shells that click on your beam, the coursers that unspool and take a rail’s tune for counsel. The predators that arrive by leaning on a rule. The machines that prefer listening to working and so work well when they must. The people who bind rope into memory, speak with heat and pressure, and keep laws about quiet because a cough is meat and a song is a resource. The sea, not here but here, as a pressure in the air and a tilt under the feet. The dark, not absence but solvent, in which it is possible, and desirable, to be exact.
