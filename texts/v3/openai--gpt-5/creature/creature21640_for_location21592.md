What lives here is finished the way a thought is finished: few, exact, and tuned to the one condition that never changes.

First, the thinness that is not thin. The world-slick itself is a city. In that translucence are the small firms that lease the place by molecule—bacteria and archaea holding onto the carbonate with charges and threads, harvesting what can be had. They pull from three budgets at once: the gentle red of light that makes it through the mist and shatters inside a droplet; the geochemical teeth of carbonate dissolving and re-forming; the organic dust sifted from the wind, faint as breath, enough to tilt a ledger. Their pigments are not for color but for protest and accounting: bacteriorhodopsins that pump when the glare leans, green-shy chlorins for a light that never softens, carotenoid armor against the clean cut of the sun.

They write themselves into patterns the way frost writes on a window, only slower. Around the lips of little resonator mouths they form extra film where the pressure dips, a rim that is constantly thinned and constantly laid back. In certain pockets the blend goes thixotropic—seems to liquefy under the plateau’s chord, then set again the moment a tone node wanders a hair-width over. Even the pearl bloom down the fissures is a grammar: consortia that move their factories to where the flow always runs and then do not move again because the wind does not. It isn’t a mat so much as an agreement.

Under the meniscus-tiles of surface water the first animals sign themselves as punctuation. Pale isopods, flattened to the thickness of a leaf, use the water-skin as a roof and the rock as a floor. Their legs are hairs and paddles both. They live in the underside of tension, skating with bellies up, rasping at the invisible vegetation with mouth-parts that look like combs. They don’t migrate; the place removes the need. Each adopts a runnel and keeps it shaved of life to the precise degree that fits its slow hunger. They groom the film and are groomed by it, leaving behind tight spirals of fecal pearls that march, grain-perfect, down toward a fissure mouth and dissolve as if they had been a suggestion.

There are other grazers in the scale of commas and hyphens: meniscus worms that telescope their bodies into the tight cracks, skin like wet parchment, blood clear to keep from making a lens of themselves under the permanent sun. Their nervous systems have learned the note of their address. They become agitated and still in time with the same unrelenting chord, a pulse that is not a rhythm but a possession.

Down a slit, a longer life grazes its loop. The fissure-eel, all tendon and cilia, moves like an argument taught to flow. It never leaves the crease it hatched in. Its head is a mane of pale sensory bristles tuned to chemistry and to the architecture of the wind above—the eel knows when a pocket is readjusting, when a lip is thickening, when the meniscus in its gallery shifts from convex to flat by the hair’s breadth that signals the right moment to reverse. It feeds by licking—there is no other word—its rasp long and gracile, with teeth that are more for giving texture than for biting. Courtship, if the word applies, is a collaboration of fluid. Two neighbors distil signals into the drop line between their lengths. Gonads ripen by ratios of salt and sound; gametes are traded into the same old sump at the foot of their shared slot. Their offspring hatch as clear pins that taste their way up the ravine and choose, once, a direction to claim for the fifty years or hundred it will take them to grow to the size dictated by the grike.

Where the wind speeds through stone organ-pipes there is a sessile life that looks like geology until it decides to feed: tone-limpets, low as a thumb joint, shells grown from the same white as everything else. Their bodies are purses of muscle pegged to the rock with mineral glue. What they do that the rest of the world also does—but deliberately—is make themselves into a resonator. The gape between shell and rock is shaped so the world-note charges the cavity; fringes of cilia line the rim and spin under that pressure, sorting mist into food and food into stone. They do not grow large, because the tone forbids it. Each settles as a larva into a frequency pocket the wind has set for it and builds to the one dimension that sings most profitably, then stays there, polishing itself into endurance. Their shells are varnished by their own condensed breath, kept to a pitch by the exaction of air. Reproduction is pulses of tuned dust: they liberate calcite-coated eggs the size of silt that are light enough to ride the upwash into the next laminar lullaby, stick where the right note tells them to stick, and become another small cup tuned to the perpetual key.

Between those cups and the slick, holding on where no hand but the wind’s exists, are kites that do not know landing. They are the only fast lives here, and they are not fast in the way of fever but in the way of inevitability. Chitin and carbonate laid into a filigree, vanes taut with nacreous film, frames notched like the bones of a bird someone thought once and then never saw again. They launch as juveniles from the gullies’ throats, finding lifts the ledgers tell them will exist, and from that moment forward their bodies are maps. Their mouths are sieves that route wet aerosol through a velvet of cilia seeded with symbionts that get a share of light and give in return sugars hardly enough to count but enough to continue. They “feed” by staying put in the moving air, which is to say they metabolize constancy.

They fertilize by proximity at crossings the plateau guarantees: the venturi between two long ridges where the same line of air slides every hour of every year. The timing is not timing; it is place. Two adults drift through that corridor against the grain and their abdomens open to seed the other’s organs, a brief shiver that is more of a calibration. Egg-threads are extruded into the bright smoke of the downwind edge, tips gummed with a mucilage the spray will later set without ever drying. The threads tear, smear on stone like a thought scrawled and abandoned, and the eggs that land in grikes or damp lee-slots hatch into crawling things that look nothing like kites, fat, translucent little grapples that scrape the film in a circle until their bodies thin and harden and their membranes overgrow into a wing. Only a handful each season make it back into the air; the rest return to the slick as peas of matter, no waste in a place where budgets already close.

In the caverns under the constant, where the chord is not a shout but a weight you set on your hand and forget until you lift it, there is an interior fauna liched to dripping-machines. Blind copepods ride the falling strings of water, catching on filaments of calcite like beads; they graze with palps that clock as metronomes against a cave’s personal node. There are little armored things like penny-sized shields, translucent, six-legged, that clamp to the ringing ribs you can see where the air has placed its nodes on the cave wall and sip from the percolation that seethes off each crest. Their shells are too thin for noise to shear away. They quiver and feed in the thick peace of rock. There are worms with faces like paintbrushes that sweep stalactitic whiskers clean of the same invisibilities that feed the exterior mats. All these inside lives are slow to a chemistry you could teach if you had the length to do so. They lay few eggs, because nothing here is lost to surprise.

Among and within all the above is the thing any visitor would forget to call alive because it has no center: the stone-fed colonies that build shelves out of condensation and patience. They are organisms only in the way that a reef is an organism—city-creatures whose bodies are the shapes they persuade water to repeat. They can be said to live because they maintain difference, because chemistry for them proceeds one way and not the other at the rates they insist on. Their “young” are eddies colonized, micro-mazes of pores coaxed into alignment; their “death” is only a failure of tone, which does not happen.

What is not here is also a resident. There are no flowers and never will be, because the glare and the wind and the mathematics of drip have already occupied that niche with a better solution. No insect lands to rest and practice flight; even the kites know that touching is something the rock and water are doing already. There are no runners or raptors, no jaws meant for tearing other bodies, because energy cannot stack into heights; it is always returned to a film. Even scavengers are a theoretic sport, because nothing stays dead long enough to specialize on it. What falls is read back into the sheet.

If a person insisted on something like intelligence, like intention, it can be found in the tuning—lives all keyed to the same note and making use of it in their ways. Communication here is not calls but couplings: a limpets’ shell adjusted by microns to match the frequency of a hole; an eel’s reversal pushed not by thought but by the felt chord inside its skull; kites arriving at shared corridors not by appointment but by the necessity of flow.

No one here is many. Opposites do not strain at one another because the place has no season to propose any. Occupants are clinging, gliding, grazing, ringing, and all of them are constant. Their biographies are descriptions of a shape perfected down to the scale of cilia. They are the plateau’s own extra organs, more function than flourish—ribs, cups, fringes—put to the work of solving the same problem forever until solution and self are the same word.
