What lives here is braided into pressure and patience. It is not crowded, but it is populous if you can count slow continuities as life. The valley’s residents are not confined to bodies. Some of them are agreements. Some are weather with a memory. A few have bones, or something like them, and make a choice to step without a sound.

The oldest inhabitant is the long accord between stone, water, and breath. It takes up residence in the way sound becomes structure and structure, in turn, insists on certain sounds. This accord is not an abstract law; it is a working thing, as tangible as peat. It can be startled. It can be fed. It rewards anything that is steady.

Among the visible, the builders are the mosses and their allies. Thread-moss, rising along the weeping scarp, does what no mason does because it has a better patience. It climbs the limestone’s small tears and instructs them to lie down, leafing stone into tufa sheets. Its colonies are organ stops. They take instruction from the tone of water over their own success and correct it—minute baffles of calcium laid where a burr in the syllables would make a winter of ice too thick or a summer of drip too thin. A handful of lichens veil the same face; they are the colored punctuation in a text of mineral that only completes a single glyph in a human lifetime, but their bodies throw shadow enough to cool a droplet into mineral skin, again and again, until the scarp has a throat.

In the wet open, sphagnum is the citizenry in bulk. It is not an individual so much as a regime. It makes elevation out of its own dying, lifts air above water by the thickness of a palm, and keeps the wind’s comb from biting too low. In its plush are thousands of small compromises: rot deferred and then allowed; acidity coaxed and then meted; a thousand tiny farms run by fungi that teach roots to borrow differently when iron is in the air. There are persons in there that do not speak in the sense we know but can nonetheless be addressed. Murmur to a barrow of moss and it will not answer. Return three days in a row and it will have redistributed wet lightly along your kneeling prints. It has a vote in where the flarks will close next year. It is not a democracy. It is a slow majority.

Suspended just above and within the water live the glass-makers. Diatom skins in this place are not only the shells of single cells. They knit themselves into filigrees fine as cold hair, into hanging keys of silica. These keys gleam dull as fishbone under a certain stretch of cloud. They do not so much trap droplets as invite them. When the wind lays a new chord across the valley, the keys tremble alike—and likeness births structure. In a squall they adjust: a little steeper here, a little softer there, until the valley’s hum returns to its preferred pitch. That is what they live on: not light alone, but the solvable error between the hum and itself. You can break one, with a twig. Nothing protests. But somewhere a pan of water gains a rim and takes less sky into itself, and an eel turns on its side to consider a new brightness in its roof.

Within the thinnest films, on the black backs of channels, colonies of glassy plankton graze the deep swell you cannot hear. Their bodies are nanotextured to drink amplitude; they convert microthrob into sugars and leave behind a faint oil-smell you would call clean if iron were not in it. They are preyed upon by nothing you can name as a mouth. They are grazed by difference: whenever the infrasonic bass is starved for an hour, they whiten and slough in sheets that the wind rolls like skin along a puddle’s lip. They reseed from any hair of moisture that remembers a tone.

There are animals in the usual sense, but few, and they have succeeded by becoming less interruptive than wind. The long-necked grey that writes itself into a question on a peat-string is not common but comfortable. It has a birth-order with the fog—first one, then the other—and both keep it. It does not speak because here a call would change the place it is standing on. It would bend the reeds and ring the hard droplets that hang on nothing and draw a line of condensation that another seeker, eel or fox-not-fox, might read as a way. It has become the answer to its own question by never asking it aloud.

Eels are residents not by number but by duration. They are very nearly the color of a thought you chose not to complete. They take the valley in cross-section, inhabit it as a thickness made of silt and reserve. In the deeper flarks where peat breathes out against stone breathes in, they drift largely still, nested in the leavings of summer biters. When they move, the water delivers a brief clarity of attitude to their passage, a glass collar of wake the valley declines to let go of until the eel’s thought has finished thinking. They keep their own map. It is made of pressures only they feel and of small hollows in the scarp that they visit the way a person visits a library aisle to put a thumb where a favorite sentence lives.

Of the fox-shape, there are a handful. They are not a species so much as a style of motion that finds itself repeatedly. They are the perfect solution to a given day. Paws that take weight as if it were a concussion to be dispersed into lichen, lungs that work in sips, eyes built not for seeing far but for seeing when the fog is too loyal to its paths and must be read for traffic by the density around reed-sheaths. They breed in places farther off where sound is cheaper and visit to run the cool like a fever, to reset their inner weather. You will not see them if you are not already balanced like a reed on your own breathing.

The reeds themselves are not furniture. They are a people that do not own a single root. Each culm is a mouth and a finger and a tuning peg. Their worth is in their number and in their spacing. They grow to the wind that grew them; they leave a little less wind behind with each season. In their nodes live small kin who tax the air directly. Call them reed-mites if you must, but they are closer to bells than to bites. They sit with their jointed bodies spread as a harp over the narrowest throat of each stem and audition the day until they hear the moment when the valley’s time changes key. Then they flick, all at once, setting the beaded cords of chilled air trembling and the hard droplets ringing just beyond hearing. The reeds lean closer to those droplets when they do, because information is falling out of them like a light rain and it is the kind of rain that fertilizes nothing but understanding. The mites feed on that. They metabolize forecast.

On the scarp the weep itself has tenants you cannot rightly separate from drip. Filamentous mats hide in the thin bright water where it sheaths over lip after lip. They take carbonate out, not to build a house, but to grow ears. The shapes they make—ribs, cusps, small pits where a trill wore at a spoutlet for a decade—are part of them the way bone is part of a deer. You might say they are calcifying their preferences. Old glissandos leave terraces because they loved a certain fall-length and would have more of it. A sudden cold summer kills a long trill and leaves it as pocked lace on the stone—empty, convinced, now only echo.

Sound-animals live here, too, but you will not meet them by eye. They are what you called chords when you saw a line of fog thicken across the valley where weather had no business. They breed by entrainment: a quiet day is a poor pasture, a windy day is a feast. Their bodies are paths of standing pressure that move when the valley is tired of stillness of one kind and ready for a stillness of another. They graze on gradients between air and water, leave behind strings of newly-wet rills that shine as if they had always been there. They have elders—long modes that have migrated the same course over centuries and are fat with memory. They are how the valley remembers itself between storms. They are farmed, a little, by the glass-makers: diatom tapestries that lean and bow infinitesimally to keep favored passes open, the way a village will sweep a lane that shared so many weddings.

Fog has keepers. Not owners: attendants. They live in the difference between a path walked and a path admired from the scarp. They are the reason the air presses on your sinuses where others have paused before you and eased when you murmur. They are not soul or sentry; they are pattern that likes to be smoothed. A person’s body is a useful rake. Pause and you are participating in an old craft—combing. Go loud, and they will test you. The next hummock will be a half-pace farther, and the flark’s edge, which was very crisp, will fuzz as if it were a thought not yet agreed upon. Step softly, and the trueness of distance returns one layer at a time, as if the valley were choosing to allow cost to match price.

Reflections breed cousins in their pans. In the basins shallow enough to show the whole day at once, what looks back is not only your collar and the bent sky but an anticipatory adaptation the water has made to everything it has watched. A heron bends and the light corrects a breath early because the pool has had enough herons to think ahead. That forward-leaning twin is not a trick. It is a small nation of film-shedders and slope-readers who have specialized in running ahead of light by a polite amount. They harvest expectation the way the reed-mites harvest forecast; they dine on redundancy. Drop a pebble and they will finish what you began in a different order than you remember. It is etiquette here to let them.

There are also the hushers. They occur where the peat-skin grows too taut over old burrow and gas pushes up wanting out. Hushers are mats of sly greens and shell-laces that span the weakness and learn it the way a hand learns a jar-lid’s give. When the under-voice vents—once, twice in a day—they spread the concussion across a dozen species, exchange a rush of sugars for a stillness that would otherwise tear, and make rain of the up-brought black water that has forgotten how to fall. They are not altruists. They feed on violence converted to manageable tremor. So do the fox-shapes. So do trees.

Alder and willow do not merely inhabit the borders of wet and slightly-less-wet; they negotiate them. Their roots are hundreds of small treaties with the anaerobic. In their catkin the pollen stays heavy not because it is lazy but because these trees have contracted to keep their habits where they would not rattle the valley’s language into new clauses. You might say they grow ears before they grow leaves. Stand under a willow and you can feel the leaves slicked like tin shift their angle not to light but to the lay of wet in the air. Willows here are librarians of dew. Their furred undersides catch condensed day and roll it down by tempi that match how sound threads past; in drought their hairs harden into a stiffer nap that keeps the little resonant beads from dropping until evening will carry them where the tree prefers them to go.

In and under everything is a guild of scribes. Slime molds write in birch, and bacteria lacquer the letters. Thread-moss does stone caligraphy in a hand made of calcite. Even rot is calligraphic; the sweet surrenders first and the brown becomes script, and the script, pressed, becomes peat, and the peat becomes the under-voice that moves like a bassoon under your ribs. In places you will find milky accretions in old narrow trenches where droplets habitually gathered and lacquered what they touched. Those are archives older than any mammal. They are the middens of chords and reeds and glass—treatises on where to be.

If there was a tuner before, someone with hands and a plan, their descendants are habits now. You can feel them when a handful of dry peat crumbs drops not as dust but as several smooth cords, as if plucked. You can hear them in the words you avoid saying loudly. A shelf of weather is still there to be unlocked by an ignorant flourish, but most of what listens is not offended. It is busy. It adjusts around your passage the way a stream adjusts around a hoof: elastic, curious, prepared to keep being valley regardless.

There are no houses because there are rooms everywhere. A long string of sedge under a wind that has decided to lie down is a corridor. The scarp is a wall that weeps its own sighs and has lichen molding along the baseboards. The black channels are stairwells with steps made of gloss. In the night, pupils glow where something with a small body discovers whether it is colder than the dark and is surprised to find that some pockets in the reeds are always colder than night. That surprise, too, lives here and repeats itself. It is as native as any willow. It is how small bodies stay near edges they can understand.

What lives here feeds on edges: the one between sound and matter, between wet and more wet, between what has happened enough to be practice and what is happening for the first time today. They live on gradients, on forecasts, on expectation, on the solvable error between the hum and itself. They are builders and keepers and slow opportunists. They are animals who have chosen to be punctuation rather than declarative sentences and so last longer on the page.

If you stay long enough the valley loans you some of its residents for carry-out. A tone takes up in your ribs. Your breath latches onto a reed-organ’s tempo. Your step becomes an argument you don’t mind losing because losing costs less here than elsewhere. For a day after you leave you will find fog where there is none and smell iron when there is only tapwater and weigh floors before stepping as if sound itself were a hand outstretched in mist and it mattered, for once, whether you took it. That, too, is a life of this place. It emigrates on skin. It takes up quarters behind the ears and waits for a room quiet enough to speak.
