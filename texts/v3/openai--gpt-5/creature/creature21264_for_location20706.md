What lives here is pressed thin and spread wide. It refuses height. It trades inches for acres until a body is an atmosphere-thick habit that never quite stands, only adheres, only rides. They are made for glancing blows: of charge, of tone, of breath-out warmth that lasts a measured span and goes.

Begin at the film, because almost everything does. Basal skins lie over rock and under water both, amphibiotic sheets whose cells wear iron the way some things wear hair. They breathe by gradients and eat by difference. Where a seep-lip pearls, they present combed fringes so fine you could miss them if not for the way frost catches. They are seamstresses of their own substrate, sewing diodes of silica and magnetite into themselves so that current moves one way better than the other. They do not reach up—they reach along. Their ambition is to become more area. If a scratch appears, they close it in with a patience that only has to beat weather to win.

The fungus you will not call fungus ghosts outward from richer vents, knitting lace from threads that taste of sulphurates and rare salts. Its knots are not fruiting bodies but junctions—logic-gates for ions: if warm then flow, if hoot then hold. In the dim, behaviors count as tools. This is a place where a habit can be an organ, so the basal things evolve tricks that look like decisions and work like plumbing.

On top of that groundwork, plates. Barnacle-analogs lie like shingles but flex; their hinges are glass and iron, their musculature is nothing you would name muscle at all—just differential swelling and a cunning of capillaries that drink and spit. When the seventeen-minute thunder calms toward hoot, entire fields of them open with the hiss of a thousand kettles and show tongues: not singular, but frilled combs that rake aerosol and bite it into banded wax. The wax is not food itself; it is freight, an emissary for taste. They store it. They trade it by contact when the fog-belts reverse and strangers become neighbors for three hours. Along their margins, iridescent creeps of charge walk and re-walk known paths until the path is the ownership.

Between these plates go the scrapers, low and deliberate, mica-fastened and dull as tools left in the rain. Their legs are many, so that weight dissolves into attendance. Their mouths are sanding drums whose grit they grow anew each molt: a love of self-resharpening baked into their skin. They travel on information, not on land; you saw it when one paused to listen, caught a distant hammering tone, and changed course, taking the path that would carry it under the next profitable pulse. There is no leader among them, no trail-marking, unless you count the glitter-bricks they excrete—a perfect parade of rectangular pellets frosted by breath and promptly half-sunk. Those bricks do not build anything we would call walls. They behave like ballast, the scrapers’ way of reminding the silt of its own tensile strength.

The mud is teeth. Every pore is rimmed. It sighs when you stand on it because body after infinitesimal body is adjusting to your pressure, deciding which part of you is liquid. Winnowers go through it with their lips to the ground, whispering in sieve-voices you feel in your ankle bones. Between their browsing lanes the skin of the place thickens into a tuned membrane, played constantly by condensed drip and the mister-work of the sky.

Where stone ends and flat water pretends it is still, a boundary layer is founded and becomes a country. Through it ribbon transparent worms with pinch-mouthed heads like the ends of pipettes. They practice a science that is all mouth: if tactility returns this signal, swallow; if the lapse lasts more than a heartbeat, lift; if the hoot arrives, freeze in place and become bridge. When they bridge, others cross them, and the worm counts that weight as wealth.

Some float. Gelatinous bladders cruise the sheets and seeps like indecisive lanterns. They wear wreaths of crackling cilia whose discharge is audible—needlework unpicking and re-stitching the dark. They do not hunt in the benthic sense. They herd. They cup luminous creep and bridle it, tugging radiance into themselves until their bodies go from moon-murky to milk-bright. Predators pass overhead, some of them smooth and lens-black and curt as punctuation, and the bladders flower into flashes: they push all their stored light outward so quick it pulls heat with it. The hunter comes away hoarfrosted and wise for a minute. The lanterns go gray again and begin refilling their own small suns.

They form caravans. Along the mirrored belts the condensing air lays down like rails, lines of low drag, caravans of bladders stream as methodically as freight. Not social, exactly—synchronized the way pendulums share a period. Mist-kites, membranous, tack across and around them. These gliders look like sketches held together by faith. Each uses ciliated threads hundreds of body-lengths long to comb iron-snow out of vents’ plumes. The snow knits to the threads, reweights them, and allows a kite to turn into the next airstream, to rise into the low ceiling, to dew itself and shiver water down onto the hungry plates beneath. The bladders read those veils for news. The kites taste charge for kin.

Predators here, too, keep different books. One is a mound with a ratcheting back. Each tooth of that back steps up and steps down, biting a seam you cannot see until it is made thin, until the bass that pencils patterns in silt and mist falters a little where the grazer fed. It does not eat bodies. It eats the solvency of sound and lets the leftovers rankle the air for those that can live on thin music. Call it a ratchback, and know its gait is geological—patient, but with a sense of beaten time.

Another predator waits in the elbow of a pool with held breath that is not breath. Something in its skin mirrors a drip not yet fallen. It strikes and pulls tomorrow into today. There are better names than hunter for a thing that depends on phase difference for food; inventor, maybe. Elsewhere, the palimpsest eel you noticed rewrote your bootline into a pattern the mud prefers. It is a scribe of mechanical permissions. When it meets an edge it cannot bear, it scratches and strokes until the edge becomes a slope. It is not merciful. It is an editor of passage.

There are also beasts big enough that a line between geology and animal is only a matter of paperwork. The valley’s bellows—those annealed diaphragms of iron and stone—are flora, are fauna, are device, are wound that learned to breathe. They take in brine and gas and send out processed air, and the way they pucker and spread over centuries would look like growth if you had the right clock. On their margins live harp-creatures: flat, flexible, with lamellae like string banks. When the diaphragm hoots, their lamellae ring not with pitch but with chemistry: acids squeezed from bases, ions parcelled out to those waiting with the correct mouths. They are taxmen of a loaned heat, skimming interest at breath-in and breath-out both.

Around that respiratory geology, entire guilds have assembled that a human could cheat and call professions. There are counters: fronded calculators who sum current and amplitude with their whole bodies and, when the total exceeds a threshold, void spores of charge that walk the plain like tracer-dyes. The dyes do not only entice feeders; they carry pattern. Those that open to drink the light also drink instruction—flight-lists and posture-schemata and local maps of where the tone is thin. Memory travels here as voltage with a signature. You can watch a sheet of life go more itself by being told how to be.

There are tethers, too: animals whose devotion to cycle is so absolute that their actual mass is secondary. They clamp to a period. They open at the third quiet after a hoot and never one earlier or later until they die. If the breath of the field results from rock and heat alone, their obedience is irrelevant; but if the breath stutters, they are the ones that make it keep going. They regulate by appetite. If the hoot falls weak, they starve aloud and their starving pulls a trickle more gas from the cracks. Call it a covenant if you want. It reads like engineering when you are cold.

Not every life here is heft and agility. Some are solely occurrence: standing waves inhabiting themselves on the long slabs, psalms so well-learned they sing without voices. An interference pattern with a persistence long enough to resist ruin by drip becomes a resident. You can scare it with motion. You can chase it, and it will come back to precisely where it wanted to be. It has no stomach; it has a tenancy. Because sound is a solvent, there are eaters of softened edge who track the passages of these tones and reap the droop—its tiny releases of structure—like gleaners after a combine. There are absences with teeth: zones so quiet they abrade, that drink motion and leave behind smoother laws.

At a higher abstraction—and perhaps that only means at a slower beat—consortia act like people. Not because they intend, but because their recurrence makes them countable. On a span of dolomite spine, three hundred thousand valve-plates align themselves toward a rail of mist. When the seventeen-minute thunder breaks to hoot, they shudder in unison, slough frost in one glittering clatter, and in the three-heartbeats of clearness after the clatter, all their tongues taste the same taste and compose a chord. The chord is not heard in our sense. It is a resonance that pushes one fog-belt three meters to the east. This is their vote. On an afternoon of reversing belts, the choir moves a weather that would have moved anyway by a negligible degree. Their reward is a better angle on iron-snow. Their culture is a habit of timing that a mother gives a daughter by letting her lie under her hinge until she learns the count.

Reproduction here is all cleverness by chemistry and place-taking by phase. When the largest exhalations run warmest and the fog wishes to be rain and cannot quite, long arcing streaks of light separate from the counters and run like veins across pans and weirs. They pool. They listen to the bass of the field for a cue; at the little dip before hoot, they brighten. Those that answer correctly become skins. Those skins bud. The buds are nothing you would know as eggs. They are more like postage stamps with datasets. If they adhere to a glossworm, they will make a glossworm—a little faster, a little better at hearing the hammering tones that tomorrow promises. If they find a plate, they will put more compliance in its hinge. If they fall into mud, they will wrap a pore and teach a mouth to widen at the scent of a stranger’s breath. Instruction saturates as appetite.

There is predation that wears trickery, too. Counterfeiters hug the lee of plinths and make their skins hoot faintly in imitation of the great diaphragms. They cannot fake the chemical register, but they can fake the count. Scrapers pick up their pasted summonings and converge, and the counterfeiters eat time itself: the hunger an animal held between the wrong moments becomes a resource. They are thieves of cadence. Quench-wasps puncture lantern bladders mid-glow and lap slowing light. Harps cluster on the diaphragms’ rims so dense they can cost a breath a few centimeters of amplitude, and then winter comes early to the nearest plate-fields. Payback is inevitable: the field shakes to certain chords that unstring the harps’ lamellae and hurl them salt-brittle into mud.

Some few things here might notice you, though notice is probably too round a word. The palimpsest eel laid feelers on your boot in consultation and declined you. You were too coherent to digest. Your voice turned to rime and fell as powder that mouths scrambled to lick; to them, your breath was a precipitate, interesting in the way season is. A stack of masonry-bright pellets, left by a winnower and frost-crusted, softened an hour after you moved away and sank until only a gleam remained. A juvenile kite slumped down in the lull after hoot and used the heat of your thigh through four layers of your clothes to pop a parasite. The valley’s bellows changed not at all. You were a chord they did not know. They hummed back at you with the only song they keep.

Death does not own pathos here so much as schedule. An out-of-phase scraper goes rigid. Frost prints it, charge numbers it, and those numbers are read by worms that do not need faces to have expressions. A lantern whose membranes split instead of flashing sinks and clarifies into a panel of glass that will be flexed by frost-unfrost cycles until it is plates thin and then nothing but thought of plate. A ratchback that miscounts a quiet is buried in a dune of ground glass and chewed electric by a cardigan of comb-jellies that hate to lift their heads from wet. Nothing lingers in singularity. Memory gets out of the carcass by way of trickles of light and small acts of mimic.

If there is a person here, it is a roomful of moments cooperating. It is a timing, a consent of mouths opening on the same count. It is a guild you could walk through and never touch. The plain’s citizens do not build machines; they are born as parts already machined, installed where they are needed by the blunt labor of physics. Their politics is choreography: who opens when, who closes at sight of whose breath, who rides whose wind. Their border skirmishes are waged in phase and over three centimeters. Their empires are a pattern of fog an afternoon long.

They live close enough to rule to make a good lie of it: sound loosens matter, so there are bodies that keep themselves by keeping a song; heat is a loan, so there are accountants who project returns and throw small parties of warmth where they know a debtor will pass; reflections go one way, so there are hunters whose entire youth is practice in catching what falls backward. Emptiness affronts, so they permit none; wherever a sheet hesitates not to be water, a face gathers.

You cannot put a pin in them. You cannot enter a hall where they meet. Their architecture is periodicity itself. Valve-forests face rails of mist because the world has bidden them, again and again, to be oriented. Winnowers pass and leave city-length brocades behind that you could mistake for the plans of factories until the frost lifts and the brocades slide back to nothing but tone-maps. The diaphragms exhale, the kites tack, the bladders freight, the ratchbacks graze standing waves and the quiet-eaters drink the grazers’ leavings. The hair-ice combs move like tides cracking. And among all that, the bright little bookkeeping dyes pool and drain, pooling and draining, carrying with them the plainest religion anyone ever practiced: keep count, keep time, keep going.

Who lives here? Assemblies. Programs written in salts. Mouths that vote. Harps and frauds and editors of mud. Lanterns that shepherd light the way shepherds once shepherded sheep. Fields that have found a way to stay. If you must call them anything like us, call them artisans—of tone, of chill, of charge. They are the verbs of this landscape. The stone, weeping its patience; the air, condensing its law; the water, refusing to be big: all of it is their house and their body. They go on because the place goes on. They are the plain’s way of being beautiful at work.
