Those who live here have learned to be smaller than their own echoes.

You meet them first as a series of small precisions: a white plane being drawn across a balcony and pegged to a rope with hands whose knuckles bear a filigree of scars that glass leaves when it is not yet glass enough; a boot lifted so that the layered cloth sole will not slap; the quick bow of a head to catch a rumor of a creak inside the stone. They dress as the place teaches them to—white and pale, cloth bound over hair, necks wrapped; faces the color of long days in breathed heat. The only persistent black on them is in their tools and their cinder lenses, smoked just enough that the olivine glitter does not slow their eyes.

Children here learn the alphabet of hands before they learn the alphabet of mouths. They are named twice: once for love and lineage, once for the first quiet skill they master—Drip-Reader, Anchor-Finder, Shade-Setter, Slowthrow. They count not in seconds but in breaths and in lamp-blinks, and they measure a person as much by their ability to keep a cable from beginning to sing as by any amount of cleverness. In the warm still hours, you see them practicing the peculiar grace of the place: a game played with a stack of ceramic saucers and a crosswalk of ropes; whoever can move the tower from one end to the other without making the ropes hum wins the right to tap a painted black circle where noon stood last month.

The language they keep for late is all in the hands and the shoulders, a choreography of sign and glance and the soft lift of chin to the line of a sky tram. Voice is for evenings under the cistern walls, when day has dropped away and the warm white of their cloth holds a little heat for the old to speak into. Their lullabies are tapping fingers in the small of a back. The first lie a child here learns is that words have weight; the first truth is that some weights do not belong on certain anchors. They grow up reading the mountain’s refusal to be hurried like you might read a mood in someone you love too much to press.

Work is the religion, if you had to be cruel enough to call it that. There are condensers to read as if they were a sky, and every hand knows the names of drip-lines the way fishermen know currents: Sister’s Breath, Left-Knee, Four-Count, Wet-Beggar. There are white planes to sew and dye with pulverized shell so they mirror more than they keep. There are black tiles to set out at night to drink the cold and to gather in again at the first warming so that their harvest does not sweat away, and slip them into the troughs that refuse daylight. There are ropes to braid with fibers that would be glass if they weren’t tamed, cords that hold a touch no warmer than a poem. There are flues that need coaxing into the right sort of laziness so they keep the air neither moving too fast nor not at all.

There are watchers who sit most of the day with their heads tilted to the stone, combing the slow chord of the place for any new note. They have a long board dusted fine where the stone sifts a sentence of sand when someone, somewhere, forgot gentleness. There are isotherm-minders who paint with chalk in the early morning, walking the shaded faces and laying out the day’s channels—a set of pale, particular arrows that show where water will agree to be persuaded. There are the Counterweighters, whose work looks like sitting by a window with a small book and a cup and standing up at the exact right time to lay a hand on a lever so that a hundred-room carriage somewhere on a face will not begin to love the idea of falling. There are the Dwellers-in-Noise, which is not a title anyone keeps for long; their task is to move through the factory tunnels and kneel at the hoods and baffles when the day’s work grows too talkative, tightening a seam here, asking a piston to remember its habit.

They carry their quiet with them in tools. Hammers wear leather like winter hats, and the rods they use to test a joint are tipped with something softer than wood. You see them taking a bridge in a kind of moving prayer; no one sets a foot until a hand tells them the span has exhaled. Some of them pilot the big carriages along the rails. They talk to the loaded world with slotted lights and hand flags and a gloss of lampwork that throws words across a gulf where voice would get lost. The drivers are all shoulder and patience, and they sit with the kind of posture that forgives the mountain anything it does not want to be yet.

They do not bow to the place; bowing suggests a transaction. They live in a treaty with it. They know exactly where to put a white tile so it makes a house be less asked-to. They have a dozen small customs to make water feel welcome when water appears: the lip kept clean on the condenser that has the best morning; the little notch cut just shy of vertical so that a bead’s indecision will be answered with a yes; the way someone will give up their shadow at noon for a difficult run to deepen. The names for their dwellings are verbs—Pause, Shade, Lean, Catch. They own little, and what they own tends to be smooth and padded, because abruptness is a vice that costs.

Beyond people, other lives have made themselves a place in the rules. There is a thin skin of living where the eye wants to say nothing could be—clinging slicks the color of a held breath, films that salt and unsalt themselves along the cleanest cracks. They lay lines of darker wet along which moth-little grazers slow themselves, nibbling the basalt for metals. Inside the cisterns, in their own weeks, white worms with translucent suits ghost along the girders and the forgotten screws, leaving them suddenly ornate, fluted as if a sea had had time. Some households keep a jar of the cistern fluting on a shelf and call it their year’s luck; others won’t look at it at all.

In the updrafts, things with fins like paper drift and drink, shredding themselves a little along the edges of sharp weather and growing those fines anew in the nights. They have eyes you have to forgive yourself for seeing, delicate as punctuation, shy of naming. A few families write down their visits by the shape of the shadow they make on whitework—Clipped Wing, Full Page, Two Commas. Children learn by accident one day to hold out a wet hand and let a paper-finned thing kiss the water off. The children come home with their palms bathed in electric amazement and are told not to talk, and so hold their amazement until the dark, which turns it into something better than talk.

Once in a season, sometimes twice, a larger presence drifts or climbs from the hot places below. It is made of the same black glass as the world but jointed at strange intervals, as if it had decided on a plan and then remembered another. It will find a white stripe and lay its length to cool there and breathe. People see the slow billow of its body and the way heat stops around it and keep a far tenderness. No one draws lots or shave-marks to know who will see it first; eyes that live in this place catch certain kinds of novelty the way dew catches on night glass. They will take the long way around to do their work, and they will come home with silence all over them and only at the cistern give one small story to the day: it breathed. It breathed twice. The rules against pursuing it are the only rules anyone would call sacred.

Cats live here too, though they have been taught their pads and their patience. They sleep in the warm white that stays above the heads of sleepers until midnight and then seek the wool-wrapped machines, draped in their small black commas along the edges of ceramic housings. They turn their ears to the deep chord of the mountain and make of it a lullaby, and when the paper-finned drinkers come too near, they only turn their heads and allow the air to change shape.

The factories are another kind of animal. They have been taught gentleness like a horse, like a tired ox. A brine works hangs from a cliff the way a lichen hangs to bark, spreading not by ambition but by finding where it can take hold without offense. Its trays set just at hand height, its baths touching glass like a practiced marriage, and inside the long galleries where rack barely kisses plate, a hum no louder than a hive. You walk through and you see a flock of wool-wrapped pistons moving in and out, breathing in a pace that makes your own lungs slow to agree. These machines do not make smoke or noise. They sort, they separate, they encourage structure to happen. Sometimes the workers let a blade of light through in the afternoon and watch the quiet world of crystals grow with stately hunger along a thread. They understand that if the mountain is a child, these machines are the toys it is allowed because they are soft.

There are also the gliders—fabric wings ribbed with reed and glass fiber; they nest like folded birds in shadow-beds. On still mornings, you see their long slow vowels above the rock as someone rides the moral lift that rises above a corner. The pilots are all bone, muscle, and a sort of ethical optimism; they do not go up to own the air but to map where it loves them today. They call down in slotted lights to those on the rails—small rubrics of hand and lamp to say storm line here, heat crack there, a hunger of weather on the west. When night comes, they unlace their wings and hang the cloth in white triangles over the lip of a cistern so the day will have left something in it when morning comes.

Food does not have a page here in the way people tell stories, which means: it is complicated and not to be spoken of loudly. What grows is mineral and light. What arrives comes by rail, in quiet tins and wrapped bundles. What is made is made in vats that ask the small slick lives to become larger, more obliging, less in love with basalt. Salt is a harvest. Sweetness is a ritual, a once-when. No pig or goat would enjoy itself on a face that erases any tuft that tries to tell a seed a fairy tale. They grow what they can on shelves in mist, in the long comfortable breath of a condenser room, and the rest they trade for. In this way they have made a virtue of needing one another.

Time itself lives here in a way you can listen to. The mountain thinks in chords, and some days, if you lie down and put your head to the wall, you can hear a swallow-note of decisions being made along a seam ten levels below your bed. The chord is not music because music has an audience. This has a project. People here have made a place for that project and, in doing so, become part of it. They keep small calendars of crystals in a cut where growth shows if you bend and remember last year; they call that little new face a birthday and share a cup of salted water by it the way others somewhere else might share wine and a wish.

They have ceremonies, but you would miss them if you came expecting overtures. A storm is a holiday in this sense: a quickness of whitework talking up and down the city like fur lifted along a spine; then the rain’s practiced violence; then a standing at the edge after with everyone using their careful hands to add a cup to a cup to a runnel to a cistern until the water has a place to belong. They will chalk the high line on a wall as if the city has children and growth to mark. They will talk softly in the hand at the places where something almost hurt them and did not.

There are other inhabitants that do not answer to names at all. The cable you saw scuffing its paint around a rubbing block is its own slow creature, fattened by work, thin by winter. The white gutters that run beside the rails learn to catch dew as if catching is a demeanor and not a function. The baffle hoods that eat noise and warm sleeping rooms accept a pat now and then from a passing lover of their hum and warm just a breath more. These are animals by courtesy, alive in how they are tended and how they answer that tenderness.

Tales take long to take hold here because the mountains have not given personalities to their faces, not in the way soft places do. People keep from making myth by way of a practice: what looks like an inscription is only fracture; what looks like a calling is only the lift today chooses to give. The things that move and have eyes insist on being themselves so unarguably that no one bothers to draw them into legend. You could try, and someone older would lay a white hand on a white tile and show you where the noon was last month, and say: this is what we mark. The rest is a thinking you can listen to but not recruit.

So what lives here are listeners. Animals that drink mist with paper for wings. Worms that write inside water. Cats with taught pads. Factories tamed to gentleness. Gliders that find the word for today’s air and spell it once. Children who learn to turn arguments into later, into the silent syntax of flags. Engineers who can persuade heat to share its days and weights to change their minds after a meal. A mountain just born enough to be dangerous and just polite enough to be persuaded on the order of winters.

Their manners are their law, and their law is the grace that keeps them on these edges: keep your voice and your weight shorter than the span of a breath; put white between sun and dark; do not move a thing before it forgives you; if water appears, honor it with a belonging. They have made themselves into a species of patience. They have given the place time to grow into itself without insisting on a story too early. In return, the place gives them small, exultant certainties: a bead that fattens into a thread; a counterweight that inches and then calls the day successful; a face of glass that one summer shows a tiny scallop where it never had before.

What lives here, in the end, is a compact. It breathes. It drinks dew. It lays white across black like a promise that doesn’t hurry and doesn’t break. It stands on pads that will forgive. It listens until the place tells it how to be kind. And sometimes, when rain laughs up the canyons and leaves in a clatter, it allows itself a laugh so quiet it is only a changed chord in the wall, and the cats, the worms, the paper fins, the slowed machines, and the people all pause as if they have heard the same sweet impossible thing: time, briefly, agreeing with them.
