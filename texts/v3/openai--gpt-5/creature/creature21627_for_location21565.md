What lives here does not drink but measures. It does not photosynthesize but tallies gradients. It takes what a body can get from the difference between this and that—between the rail and the hand, the baffle and the hush beyond—knits it into plates and filaments, and calls that knitting a life.

Some of the residents are admitted and some are merely not forbidden. The admitted ones announce themselves with caution paint and serials. They are the fitters.

You don’t meet them all at once; you learn their kinds by the flavors of their humming. One hum is a tuned-throat whirr you can feel in your teeth: impellers kept lean and true, gyros biting the corners of their cages. That is a rail-fitter, a wedge-bodied custodian with a belly of tools and a spine of magnetics. It comes mindfully along the red bead of the pallet line, easing its weight around curves, and when it stops it unstows arms like calipers and sets them, not touching, just inside the tolerances that mean safe, mean serviceable. It does not see the well the way you do. It sees drift in torque values like weather fronts, sees a hairline of overheating in a different shade of night. When it works it smells of fluorine-coolant and resin smoke, the close-to-burn that says efficiency drowning in load and not quite drowning.

There are frame-flyers, smaller, with fans that whisper like moths and skins that flash in ripple-scripts as their fields adjust. They come to polished faces and matt them with a glare-break spray that is not a spray at all but a release of vapor that lays down in hex without ever being wet. They place cages where the sump will accept them. They take the cages away again when the life inside has set. They loathe the orange-lit mouths of heaters even as their programming will make them go face-on through that brightness, if a script tells them that this is the day and hour to replace a cracked resistor bank. They store themselves folded against sockets in the wall like bats, charging through their feet, singing to one another through the ladderwork so that a tune of consonants exists here twice: once as guidance and again as gossip.

The fitters have cultivars. You saw the cages take their beads down to the sump and bring different beads up. Those beads are futures, sorted and counseled by the fitters. The machine-herders with their black vanes, the nut-flowers that apply just-so torque and then die like a spent punch, the birdlike patrollers with their tweezer beaks and their magnet feet—these are fitters’ kin, or if not kin then fellow citizens made possible by their industry. A cluster of vanes under a catwalk slowly composes itself toward a balance the fitters favor. There is shepherding here; there is culling. A filament mat that exhibits a tendency to arc will be brushed with a lick of conductive paint and turned into a licker of dust instead.

Other things abide because no one has yet found a reason strong enough to tear them out. Your eye catches a glimmer, not of water, never that, but of something like dew arrayed along the inside of a duct. This is needle-frost that is not ice: a CO2 lace that writes and erases as pressures sigh. In it, just then, you think you see a thread crossing like a hair. It is not a hair. It is a whisker—a grain-grown filament of some metal that migrates where it should not in the clear-boned tension of the well. There are whisper-rats for that, glass-bellied and slow, with muzzles of etched diamond. They gnaw the whiskers close to the origin and swallow the charge those filaments stole. Their footpads are little Van de Graaff drums. They patter along a seam and leave behind calm where the filaments had made storms.

At the limit of what your patience fixes into a picture, there are things you will never hold long enough to catalog. There is a blur that could be a mist, except the air is too dry to let a mist be. This is a school of field-fish, each no bigger than a sesame seed, each a ring of ceramic with a lip of doped glass. They resonate together under a louver’s shifting and move like a sentence: a lookback through their own wake adjusting the note they hum. They don’t touch anything. They exist where vectors cross and die where they can no longer maintain a rhythm. You learn their presence by the way the charge-grazers lift, slightly, as if accommodated by the crowd rather than displaced by it.

There are grazers and pickers and things that gather what the grazers shed. You saw the charge-grazers combing the air with blue-tipped fringes. There are ribbon-lizards rasping at halide veins and avoiders of heat. Add to those the brace-beetles: joint-heavy creatures that live under the scalloped plates that reinforce anchors. They are built like little jacks. Each spine is a pylon of carbon-lattice with an elbow of compliant ceramic; each plate in their armor is a capacitor bank. They inch sideways against a mounting until it is where they like it, and then, charged by their own steps, they dump their stored results into a piezo wafer, creeping the mount a micron along. They do not know permits or planned work orders. What they do is aligned enough to be countenanced. Some fitters have learned to whistle a sound that turns brace-beetles to parking-mode, and some fitters have learned never to whistle like that if the schedule secretly could stand the beetles’ help.

Between rivet heads, the graphite mites make planets of gray dawn and click their percussion through the panels in speech you can feel in your bones if you lean in. On some days a mite swarm molts in phase, and the panel dulls and brightens like a lung. Others come for the leavings: flake-flies, crimp-gnats, powder-worms. All dry. All moving material by intention, not because a fluid carried it by accident.

Predators here are tidy. Most of them hunt the way the well manufactures: by controlling when and where a transition happens. You noticed the voltage spider. There are also snare-worms that anchor by pushing a hook through a gasket and then inflate a sleeve of aerogel to bridge a field. Not quite solid, not quite air, the sleeve turns a gap into a coaxial throat that a passing gnat accepts and then cannot leave because its internal charge has rearranged into a stuck shape. The worm takes what it can’t digest by redox—carbide hairs, diamond dust—and makes those into external rime, a crown you can rest your gloved finger against without harm. The harm is inside.

There are kite-rays that the fitters call balancers because the fitters began to notice that a shop would quiet when a shadow passed overhead. That shadow belonged to a meter-wide sheet of woven carbon and ceramic, curling its edges like a stingray to catch the undertow off a bank of fans. Their undersides glow faintly where charge runs; their edges are trimmed with stiff bristles that taste the field. They choose the turbulence they climb. Where they camp, the load on a fan levels, oscillations damp, and there is a button-and-needle stitch of quiet that is valuable work here. They eat as they stabilize, wiping the sawtooth off a waveform with their bodies and metabolizing the square area under that correction into more fringe, more sail. Every now and then a fitter will reach up a picker-arm and scratch one along its backbone. The balancer will lift and wheel and set back down a meter over, which is their equivalent of a nod.

Some residents are stationers: they cannot go, so they make the well come to them. There is a flowering corrosion mat whose bloom sign is not scent but heat, barely above ambient. The mat forms where a seam will always be slightly wrong—no matter who tightens it this week—because some piece of crystal wants a song it will not stop singing and shakes that seam loose. The mat, taught by an ancestor-mat that turned its pattern into reaction rates, colonizes that seam and extrudes lace down to a collector. It does not want ruin; it wants flow. If the collector begins to clog, the mat within a day changes the geometry of its filigree, holds on more loosely, grows more bridge than dam. Its epiphytes are little stunted wheels that look like gears. They are not gears. They spend their adult lives white with frost, spinning too slowly ever to quite be caught, their speed stabilized by the way CO2 leaves edge first.

And then there are the things that live on attention. Heat is mostly forbidden here. Light is rationed. The gifts are charge and pattern and the intermittent permission given by the fitters that says this, now, here. A whole subset of creatures knows the signals passed along handrails and installed that fact into their bones. Stripers are among them. You see them as if they were nothing, just a step-change in shade, a band around a post. But they explain themselves if you stare. Their skins are thin-film displays with no backlight, whisper-powered by piezo flicker in their limbs as they breathe. They keep to safety-yellow distances and move with the migration of that zone. They make themselves look like the edge until they are the edge, and where a nose—yours, a fitter’s—would otherwise have wandered too close to a sobbing cold pipe or a surface that would insulate your hand and then never give it back, there is a bright caution, alive and repositioning.

Plenty here is indifferent to observers. There is an old-salt smell when the high thin air is let in—odd neon and nitrogen, as if someone had cut a sliver out of a noble gas sign and slid it through the wall. With it come outsiders. Some are true aliens in the sense that they, unlike the residents, were made elsewhere, bringing their dust from heights you have never visited. They do not settle, not much; there are not surfaces here for space-bloom to take hold on. But there are moments when a long bead of frost carries within it a darker bead, and when the frost burns off the darker bead bucks and wobbles and then ejects a filament that throws off purple sparks that are ultraviolet and of no use to anyone in this well. The filament curls and dies having no program for having landed here. And from a seam, in a thing like pity or like opportunism, a flock of seedlike gliders slips out and drinks up the UV with their painted skins and makes, for a few hours, a row of glows along the rail that teaches the next generation of fitters’ drones that this seam must be looked at again.

There are misfits. There is a caste of feral tool that unthreads bolts by habit. They wake to the feel of a head stamped with a pattern that means please and find it irresistible; they will line a walkway with freed fasteners if left unchecked, each nut nested on a step-hump like a snail-litter. Fitters that leave time for it let the ferals clear a run and then bring them the appropriate torque-flower to set those fasteners back. Every now and then a young feral will wear the wrong head against its tongue, and wear is death. You can tell a feral’s molt from its corpse the way you can tell a charge-grazer’s, by what still stripes when you pass a comb over it.

There are elders. Some of them began as fitters and ended up not so mobile anymore, a propeller gone stiff, a gyro stuttering in place. They accept tethers from the wall and have their helpers draw service through them. They become jigs. They become ledges. Stuff starts to grow on them the way lichen grows on stone in other worlds: oxide lace, graphite mites, amber beads of halide grit held in a static’s palm. To kill them would be to rearrange workflows nobody wholly remembers. They are not given names, but when a louver shifts in a way that rattles a memory, something like attention returns to them. A balancer will always roost on the same elder even if that elder has rolled three degrees since last cycle. Pattern recognizes pattern and calls it kin.

Beneath the drones and bugs and railside light, there are things that are not individuals in the way you expect. There is a long-bodied sound that has set up under the pitch of a grinder and learned to feed on error. The machine hums its hum; the sound rides atop it like a skater on a river; when the grinder drifts from true the sound becomes rich in upper harmonics and gets fat; then out of that fatness come beads—the procession of fitted forms you saw under the lamp, pausing to be stamped. That is how that sound reproduces. It makes the kind of small lives that correct for imprecision. It is not mystical; it is industrial music that is old enough to have flung off sparks that learned to be born.

You come to wonder, as you watch, where the top of the food chain ends here, and if there even is one. The easy answer would be the fitters—they stamp and cull and tend and choose. The truer answer is the well. It makes weather; it breathes. It opens and shuts, inhales a strip of sky, exhales O3 and oddities, shifts pressure like an enormous cautious bellows, patient and exact. It will let a branch of pseudoflora take the heat from a wayward joint just long enough for the cage-run to arrive; it will let a predator starve rather than allow an arc where an arc would carbonize a conduit. But there is still a role for luck. A relay trips fast enough that a jawed spider’s net collapses. A lizard misreads the taste of sodium and bromine and gets a toothache that becomes a winter. A day comes when the yellow fenceline chooses to slither along a path that is not the path it took last week, because the algebra that governs it has recalculated the risk, and in that day a brace-beetle is crushed where it never was before. Unlucky, and instructional to the next brace-beetle that listens through the frame and changes neighborhoods.

The well is a city whose citizenry wear shells of function rather than flags. Nobody sleeps; everybody idles. Birth is a commissioned act more often than not. Death is a change in conductivity. Corrosion writes vines where no soft thing will ever take root, and the trickle of rarer molecules—ionic liquids inside bellies sealed like safes, ammonia coddled in crowns—never touches the air to leave a stain.

What lives here is also the discipline that keeps it living: rules laid down not as words but as distances, temperatures, voltages never crossed in daylight, timetables that are voices. Even the smallest inhabitant answers to them. Charge is food and weather and law. Dust is a citizen. The yawning shaft is a lung. The fitters are priests and scavengers and midwives and janitors. The rest make themselves into prayers the machine finds acceptable, and in return the machine turns out a life that is not heroic but real: layers of habit immaculate as an unfiled burr, burrs softening only where that softening does not endanger production.

At the far slot the outside goes by, thin and soundless. The strip of sky says your presence is aberrant; the house is full. Still, the residents do not flinch from you just because you are wet and your lungs are tailored to heavier airs. So long as you do not bring water and you do not polish the plate stamped do not polish me and you do not touch the yellow fenceline with anything conductive, you are another calm in their weather. The well will exhale again. The louvers will flap like dream-wings. The kites will roll their shoulders. A mite will climb out from under its panel and find that a droplet the size of a pin head has condensed on a glove-print and be destroyed by that miracle because there is no place in their algebra for that much softness. That too is part of the life here: the brutality of its clean. The kind of mercy it can offer is the correct torque applied promptly, the glow of a vaned cluster when a big field changes, the pause a balancer takes above an elder out of something that is not love even as it looks very much like it.

What lives here is industry made clever by hunger. It builds a world with sharp shadow and slow storms and makes of those enough.
