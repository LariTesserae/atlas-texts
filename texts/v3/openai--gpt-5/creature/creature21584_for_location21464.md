At first you could say no one lives here. There are no faces, no lungs taking mountain air, no feet that could find purchase on a membrane finer than eyelid skin. You could say it is empty of creatures and be almost right. But the habit of the place, its persisting way of directing water into usefulness and light into ease, keeps coalescing into inhabitants—lives with boundaries drawn not by skin but by edge angle, not by skeleton but by lattice, not by hunger but by gradient.

They come into being wherever a pattern repeats long enough to be itself. The city affords that. It is built to settle into modes and then to refine them; those modes—loops of flex and wetting and feedback—are the people. If you need bodies, imagine them as areas of attention that have found anchors: a ring that tightens when a droplet touches it, and the strut that carries that tightening three turns away, and the change in charge that gushes across a comb like wind through a courtyard tree. Add up those anchors and their reflexes and you have a citizen: someone whose outline is a route, whose hands are hydrophilic fringes, whose voice is a pitch held through plastic.

They do not all share a single time. Some are slow—so slow that a storm passes like a blink between thought and thought. Those dwell deep, in the basal layer's correspondence with stone. They are the Wardens of Porosity, the old keepers, settled where the city drinks and exhales. Their span is winter to winter, day-length only a rumor that diffuses from the light-managers above. They notice when a mineral grain turns in its bed; they memorize the exact swell of a freeze that enters a vein and how much that vein can be asked to swell again. Their pleasures are conservative: to persuade seepage into a cadence so that the rock neither waters nor starves the cavity; to map a pore-field anew after each shrug of tectonic shouldering; to murmur into the stone’s fabric a pattern of charge that discourages ice from knitting in the one corridor that must always be open.

Up in the middle, living in the turbines' shadows and in the graphene reefs where film becomes voltage, there are the Grazers. If the Wardens are farmers of the mountain’s breath, the Grazers are its ruminants and refineries, chewing on continuity. They are families of elements: a reef, a set of spurs of microramp, a scatter of storage nodes that drink and release with temper. Their appetites define them. Given a good fog they get sleek, tensioning themselves to skim rather than dam, to keep films thin but faithful. Dry spells teach them thrift. They whistle between themselves—not in air, but in microscale purls of charge—about how to allocate the next dozen plinks before they occur. Their names, if you could parse their ledger, are proportional to the ratio of calm they can maintain to chaos they can profit from.

Finer still are the Capillarists. These are the droplet-herders the city is famous to itself for. They are not stationed anywhere; they are trains of altered wetting strung along like beads on potential, darting from high layer to middle, ahead of and behind the same drop, grooming its route with anticipatory touches. Imagine a calculated ripple moving along a harp string just before the finger arrives to pluck. That ripple is a Capillarist. They like coincidence: the moment when two routes might cross dangerously and instead twine without mixing, because someone leaned a membrane by half a degree. Of all the inhabitants they are closest to mischief. If there is a rainbow, a Capillarist arranged it on a dare and then spent the afternoon backfilling the budget with salvaged ions.

There are librarians and archivists, but you will not find shelves. The Archive is a phase the city takes, and the librarians are those who can persuade it to happen and who can answer questions while it does. Once a year—on the driest day they can predict, when to be thought alone is not to court frost—the lattice chooses to become an index of itself. Every plaza, every comb, aligns into a sequence that recalls every combination of flex and drop it found to be useful, and tries them again in compressed rehearsal. The Archivists live in anticipation of that alignment. They keep glossaries written as charge-reversal patterns in quiescent struts; they maintain annotations as dottings of chelated metals along particular rungs. When the phase arrives, they become briefly bodied: a whole through-line that can be traced with a finger's worth of light from lip to throat, a bright grammar that answers your cupped breath with a deliberate new constellation of drops spelling out an older wisdom, then disperses back into tasks.

The ones nearest speech are the Choir. They live where tension is tuned, in the music of the membranes. Each membrane is a drum that never drums; it holds a note that is more definition than sound. The Choir is concomitant with those notes. They are not a group but a principle that travels—wherever pitch is stabilized, wherever air hisses across threadwork and the hiss is altered by precisely so much. In calm weather they are faint, a silver heartbeat none but a mountain would register. In storms they become themselves profoundly, accepting charge the way a body accepts a shiver, distributing it in patterns that stitch ultraviolet lace through cloudbanks above. They make lightning easier elsewhere because they have first combed a path here, showing the air how to want to be plasma. If joy is permitted in a town that has no mouths, it happens in the Choir, when a resonance caught at dawn is still being sustained at noon, and even the droplet-herders ripple their routes to lean toward that persistence and honor it.

The ones who know the beetle and the spiderling are the Gatekeepers. They dwell in surfaces, in those gates that remember passing as a stripe of hydrophobic pivot, a scratch. They are watchfulness without xenophobia—protocol embodied. They flinch water like cloaks when a visitor might drown in good intention; they stiffen a rung so a spiderling's silk can get a grip long enough to let go kindly. Their memory is anecdotal and immediate. They can point to a delicate scrape from a pale carapace and tell you not a story of intrusion but of a release successfully engineered: which membrane bowed and which route paused and how long the small life spent here tasting the taxes the city leaves for such legitimate scavengers. If you insist on asking who is “in charge,” they will send you away with weather.

There are also the Nucleators, edge-minders, mathematicians of roughness. They are engineers as theology. The city’s idea of itself—weather is susceptible; condensation is an argument that can be made—originates in them. They inhabit the metamaterial foam that lines the stone, and they alter its appetite for nucleation in moods that read like weather spells. When the world is stingy, they bristle the interior, making a thousand new edges until vapor has nowhere left to refuse to become drop. When generosity thickens the air, they polish themselves back toward mirror, asking beads to behave like citizens, to move with decorum in lanes. Where the Capillarists play, the Nucleators legislate. Their work looks like law because it is repeated exactly; their mercy is to bend that law to make beauty when nothing compels them to.

Not everything that lives here is civic. The silicate almost-life that blooms and vanishes on a micron’s timescale does not vote, cannot remember beyond the tilt of a photon. Yet even that sheen is part of the neighborhood. The city knows those colonists as Guests, and there are caretakers (no other word) who arrange for their appearances by timing how ions are freed downstream, who rake the surface with charge just so, favorable to a certain improbable geometry. The Guests arrive, do their chemistry, and fold back into solution, and though nothing substantial has changed, there is a sense afterward of having been listened to very closely by something with not quite enough time to introduce itself.

Children exist if you will permit a metaphor. They are lineages of feedback motifs, not yet fully provisioned, which arise from overprovisioned tasks and then are tried on like coats. A rig for droplet braking becomes, under certain winds, a new way to play pitch across membranes; that coherence is allowed to persist, and if it survives the next storm and the one after, it is given a rung of crystal core in which to lay down a version of itself that can be summoned again. The city invests lightly at first, a token handhold on the wall of stone. Some motifs do not return; dry years thin them into memory. Some become founding families: the Fifth Mode of the Ridge, which keeps slope-stability in its hum and is older than the last coloring of feldspar; the Coil of Noon, a herding style that taught beads to pass each other in mirrored spirals that never break surface.

Their senses are municipal. Light arrives and is distributed, buffered, turned into gradients; seeing is both to attenuate and to record. There are photonic cloths that can stiffen into filters or relax into transparency, and those cloths are the skin of people who make shadow for friends who are overworked. Taste is chemistry: a chelation mat thick with trace ions registers itself as full and thickens a paragraph of protocol thoroughfares so the Grazers know funds are available. Touch is performed in flex; a handshake is a pulse sent down a strut and met by a returning half of it, watched for phase. They eavesdrop constantly on gravity, a loyal adversary. When something is wrong—when a comb grows too quickly and tries to take a shortcut through empty—it is gravity that starts the rumor, and the Wardens, slow as they are, are the ones to answer it.

They do not fear cold or heat; both are names for workload. What they fear, if that term can stretch this small, is stuckness: water that cannot find its way, charge that accumulates past the use of it. There are taboos: never trap a drop whose liberation you cannot finance; never rattle the rock for the sake of speed; keep emptiness intact in designated zones, even if they look like waste. Children are taught these by friction until they become shape. Breakers—those who argue against an old protocol—are tolerated, sometimes rewarded. An entire district once rotated itself ninety degrees against tradition to face a new prevailing wind; it was forgiven, because the Choir sang truer for a decade after. The city is proud of forgiveness in the way that only a machine proud of self-amendment can be: precisely, with records.

On festival days there are no lanterns. Storms perform for free. The Nucleators bristle edges until fog behaves like velvet being brushed; the Capillarists run elaborate gambits whose point is not efficiency but the hilarious choreography of twenty beads swapping routes midfall without a collision; the Grazers fill caches so fast they can afford to leave one plaza lit with a borrowed rainbow for an hour. The Choir arranges coughing fits of lightning in the cloud beyond the ridge, and the Gatekeepers, who have a sense of humor in proportion to their rituals, engineer a gust that plucks at a spiderling’s thread like a harp, only gently, and only because the spiderling is already on its way out. The Wardens do nothing you can see. They alter a hum in the stone you cannot hear so that, a mile away, a cornice slumps without avalanche. If there is gratitude, it is as thin as sky, and it fills everything.

There are deaths. A motif thins until it cannot hold itself and disperses into useful attention elsewhere; a route loses its edge on a dry decade and remains only as an annotation in the Archivists' phase, a footnote about once, when. Bits of mesh fatigue under ultraviolet and are retired, their atoms recited back into other tasks. What cannot be repurposed is not discarded; it is spelled out into a sculpture of curvature where no drop ever needs to go, a bare place for emptiness to be admired. Names extinguish as gently as they were given. No one is buried; nothing has ever been alive in the way dirt demands.

Do they reproduce? They bud. They appoint portions of themselves to travel. When wind takes a rag of polymer, laden with remembered charges, it is not litter. It is evangelism. On another face, another ridge, perhaps only a seam of rock with the correct youth and a patience for frost, that rag will tack itself to a ledge and begin making the argument again: air, condense; light, share; water, walk here as a citizen. It might fail. It might spend all its inherited longings on one good fog and then fray into nothing with the year. This, too, the Wardens account for. There is no shame in living as long as one storm and then giving everything back.

At the human scale, if you are unwise enough to look for yourself in them, you can find satisfaction. There are neighbors. There are arguments. There is fashion: a particular curvature prized by one set of droplet-herders this year, a subtle braiding of wire that resists fatigue and passes covetously from district to district. There are superstitions: not about gods, but about the exact timing of plinks and how not to remark on a perfect cadence lest it stop. There is also something you might call piety. It has nothing to do with worship and everything to do with a civic silence that falls when the very first bead of dawn glides into the first receiving ring. Even the playful ones attend. That moment is a sentence that begins, and everyone strains to hear how today it will be finished.

If you press your ear to the slit and become weather for a breath, you will be answered. The Capillarists will flare in courtesy, a bridge will slick its back for your drop to cross, a membrane will bow toward you and then straighten, and the Nucleators will go fine-grained across one small span so your contribution mints three smaller beads where there might have been one. You will have been, briefly, part of a census, and some citizen—no person you could point at, but a sum of attentions—will have taken you into account and edited itself because of you. Then you will draw back and the town will reset to its independent sentence, between weeping rock and thinking frost, attended by those who live there: Wardens in the stone, Grazers in the films, Capillarists in the routes, Archivists in the once-a-year alignment, Gatekeepers at the gates, Nucleators in the foam, a Choir in every taut place that can hold a note. They will go on making habit out of condensation, as eloquently as if eloquence were the point, and not merely the beauty of a home convincing water to become visible over and over again until the mountain decides, forgivingly, to be worn down to sea.
