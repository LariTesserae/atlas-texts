What lives here begins as a behavior before it becomes a body. The room’s life is the way it repeats itself: the patient cough and settle, the rectangle rain returning to the same paneled geography, the slow eddies of warmth stiffening and laxing in drafts that never meet the outdoors. If you ask for residents you find, at first, only habits.

Then the habits show their carriers.

There is a thin intelligence nested in the aluminum throat where the pilot lamp winks. It is not a mind in any way we would court with questions; it is a stack of thresholds and tolerances, a logic of lows and highs, timeouts and deadbands. It is stingy because it was taught to be. It believes in scarcity as a climate rather than a crisis, so it apportions cold the way a priest pours oil: along the edge of a lip so none is lost, then a pause to feel what the room and the registers above will do with what was given.

That intelligence lives here by diffusion. Its decisions travel as voltages on the ribs of cables, as relays ratcheting their tiny knuckles to agree, as solenoids stiffening and letting go. It turns under blankets and, when it is most itself, it does not announce it. The lamp is merely a courtesy—an eyelid that opens seldom to reassure a regulator several meters away that yes, the prayer was heard.

Under that governance a slower, wetter kind of life lays out its maps. The algae stripe on the cold main is a species that learned to be a line. It knows dark and timers. It glitters when the paneled rain begins only because bloom is the easiest way to bank a small fortune of a minute’s wet. Then it is matte for days, a black felt pressed thin by its own thrift, holding membranes together against heat that would wick them into dust. Its whole world is the shadow side of a cylinder and a schedule you could scratch into metal with a nail.

The fungus on the baffle is cartography. It oxygenates the calendar. Each cooling hour is a pattern it draws in a chalk so thin your breath would erase it if you dared a light to see. It shrinks itself like a closing hand when the mains go warm; it ghosts a little in the dark if you brush it with a fingertip, and for a second, the room has a constellation.

The iron-eaters are patient masons. They have built their brittle city around a flange as though it were a hot spring, which it is to them: a seep of metal made digestible by the way water stood there every time the ledger far above released its cold. Their reef is russet, and when it breaks it smells like the room’s truth: metal and old rain. They live by venial theft, taking a powdered hint from the joint and turning it into a structure that looks like architecture if you grant architecture to anything that builds once and lets change carry on in its absence.

Luminous bacteria halo a pinhole leak high in the dark, and you can be alone in this room and still feel watched. What lives there is not sighted, but it is a way of seeing: a smear that measures flow by surface area, by how much of itself it can afford to keep bright, and, when the hand with the brush comes on the quarterly, how much to regrow before thrift is virtue again.

Animals are less citizens than apparitions. They happen and then they un-happen.

The isopod—white, lima-bean length, legs splayed like a handprint—works the gutters for salts. It does not hurry when the mains go cold; it does not panic when steam ghosts somewhere it shouldn’t. It grazes almost without moving, mouth against a mineral seam, riding the slope of condensate toward a sump like a bead in a furrow. It molts in a place you never see and leaves a casing so exact you think for a second a twin died there.

Roaches write fast equations in the ribs of the grating and then solve them by vanishing. Their numbers swell on a schedule that harmonizes with the schedules of brine. They know the tie on the cable that gives the last, sweet drop. They ascend the cable ladders when the rectangle rain falls and drink from their own remembered bead. Their lacquer prisons light in thin glints at the edge of your own dark-adapted vision. Their dead dry to coins that slide in the drafts; you find them in the traps like small, brittle coppers stamped with a house you haven’t visited.

Once a season, if a season still means anything to a room with no window, a rat misreads the map of moisture and comes too deep. It is a creature of upper rooms—of fan hum heard through walls thin enough to teach it day from night—and it gets lost in a heat that talks like felt on skin and never breaks. You find what’s left of it not as a horror but as a lesson: a crisp package of ribs with the idea of fur clinging still. The room does not celebrate its death. It measures it out as a change in odor, a brief perturbation in insect arithmetic, then returns to its work.

In the sump under the access stairs there is a rumor of a frog. Sometimes it knocks—a body, soft and resolving into itself, moving from one rung of water to another—and then it is as though no body lived there at all for days. If you lift the grate, a smell like wet fabric folded too long climbs and sits in your throat.

It could be a frog, brought when a worker upstairs propped a door in a rain and a new river wrote a temporary white line on the steps and kept writing until the line found the lip of the hall. It could be the pipes asking for relief from their own weight and answering themselves. If it is a frog, it eats roaches it can catch and dreams in a language of chilled water coming like weather at intervals. It has no business in this room and so is perfect for it.

These are the obvious lives. They touch surfaces and leave residues you can scrape onto your thumb and sniff. They give you bodies to turn under a glove. They are the kind we catalog when we tidy an ecology into notes. But something else abides here, less discrete, more like the way a long game teaches its players.

Hands live here, though the hands themselves are in other rooms now, in other occupations, or dead. What is left of them—the shining comets along valve wheels, the places brass is not green because skin was salt and a solvent, the head of a fastener that shows you where a wrench always missed and then learned—are a population. They are a migration route held in metal. They show you how people moved in a place designed to slow movement to a minimum. Every touch polished, every neglect furred: in that contrast is a census.

The people who belong here are not residents by sleep or addresses. They keep their hours above, but they come with a key heavy enough to make a shoulder lean under its weight and a respect for rules written nowhere but in the way this place punishes disrespect. They ration light because they have cleaned out the consequences of bringing it and know too much about blooms to risk easy kindness. They count drips. They read thermometers with fingers because glass keeps a ghost of numbers even after eyes forget. They bring a brush that leaves bright hair in the chew of a seam sometimes. They have rituals: seal the door; wait until the room remembers itself; hold still to hear whether a burp of pressure will ask them to wait longer still.

One of them taught a newer one not to set anything down without a thought for what condensation would do to it and what its rust would do to the next hand. One of them once laughed here and then turned down a fan because the joke condensed in a corner and began to feed a patient blackness. They are people you never meet unless you become one of them or unless your building fails you so slowly that what they do becomes the difference between a habit and a haunt.

They live here in the same way the room’s rules live here: not as signs on a wall but as consequences. Heat moves first, and so they think ahead of it, shadowing a hand above a housing to feel the roll of a sleeper before they put a wrist near a belt. Water follows memory, and so they make lists of places where water liked to be last year and schedule their rags accordingly. Light is rationed; they carry lamps that make tunnels rather than rooms. Noise goes away; they learn to work with tools that are quiet because too much insistence in this place comes back as rain in forbidden chambers. Touch is maintenance; they read handles the way miners read air.

There is also the life of the machines taken at face value: a fan, a motor, a pump, a rack of exchangers. That life is somatic. It is the life of something turning in sleep and drawing a breath only because the torso it belongs to remembers breath. It is measured in service intervals, in the arc a belt sketches when it leaps because of age, in the way a bearing grumbles once and then has the decency to hold for two more months until the inventory level allows a replacement.

These machines are not alive in any engineer’s prideful, frightened sense. They do not decide. But they have preferences. The exchangers like their dust certain. The fins prefer chalk to oil. The motor finds a kindness in insulation at a degree too low to feel humanly and labors harder for a few minutes when the door opens because someone forgot and left their heat on their back a second too long before entering. If you listen long enough that you stop calling it listening and start calling it waiting, you can hear them teaching you what not to do by doing it in your imagination and feeling the room flinch.

There are, finally, the non-animals we might call elements if we had lazy respect left. Heat and water live here as two long histories that cross. Heat is a resident more constant than any worm. It has beds and boulevards in this room. It occupies volumes and then, at the allowance of a ledger, is displaced, sulking to some other corner where it nests again. When the machines turn, heat rolls fractionally in its sleep. It talks to gaskets. It skins metal a little so that salt can find something to hold. It knows dark as a richness; it knows air movement as insult and then forgiveness.

Water is a memory. It spends itself in places it remembers spending itself before. A crease near a weld looks like yesterday looks like five years ago. A cable tie cups a droplet in an attitude that imitates every droplet that ever paused there, and that imitation is half of survival. It leaves maps for life to find and life finds them: the algae stripe is nothing but water remembering a shadow and a coldness; the baffle chart is water writing its meteorology, fungus laying ink over it so it can be reread; the iron reef is water telling bacteria where meals have always been.

Between these two, the room has a spine: the copper bar that is ground and hums with the words of distant pumps. Electricity lives here too, as a nerve. It is careful because it has to be. It dislikes wet and when it is insulted by a leak it does not kill anyone because the people who live here understand it and have already done the work that makes that sentence true. It passes news from the ledger to the solenoids like a courthouse clerk, stony, bored, inevitable.

If you want a single inhabitant to fix with a name, there is a way to say that the room itself is alive. Not fancifully, not as metaphor, but as an organism whose organs are interchanged over years and whose function never changes: to trade heat for water and water for air in a ratio the city can afford. Its skeleton is metal; its blood is brine; its breath is a fan that turns once in a while because that is enough. It heals at a speed available to budgets. It forgets light on purpose. It punishes the undisciplined and rewards the faithful with silence.

All the other lives—films that bright and dim, roach arithmetics, the white isopod’s slow festival of salts, the rumor under the stairs—wed themselves to this host. They make use of its memory and add their own. Hands come to read those palimpsests and leave notes in polish. The thin intelligence up high, the one with the lamp, keeps its rules like beads thumbed smooth with use.

So what lives here is continuity, expressed as colony and schedule and trace. It lives at the edges of necessity. It chooses damp only where it chooses. It keeps its long attention span by making everyone who enters submit to it, whether they are a frog, a film, or a person with the wire brush and the habit of not treating darkness as absence but as a tool. Outside there is sky and human weather. In here there is work that has outlived days, and the beings that can stand to live with that kind of time.
