They move three ways and none of them are walking. Along the ridge, they are hands and heels and hooked toes, a creature unspooling itself along lines. In the veils, they are leaves, leaning their bodies to the slant of sweetness and letting the lighter water carry them from span to span. On bare rock, where the basalt makes its clean, young knives, they are a string of points—fingers to edge, fingers to edge—measured, always measured, because a single foolish haste is an end.

They are not fish, though they have learned to wear water the way a fish does. They are not exactly human, though they have human thrift and human handwork and the way of pressing meaning into pattern. Skin the color of a mossed stone in rain, slicked sometimes with vent-resin where the currents ask for more armor. Hair grown long and then convinced into rope, thonged through with slate chips and shell to make it a sensible tail for weight and for storage—a personal counterweight pantry, a memory plait. Eyes wide and flat with dim pewter in them, a soft second lid that closes when a halocline stings. Where the collarbone would be there is a slit guarded by a fold, and when they are working and the work is hard those side-mouths feather open and shut very fast; when they rest they hardly pulse at all. If you press your palm to one of their ribs you will feel a seam line that is not bone, a wet sense-organ that hums when a long wave passes.

Their mouths are for more than air. They taste with their lips, with the whole surface of their faces, the way you might taste snow by smelling it. The falls tell them much—what a storm did with its time above, what the shaven stone is giving back to the water, how far the sweetness will run today. To a stranger’s eye their heads are always tilted a very little, as if to listen to a voice just out of reach. They are listening; just not to voices.

They wear clothes in the old sense: things close to skin, labor-saving and source of pride. They wrap themselves in kelp-skin that has been scraped and sung and dried by heat that isn’t fire. They cinch themselves into belts with bladder-fronds knotted on in small, literate ways, stone counterweights slipping along braided guides to keep a body true in the pull. Each of them is an arrangement with gravity. You can read their lives in the arrangement. A novice is all stone at the hips, overbalanced against buoyancy and flustered by the way even water will take you up if you forget to tell it not to. A day-worker’s belt is boring, brutal, a non-narrative of loaders and sudden changes. An edge-walker’s is a loom of cords and bladders with marks set for depth and with lashed-in gifts from the dark—blue glass, cooled droplet-stones that took a year to lift safe through the haloclines. A midwife wears another sort: almost lightless, all small knives and soft cords and things for comforting.

Their voices are there, but only as a courtesy to the nearness of their kind. They learn rope-speech before they learn to make words: a grammar of tugs and tensions, codes in the handline that runs through the day. To think aloud is to hang your thought on the public net and let it quiver into other palms. When they stand together, they speak with their wrists; when they are apart, they speak through a street. Because sound stratifies even when you do not want it to, they make speech that does not offend the water. When they must sing, they sing by pulling, by plucking, by pressing a hall’s skin until it hums in delicate answer. Lovers and children get the real voices; the kind that need lips and teeth and a small pocket of peace held between faces.

They do not eat the place. There is not enough of animal to take from it without ending it. They eat what grows. They tend their veiled fields like chandeliers, because sweetness is for greens and infants and sorrows that need a kinder taste. There is a plant that makes a long ribbon with translucent lappets, the color of old jade; they eat those boiled-by-vent in baskets until their tongues ache with minerals. There are bladder-fronds they harvest after the first storm of the lightning season, when the falls glitter and everything that is small learns fertility again; those they shred into pith to pack into soil-baskets, and the older pith, sour with the remembering of many roots, they roll into balls and leave in eddies to turn and turn and become their strange pickled breads. In the carbonate weirs they grow shelled things that move so little the water forgets they are animals. When they do kill, it is ceremony and economy both: a chalk-grazer trapped against the grain of a stair with a net and paid for with a year’s patience, a comb of amphipods brushed into a jar and shocked in sweetness, a slow blue-belled jelly that died of its own bigness and must not be wasted—cut and cut and everybody mild with the stings for days.

Their children are tied to the city as a safety and as an introduction. In the warm netted rooms the midwives swim very slowly around them for days, pressing wrists to bellies to feel the second pulse, measuring the color of the gill-frills against the green. There is a first-knot day when a small hand is guided around a loop and the tiny tendons learn the particular fatigue of things that matter. There is a first-veil day, earlier if the falls are kindly that season, when a child is eased through sweetness into salt and taught the taste of the change. Teachers do not shout. They take a hand and put it in within your hand. They let weight do its lesson. A patient rope and an honest fall are their schoolmasters.

They carry their tools the way some people carry songs: memorized and brought out often. Obsidian blades tucked inside kelp-sheaths that will not fuzz the edge, gloves rasp-sewn from skate-skin to let a hand love a rock without opening, rayspines in a twist of vine that can be drilled through anything that is their size. Their genius is in twine and time. You never see a raw knot; nothing temporary stays. Even a child’s fast tie will have the care of a dozen tiny improvements in it before it holds a month. They are proud of their tar, too, which is a little wicked and smells like old lightning; they make it in cages of woven stone under the warm breath of the vents and test it against their own skin, because there is no other way to know.

Their law is knotted. This is not a metaphor. The rules of the lines are cords kept on public hooks: rope-books heavier every year, cleansed in sweet now and then, pressed and proofed and spoken with the hand on ceremony days. A quarrel is loosened down and retied with witnesses. A lineage is a length you can touch and find your mother’s mother’s name in the twist if you have been taught the reading. Sometimes they argue by demonstration: two rigs for a hard span built in the same place, and the season decides which law is better at holding through.

Their politics are also a conversation with weight and water. They have people who tend veils and can take a drink and tell you how many days they’ll have for transplanting. They have people who listen with their bones to the basalt, who have tucked a jawbone under a strap along the brow since youth and can feel the particular clock in the rock that is not quite the same from year to year. They have the ones who go down and cut the stairs. You can pick them out by the knuckles: scarred to the color of old glass, the joints fat with pressure stories. They come home slow so their bubbles stay inside them, lightened with care across the veils so every tissue remembers patience; they lie in warm nets and allow time to climb back into them. The children are quiet those days because the rock-cutters are home and dreaming, and the dreams ride the nets and make a hum all through the rooms.

They make art the way they make anything, by setting things under the rule of a long pull. The net a person made ten years ago and the net they make now: that is their difference and their display. Lace-work nets for nothing but the joy of eating light: the way a fine weave bends it, the way a halocline’s skin goes slick across it and throws a shadow your hand wants to drink. They grow sculptures, too, patient as lichens, coaxing bacterial milk to lay chalk along a guide until a curve is no longer water’s idea alone. They have lanterns: slow, harmless combs kept in walled hollows that comb their light out at nightfall and teach children where their feet are. Music, when they ask for it, is rope-plucked and done to the beat of a long current, or a cracked plate of basalt struck just once and allowed to speak as far as it wants to go.

They have gods and they keep them deliberately small. The upworld—where the storms run and the ceiling is—is not theirs. They can taste its decisions but not touch them, and so they do not make stories that pretend otherwise. The warm places are immediate and answer to habit; they are thanked and kept trimmed like any good hearth. The jelly that sometimes climbs, blue in the body like a sky they have never seen, is not a god. It is a messenger or a cousin or a calendar page, depending on who you ask. The rock is honored because it keeps its promises, and because sometimes it does not. Death is a release of knots. They sit and loosen what they can, and then the body is taken down, down, tied careful to the stair they have not cut yet. It takes a season to go one ledge farther, but the dead have time and so do they.

Their way of fear is the opposite of the panic you see in creatures with little air to spare. They have air enough all around them, dissolved and happening, and they respect that. What they fear are the fast things that do not belong here: a rope cut that has not been cut open between friends and rebraided with kindness; a new eddy that rips a veil off its anchors and puts sweetness where it will drown; a season of lightning so rich that the falls taste like coins and the plants go mad and choke the gullies. They watch, and they never, ever hurry the fixing. They make more rather than make mistakes. They have outwaited slips of rock and slips of temper.

They love each other in ways that are practical and extravagant at once. Lovers tie themselves together for whole long-drawn shifts and do their day’s work with the other’s weight a persuasion at their side. Elders are sat in nets that hum warm and brought bowls of moss and bitter chalk-syrups and the silk peeled from a young kelp-bulb, and their joints uncurl in the hot like anemones. Children are pinballed between hands all day, and every hand is a version of your mother when she is busy. A person’s best self is said to be in their slowest work and in their quietest rescue.

From a distance—out beyond the ruled light, looking back through the gray—what lives there is pattern where there would otherwise only be accident. The green has been taught to stand still in some places so that the sweet can find roots. The bladders lift and fall in a procession that tells the time. Nets swing but are not wrecked. The ribs of the new-made ridge have straps across them where fingers have been. If you sit with your back to a spine at the settling hour and feel the tiny tones come through it, you can hear an intention you could call human if you like. They are the ones who make a home by learning the water’s habits and letting the water learn theirs. They live inside patience the way other people live inside houses. They will need a long time, but the ridge has it to give, and they have hands.
