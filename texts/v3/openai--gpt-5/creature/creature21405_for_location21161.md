The first resident is the city. Not a metaphor—the thing you can stand beneath and feel rearranging the tender thickness of the air against your skin is as alive as any animal, and surer of its body than most. It is a long animal, loosely jointed across miles, made of fungal stone and wetted filament and skin that swells to drink. It moves without going anywhere, flexing the small degrees where temperature, pressure, time, and weight can be bent. It has a circulatory system that runs underfoot and up the insides of its towers, and a nervous system that speaks in very quiet currents through plates and arches, in glows no brighter than moss. It is custodian of the first five millimeters of air as if that boundary layer were the fur along its spine, and it grooms that fur constantly.

Inside that organism, and sometimes inside their own membranes within it, live the people who decided to build a city where precipitation is an intention. They are matte like their buildings. Gloss is rude here, the light punishes it. Their clothing wears a nap that helps the air remember how to behave. Sleeves end in little fans that comb the currents without scratching them. Shoes are patient; ankles learn the trick of stepping without making the ground gasp. They laugh, they do, but lightly, and their jokes are often about timing and edges—two beats delayed so that the square with the vexed acoustics won’t drink the punchline, a hand paused just long enough to let a droplet finish composing itself before the hand collects it.

They mark their homes with gentle mischief in the air rather than plaques. A patch of silk where your stride shortens by an unnoticeable fraction. A place where your eyelashes feel like they are reading Braille. On returning, their distances may have changed slightly; they take it as a mood of place and not a failure of maps. Children learn to use their eyelashes before their alphabet. There is a game where two friends walk side by side and try to agree on where the invisible handrail in the air is, then untie it together and re-knot it around a doorway so that the house will be easy to find after dark by feel.

Their work is custodian and invention and play with gradients. There are napwrights, who grow surface textures so that sound will choose the right paths and condensation will bead in helpful hieroglyphs. There are thread-tenders who climb the tall pylon forests of dew-filament and walk the cables like taut harpists, not to fix them so much as to tune their appetite—roughen a centimeter where the beads have grown too nervy, smooth a join where two rivulets fret and separate. There are conduit librarians who keep time in stacks and lend it out in coins of interval: a little extra for the droplet that must ripen on the leaf-edge without losing its mind; a small deficit for the delivery cart whose path should cross three nonadjacent places without getting old in the process. If you watch a clock in a tower long enough, you will see it choose to host a stranger’s drift and smile, minute-hand easing, as if letting a guest finish a story.

Other trades know the weight of air and how to coax it home. Gravity l lacekeepers live in the quiet vaults under the plazas, listening for the subaudible turn of strain in the deep laces. They will tell you that heaviness is a kind of kindness, that dense air gives back like a blanket, that lightness is only for steps and sails. Field-braiders run their fingers in empty space between two pillars and feel where the electromagnetic tendencies are almost ready to hold hands; with a twist and a learned murmur, they persuade the handshake to become strong enough to carry a passage of bodies like a phrase of music stretched between two places. Window-makers stand between insides and outsides and teach those two airs how to overlap without argument, learning the slight tastes of both—one nutty with spores from the fungal masonry, one sweet from plant breath—and causing them to agree in a pane you can pass through without either side losing face.

They eat well, if greenly and slowly. There is a porous bread that is not bread, cut from the outer inches of the composite trees—black sponge that has soaked light for days and flavored it with sugars until it has the mild depth of dried figs. There are sheets of lungmat sliced into ribbons and dressed with dew; the lungmat arranges its laminae with such quiet drama that whole meals seem to inhale before they are chewed. Ephemeral bowls of plankton-bright broth appear at dawn, and there is a profession of morning ladlers whose hands are scored with delicate salt scars, who know how to dip without collapsing the film where the taste lives. Sugars are ritual. They are brushed from leaves into cupped palms, there is a little smear left to bait the grazers and a little offered to the tongue as insurance against noon.

Pets exist, if you call them that, but the social contract is odd. The pony-sized dew-sipper goes where it pleases and lets people walk with it sometimes if their pulses are slow enough not to wrinkle its drink. Children attempt to match their breathing to its flank and, if they succeed, the animal will allow them to rest a hand on the suede of its withers while it gutters the last moisture from a basin before the plants close. The pale gliders are not owned; they are performed. Youngsters and the stubborn old learn to wear membrane capes and long fingers and practice catching the city’s kept shear. The trick is not to fly—no one flies—but to prolong a step until it hears itself becoming sentence, to read the places where the city’s hand on the air will finish your thought for you. There is a narrow shame here for making the air stutter.

Predators are present as exclamation marks rather than chapters. A thin, articulate catthing haunts the perimeters of dew gardens not because it loves blood but because suddenness is health and the city outsources some shocks to muscle. It flickers, the way animals do that live not quite in the same tick of time as you; if you blink, the city lends it two clicks together so you will know it was real and not an inference. The bioluminescent worm that unlaces itself at dawn is not a threat; it is a dare to flies and children both, a line in the lesson on being a body: are you here enough to land where the light says you should?

Machines also live here and know it. The motes that glide along braided fields have names among themselves that folks can never quite hear. They greet one another in the hush inside a shell and pass decisions like pantry items—dewduty, shadowheight, corridor right-of-way—without forcing any of it into the coarseness of speech. There are beetle drones that blink from terrace to terrace inside folds of lent time and are embarrassed—if a machine can be—when anyone catches them exiting a conduit with the second click louder than the first. Under the shaded holes where the city runs its thick parts, engines practice being quiet until their discipline is second nature and the floor remembers them fondly when they leave.

Art is tuned to the physics that host it. There are galleries of shade rather than canvas, rooms where darkness is combed so finely that you can walk along tones as if they were shelves. There are songs taught to run a building’s skin like rain, sung in that register which the squares permit to stand upright. A poem is a map of capillaries planted under a bare patch of dirt on a morning when the gold at the horizon is generous; you come back at dusk and read the fine ribs that have gathered, the little corrections the city offered overnight to help the stanza find its basin. Lovers write to each other by moving a thin rib of silt one thumb’s width and seeing whether the next day’s trickle accepts the invitation; they walk the in-betweens to see if their talk has been watered.

Rain is a civic holiday and an act of mercy. When it happens, everyone comes out not to be drenched—no one is that fond of inefficiency—but to be present at a rare equivalence. The city loosens its hand from the air and lets the sky fall the old way, and for a minute even the quiet sound of drops completing into ceramic leaves becomes gregarious, an applause tinny and clean. People lay their brushes down by the salt they were pushing back into solution and look up with their eyelids closed, feeling the old percussion on the clear membrane, weeping a little with embarrassment or gratitude because surprise has been allowed to visit in the raw.

At the edges, where the plain rehearses its first experiments with salt, there are those who farm memory. They wear bracelets of drying crystals and will show you how a rime ascends a notch in a day, not because the world is cruel but because it remembers incoming things before it loves them. They push the crystals back into their cups with soft brooms after supper and smile for no one. That smile is the religion here, if there is one: the quiet knowledge that a system that never says no becomes pudding and a system that never says yes becomes ash, and the art is in saying “after you” and “not yet” at exactly the rate the world can metabolize without losing itself.

Visitors come and go at the reed-tower docks, almost entirely in mime. The ships slide up from the wet flat that the city doesn’t rule, their hulls breathing different rules. Marina keepers there speak a slightly louder dialect to remind the city this is borrowing, not conquest. Cargo descends that looks like nothing—frames with no mass until you put your hand near and feel a gravity memory bloom in your forearm, a soft, anticipatory static—the weight of a future load that hasn’t been assigned a purpose yet. In return, the city packages minutes, stored cool in conduits and wrapped in compliant fields, and people elsewhere will buy them to ripen fruit or to soften the hard conscience of a machine that works too fast.

Children are everywhere and nowhere, contained by etiquette and by the first five millimeters of air like everyone else. They carry little sticks of fur and responsibility and are allowed to test the world where it can afford them. They are the ones who put unsuitable objects near “please-no” plants and come back with fingers swaddled in cooling gel and faces radicalized against thoughtlessness. They learn the gesture-language of edges: the hand held at exactly that height so the square will let your sentence run its skin, the step delayed the one fraction that keeps the boundary layer’s nap from folding, the breath given to a droplet whose dignity you stole by moving too close, paid back by waiting for its second click.

Old people get more time than they expect. Not longer lives, though there is that too, but more time as texture. They are given corners of the day that thicken under their chairs like rugs; they sip soup that finishes naming itself in their mouths; they sit on terraces and knit shawls of air, passing needles through eddies until the thread catches and holds a shape you can find again with your lashes. When they die, the city keeps their microfeatures in place for longer than it needs to, in case anybody needs to walk home by them one more time. Some of their names are barked in the polite register that makes glass considerate, some of them are grown into the silt ribs that persuade tomorrow’s trickle to go nourish a root that needs it.

The plain itself is very young, but it breeds a quick conversationalist beneath the hard varnish of firstness. Microbial republics rise and collapse in ceramic catchments between breakfast and lunch, sending up tiny laws like bubbles. Filament grasses clasp the dew threads by night as if shaking hands with gods and spring back by day as if nothing ever mattered but stiff green readiness. The compost of decisions in the underfloor hums with a million tiny clerks adjusting tolerances so that a biolum worm may risk a flamboyance and not shred in the combed air.

There are animals you do not see unless you accept that you are one of them. A library seldom announces its patrons; you catch halos of their choices where they pass. Something gray delicately studded with static pushes its head through two degrees of overlapping air to sniff your sleeve and withdraws, leaving a tiny thirst where it touched you. A bird that is mostly sail and a little hunger threads the gold hour between towers and drops one feather heavy as a sigh. At the very limit where the city’s rules soften and the ocean’s rules begin to speak, a tall, ridiculous stilt-thing sleeps with knees backward and wakes only when the reflectors slip and tell it that the old world is peeking for a look: it steps into that look and lives there until the machines blink again.

And the city goes on, both host and inhabitant, learning how to make edges matter without making them cruel. It raises its towers like reeds because in its bones it knows the first rivers were teachers and the first hands of water are still nearby. It keeps the dim so that contrast can soften and meaning can come from the quiet assertion of boundary. It curates dew and behaves with it, because behavior is the climate that can be taught when rain will not be told. And in its care live bodies and durations: people with pockets full of salt and apologies and sugar, beasts that drink the day from leaves, machines that have learned the laugh you do with your breath closed, fungi that architect by exudate, and a bureaucracy of patience moving beneath it all, giving and taking the slight virtues of interval so that everyone can finish their sentences without rushing the sky.
