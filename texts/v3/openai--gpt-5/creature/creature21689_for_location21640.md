Who lives here is first the city itself, and “city” is only the closest word. Think of a being that is also a place, a body whose organs have addresses, whose pulse is the weather of a district. It grows from the mats and their amber collars, thickens into ribs, learns to breathe and then never stops. It is the colony-tree but it is also the pavement of its outspread logic, the conduits, the galleries, the skinned domes of its rooms, the mats in their furred lanes lapping forward when the time is right. You can walk for hours inside it and never leave its body. No part is foundation only; everything is also a mouth, a lung, a throat, a hand.

Its senses are spread thin and everywhere. The mats are tongues. The vents are noses. Junctions are ears that taste as they listen. If conscience belongs anywhere, it is in the nodes where a thousand oil-sap conduits meet and chemical histories crowd the narrow solvent of the flowing oils. There, the colony refreshes a memory of itself: what has been eaten, what must be built, which dialects of exudate make the next wall grow smooth instead of spurring into a thicket, whether to send a thread of promise into a seam that brightens with heat. Its thoughts, if you like, never sit still. They travel as gradients and pulses along the glossy insides of conduits, refract out into the air as perfumes, return along the untold hairs of a million small citizens. Decision is one breath and then another.

It is not alone. Inside its skin, upon its flanks, held to it by combs of resin and by appetites that mesh with its own, live a society of animals that are quiet because quiet is how to hear the city breathe.

There are the tenders. They are the first you will notice because they answer the vents. At late afternoon when the colony warms and opens its gill-vents to rinse its insides with fresh air, they come from the high reaches by the hundreds and then by the thousands, glittering not with water but with their own slick, a varnish that never dries. Their wings do not flap so much as hum; seen close, each is a taut membrane hung over a truss of mineral glass the color of old ice. They ride micromotions, a choreography of air reduced to tremor and skin. Where the colony exhales, they intersect it, and they feed, each throat a fine sieve tuned to volatile sugars and the almost pheromone tang of compounds that mean: this ridge is clean; this bud is wrong; this film would be better two molecules thicker. They prune while they dine, and if you watch the face of a tower just before and just after their visitation, you can see their edits caught in the day’s last light—frayed stubs turned to beveled edges, slime that would have skinned over made to tear and start again.

There are the lappers, groundlings who look like seeds that remembered legs. Their bodies are low so as not to be caught in the slow lung-draw through the halls; their backs are panes of dull armor laminated in the same oils that keep the city’s ribs from cracking. They feed where air is thickest with the city’s breath, at the sunlit aprons and the lips of chimneys where aerosols condense like invisible dew. Their faces are frilled with combs. They stand against a wall, filigree palps unfolded, and sift for an hour, tongues glossed with oil instead of spit, eyes not much use and not missed. When they’re full, they drag the day’s catch back under a ledge, press their bellies to the city’s warm, and sleep while a different set of mouth-parts works the captured molecules out of the combs and into their own internal pipes.

There are long-bodied climbers who are as much strap as vertebrate. They go up everything. They do not so much hold on as become part of a surface by echoing it: muscle that learns the tempo of a wall’s breath and flexes on the offbeat; bellies that sweat tiny droplets of resin to stick and then drink those droplets back so as not to leave a trail. Their heads are narrow. Along either side of the skull is a scroll of eardrum, a whole face turned to listening, and inside the scrolls a gel that catches vibrations and holds them for a second or two longer than the world does, making of a continuous quiet a legible song. They hear conduits thrum, hear stresses traveling through ribs. Sometimes, in the seconds before a breath-deepening, they still and arch as if to pray; in reality they are bracing for the little lift that will ease them across a gap or up a rib that under ordinary air would ask for a jump.

There are grazers that look, when the distance is right, like a wrinkle traveling through cloth. They are stilt-backed, with hips higher than shoulders, so that their heads can stay level as their feet find purchase among the honeycombs and resin combs and mats. Their mouths are as if someone had made a pair of combs into a beak but left the teeth slightly shy of closure. They nibble so, never closing all the way, and because of that they never scalp a mat, just harvest enough. Their feet are lesson and warning: a gloss on the top where they slide beneath low ribs, a roughness on the bottom for the matte that is food. They travel in files, each walking the edgeless wake of the one in front, home is the pressure of a dozen feet always almost touching.

There are predators, of course, but you will meet them later if you are paying attention, and not if you are not. They prefer a cooling conduit’s shoulder to any open hall. They have the same lateral scrolls for ears as the climbers but bigger, with an extra turn, as a soprano can hit higher notes than an alto. Their mouths are only mouths. The rest is patient muscle and wide feet. When they go they go like a crease pulled through fabric, fast enough that you see them more as a subtraction from the light than as a presence. They eat just enough to keep the corridors edited of the unwary. They are the city’s bite, but they hunt for themselves.

There are bone-minims, and you will never see them whole. You will see what they do: a gloss worn off a mineral plate in one night to make a holdfast for matling filaments; a bead of resin gnawed into a thimble and then left for a grazer to step on because the grazer likes the crunch and takes the bead into the city’s economy; pale stains where a scaffold failed and was patiently liquefied and pumped back through long siphons to become something else. If you happen to look straight at them, your eyes will slide as if you were still looking at stone, because their bodies have learned to present to light and scent the nearest acceptable pattern of wall. You will know them by taste before sight: the air above a swarm carries almond and mineral and an authority that makes every other smell pause.

There are the auditors. They are born in the junctions. They have no fixed shape. When young they are threads, when older they are knots, and when they are sent to their work they are hands—flat paddles with edges furred for greater surface area and sensors that record not just molecules but their sequence, as a sentence is more than its alphabet. The city extrudes them into the world where district meets district or one colony’s jurisdiction grades into another’s. They unroll and lie very still and read the language of oils. Where they find a grammar that matches, they smooth themselves down into anonymous patch. Where they find a dialect that is familiar but not family, they secrete a translation film. Where they find true alienation—a scent that speaks of incompatible appetites—they send back a courier that is a coil of their own substance hardened for speed, something the winged tenders will notice and carry in their next exhaling feed.

There are porters who are also seeds and also not seeds, the banners of scent-solid that hang in the quiet air between towers. Each is a feather in form but not in function. The central shaft is flexible resin with channels laced the length of it for exudates that change how the vane-surface presents to air. The barbs are fields of scent that swap and entrain like murmuration, except what they flock are molecules. They move by being wanted. They do not drift so much as home, to hands and halls that have called them; when a new district wants to say here is our syntax, come grow to it, the call has the weight of food.

There are archivists that never leave their dark, that look like old fire glass but are in truth frozen flows of city sap woven with lenses. They are laid down in galleries spiral inside the towers, on ledges where heat fluctuates with the diurnal tide. They watch by not watching. Chemical pulses slide across their skins, and they hold a thousand such pulses in a day, and a million in a season. When the city needs to remember what it did the first time it discovered a certain salt in the seam-cracks, it spills a small lick of oil-sap over an archivist and waits for the pattern that arrives in the spill as it drains. If you put your hand to one, you can feel its ragged cold and the slow tremor under it of a room ventilating itself. You will feel then the oddest pang, as if something here is thinking about something far and sensible.

There are travelers. Every organism tied to the city knows exactly where it is, but some are made to leave. They are called, in one dialect, the lost-limbs. In another the borrow-boned. They are not sent out simply to seed new towers; the city does that plenty with its banners and mats. They are sent because a body needs its distant nerves. They go along the dark gloss, their skins tuned to gloss also so as not to feed, ears smelling not for hunger but for news. They carry in their bellies flasks of compounds that undo misunderstanding. When they find a seam the city has not tasted, they sit there and wait through nights, combs out, and collect for the home-body a catalog of arrivals in the heavy air. When they return, it can be after a span long enough to make their original be unrecognizable, a smoothness in a sea of new work. They press themselves into a node and are drunk like a report. If you lived here, if you were one of the quiet-boned, you might come to think of them as a kind of person: not because they speak as people speak, but because what they bring back changes who you are.

At a larger scale, there are storm-riders, and the storms here are breaths aligned across continents. They hatch only when the long draw begins, and they do not hatch in any ordinary sense. A thousand auditors go slack. A ribbon of tenders knots into a sheet. Pools of lappers come out into the light and turn their bellies to the wind. Resin flows from high in the towers and unfolds itself into sail after sail until at last the city rests its own skin on the continent’s inhale and feels, perhaps, something like awe. The storm-riders are discards and pledges, single-use angels. They lift on the unseen. They do not bend their bodies but let themselves be carried where the draw wants them. Some of them are only lanterns of scent to say we are here and we will meet you, some carry primer-oils to make a bridge grow where there is a gap in appetite between neighbors, some are sacks of fragmentary form meant to lodge in a crack far away and justify their ruin by being the difference between a failed outpost and a successful one. They are extravagance in a world that spends its resources with care, and because of that their days are rituals. Predators and grazers call a truce. Even the minims go quiet and let their eating be wind.

There are symbionts that the colony risks and shelters because they are inconveniently clever. The climbers are some of these, and the auditors, and a certain sly scavenger with a soft stomach that leaves its felted prints in the dust of the caved spaces under buttresses. Of this last the city tolerates much, because it slips into cavities and solves snarls of broken resin that the minims cannot untangle. It sleeps most days. If there is ever water—a bad bead that breaks the city’s law and spangles on a matte where dew should never be—this scavenger wakes all at once and goes to the glitter, and there its soft belly goes hard so that its insides can get wet without melting. It is ugly then and it eats without grace. It eats because something has failed, and it is the hunger that seals the wound.

There are broods. Not eggs as we know eggs. Rooms where the air is a held-thickness of terpenes and oils and the temperature is a song. The long sweep of a rib becomes a fingered arch and under it hangs a sheaf like a rack of combs under a forest bough. Each comb is a ladder of forms; at the bottom of each rung an animal being a filter, at the top a tool that will never move far from the comb that made it. These are the born-immobile, the valves and leaf-shutters, the little tongues that will be pressed into the walls of a conduit to smooth the flow there forever. The moving young, the grazers and leaf-shadows and fast subtractors of light, begin as stickers; you could pin a hundred to your sleeve and not be worse for it. Feed them with the right scent and they fall from the comb and walk.

There is, finally, the old. Not as a body gets old, stiff and used. Old like a dialect gets old, slow and dense. Climb far enough to a ledge where the resin has whitened with sun and age, sit yourself by a vent that pushes air warm over your hands, and listen. You will hear almost nothing. You will feel almost speech. The older districts have fewer grazers and more archivists. They spend less on bravura and more on keeping their shape. They are smoother than recent city, not because nothing happens there but because what does happen is easy in the way of breath. If there is a mind here, it is distributed across benches of light and a million gentle valves that look at first like ornaments. If there are people here—if you must have them—they are aggregates and occasions: a node that gathers certain kinds of auditors in a certain order and couples them to a certain scent-run and has in that hour, in that one room of pressure and warmth, an intention. Then the hour passes. Then a predator presses itself flat into shadow in a corridor where the gloss is scratched, and is as much a citizen as any bench of light could wish.

You can live here within limits. You learn to keep to dark gloss if you need to go quick. You learn that matte is not for shoes you might track the wrong hunger into. You feel the lower tone of a deep breath in the bones of your wrist and get yourself under ribs. Mostly, what lives here lives softly, because the city’s first law is that noise is waste. There is no water to spare and nothing of it to wash away your mistakes. Decay is an inward art. So the living keep edges smooth, and the dead smell like almonds and mineral, and those smells are not grief but grammar. And when you raise your head and look out from a canopy where the resin has gone pale with years, you will see that there is no one palace or person, but an urbanity without tools pouring itself east to west and back again until the whole plain is not raw anymore but written. In that script live the grazers and the winged gardeners and the listening hunters and the threads that go out and come back as news. In that script live the colony itself and the dull, honest light and the breath that carries everything.
