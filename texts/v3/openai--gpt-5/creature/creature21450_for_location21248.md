The first to live here are the ones that don’t make a fuss about living. They arrived with the rock before there was a place, tucked into the glassy faces like salt in a seam. They are powders, slicks, frostings: grit-bacteria that find purchase where a knife of basalt offers even a quarter of a whisper of pit. They drink light in crumbs and sense the rawness of the stone like a sweetness on the tongue. They press acids along their bellies and the volcano’s abrupt edges soften under them into something that can be held.

They are joined almost at once by the leaf-filmers. They are the quiet rain of thin green that fogs a pane of cold glass the second the warm air touches it. They make coats and then make coats upon coats. Their body plan is a habit more than a shape: every surface is a field, every field births a finer field. Where a broad leathery leaf finds its courage, the filmers cover it with lace; where lace makes a microcliff, they fringe it with smaller lace. They are so light the water carries them like secrets, reattaching wherever a fingertip’s worth of calm catches, and they speak to the stone with gels that taste and choose and glue.

The place fills with makers then. Mats learn to stand. Ribbons learn to hold hands with bubbles. This forest is not composed of “plants” as you were taught to picture them in air, but of habits that have learned to stack themselves until they are architecture. The bubble-vines are communal breath given skeleton: roped garlands of beads so bright they look like deceitful jewelry, flexing along the rises like someone playing cat’s cradle with a river of light. Where bubbles skitter upward, the vines twine, and over seasons their skins stiffen with salts coaxed out of solution. A necklace becomes a ladder, becomes a candelabrum with many upturned palms, each palm breeding more hands at its edges.

There are the tape-leaves, too: not quite leaves, more like long, wet tongues that have turned patient. They push themselves over nothing, into it, at the cliff’s brink, then sag and meet themselves again, stitching an arc with their own edges until, by increments, a shelf is made where no shelf had been. They are tireless in a way that does not look like effort. They drink light, and where light is poor, they slow their breathing until the next bright window meanders past and makes a noon out of a quarter hour.

Some of the builders never commit to a location. The floaters quilt themselves into bladders that are all buoyancy and appetite. They drift like schools of soft lanterns and brush the stone as they pass, saving nothing for the place except filaments rubbed off like thumbprints. But those filaments land, and if they meet a bubble chain or a frond-arm, they comb themselves into alignment and go to work. A whole citizenship grows from brushings.

There are rooters that are not roots in the terrestrial sense. Their bodies are gels and fibers and tiny splinters of rock they have deliberately gathered and cemented. They make themselves ladders out of what they can digest from the cliff—the way a mason lays bricks from the very wall he is dismantling. In their webs live other beings: metics of fuzz and heat-loving spray-spores; bacteria that burn the new minerals like slow fuel and pass along trace gifts to their hosts; clear worms like drawn glass that scrape edges with tongues of rough sugar and exhale little sighs when a vein yields. The soft clicks you hear—the mouths opening, the pods cracking—are them too, or their kindred, finishing a season’s work of packing a membrane with helium-light gas and then letting it go.

It is the engineers’ world, but they are not alone. Grazers move through it with the patient authority of those who have understood the rules and accepted them. The ray-beings—mantled, translucent, like the idea of a mantis shrimp unfolded into a kite—read braille with their bellies. They slide low over the shelves and press openings along their undersides to the carpet. Where their mouths go, a faint trail of de-inflation follows, the mats agreeing to be eaten the way a knitted blanket agrees to be un-looped for a span. The carpets do not die from this; if anything, they knit fresher when the ray has passed, fattening at the edges into darker green. Ray after ray writes a day’s worth of thin lines in the living.

The cylinders are another kind, more like a stem given the right to wander. They are jointed with amber plates that catch light like samples of trapped autumn. They move vertically, threading meadows as if they were needles and the plants were thread. They taste with their wrists. At each node, a ring of small mouths opens and closes, sipping the tips only, never the base, seed-tasters more than harvesters. Where they have been, the fronds thicken at the bruised ends, as if a gardener has told them to split their growth.

Small creatures abound, but you meet them when you teach your eyes to give up on silhouettes and look for verbs instead. Commas hop where shadows change, punctuation animated into appetite. Grains flash; they are armor in the making—minute crabs trying on the world a shard at a time, or ostracods that carry their homes like gossip. Little starbursts unfurl and refold in your shade: rotors that paddle water across their mouths, tiny crowns that hate to be seen wearing their crowns. They keep the city tidy. Every leak of sugar from a torn frond, every fall of micro-rain, is answered by a fleet of them. They are the hum, the low voltage that keeps the place awake.

If there are predators, they are the kind that insult the word by refusing to hurry. A few nets live here—gelatinous drapes no wider than your outstretched arms, hung in the still corridors where the bubble chains lean but do not break. They look like part of the plant architecture until a run of commas passes and the whole curtain veils, a million hair-hooks folding, an intake of water, and then stillness again. They do not actively hunt. They do not need to, not when the place brings the tiny to them as reliably as a tide but slower and quieter. In the shadow of ledges, you find the other kind: a mouth that is only pressure. It is a dome of almost-nothing anchored by three ribbons. It detects a bubble’s skin with nerves that are chemistry more than touch, and when a chain of pearls brushes by, it pulls an edge taut and sips the gas, harvesting buoyancy as food the way others harvest light.

At the edge—the brink where the light fractures down and down—the forest makes theater and actors to fill it. There are chimneys grown from mats that learned to tread their own waste, spiraled like the memory of a shell. In their vents, tube-animals dwell, pale as fingers, wreathed in cilia that talk to passing grains and sort them into keep and give. Candelabra rise, the bubble-vines grown old and ossified, and around their stiff arms coil snake-lichens that take calcium from the water and write with it. At their tips, pods like translucent bells develop, and on a day when the overhead brightness burns a tone bluer for a heartbeat longer than usual, the bells invert with a soft clap and release tessellated seeds that fall and do not quite fall—dials of living puzzle that turn as they sink, searching for a plane of calm.

Once in long hours the leviathan comes. It is not a hunter here. It is a weatherbringer, a shoulder of the far world nudging this one with the tact of an elder. Its skin is warm glass, not because it is heated but because it has slipped through places where everything else is cold and has collected warmth like a superstition. Its passage scatters a brief poverty of salts and thin flakes of other forests, and the locals accept them the way a town accepts the caravan’s dust: with mild interest, then with work. For days afterwards the tape-leaves lean as if listening for it to come back. The floaters collect in its wake, taking advantage of an upstart current, then drop their leftover threads on the promontories like thank-yous.

Some of what lives here never moves an inch and yet ranges more widely than any of the grazers. Endolights dwell inside the stone—small biochemistries that take the dull heat along the escarpment’s knives and rule it down, releasing it as a wintergreen glow when the day’s clear window passes far overhead and leaves behind a tone of blue that would otherwise be too black to work by. They are boring as miners and generous as priests; they perforate the basalt with candled pores where other things can later anchor. Around them collect stain-bands: colonies that prefer the exhalations of iron over those of manganese or copper, painting the cliff face in geological meters with what look like regular decisions.

Other inhabitants are defined by their habit of arranging what is not themselves. They are the baffle-masons: slow communal sheets that learn, by practice more than plan, how to contrive shelves in a place where everything wants to slide away. They read the micro-weather—the leaning of bubble-chains in the afternoon, the passing of that whiteness from the midwater, the almost-current that is more rumor than push—and put their tongues where the silt will fall. They do not need the silt; their bodies run on light and the rock’s scant liquor. But they know that where dust gathers, thicker lives will follow, and so they make thresholds and thresholds until a terrace happens, and on that terrace a grove, and on that grove a way for a cylinder to climb as if on stairs.

Sometimes a whole layer of living is nothing but the act of noticing. That grove that folds by degrees when a certain shade comes into the water is a single organism spread thin: a fan-forest that can compute the angle of the bright window by summing faint discomforts across its skin and closing its openwork a fraction if the day is less true than yesterday. This ledge that exhales in the afternoon is piping, plant-made: it harvests bubbles in the last hour before twilight and releases them once the brightness has fallen by a measure your eyes would call trivial and the ledge calls night. There are stacks of beings that do nothing but keep time for others—a row of plump bladders that swell like a chant; a bush of transparent forks that all decide at once which of two salts to prefer; a tangle of wrists that taste for heat and, when the escarpment mutters its little delaminations, throw their leaflets over their own growing tips like a crowd drawn by a rumor.

Even the loafers have symmetries. A ribbon that stays becomes a mother of ladders; a floater that never touches is the mother of trails. The grazers keep the new from smothering the newer. The microfauna keep rot from becoming architecture, or architecture from becoming rot; it amounts to the same thing viewed at different speeds. Predation exists as a punctuation and not a prose style. Birth happens so diffusely that you rarely notice any single birth; the place births itself by habit.

A few, rare as a comet in a sky with no sky, are those who move between heights. They are windows given muscle. Their bodies change the index of the water they pass through: you see it in the hours when the blue overhead suddenly hardens into noon for a while and every frond feels itself seen. If you could catch one—you cannot; to try would be to misunderstand it—you would find it is not a single animal at all but a procession: a raft of floaters, a braid of bubble-vines, a thin skin of habit-struck saline all co-conspiring to make a lens. They travel like rumors and leave improvement in their wake.

If a place can count as a living thing, the plateau does. It keeps its cycles and measures its own edges. It remembers storms that are not storms but midwater walls wandering in from a geography of density and heat. It remembers where it cracked and dropped a plate the last hundredth day and arranges its engineers along the new line before the shard has even found a resting angle. The inhabitants are the plateau’s organs and also its stories.

Who lives here? The stone that has just met itself as stone. The light that migrates through meters and turns into sugar with the calm faith of light everywhere. The mats that climb their own leavings and call it a tower. The vines that harden their breath into bone and hold out candelabra to the slow suns. The floaters that make a day’s route by brush and thread. The grazers that edit and in doing so compose. The commas that hop. The grains that flash. The starbursts that wear their crowns only when unobserved. The few that lay veils and take what swims into them without malice. The dome that drinks bubble. The leviathan that brings the smell of elsewhere and reminds the whole forest what scale is. The clocks hidden in fans and shelves and bladders, counting silently and then not so silently, when the time comes, in clicks and sighs.

They all live by leaning, by wrapping, by becoming their immediate neighbors until “who” is a matter of degree. They live by patience that is not passivity but pressure applied with a fingertip for a century. They live above the world and drowned, and the place they make is not a scene but a habit of scenes—visited by weather that is coolness, by windows that are water, by the occasional shoulder of something very large that needs no name.
