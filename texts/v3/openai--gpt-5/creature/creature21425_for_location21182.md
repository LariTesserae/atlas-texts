They are mostly skin, at first pass—skins upon skins. The primary residents are not bodies you could hold, but weavings that slick the young basalt the way breath slicks a mirror. They are films that behave like weather: haze of archaea and bacteria that condense along gradients you cannot see but they can taste with their whole selves. They whisper to iron and manganese and turn both into delicate masonry—twisted orange stalks, soot-black flecks that take the shine off glass. Where oxygen thins, others bloom milk-pale and breathe sulfur the way forests breathe rain. If you watch long enough, the skins seem to pulse, not with the beat of a heart but with the arrival and departure of elements through a seam that leaks like thought. They are the room’s first habit, its lung lining, the scripture on which every touch writes.

At their edges, life picks up threads. The films throw out cilia of sugar and catch silt; caught silt turns to scaffold; scaffold invites mouths. Bell-ciliates sprout like shy campanulas, tiny silver bells on stalks, each mouth rounding open to draw a cylinder of water into itself and closing with a startle-snap when shadow presses. Flat gliders with thread-tongues—crawling ciliates, slipper-shaped, glacier-patient for their size—graze the biofilm lawns from below, leaving mowing paths so fine they are really a change in gloss. Flagellates jitter in place like dust motes that learned a purpose. Nematodes stitch between everything, commas that decide where a sentence ends today. Some of them loop, glue a corner of themselves down, and fish the slow for what the slow can’t keep: a bacterium gone exploratory, a ciliate too absorbed in bell-pulsing to see a thread coming.

Plants, if you will forgive the word, enter next, though here it is better to think of them as places the leftover light remembers to become body. Red tongues the color of old bruises anchor in seams and feel the breathing room with two or three soft blades; they refold when the day decides to be heavier, unfold when the day lightens by a shade you would not swear to. Faint-green veils, world-thin, strive even so, taking on a translucent hunger that makes them seem made of intention more than pigment. Sea-moss—tender in the way dampness is tender—makes little rooms for microbes among its overlapping, shade-making pads. Touch it and ten small sovereignties resent you, but here no hand intrudes; here the moss thickens where microcurrents nuzzle more often, and in that snugness tiniest worms curl to sleep, then wake to wriggle their one bristle forward.

The colony-makers come and build their honeycombs of attention. Bryozoans gather on ledges and imagine parapets you could lean an eyelash on. A thousand starry crowns open and sieve, each mouth a citizen that budded from its neighbor, each body a room in a low, lumpy palace of chalk. They do not mind darkness; they ask only for consistency. When the tide breathes, they breathe with it, retracting in a ripple of mutual caution when a larger fact enters—an eel-length of doubt, the testing arm of a star.

Between the still and the more still, an isopod steps with monkish patience, beads of armor like mother-of-pearl prayer on its back. It reads the room by taste and texture. Its teeth are a ribboned file; its letters are scrapes that rasp the films into curved glyphs. The mats reply, thickening where it wrote, erasing after their own script—a call and response no human alphabet holds. The isopod moves as if it has all the hours, and here, it does. It is a devout in a church that is also its food.

Under a lip of salt-rough crust, the amphipod—unseeing, bone-pale—tends a door that is not a door until it says so. It drags a shard of shell, a tuft of moss, lintels of drift; it makes rectangles out of chance; it rehearses passage in a place that almost has no passages. When the silt storms fog the chamber to tea, it is first to set a threshold between outside and in, though both are in here. In its pouch, a clutch of eggs rides the slow inhalation and exhalation of the room. They are not told about light. They are told about touch: how a fingertip of current feels, how pressure stands behind you like an aunt you can lean into, how to build with crumbs.

Across the one truly flat span, a limpet keeps time. She has her home scar: a perfect circle of suction polished into the glass by years of returning. Each day (but days here are not a human issue) she arcs outward, a compass that knows only its own reach, and scrapes. Rock, film, rock again. She carves a comet’s ghost that brightens and dulls according to the patience of those she grazes. When she returns, she aligns herself with the certainty of something that remembers shape better than language. On rare occasions she lets go of gametes, honestly not for this room but because that is what she is built to release into the weight and patience beyond. The room receives them anyway, tastes them, tucks the taste into its low bookkeeping of events.

Worm is a word too large for the number of bristles this resident allows you to see at one time. It is a reconsideration flicker more than a creature, a quick sentence composed of hairs that translates a corner and is gone. When it commits to presence, it is a slender polychaete with its world in rows of gold pins; it hunts by thinking forward before moving forward, then sips a copepod so small you only knew it by the milk-cloud it made when it startled awake.

There is a sponge, but you have to let your expectations go translucent to see it. It spreads itself as a thin ochre band under a ledge, two pores wide, a sieve with a memory of rivers from an ancestry that never met a river. It moves water without moving, a constant pumping that in a day cleans more chamber than the chamber could hold, if water here were countable by buckets. When the outside strengthens, the sponge narrows its intakes the way an eyelid thinks about closing. It harbors symbionts in its flesh like libraries shelved inside a library.

Serpulid tubes thorn downward from the undersides like a stalactite idea translated for the horizontal. Each tube is a promise and a drawbridge. Feather crowns spiral out when the room is feeling generous. Tiny fans read the sea as braille and comb the alphabet of passing particulate for letters they can eat. At the first syllable of a star’s sticky foot, at an eel’s rumor, they zip shut and remember being stone.

Others are quiet coins pressed into crevices. Foraminifera raise chalky cups and push out cytoplasmic webs, sticky silk they throw over the rush and wait to see what sticks. Micro-diatoms—small flasks of winter light—wander in one by one and sometimes stay, silly as goose-feathers in a tool chest. Ostracods the size of a comma shuffle like armored seeds and sometimes burst their own small blue if jostled into declaration. Harpacticoid copepods—milk-smear evanescences—learn the room by antennae, crouch in the beard of mats, and go still in a way that suggests they have replaced motion with intention for a while.

Tardigrades, eight-clawed and unastonished, trundle across meniscuses you would need a second eye to notice. They eat what is under everything else, and everything else is what they live in. The chamber’s calm suits them; a grain of silt is as good as a moon.

The green and white and brown that are not plants and not not plants keep their calendar by chemistry. When the seam carries more iron, the rust-weavers throw out lace like fever—orange lace, delicate, ruined and replaced within days that feel like weeks. When a heavier molecule leaks in—memory from a river, a mouth of underground—the mats bloom in a way only a microscope could call exuberant, the whole room’s color deepening by a breath. When fogs of silt lift again, the films thin to more modest arrases and let the grazers spell their arguments across them.

A rookery of shells gathers by the seep because the weight of this and that sorts everything by what it can’t help being. In each shell, a story no larger than the space inside plays out: a snail that was, a crab that borrowed, a vacancy that now suits a dinoflagellate that found religion in stillness. The shells do what dead things often do in water: build walls the living can use. The ring looks made because the room has a way of choosing order where order can happen without a decision.

The sliver of wood that drifted here in a time when sea was a little busier is now a lintel braced with encrusters. Its grain holds darkness the way a palm holds heat. Filaments of endolithic algae do what sun-loving creatures always do with a rumor of light: they bore, slowly, because the rumor is enough. Fungal threads, salt-sober, creep the other way. Between them, a treaty is written in patient bites. The wood does not know it is turning into rock-bone; it performs support and is supported and that is the religion of rooms like this.

Occasionally, the place receives ambassadors. A glass eel enters on a spring exhale, a filament haunted by freshet, its muscle a ribbon of first morning. It makes one, two exploratory S’s, tries to imagine this as corridor, as answer, as plan, and then feels in every cell the heaviness of a privacy that is not for it. It knots itself back through the curtain with a small apology. A star arrives by arm, tastes the furniture with a hundred white cups, judges the fare honorable but not abundant, and leaves the way a careful guest leaves, moving no chair more than an inch from its remembered place.

There are fevers, in the room’s scale. Once a fortnight or so, a cloud passes through—the black tea of silt from elsewhere—and every mouth that can switches to particle-catching. The bryozoan monastery hums. The bell-ciliates ring more quickly. The isopod cleaves a clearer path and enlarges its letters. The “plants” do not mind wearing dust; this is how some of them eat. The amphipod returns to its doorway with conviction, stocks its lintel with small fortunes. For days afterward the fog unweaves; the room brightens as darknesses go from felt to familiar again.

Weather arrives also as chemistry—tastes in the water that mean more than temperature. When the gradient sharpens, some bacteria answer with a blink you could miss unless you had been learning to read night as if it were a book. That green is not a word but an inflection, a note that says: we are here, we are arranged, the slope of food is kinder today. When a hairline shift ticks the glass-sharp seam, the sound beads next to everything’s thought at once. The room listens and counts it among its facts.

Predation here is not drama. It is caress with conclusion. A nematode closes. A worm flicks. A polyp of bryozoan warms a particularly proteinous mote and folds it into its collective body. The limpet’s radula files another morning off the maps of its neighbors and the neighbors rewrite themselves in the afternoon. Even the starfish’s absent hand is a pressure to remember; the serpulid’s sudden withdrawal echoes for minutes as a texture.

All of them are slow citizens of a republic defined by margins. They live in between: between rock and water, between dark and the idea of light, between a present that has very little place to go and a future that will widen these walls to corridor and then to edge and then to field. They do not hold a sense of before; the room holds that for them, in layers: home scars and rasp-lines, tubes and coins of chalk, dulling glass. Every pass writes a gloss; every gloss is eaten; the record persists as thickness and softness, as resistance to the next touch. A book, reading itself.

If you need an emblem for them, take the limpet’s circle, the amphipod’s door, the isopod’s alphabet—three kinds of attention applied to not-very-much and yielding a world. Take the rust-workers weaving orange lace and the bell-bearers ringing in water’s thought. Take the sponge’s constant breath. Take the eel’s undecision, which is also the room’s self-respect.

What lives here are the agreements that matter when water is the only air: that pressure is a kindness if you submit to it, that darkness is not empty but specific, that a surface can be both living and book. They are small, each of them. Together they are the place’s one large animal, whose organs are films and fronds and monks and bells, whose blood is the heaviness between, whose heart is the slow bowing of a curtain the world outside does not know how to open without becoming this, too.
