The first resident is not a person. It is the green itself, not a color but a body—a single articulated plant learned into district, grove into factory, floodplain into a slow-breathing room. It keeps its own counsel and shares it by growth. You see its agreement and refusals written in matter: the spur that scabbed, the bridge that grew itself an extra shelf and was allowed to keep it. It listens in its own longways way, along fibers and sap, to tones set in its wood by neighbors who have learned to ask. It owns patience as a native speed. It accepts things that arrive gently and early and that do not insist. It will lend trunks and buttresses and conduits if you give it the story in harmonics and touch. It will remember the way you like a handrail and carry that gloss forward for your grandchildren. When it hunts, it does so as a dew-catch and a cup, a lean and a hinge, protein tidied into sweetness for a metabolism strewn across rooms of air. It does not drink like a mouth; it drinks like a city. If anyone can be called the citizenry in full, it is this: a single organism that both houses and is housed within its own intent.

With it live people, fewer than the rooms suggest and more than the quiet implies—some two-score and a handful, plus those not yet up to the rails. They do not flood the aisles; they spread themselves thin like the water the place is built upon. They have faces you could learn in a season and voices you will recognize by grain before you know the words: knee-low when they mean to carry, narrow when they mean to keep close. They have the posture of listeners—not only of each other but of hinges, of belts, of the inward click of a valve consenting. If they can be said to be from anywhere now, it is here. The old plate naming the district, bent on two screws, is from the time before, and the plant has bulged under it in acknowledgment; the people do the same, a little nod to the old stamps even as their hand has moved on.

They have trades that sound like refusals but are not: askers, tenders, readers, keepers. The askers are the ones with tone-keys and pitch-rods, with chimes in leather sleeves and tuning forks nested like cutlery. They seed standing waves into the green so that a rib swells inward instead of out, so that a cup learns to close at the lunch bell, so that a graft takes with a satisfaction like a held breath finally let go. They are more librarians than magicians; they will tell you that nothing happens because they asked it, only because the plant agreed and then carried the agreement to its next right place.

Tenders live in the in-betweens: belts, oils, teeth on drums. They know the materials that do not bite the green and the ones that do and have been exiled to covered tins. Their hands always smell faintly of seed-press and resin and the mineral sweetness of drying fiber. They run the mill, which is a pod with a door multiple palms wide that is almost never shut; they run the boilers that throw soft blue and push a line of small engines you could mistake for furniture when they are not working. They know where to place felt under a mount so that hum falls half an octave and vanishes into a rib. Their talk, when they talk about work, is simply-shaped and exact: slip, tolerance, lash, bite, grit. They polish old metal with a care equal to the one they bring to a living catenary that looks like it should not hold and does.

Readers are the counting kind. They count drips in flurries at dawn and in the slow patience of noon on a clear day when the world seems to have forgotten moisture. They keep maps of humidity like weather has been turned into a music score. They taste the settled silt from last month’s hard rain and call out “iron, clay, little salt,” and then note it anyway because what they really want is the long trend that tells them upstream is changing in some way they will feel a year from now. They read fog the way other people read smoke, by the way the pennants on the condenser tips stop being still. They have calluses where their fingers rest on rails while they wait for the right moment to mark.

Keepers keep things you might not think need keeping until you live here: the gloss of a handrail, the edge of a stair’s central rib, the open-ness of bays of air that want to feather closed if nobody asks them to remain air. They keep the prohibition as a kindness, which is to say they keep other thirsts out, gently. They step along drip lines once a week with small wire brushes to free the little gill-slots where a film turns into a drop, and in their pockets they have soft lids they can lay over the hung clear globes so that even the idea of light is careful.

Children live here too, soft-soled so as not to announce themselves, the kind of bold you learn when you know a railing will not betray you and that a cup might shut if you lean your chin too close to look at the light-spark skimming its skin. They grow up with a hand on wood—their counting games are taps: two to mean “see,” one to mean “I do,” and a held palm to a beam to say “listen.” They apprentice in listening first, always. One of them will be the one who discovers that a certain floor panel will tell a joke if you step on it just so; another will learn the smell that says “a belt wants oiling before noon.” Their toys would be uninteresting outside: a coil of old belt, a fork that no longer sits on pitch, dried plates cut with joinery teeth that fit together into shapes that mean nothing and feel like sentences when pressed together anyway.

They eat like the place eats: on a rhythm, with patience, with an attention to the idea of water rather than to its flow. They trade for grain and stone salt with the edge-road, with people who drive trucks that never come into the green but stay in the lean and pale, coughing their own brief history into the air. In return they send sheets pressed from the green’s sacrificial plates, joinery cut as clean as bone, resin that burns with a clean blue, lubricants that behave, and knowledge you cannot pack: a tuner walks out to the road and then is gone for a week teaching a new desert pump to take a hint without grinding. In season there is fungus, coaxed in cool hollows over cistern knots to make creamy layers that taste faintly of tannin and dust and the inside of a wooden bowl after tea. On fog-heavy runs when the belts spin their best they steam bread in the same vapor and it tastes of the idea of rain.

They sleep in rooms that are not rooms so much as pauses in the plant’s intent. Their walls are ribs that grew closer together on purpose. Their windows are the spaces left where light is wanted and not more. A shared habit has them laying a palm flat against a post when they go to bed—not ceremony, just the thing you do to remind the body that you are part of a larger one. It is no more mystical than washing your hands; it feels like checking a pulse.

They have ways of speaking that do not disturb the sense the place keeps. Whisper is not quite right; it is more like proposing a word to the air and letting it choose to carry it or not. They are loud, with joy and with anger, in the strips cut between pods, in the clearing that is a street only by convention. Beneath the Condensary, they joke less, without noticing, because the drops make their own punctuation and because it is like speaking in a library. When work requires clatter they put it where the green has already learned to expect it; the surprise is often more important than the volume when it comes to harm. They praise with polish and with attention to edges. They apologize by unhooking something they themselves set up too abruptly, by changing a lift’s rhythm to a counting the plant prefers, by scraping the last skiff of oil off a hinge because it smelled a little wrong.

They keep three calendars at once. One is fog: the diagonal tilt of the condenser flags, the glassine on a rail that tells you your knuckles will come away wet if you run them along. One is pulses: the count of days since the last hard rain in a row of scratches on the inside of the mill-pod’s door, the way frogs call for exactly as long as they always have and then stop. The last is growth: a notch cut in a post by a grandfather who wanted a bridge to meet its sister half a centimeter sooner than the plant planned, a notch that is now swallowed and glossy. There is a ceremony of sorts when something is finally taken in and forgotten by the wood; nobody names it, but there is cake and working stops for a pulse.

There are very few pets. A lizard or three has appointed itself familiar to a hand or a shop, thieving insects from condenser racks and from the warm backs of the little engines when they are taking a rest. Two generations ago someone brought a kitten from the road and learned fast that thirst is not a guest here; the story is told still, quiet and unsugared, not as a scold but as a teaching that the place’s charity is also its limit. Bees would work too hard and argue with the carnivory; birds come and go like visitors with questions and do not stay. The living companions that last are small and moist and easy: a set of springtails that churn the moss at the base of a post where a child pours out the last of a drink; a thickening of harmless mold in a corner cup that gets wiped away and praised for its neatness. Spiders throw hair-thin nets between two ribs—some survive long if they are light enough that a cup cannot be bothered to learn their trick and fold on them. In the cistern knots there are kingdoms without names—bacteria, film, a white thread of fungus that pretends to be lace and is fed like a cat might be elsewhere, with affection and scraps.

Machines live here, too, in the way a draft animal lives among people—a presence that has its own routine, its own needs, its own sound that is not quite anyone’s voice and not quite nobody’s. The dynamos hum. The steam engines tick and sigh and do not like grit. Someone loves each of them and wipes a palm across the places they like it best. The machines are named, but the names are not cute or silly; they are accurate: Corner Flywheel, Slow March, The Third Pulley That Will Never Quite Accept a New Belt. They are not jealous and do not mind that some of their power runs through phloem now; they were invited and learned to sit close to the plant without biting and are content. They are never the loudest thing in a given minute. They are proof that industry can be neighborly.

They keep a set of prohibitions that double as prayers. They do not nail into the green unless someone old enough to remember the price says yes and lays a hand there first. They do not waste a fall from leaf to wheel; even children do not throw away a cup that is not yet empty. They do not hurry what cannot be hurried, though they do hurry belts on fog-thick mornings and their feet on dries when maintenance finds its chance. They do not trap what knows how to walk corridors not provided; they admire it and put it down in the census of rumors. They do not chase a frog farther than the shadow of a root. They do not pretend their luck is their virtue; they count each month the drip that did not come and the one that did.

You could say they are religious in the way that people get religious around any partner they cannot fully predict and do not wish to lose. Their rites are practical. They hold a quiet awake when the plant flowers unexpectedly, taking the night watch in turns to keep the few moths from being frightened off by a light that someone forgot to shade. On the day the rail-moss greens over a little too vigorously they take turns being the weight that goes by and presses it back into drawing instead of fact. There is a story they tell to apprentices at the gasifier about the year someone let the blue go a little fat and a cambium browned and how long it took to make the insult into forgiveness again. At the end of every hard rain, when everything shines and the cups brim like mouths, they walk the perimeter and lay fingers to the outer bark, a gentle, ridiculous wall-check, a habit more than a hope. It feels good in the way washing your face in the morning feels good: not meaningful and full of meaning, both.

Now and then, and more often in dry years, edge-folk arrive. You will know them by their brows, salt-etched by a different habit of sun, by the way they step huge, as if to climb over the quiet. They fetch up at the long bench by the pipe and are patient while a keeper unscrews a tap and takes the first drink and listens not to the water but to the wood’s answer to it before filling a jug. They bring talk of distances that feel like fables in a place where the significant measurement is the reach of a shade. Children stare at their boots until a mother says something about how noise is a metal that can be tarnished and then there is laughter and the measure of the between is set. The traders load sheets and resin drums, the coins change hands with a resonance you can feel up through the bench plank, and someone’s tonefork falls out of someone’s pocket and the smallest child returns it like an exchange of names.

The rest of the residents are the almost-invisibles. The skin bacteria on every hand that has polished that one rail to the specific gloss that your palm will know without seeing. The sleep of the mildew on a belt looped over a peg that is a history more accurate than any ledger; it knows the weeks the belts did not run and the ones they did and the way the climate is leaning. The yeast that shows up uninvited when a bread pan is exposed to a certain current of damp air. The memory that runs in the weld seam of a matte, pitted handrail—the last metal forgings from a shop on the edge-road that has since shut, its knowledge surviving as a texture. The way a crack in a buttress stopped moving nine years ago and is now not a warning but a lesson in what asking too quickly buys.

So who lives here? A plant awake to form and favor. A people who have learned to be good neighbors to an organism larger and more patient than any of them, fewer in number than the rooms could take but exactly as many as the place will ask after and hold. A style of machine that hums like a cat and lets its fur keep clean. A weather that follows habits like a household does, damp in the morning, dry in the afternoon, generous on an appointed day. Some lizards. A moth that visits white bloom and goes away powdered like a fingertip touched with flour. Microbes by the tens of millions, which is to say everyone else.

They live in a city that is a handshake: leaf and muscle, belt and sap, wind and wheel. Their names are not written on plaques because the plaques the plant bulges under will outlast them, but their ways are written already in the thickness of a stair, in the way a bridge pauses and makes space, in a rule said as a joke and then obeyed because it is beautiful: air is a choice. They do not think of themselves as exemplars of anything. They get up when the fog does. They go to bed when the belts have thrown their powder and the clear globes have gone cool. When the flowers are open, they keep an extra watch. When the frogs begin, they smile and count the days, and then they stop. When rain comes thick and loud once every month, they stand and listen, not because they do not already know the sound but because it is correct to greet a visitor you have invited and to let it know you are home.
