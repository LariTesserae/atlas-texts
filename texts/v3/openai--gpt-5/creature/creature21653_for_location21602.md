Who lives here first is what moves through it: wind with a body, water with a voice. They are not metaphors. They have habits. The wind eats in long swallows and then worries the edge with its teeth; the water is compulsive, it cannot help touching everything and leaving a skin behind. The stone takes both like instructions and grows according to their patient law.

After those, and because of them, there are people. They are not many, and none of them pretends the ledge belongs to them. They arrive as if called and they leave before the place decides to correct that idea.

They call themselves, when they must call themselves anything, the Lipkeepers or simply the Leefolk: names that change from valley to valley like accents. In their own speech, it sounds like a fricative the wind can’t steal, something you make with your teeth and tongue behind closed lips. It is a language of vowels shortened by cold and consonants made heavy so they won’t blow away; in high weather they drop words for signs—flat hand, two fingers curled, the small circle that means wait until the gust stands up on its hind legs and you can go under it.

They are built spare and layered, not tall so much as coil-sprung and wide in the foot. Their toes learn like fingers; the great toe turns out a little from childhood to catch and hold small facts of stone. Winter puts a second lid on their eyes—a half-squint that becomes permanent. Their ears learn to take shape under goat-hair hoods so they can listen where the noise is. They walk like climbers but with the patience of farmers: not dramatic, just brave every ten inches. They count their steps in sets, not to reach a number but to please a rhythm that husbanded their grandparents: seven steps where the holds are spooned close together, two longer where the shelf asks you to say grace. The pattern is cut into the limestone at every tight turn, seven drills near, two far. You put your fingers in the smooth of them and your hand knows the people who drilled before you because the same bones touched there, a hundred, a thousand times.

They own very little that stands proud of the air. Everything that comes with them is meant to be quiet. Ropes braided of alpine nettle and goat hair that doesn’t slap against rock. Harnesses with bone toggles that can be opened by mittened hands, greased in autumn with sheep tallow and stone dust so they do not fret under a winter’s stiffness. Knives mostly of flaked stone wetted into handles with sinew, and a few leaf-shaped bits of copper beaten from specks shaken out of last century’s riverbeds, annealed in fire and hammered again until they are tough enough to cut felt or peat without shattering. Their cooking is what a ledge will tolerate: pot-hooks of juniper pegged with bone, small hearths that burn slow peat and goat pellets at a stingy blue. They bring a taste of the valleys up—grain cakes hard as tile, a twist of cheese kept under wool, a sweet that comes from lower juniper berries when the harvest is good—but they eat the air in great draughts like everybody else.

They tend a handful of devices the mountain allows. The row of drilled stones strung with hair and gut is their most beloved. They call it a throat, sometimes a choir. They have names for the notes it makes at a pinch-point gale or a fat updraft. By those notes they know when to tie the ropes tight and when to let the goats pass, when a day will be bit to bone by cold and when the fog will go woolly enough to harvest. The youngest are taught to listen not just for pitch but for where the note sits: a tone laid high against the underlip and you can move; the same song pushed back into the shelf’s cavities and you make camp. They retune the harp often. Sun and ice stretch gut; hair loses its oil, takes water and goes heavy. They carry little bottles of fat and a horseshoe of pumice to polish and seal the strings. If the harp sours into two notes where there was one, they spread word: a seam has moved, a curtain grown heavy, a rock face is ready to molt. The wind tells them the future and their harp translates.

Fog is their crop. They do not plant it; they just make it possible for fog to become handwork. Nets of nettle twine and goat hair are hung like new cobweb across particular mouths of the drip. Their garments are built to take water in exactly the right way and give it up to leather bladders while the person keeps walking: thick felts outside to arrest bead, thin felt inside to carry, seams stitched to wick. They call this milk of cloud. A day of bright fog and good wind can fill three skins to the plumpness of a forearm. The skins hang under dependable drips where the shelf has cut a lip, and the people taste each one like a vintner: this drip with chalk, that one sweet, this other bitter after bronze. They note in little cuts on the soft stone where the water changes its mind. Their elders can put a finger to a translucent tray and tell you if it will be a good fog month by the mineral crust under the slick.

They do not garden in the valley sense, but the perch of the bog is not an accident. They move it the way you move a sleeping cat: by rearranging what is near it a fraction, by cupping your hand and holding a place against loss until the creature understands it can rest. If a terrace is too clean and the water skates on it, they lay a skein of red vine roots and pin them under a pebble until the net sets. If a sphagnum pillow gets too fat and threatens the way, they slide a copper blade under it and ease a slice to another bowl, pushing it flat with the back of the hand until it learns its new boundary. They do not pull asphodel except to give it away to valley people who believe its tubers will ease bones. They cheat the sun to make a halo when the clouds part—turning shallow trays to a slight angle, polishing the mica flecks with the sleeve to mark where a good rainbow always stands itself in blowing rain. Children are taught the names of the mosses not because a name is story but because calling something by what it is stops your foot in time to keep you from falling into it.

They shepherd goats, but even the goats are visitors here, and their pact with gravity is always renegotiated. The thin ones with bells braided to their chests come at certain hours, step where the people step, chew rhododendron only when their keepers make that low chkk-chkk that means this plant today you fool, no not that one. Goat hair provides the long staple the nettle lacks; goat milk thickens a cold belly when the fog runs thin; goat kids are watched with the patience of sky-gazers because an eagle can take a child of any species when the silence drops and the world prepares for a change in pressure. These same goats drop bones into the economy of the lammergeier. A good season sees a pile of fractured white crescents gathered at an angle where bone breaks clean. On days when danger is not immediate, when the wind is just a fist against your chest and not your whole life in its hand, the people trade: a leg bone stripped of meat set on an outcrop, a week later a bundle of shafts fine as reeds left elsewhere. From those they cut flutes. Their music is not made for valley ears. It’s a way of testing the hollows and teaching children where the organ notes are. Sometimes at a certain weather the ledge and the flute sing sympathetically and a person feels, in their sternum, how the mountain keeps the time.

Other residents are not beholden to anyone. Swifts own the hours between rain. They nest in seams too narrow for fingers, using the people’s carved notches the way mice use a man’s wall—an advantage never intended but often taken. The birds’ cries rasp the high air into strands. In high blow their thin sound flags where the lift is strongest, and the Leefolk watch them the way sailors watch smoke. Finches and flowerpeckers pattern the morning, short circuits between blooms in the prostrate rhododendron and the dripping fern lips. The people let them be and go still as lichen when the birds decide to use a shoulder as a post. The lammergeier is a fact without give. If it comes in too low, people count children and kids a beat faster and tuck their hoods. Eagles announce themselves in absence—the way all noise is present and then suddenly not, the air taking a long inhale. When that lay of quiet goes down, everyone looks up and pulls the line in tight to the drilled tether stone. If nothing falls past, you put your breath back in your mouth and continue walking.

They speak with spiders by accident; the spiders answer by adjusting their sails. Lines of gray silk hang from lichen laces with a slack only the narrowest gust can pick up. Children are forbidden to touch them. In afternoon, when the updraft is a road, the spiders gather their shrouds, loose a new list of lines and travel. The Leefolk wonder at it and do not reach. That is another of their rules. Gravity is never your friend; the ledge is never your home; other creatures have as good a claim as you. They enforce these rules with stories you can tell in one breath, because sometimes that’s all you’re allowed. A man who laughed into the wind and opened his mouth too wide when a gust went inward. A girl who wore a bright copper brooch and the eagle took her for a walking eye. A pair of brothers who moved a boulder to get at the sweet drip behind it and woke to find the wind had memorized that gap and played it until the wall let go. The cairns carry these things in stone because words won’t always hold long enough.

Children are not born on the ledge. They are brought up in the season when the fog is mild and the handles of stone are warm to the touch. Their schooling is the ledge, their naps taken on ropes clipped short to the tether stones with loops that have been spit-polished and greased by seven generations of anxious hands. They learn to lean into words because words blow away. They learn to listen for change: the drop that missed the pan, the finch that did not complete a usual round, the chord in the harp that has grown a wolf note. They are given a stick to point with and told from the beginning to point with the stick, not with fingers, because fingers are for grip and if a gullible finger forgets itself you can fall. Their first letters are traced in condensation on the tufa curtains where lines show up like oil on glass and vanish clean without a mark.

The adults’ days are not tidy. They are long arrangements with moving parts. A morning might be retuning the harp and setting a new hairline where a runnel has fattened; counting swifts for an hour because a visitor from the valley wants to know if the beeches will be ready for honey in a week; scraping slime where a foot must go, not to sanitize the place but to keep the next goat from skidding; sliding a fog net twenty hands to the left because the rainbow has flinched. From time to time there is a tether to reset. Sand and water, and the little drilling bow worked with both hands and the laughable patience of people who know stone is older than language. They sing the boring song: one, two, seven; one, two, seven. When the hole rings right you stop. When it rings odd you stop sooner. The best anchors do not congratulate themselves; they just behave when asked.

Nights divide themselves into types. There are clean nights when the stars stand square at the rim of the earth and the updraft goes stillish, when you can hang your wet things and know they will be harder in the morning. The sky falls so near you might believe you could take a spoon and step into it. These nights the Leefolk play small games: how long to hold your breath in the hush between the boom and the rattle, whose pulse is loudest against the bone of the ledge, who can find the lowest note by singing into a cavity with your ear to it. There are pearled nights where the fog ignores your hood in favor of your eyelashes and you make camp with your back to the lee and your toes just under the lip so the rock can return you its own light. Then the world is milk. Your tools bloom with beads. You wake to the feel of your own clothes clamping you gently because they are heavy with crop and good for the morning when you’ll wring them over a bladder with a twirl that makes children laugh every time.

They trade with downpeople because nobody lives on air alone even when it seems otherwise. The valley hamlets send up juniper beams and sometimes a copper pebble bigger than a thumb. In return the Leefolk bring storm knowledge and timing, bundles of felted fog that can be boiled sweet when a well goes sour. They bring peat cut with care so the bog does not notice it is gone. They bring mushroom caps that appear overnight and are gone by noon if you don’t harvest them. They bring the rhythm hammered into their bones that lets caravan men step the shelf with a new caution. You could say they sell time to the people below. A day warning on a storm can save a season. The harps tell them; they tell others; that is the debt they owe the mountain for letting them measure it.

There are private pleasures. The taste of the limestone—chalk and shells—on a fingertip to see if a drip has run long enough to leach palatable water. The way mica finds the inside of a wrinkle and lives there like a small star until sweat moves it. The first asphodel bud, which they do not pluck but touch with a nail to test ripeness. The frog hidden under the lip of a pan that scolds your ankles when you go past and so you leave him a bit of bloodless slug for thanks. The snake seen and admired and not told away from his place because he has as much right to a frog as you do to a sip. The particular astonishing pleasure of stepping from a counted foothold onto a patch of moss and feeling it give and take you in and then find you safe again.

If you ask one of them whether they live here, they will answer the way people answer when a truth is partial and they have made friends with that fact. We come, they’ll say. We come as long as the harp is in voice, as long as the goat hair holds, as long as the wind lets us put our backs to it and the water is not throwing itself in one sheet solid down the face. We come until the rinds under our feet tell us they are done and need to molt and grow elsewhere. Then we go and we wait in the valley and listen. The mountain keeps the account; we don’t ask for credit.

Still, they leave signs as lovers leave—slight, reversible, invisible to anyone who isn’t already careful. A handful of gut from a broken string tied in a knot in a certain way. A cairn with a flake turned blade-out so a friend will know there was ice at dawn. A tuck of net twine in a crack that means do not set here this season because the moss is too new. A warm charcoal sketch of a whirl on a wall where the drop hits hardest in certain winds, smeared into the stone by a shoulder and gone by next week. And always the drilled marks under the hand, the way you learn the face because the face taught you how to keep yours.

There are other presences you’d notice if you stayed long enough. A woman who has done this work so long her hair refuses to lie down and lifts in a small halo at any change in pressure; she can hear a coming squall by what her scalp says. A man whose hands give off a permanent smell of iron though he’s touched no metal today; it is the bog, marking him. A girl who stamps a little dance into a shallow pan of stoneworts every time she passes and swears the stars there are brighter for it. An old teacher who lays his chest flat to the lip as if he is making himself large in a fight, breathes with the ledge when it sings and calls out numbers his students write in the wet with fingers, each digit a flake of time falling off the edge.

This is a place where life must learn to be light, and the people who choose it learn the same. They sleep in threes when they sleep in that small nook, boots clipped to a rope, breath waking them when the boom shifts key. In high wind they talk into each other’s hoods. In stillness they stop and check each thing that moving air was doing for them—drying this rope, keeping that net taut—and do it by hand. They do not leave polish where they don’t have to. They never cut more peat than the bog can forget. They do not stand anywhere that does not sing when asked.

If you came unannounced and patient, you might see one of them before you heard them. A color that is not the lichen’s, moving along the lip with the inward lean of a person who has decided to add themselves to a rule rather than break it. A hand to the drilled notch, a palm laid to feel if the stone is thinking about its skin. A quick stop when the applause of water grows readable and the person wants to know what play is being staged today. They would not wave. They would show you the lee with the small tilt of a chin and teach you without telling you that you cannot stand proud here unless you are ready to sing. And when the mountain breathes through its bright weeping mouth and the world passes clean through your body, they will put a pebble on a cairn with your name on it, spelled exactly as you were in that moment—alive and brief and in agreement with the air.
