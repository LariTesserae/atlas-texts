What lives here has more than one body. It does not all begin and end in a single skin. If you insist on starting somewhere, start with the thing that pretends to be floor. It is not floor; it is the oldest resident, the first intelligence you meet, an organism whose face is a million faces and whose back remembers weather the way bark remembers lightning.

The mat breathes. It is plant, yes, and fungus, yes, and also the memory of both in a brown that is not quite soil. It trades in water and in patience. Its white ropes of hyphae are the long nerves of this place, their signals slow as dusk, their speech chemical and cool. They taste footsteps. They keep the accounts of salts. When something dies up here, the mat interviews it in molecules, takes its answers apart, files them in humus, loans them back out green. The mat keeps time: it swells on the daily inhalation and lets itself down again, not quite as a tide does but as a chest does when it is unobserved.

The mat also keeps promises for longer than anything else has a right to, and that is how it has made bridges of vines and walls of roots. It remembers every repeated tread and thickens under it. It remembers where a hand traced a curve on the weeping face of a stone and will make a rail out of that curve given a season. When you sleep in a warm hollow, it will call in the spider-hands to build a basket around the vent of your breath.

The spider-hands are not spiders, not in any book a lowland child would carry. They have six panes of limb and more hinge than grace, pale as fog when idle, burnished leaf-brown when at work, always with some borrowed strip of petiole in their palps. They are good with right angles without believing in them. They comb the place for small rigids: the spines of last year’s bromeliad, the rib of a failed fern, the ribbed strap of an old air-root; and with saliva that is half glue and half blueprint they make reefs around warmth-sources—sleepers’ mouths, fissures in stone that breathe out earth-temper, hollows where large animals fold themselves to nap. Their basket-reefs catch condensate, their gutters lead it to their nurseries: a raffish suite of cup-nests that never dry. They are too shy for trade and too practical for worship. They trust warm air the way lowlanders trust rivers.

Filterers live here in an abundance that would scandalize a pollen count. Think of hands unfurled and thinnest skin stretched, held open on hinges like borrowed library screens; think of bodies no larger than a plum, clothed in fineness and serious about air. They hoard the falling green snow. They trap spores that smell faintly of bread and distant cousins, they lick pollen with fringes of cilia. Their faces are ledges rather than eyes. Some glow, but not all the time—only when they’ve found richness and want to tell their neighbors. They perch in the drafts the mat makes when it breathes, tipping their fans to clock the slow gust, never far from a hatch where they can disappear if a vine’s patience snaps shut.

There are browsers, small and polite. They fit in both hands if hands are careful; their skins reject rain, they are always as glossed as an oiled stone. The front half of them considers insects; the back half considers lilies. They go about on whisper-soft points and lay out their tongues along algae-painted rock, cleaning with a devotion that is not sanctity but agreement. They love corners where two slick planes meet and make a tiny seam of green. Their heat is modest. Burrow a fingertip into the hairs along their spine and you find the warmth of tea against your lip. They leave little double commas where they went.

Other tongues—longer, thicker, heavy-lobed—sweep the weeping stone domes. Those belong to the stone-lickers. Their backbones are a rumor under a skin that seems always newly varnished. They clamp themselves to the impossible faces of rock and become, for a while, one more part of the weeping: they press their nostrils so close to sheen that the water can hardly find a way to drip. Their teeth are not for cutting; they are for combing minerals out of a film. On their tongues live fields of algae that pay rent by digesting what the stones have to give. When they raise their heads at last, they are always marked by moon-silver—the gloss of the dome they worship printed on them until the next sweep.

The gliders do fly, in their manner. They do not hurry. They are all membrane and hinge, like unwound seedpods repurposed by a suggestion from a bat. Their veins are the color of brass through rice paper. They bank by thinking about banking. They cannot tolerate open sky; on the rare clearings their shadows panic, so they sail corridor to corridor where the air is furnished, wings almost brushing the moss. They wear lichens on the outside so that lichens will not move in on the inside. Their mouths are small, their catches smaller: a constellation of air plankton a mouthful at a time, taken as though in prayer.

There are hunters, of a sort, but they lack theater. The best of them do not consider it a blood art. A vine has learned to move—this is how people speak of it, though “learned” here must mean the aftermath of a hundred generations of small, insistent selection. Bend, cohere, pull, repeat. There is a snap, soft as a knuckle cracked under a blanket; there is a single syllable of complaint from a filterer; there is a folding. The vine eats chemically and for a long time. Elsewhere, a leaf has found out how to make a pocket that wets and unmakes what falls inside it. Elsewhere, a fungus puts out a net finer than conversation, sticks to a throat that was made for calling and calls it no more. It is all terribly courteous. No scream outlives the first second.

The large ones are the shape of a cautious thought. You will not see them unless the mat chooses to let you cross a path at the same time. They are low rather than tall, as a suspicion is low; they are many-footed in the sense that they are not willing to put very much weight down anywhere at all. They spread themselves like a question across a room. Their pads are not hooves but gardens, hair growing on hair, wicking water sideways, warm as bread under a cloth. Each pad has the polish of old use, and their routes are prayers written repeated until they are roads; they will walk the same corridor for a hundred years if it is a kind one. Their heads have the gentle architecture of a skull that learned to push vines aside without bruising them. Their mouths are furnished for negotiation rather than bite: tongues like test papers that taste iron, salt, cellulose, and then press a decision onto the mat about what to prune and what to leave. When they drum, it is not sound you hear but the memory of it—the faintest agreeing thud that the place has kept and will play back for a long time after the drummer is in another room.

Birds? There are birds, but they are threadlike, more needle than wing. They go where air is as narrow as a word between lovers. Their beaks are not beaks; they are oars for pushing aside fog. The fungus that finds their throats and lines them with iron-red does not ask rent beyond being seen. When a thread-bird sings, it is a distillation, so fine and so traveled-through by surfaces that you cannot be sure it was not a creaking buttress, or a droplet that let go with an attitude.

Edge-dwellers are a different kind of devout. At the brink where mist spills over forever, plants have taken to making combs—not webbing so much as a thousand parallel appetites directed toward the upflow. The fern that coils there with the elegance of a hand fan warms very slightly at the teeth of its comb, pulls water against sense. If you set your finger on its midrib, you feel the work: a pulse too patient to be a heart and too brave not to be. Around these upward drinkers congregate the gleaners of salt. Tiny bodies with ringed mouths touch the exuded drops and make a living out of trace elements. The comb-ferns, being clever, pay the gleaners in an overdose of what they do not need.

There are sleepers. Their beds are vents where breath from below is a whisper warmer. They are creatures, but they could be mistaken for moss if your eyes are not trained to notice what moss cannot do. Sleeper-fur tastes like mint left in a pocket. They sidle themselves into a weft of root and leaf and do not wake for weather. At their nostrils’ arch, the spider-hands have built coral. They sleep long enough that the baskets grow round them like reputations. When they wake, the sleepers do not struggle out; the baskets flex, admit them, accept the shape of their leaving, and then go on being baskets.

Light-literate things live here as well: not plants that only do photosynthesis as a transaction, but ones that use light as conversation. Their veins pulse in wholly unbotanical rhythms—throb, quench, stutter, tide—and the pulses run from leaf to leaf where they are in contact. At dusk, you can see the news go under the furniture. It travels along the undersides of vaults where only small things can fit and tells the fungal ropes and the shelf-ferns and the spider-hands and the filterers’ nests about what has ripened, what has soured, what path opened today and may be taken again tomorrow if the taker intends to keep it. Sometimes, rarely, an animal will answer: a filterer will shift its fan to inscribe a shadow; a vine will make the letter of a bend it did not need to make. It is not dialog in a human sense, but it is exchange.

There have been other hands. You see their convictions smoothed to kindness by fog at the tops of bare domes and on the backs of old tors. They made dishes in stone and fed the sky to them, they cut steps which now are stairs for moss, they stood pillars in rings the size of rooms, and they softened the edges of everything so that nothing would catch or chip or resist the way water likes to come. Those hands may have given up or they may have learned the gospel preached by the place: that green will do it better if you teach it the move and repeat the move until the move knows you. Their artifacts are not ruins. They were never buildings so much as petitions to air and gravity. The petitions have been granted and are now held under leaves.

And there is, sometimes, someone you might insist is people. They are not absent the way the stone-hands are; they are simply not numerous. If they have a name, they have given it to lichens to hold. They go with a decorum that makes you ashamed of your respiration. Their bodies are slow on purpose. They have learned to fit warm two fingers into a hanging curve without adding weight to it. They do not steal from the mat; they borrow and return interest. Their hair is seeded; it blooms a little under fog and after a while puts down a green along their shoulders that belongs to the place as much as to them. They exchange with the filterers: woven fans in trade for hand-warmed ridges on weeping stone, a pair of kneepads made of old root for the right to sleep under a particular balcony of bromeliads. They train living bridges with means you would call superstition and science in the same breath: a hundred passes with a weight in one hand, a hundred days of rubbing, a compress of chewed leaf saps that teach one vine to adhere to another and stay. They do not carve steps now. They step in the places where steps might have been desired a long time ago. When they need to speak across spaces, they borrow the luminous veins and send slow beacons that are a pulse per minute, answers hours later that mean yes or come or I am old on this ledge today, do not ask me to cross. They mark their dead by repeating the same palm one leaf above the water-line of a weeping stone until the moss decides to keep that hand for good.

If you do not meet these people, you might still meet their habits. A path that is not animal-clean but person-slow; a neat ring of purple pebbles, chosen for the way they insist on an edge, snug around the mouth of a seep so the place does not forget it is a source. A coil of vine left beside a failed wall—a hint for the next living thing that wants to make that wall stand. A deck of petioles dried in the hush under a gallery, waiting to be reefed into a basket by spider-hands who notice such abundance and cannot bear waste.

What else lives here? A counting of microthings would break your patience and your tongue. There are beetles that keep their oil on the outside so that moss does not take their chitin for a sprouting place; mites that read the night's light-code and adjust their moltings accordingly; midges who are only evident when the weekly spore-hail thickens and they open like mouths in a rain. There are slimes that are only ever a line—the damp equivalent of handwriting—snaking along the underside of a root and leaving behind the ghost of their mineral thought. There are occasionally—if old stories are truthful and the mat permits—greater airs, volumes that move with the combined musculature of a glade's worth of leaves, a slow wave that is a room relocating itself a body-length to the left. When this happens, nothing panics. The place has discussed it with itself for days.

The law of here is touch. Everything that means to be permanent leans on something else hard enough to leave an intention behind. Nobody spends long in straight corridors or uncluttered views; there is little use for them. Even sky is diluted. Even horizons are privately consumed and then offered back in pieces, a thousand little green windows you stitch into a wholesight as you go. The creatures that mind this and flourish by it are the ones that do not ask edges to stay edges. They fatten corners. They smooth steps. They will meet you more than halfway if half a way is all you can manage, and then, because it is their nature, they will continue meeting you at the same place until that place becomes its own limb.

So: what lives here? Damp. Habit. The sum of many small anatomies that have agreed not to hurry, accompanied by some larger anatomies that have learned how not to be a problem. Builders that do not so much build as rehearse, until rehearsal becomes structure. Predators whose manners outlast their appetites. Unmade cities of leaf and coherence. Old stone that has been forgiven. A people, sometimes, slow enough to be believed. A mind you can walk on, if you are willing to think at its speed. And all of it drinking, not from rivers, but from the act of the air deciding to be water and then, very gently, deciding where to go next.
