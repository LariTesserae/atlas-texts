Those who live here have taught themselves to listen for water the way farm people listen for rain. They are quick to catch the pause before the purge and the difference between the turbine’s ordinary baritone and the half-note that means a bearing wants attention. Their coats are cut for movement in ladders and around corners; the cuffs stay turned down even in summer because the shade in the loading bay remembers winter. They know where not to whistle.

They are newcomers and veterans of other edges, drawn in from inland hardpan, from coast towns where the canneries went quiet, from cities that got tired of being brittle. They call the fog fields “work” the way someone might call a hillside “the back forty.” You meet them as pairs striding between things, in the long intervals, measuring with their eyes. When they stop you see the small tools of their kind: a hand meter clipped to a belt loop, a rag tucked into a back pocket and washed thin as gauze, a pencil stub trapping hair behind one ear. Their badges go blank with condensation at the line between door and out-of-doors; they wipe them on sleeves without looking.

Their jobs sort roughly into weather and consequence. The mesh-tenders go up first, before the day is decided. They move along the fog farms with the easy balance of people who have put their knees and ankles to use every day for a long time. They read the nets with their palms, gather the beads where the lines sag most, retie the stays that strain in the oblique wind. They can tell when the fog will sit and when it will keep walking, and they have names for varieties that the meteorologists upstream never bothered to keep—salt-shy, kitchen smoke, pearl-thread. On mornings when the mesh sings with a faint glassy chime they take it as a benediction and lay the first cups under the down-pipes as if some ceremony demanded it.

Then come the tenders of consequence: the gutter-walkers who make sure every runnel can hand its load to the next; the basin-women and men who rinse the grates clean before the three low chords of the noon purge so the sudden local rain can find its channels; the line-watchers who test the catch basins with the back of the wrist and tag a slow drain for a snake. If you ask them about their days they will show you their shoes. The ones who spend their time under the condensers have a line of pale mineral along the leather where the basins splash. The canning crew have knives polished to a gloss that mirrors the ceiling’s V and a damp along their shirts like a map of the body’s own fog. The turbine people never take off their hearing protection even at lunch; their hair is ironed straight by headbands and their speech is punctuated with careful pauses, the better to fit between the place’s other voices.

Kids live here in the rectangles of grass and in the spaces under the outside stairs. They have learned quickly the grammar of the hazard stripes, that a black-and-yellow chevron is not the same as a striped pole and that a red tag means you call for a grownup with a radio. They race leaves in the channels after the purge, guitaring their hands along the guardrail to return a little water to sky in a spray, then scuffing their shoes on the grated steps to hear how the pitch climbs toward the catwalk. They keep a personal taxonomy of foxes—Rust, with the notch in the ear; Cinnamon, half-grown last month, now nearly adult—and they know which dogs will tolerate the laying-on of stickers salvaged from pallets. On mornings when the frost webs every fence they are the first to make prints on the blank pads of concrete, their breath as white as any machine’s. They speak two languages at once without knowing it: one made of the clipped words the elders use for work, one made of fingers to show each other when not to talk.

Old people live here, too, because work in a young place prefers steady hands. Their wrists have learned to feel for laminar before they see it. They can tell you if the south mesh is too tight by looking at the way it sags under its own harvest from a hundred meters off. They have stories about the days when the white geotextile showed along every cut and about the first autumn when the phragmites tried to push in like a crowd at a bus door. They play cards in the canteen where the air smells of cardamom and fish and a little like coolant, and without looking up they will say when the crane’s hoist is winding down on a bearing that wants oil. A few have worked in real rain, in places where gutters were a courtesy rather than a system, and they find the discipline here elegant. They will say, if you get them to talk, that the difference between luck and work is a diagram and a schedule.

Many of the people are in between, neither old nor new—mid-career, mid-sentence, with midnights spent on the swing shift feeling the place get large around them in the dark. They date across shifts because the short day makes a different animal out of the long night, and they have learned that compatibility here is less about taste than about pitch. You match someone by the frequencies you don’t flinch at. Their apartments are clean in the way small quarters are when there is plenty of running water. They hang laundry on bars meant for something else and the drip line marks out their patience. They decorate with found things that have cleared the safety committee: the stamped number plate off a retired valve, the square of paint cut from a pad where someone repainted with care and left the margin sharp as a rule.

The machines live here as well, and not just as furniture. The turbines keep to their pulse—the slow faith of great things that already know what they are for. The conveyors are fidgety by design; they are happiest when things move over them and get jumpy when the flow breaks. You can feel the canning line relax into rhythm, each slap of a lid its own measure in a long piece that has been playing since it was first switched on. The fog meshes are creatures of habit and wind. They bend, they catch, they sing, they let go; and on days when someone hums under them they seem to sift a little finer for the company even though the sign says not to. The condensers like being purged as much as any organism likes a good wash. When they are full past comfort they gurgle under their breath and the supervisors say they are ready to rain.

Water lives here, domesticated but with a memory of wandering. It runs in planned ways beneath the catwalk and along slate-colored channels, but it also invents itself wherever metal meets a draft, beading its hands along the underside of a handrail, stringing itself into necklaces on guy wires. It behaves like a citizen with civic responsibilities. It submits to tagging. It appears at appointed hours, in appointed places, to be collected and apportioned. Small cups, bigger tanks, then pipes down to the storage drums where someone in an office marks down the day’s amount and thinks, with satisfaction, of orchards hundreds of kilometers away that will drink this in six months’ time. The people here love water the way some people love an animal that will not love them back in any particular way. They are kind to it because kindness changes outputs. They wipe where it condenses. They shelter it where it would squander itself as mist. They have built brackets and troughs like a body builds veins. And still it surprises them; it still comes where it is not sent, inscriptions of a system’s one indulgence in poetry.

Plants live where water lingers or merely threatens to linger. Sedges make choir-lofts of the swales, a permanent congregation bowing in the constant wind. Willows volunteer then are tolerated because their roots hold the blackened puddles together and because they make a sound like applause when the purge comes on and there is a thing to celebrate. Moss finds the north faces of walls and keeps a record of shade. Vines find ladders and declare them an ecosystem. Ferns practice dying and being born according to the basins’ schedule until you begin to think this is what ferns always wished for. The bright-eager things—poppies, clover, chicory—occupy the berms the way tourists find the sunny benches in a gray city. People learn them by their patient extravagance. They talk about chicory’s stubbornness as if it were a neighbor’s temperament, and it is.

Animals live here thick as grammar. The egrets stab at the small industry of the retention ponds as if it were obvious that something always comes of a surface if you attend it long enough. The gulls are the roof cops; they look down their white shoulders at your lunch and at your work and make a language of droppings on rail that the maintenance crew reads like weather maps. Swallows stand their posts at conveyor mouths and edit the air of gnats, and nobody begrudges them the rent under the V of the roof trusses because they pay on time, in song, and because their droppings are a punctuation the canning crew can work around. At dusk the frogs come on like a backup generator taking the load when the shift of day slips. They are not shy. They yarn their throats in long cords of sound that tie the place together at a different frequency, and you can feel the turbine’s hum and the frog’s music become one organism for a minute and realize you live in a choir where nobody knows the whole score.

Rats have their places under the pallets and are discouraged but not astonished at being disliked. Foxes cut their neat lines between stacks like diagram arrows that repeat the logic of the place in fur. The stray dogs put themselves on a schedule—you see the same one asleep behind the warm bulk of a cabinet at ten every morning, raising an eyebrow when the warning chords sound and only truly waking when the smell of the canteen declares that meals have begun. They have names both official and private: Safety, who will sit before crossing painted lines; Piston, who dreams and makes running motions with his paws; Lady, who once stole a glove and would not return it until coaxed with a piece of fried bread. Someone in the clinic keeps a stock of hi-vis collars for them out of a sense of symmetry.

People who don’t belong here in the residency sense pass through and leave their portion of living behind. Inspectors come with clipboards like small shields and try to put a tidy into everything. Traders on the rail spur arrive with crates and curses from inland and leave with tins stacked like gold in a dragon’s cave, their ends shining with oil and light. A teacher from away comes quarterly with books that make the kids’ desks into archipelagoes, and he returns with his shirt salted faintly up the sleeves where he has leaned on the catch basins to explain something about Dew to students who already know it by wrist and breath. A poet visited once, wrote something about the “cathedral organ of the hall,” and somebody printed it and taped it up in the locker room until the tape’s glue went white with age and fell, which felt fitting.

There are rules like anywhere, but they are close and sensible and as much about orchestration as about enforcement. No whistling beneath the nets because somebody proved it made the flow turbulent and cost a little yield, and even those who don’t quite believe in that experiment respect it the way you respect a boundary stone: not because a fence will shock you but because it helps the place remember its shape. “Everything grounded, everything tagged” is printed on the back of a hoodie you will see in every hallway. You wear your hearing protection. You test a ladder with your heel. You hold a cup under the purge if you can, in the small pause when the spray turns to rain. You talk with your hands. You learn your neighbors by the way they inhabit quiet: some lean into it, some lean away.

Even the abstract things have taken up residence. Schedule lives here—a large, mild thing, firm with its family but not cruel. It brings the morning fog in and lets it go by noon. It sucks the condensers down to empty at twelve-oh-five. It kisses the turbines on the crowns and says, “Continuity is the shape of care.” The young place’s conscience lives here, too, and not in an office. You can see it in the square where paint was reapplied just so, in the way a bracket got added beneath a run of ductwork because somebody thought about one day’s fatigue forty days from now. Pride lives here in the plain clothes of having done a thing correctly and then doing it that way again tomorrow. Time is a slower animal than in other cities; it lies down in the rust at the legs of the fence and it sits up in the child who was knee-high to a guy line last year and is shoulder-high now. Reputation lives in everything. A leak is “the polite one” or “the brute.” A mesh panel is “singer” or “sleeper.” A fox is a friend. A worker is not their job title but the way they laugh into their sleeve when the horn interrupts a story as if the interruption were another character in the tale.

At night, the place doesn’t go quiet so much as change what quiet means. The sky leans harder; the berms become the edges of a bowl. You will see a figure moving along the fog field with a headlamp—one beam making a brief cave around the thing being mended, then going dark when the person moves on, as if the place were blinking in slow contentment. The frog’s choir carves stanzas into the turbine’s held note. A gull peels a long shape out of the air and then the dark takes even that away. Somewhere, the clinic’s windows are still open to the sea-wet, and someone laughs in a stairwell loud enough to read as happiness over the machinery. In one apartment a set of hands is mending a hole in a glove with bright thread. In another a child is teaching a dog how to take a biscuit gently and the dog is imperfect at it and forgiven. Out past the buffer painted hazard-yellow, a fox looks back once and disappears into scrub as if passing from a sentence into a silence.

Who lives here? Whatever knows how to make a place by repetition: people whose gestures grow exact from use; machines designed to hum without ego; water with its bright patience; plants that do not argue with schedule; animals that bless routine by folding themselves into it. A city the size of a room and a hamlet the size of a city live here together, the one inside the other, practicing their harmonies, accruing their reputations, keeping the edges crisp, the gutters clear, the rules light and sufficient. Those who belong do not say the place is beautiful until one day in winter when the frost writes every wire into script and the breath of the turbines is warm enough to unwrite it, and then they say it and never stop. Then they live here in that word, too. They carry it between shifts like a cup under a local rain. They offer it to newcomers. They drink. They get to work.
