The oldest inhabitant is the plant, and it is not an inhabitant so much as the place’s own body made enormous, awake in the way a metronome is awake. It has settled its muscles into columns and ribs and great chambers and the habit of counting, the way some beings settle into language. It maintains its shape on a schedule you can feel in your molars. It remembers itself in wood and scar and the long, practical drift of sugars. It keeps invariants now: pressure ranges, pulse cadence, where a duct will rest, which hollows will twine and which will stay open. When something injures it, a dozen prepared patterns wake and rush to shape: foam, resin, seal, reroute. It has learned the turbines’ weight like it learned wind and rain before there was anything called a lamp. If it likes, it can cuff an engine into steadiness. If it likes, it can pinch a passage until the sound thins and work slows and everyone understands they are early or late or wrong.

The second oldest inhabitant is the rule of pace. It lives in hands and habits. It eats impatience. It sings in the direction of voices and the lay of cable. It is the thing that makes a flood of bats predictable enough to nail your spoons down. It makes holidays of the long exhale, loves a line of chalk numbers struck out because the plant decided to hold them on its own now. It is not a person, but everyone in the Works talks to it like one. You hear them say: we’ll ask the pace; we’ll see what the pace decides. Children swear by it before they learn the plant’s other names.

Then come the Operators and their city stretched thin like a skin across a void. They are narrower than their ancestors and broader in the wrists. Their ear bones have changed a little in three generations and nobody marks when this happened; they simply make headpieces that press their auricles in certain ways and threads run from these to rings at their throats, and their speech sits high and pointed, bolts of word aimed where they need to land. They grow a second set of calluses inside their hearing, small hardnesses of attention that let them listen under a shopful of belts to the shiver that means a seal is failing two platforms away.

They smell of sap and pitch and the coriander-sour of gnat dust. Their hands wear flat pads of resin when they work sealant or hold a bucking valve; the pads are replaced as easily as a glove is turned. The palms are burnished to the gloss of old bark from testing heat with the back of the hand. Their teeth pick up a green haze where sugar grits the enamel. They cut their hair short or braid it into ropes and dip it in resin; there is always something trying to live in your hair if you forget. Their clothing is made of what the plant gives most gladly: spun cellulose ribbon, rope, fiber pressed into soft boards that flex and can be mended with glue. Boots take a mink-like nap of fungus that sheds fine and grows back by the end of shift.

They do not say they live in the plant; they say they live with it, or inside the Host, as if you could live inside an animal and still call it a building. Some call it the Lung. Some call it the Green. A few of the elders, the ones with silvered scars scalloping their forearms where thread-wolves once took a lick, still use the old joking name—Mother—when they are in a good humor and the breath is steady. They don’t pray much, not the way a cathedral makes you pray; they count. Counting is the reverence here. It is the way you hold your tongue between thumb and forefinger and smooth out luck into practice.

They have guilds and they have families. A family is a cluster of hammocks slung where the breath is mild, with a place on the rail to set the cups to catch dew when it comes on a schedule someone’s grandmother knows by the click in her knees. A guild is a schedule with teeth.

Operators are the ones with the wall slate and the habit of taking their hats off on the old turbine’s walkway. They climb the rib once a week with a brush dabbled in oil and polish the cuff of wood hugging the engine, not to make it shine but because their hands have learned that movement and do not trust luck without it. Counter-Clerks keep the numbers of flow, write songs to teach the children ranges of pressure safe for cutting live bark. Flywives tend the nurse-fly egg jars—brown sugar sludge pricked full of expectation—and post up with their sealing poles whenever someone lifts a blade to the Host. Ropewrights sleep with burns ankled around them in resin-halation patterns from a snapped line. Millhands can tell you the difference between a curl of damp fiber that will press into sheet and one that will buck and delaminate and take your thumb if you aren’t listening.

Telephloemists are half musician, half beetle-thief. They place their iron hooks into sap veins with a tenderness that isn’t faked, listening to each vein as if it were a different dialect. They can rasp messages into the plant so cleanly that anyone with a bone cup pressed to the bark a hundred paces away will feel it like a hand under the sternum, a rub down and to the left. They keep a rack of raspers like an organist’s stops and can swear through a mile of green without a single word falling to the gnats.

Lamplers go up in pairs because there are glasswings. They wear hoods dark as a bruise and shades even in the dim, and their hands are full of fruit—lamps that pretend to be fruit—strapped to their belts on cords the bats cannot steal without taking the man. They know how to bruise the lichens awake with sugar and a palm and how long a covenant will glow and when it must be wiped before it teaches a predator to wait there with its scissor half raised.

There are Cisterners whose work is water. They think in ten-minute rains and an hour’s sifting fall and the timing of children’s wash. They have learned to talk to dew by drawing cold from metal fins, by arranging edges so drops will bead where the gutters can catch them. They can make a pool where no pool should be, and in the time it takes to name your mother the water will fringe itself with life and there will be fish you can watch happen. There are ways to catch those fish: a cloth held at one end of a tray, a shake and a lift, and in your hand a clicking handful of seed-things sleeping. Cisterners have jars of them that thrum at the edge of hearing. Come dew again, a bowlful becomes soup either for your mouth or for the things you nurse. You never waste water without calculating where it will land.

The cookshops sit where the breath runs light enough to keep a flame but strong enough to draw the smoke into the Host and out somewhere else. They feed the Works sugar pressed into canes that a child can break off the stalactite grove, eaten until the tongue sands and sleep takes you raw. They fry hammerhands cracked with a twist at the hinge, marrow laid bare, and the oil leaps like dew forced backward through a membrane. They stew the white cambium grazers, their glow-cord guts pulled and dried for thread—beautiful wedding thread that shines in the long afterglow when you hang it out by a membrane that’s half-open.

As for the children: they count fish lives—eighty heartbeats—face down beside a tray and believe this is what science is. They learn the feel of a thread-wolf’s harp-string in the air before they ever see the beast. They are not allowed white light and take it as a personal affront that such a thing could even exist. They grip the rail with their toes because a boot-slick step can make a thumb-wood bump rise under you and pitch you sideways into a mantid’s scissor. They bring back the bats’ stolen things decorated in moss and tell anyone who will listen the story of how their spoon returned taller than before. They get to post with a sealing pole for the first time at twelve cycles and swallow the egg smell like a dare.

The quiet ones are the Elders of Invariants. Not priests, nothing like. They sit beside the phloem board with thin cups pressed to bone and decide when the Host is keeping your schedule or when you are trespassing in hers. They carry stories in resin lumps—gnarled little amber-rounded things that once were drips and now are keepsakes. Each has a name notched into it in rasp-code no predator would think to learn. They tap them against the wood to feel their weight when the hall goes strange and something like a wing that has only remembered wingness moves through, and all the works go still. You don’t say its name. You don’t have one. You let the pace speak it through you.

Animals live here as an argument that went on longer than anyone planned and became law. The grazers’ bellies are a theology of green gut cords coiling. They polish the Host where they rasp until it shines like thinking. Nurse-flies live like first responders: bloom, foam, forget. Gnats turn fungus tears into fragrant dust that the beetles eat back into quiet. Thread-wolves tune themselves to the new seams the Host grows and then move there and are not individuals any longer so much as a musical instrument with hunger. Hangers: mantids that erase themselves into bark until motion crosses, and then half the room is a subtraction. Hammerhands knock holes in water with fists. The bats pour through like the punctuation of an old story nobody admits to understanding, leaving sentences of droppings that spring green a day later. People work around this as if the verbs were given and they were hired to place commas.

They keep some of these things. A pot of nurse-fly eggs is a standard tool. Everyone has a handful of dew-seeds if they keep a bird. That is what you call a child who tends mantid sloughs and lichen skins and learns to read the Host’s heat with the back of the hand before they are given a knife. Some households herd grazers by music, an old theft from the thread-wolves: a string run across a doorway in the right key will turn a rasping mouth aside. The millhands weave rope with glow thread in the lay and tie children up in a joke you wouldn’t play in a darker place.

There is governance but it does not look like a council hall. It looks like a posted list of times when the belts will not slip and when you can bring a tank open without your lungs being stolen of oxygen. It looks like a gentleman of twenty and a grandmother of sixty both putting their hands on the same bark and agreeing that it is too hot to heave a beam today. Arguments are often decided by lying to the plant in a careful way—a rope dragged to tell it to fatten here instead of there, a pattern of footfalls walked for three nights until a thumb-wood rib grows under one future path and is not grown under another. You come back in six months and the choice has been made in wood. No one complains, or if they do they do it in the half-shouted politeness you use when the orchestra of belts is playing and you still have to be heard on your platform.

New people arrive rarely. When they come, they come on sister-cables from the neighboring chamber where the algae are fractionally bluer. You can tell where by the tint in their hair and the way they expect dew ten minutes out of phase. They marry across cables, and some days lines sag with baskets not of goods but of voices calling questions across the void: when is your bat-night? are your membranes open? is the old engine still steady? They are taken to the old turbine’s walkway and your people watch where their hats go. It is better if they remove them. It is better if the wind cleans their ears of the noise until they can hear the rivers in the tissue underfoot.

The dead aren’t dead for long by any definition you grew up with outside. The Host is always testing for edges. You do not leave a body set against bark except on purpose. The purpose can be many things. Sometimes it is a release: you sleeve the corpse in raw fiber and lay it in a place the Elders of Invariants have marked as free of grazers and swift of sealing, and you talk while the white threads find it. Sometimes you have arguments with other families and with yourself about pitch and flame and whether breathing smoke into a place that is already lung is a blasphemy of handling. Some put their dead into boards: a sheet of cellulose pressed from fiber with a person in it, made into a door that sings a little when the phloem runs. Nobody writes their name in glow-lichen on that door. You would be teaching a mantid to wait where your mother is.

There is art and it looks like engineering. The phloem telegraph messages are scored like hymns. A length of rail with sugar-resined palms in a parade is a mural that glows for an hour and is then eaten by respect. The old turbine’s cuff gets polished and you could call that liturgy. There are balancing acts you could call dance: men and women swinging baskets between columns at dewfall when the floor is slick and the air is full of laughing shrimp, the rope’s lay taking a set from the way a body learns to move in a half-sky. There is a song that is only sung at the beginning of the long draw, pitched to carry through membrane and machine both, and the plant seems to hold its count for the duration.

Some nights the place decides to be an empty hall with a moving wind and no other lives in it. Of course this is not true, but the emptiness is like a tide: it rolls out of the walls and soaks the noise and gives your eyes a place to rest. Those are the nights when the big ones pass, the ventilators without bodies, ripples of more air crossing from a duct to a duct. You make no schedules in those hours. You call it the Host turning in sleep and practice keeping your weight gentle so your presence does not wake the wrong skin sense.

What lives here, finally, is not a list of creatures or a census of skills. It is a compound animal made of plant and machine and the swarms that police the edges and the people who learned long backs and sloped shoulders and the right tone to shout across a humming chamber. It lives in rules that became polite and superstitions that became insulated washers on a bolt. It lives in sugar and copper and the comforting accounting of pressure. It lives in ropes dragged to lie to the green and in the green’s sly, generous habit of believing you for a time and then loving what you asked for until it is yours in wood. It lives in hatless heads under a steady wind that makes sense of noise long enough for you to hear an older arithmetic running under your feet.

Here, each thing is a way the place continues to say itself aloud. The Host exhales and a turbine turns and a boy catches a fish that is not a fish yet in a cloth and a mantid waits in a shadow made more honest by a lamp pretending to be fruit and salt dries at the edge of a tray and a grandmother runs her palm over the bark’s heat and says not yet, and everyone pretends they made a decision. In that pretending is a kind of citizenship. In that citizenship is a people. They live as long as the count holds, and they are very good with numbers.
