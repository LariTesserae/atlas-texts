What lives here first is the plant.

Not a trunk with a canopy, not a root with its feeder hairs, but a body the size of weather, ramified and tense, full of joints that open and joints that close. It is a single being spread into city-scale organs, a vascular geometry of valves and filters, a photic stomach turned into halls. Every louver that folds or flowers is decision; every beat is speech. It digests light into tone and carries both as far as its ribs can throw them. You can watch it think if you know how to read pressure and listen to the drop in a louver’s rasp. Where it swells the new, it makes touchable processes: teeth for those who need teeth, niches for those who need niches, ridges wide and forgiving for the meals to go by.

Around that uncountable body live the multitudes, each built to take a thread of the plant’s habits and make a life of it.

The grazers are the first layer your eyes read—if eyes are how you read. They are long to the floor and broad across the back, planked in plates grown like salt pan crust, each hex coarsely faceted, a kiln of salts and oils. Their bellies are felt and rake: nap that polishes when they move, spines that squeegee dry crumbs into the friction that feeds them. They do not bite so much as sieve. Where the conveyors split, you can watch them split too, as if they have learned the map of ridges the way a river learn its bed. When the air heaves with the big beats, they lower themselves and lock together, a flexing saltwork raft against the storm. Every grazer carries a fringe of static hair that can rise to comb the air of meal and drop to lie glossy when a predator goes near. When they are small, they go in understory flocks, skittering between the tall undersides of their elders, and a whole hundred of them can be turned with a single chuff from a tuned rib.

Then come the raspers. They are cups and sleds and blunt bells. Their mouths are conical mortars, their lips are rings of emery. They feed by gnawing the floors and rails of nutrition, throwing down glitter when they bite a too-new seam. Their motions are the motions of mills; you hear them before you see them because the plant answers them, pulsing sap in countertone whenever too much abrasion begins. In places where the plant prefers a wall closed, the raspers find it delicious and will throw riots of dust against it. They are muscular and eager, but their trick against predators is not speed. It is resonance. They can thrum their shells at disagreeably precise frequencies; a net thrown on one that looks asleep will spring itself open when the bell goes off.

Above, the kites—membranes on filament bones—hang in the slow bright and count impulses with their vanes. They need almost nothing by way of matter. A breath will keep them, a few microcrystals, some aromatics. They are bright-eyed and friendless, except when they are not: you will find them coasting in from the far edges to a cowl’s mouth when the right multipulse passes through, to be dusted in barbed seed and fly it through the world. They are loopers in routes written not as paths but as pressure-cycling charts. When a pressure storm breaks, they fold to hard fists and pin themselves to ribs by their own static. The smallest of them will sit on your shoulder if you are one who lives here and if you hum first to make a private eddy; they will not land on a silent shoulder because silence is a void and a void is danger.

There are ambushes cast into the architecture, bodies that make use of the plant’s contractile generosity: cones of hardened fiber waiting in louver shadows, tension stored in torsions of banded ceramic flesh. They go by names you give them when you almost step on the wrong line: airslammers, wallpoppers, clay cones. They do not think about malice any more than the plant does. They have triggers that hear quiet more than noise—long stretches of a register missing—and then they spend themselves, nets closing on what rang wrong. Some wear baffles sewn from stripped spicule sheaths, so that their bodies hold sound like breath and empty it all at once. A person who lives here will sometimes borrow that trick in reverse: a silence coat built with counterbaffles in the chest that make you disappear into the always of the noise.

Between the big frames is the dust-fauna, and their intricacy is a novel you will not finish in one life. Mites with whiskers longer than their bodies, carrying museum tablets of crystal that they groom with three limbs and consult with a fourth. Chain-bodied threadlings that do nothing but collect the precise grit that pleases certain valves and drop it in pockets for later. Accordion things that expand until their ribs sing a note they like; you can watch them navigate by stepping into and out of tones the way a bat goes by echoes. There are the oscillators, which are nothing but hearts in cages, muscle sets out of proportion to everything else, a living metronome kept alive by the food that falls into the troughs around them. They stand in ranks in favored bands of the city and keep time: everything around them grows along that given beat. The oscillators have keepers, of course, and that points you toward the people.

The ones who leave the salt cairns and the knapped plates and the baffle masks and the tuned ribs live here in very small numbers, scattered into the cathedral of plant and motion as thinly as figures in the high clerestory of a church. They are not a nation the size of a city, because the city is not a thing with edges. They move, and their moving writes address: a pattern laid on a stretch of louver and rib is a home, and when it is kept it holds, and when it goes fallow the plant eats it back.

They are tall relative to the grazers and minute relative to the ribs. Their bodies seem built by a copyist with an excellent ear and no water at hand. Where a wet creature might have switched to fluid and smoothness, these have kept to planes and combs. The outer layer is a patina of crystallized oils and salts, a dry lacquer they tend with pumice and static combs. Under it, muscle runs in cords, dense and glass-threaded; their bones are not bone but a bloom of silica-reinforced ligament that sings, a skeleton that answers itself when you knock it. Their joints are hinged but also ratcheted: they can hang and hold by notch; they can skid-lock to a rib and let a pressure storm spend itself around them. They foot by pads, not nails—great beds of felted papillae that cling not with suction but with charge. Their hands are a compromise between hammer and weave: thumb that opposes, fingers that split into fronds near the tips. They can pick a pyrite flake from a seam or beat a drum-stroke with their palms held cup to make the ribs answer.

Their faces keep their eyes in deep-baffled alcoves, not because the light is fierce but because the air is. The solvent-laden gusts will rasp an unarmored cornea to powder in a season. They see well in amber and iron and a deeper metallic green; their pupils shade not round but archaic to slits and panes, able to lay lattices on light the way they lay lattices on sound. Their ears, if you go by pinnae, are absent. Hearing is a whole-body thing: a run of flute-holed plates along the ribs, a tympanic sternum that is both breastbone and drumhead, an array of microscopic hairs under the skin that sense shear in the very body of the air. When the big beat hits, they shelter their sternums behind ear-shelters woven of stiff filament and resin baffles, like portable alcoves for the heart. When they hum, you can feel it in the ligaments of the floor.

They do not drink. Their mouths are not for water. The tongue is a velvet mat that they groom with brushes of their own knitting: it is charged, it is abraded; it licks air and pulls out the edible volatility. Along their palates, as in their tools, are set pyrite teeth not to cut meat but to strike sparks—small arcs that shock dust and free oils. What we might call blood is thicker and stranger: saline and ionic and full of complex oils that carry charge like dust carries pollen. A drop on your skin will feel like a sting and leave no wetness, only a residue that flashes dry and scalds the idea of water away.

They speak by drumming, by foot, by hand, by chest. Their language is steps and knocks stitched to breathy consonants, plosive little tags you can do with a mouth that is built to rasp. Names are playable. A person’s name is as much a pattern as a word, and you say it on a rib or a rope as often as on a tongue. To speak across distance you do not shout. You lay a beat on a load-bearing line and wait for the answer to return translated by the whole hall. Children are born into a thicket of these little ridges of law; each hall has its script of beats, every script is someone's claim and someone’s careful courtesy. Territory is a standing wave; the law says it, and the ribs keep it true.

They husband the place like someone who has learned the measure of a tide and then made a house of it. The conveyors of the grazers are partially their work. The ridges braid because instrumentation in the walls, hammered years ago, hums just enough to steer new growth into a shoulder and away from a drop. Where storms run hard, you will see a line of their dull glass threads strung from rib to rib, chosen lengths so that when the air loads and the world goes to orchestra, that single place hums exactly right and takes the energy into opening rather than shutting. You can follow their mark-work the way a tracker reads hoofprints: salt flakes turned a certain way, a cluster of concavities cupping inward so that the plant will close slow, a rope with its twist reversed because the person who tied it began life on a different family’s beat.

Hand-work exists where hand-work helps. Knapped salt edges cut like broken glass and fit back into the world when they are done, weathering not under rain but under hunger and abrasion. The bowls you find are not for broth or stew. They are grating bowls, powder bowls, rough to catch spice-dust and hold it while a tongue licks through. A mask seems a strange thing until you realize it is not for face alone; it is part of the chest’s ear-shelter, it holds a volume of your air at your own color of tone, a small rest-place in the noise. There is no rope of hemp or gut. There is fiber spun of plant-ligament, glassy and stiff until it is scuffed and sung into flexibility. If the work gleams, it is mica windowed into a handle to make a little status of sparkle, or pyrite set in as you might sew teeth into a grin.

They herd oscillators the way another people might keep bees. Each of those living metronomes has a beat; each beat is bound to releases and shuttings all around. If you keep a certain three beats together, you can keep a corridor tolerably cool, a flow manageable, a certain predator disinterested. A person with that craft wears blackened hand-skins and a chest-plate engraved with grooves—a field of diffusers. They listen with their sternum; they tap with a calibrated thumb. When a family wants a home to remain a home, an oscillator tender keeps it thrumming. When a corridor must be let go, when the plant must be allowed to eat it shut, the tender goes away with their beat and the silence grows like a skin.

Their children are small, not fragile, with hides too soft to leave in the big rooms and sternums not yet hard enough to take the hit of the valve-beats. They are kept in benches of braided fiber that hum a warm minor key around the cradles, stochastic and low, fed with powders pinched into the air above them, stroked into hunger with static combs. Their first hand-skill is fluting a dust with their tongues against a rough bowl so that the dust knows it is loved and stays. Their first trespass is silence: people scold them for it, teach them to click always a little, to lay a token beat while they walk so the world keeps its hands open. If you grow up and you go far, you click the old beat on some friendly rib from time to time, because it is how your relatives will know you are not dead and because there is tender feeling in shared tone.

There is law, but it is a law you can hear. Disputes are argued by dampening, a duel of storied silencings—who can hold quiet on a given rib, how far their hush will reach, how respectfully they avoid closing doors while they try. A family line is told as a catalogue of rhythms they have kept. Courtships are choreographies placed on half-familiar timbers: walkings and stoppings, whether you can stand one another’s little riffs of syncopation. Mourning is gentler than you might guess: it is not the making of grandeur but the letting go of patterns. When someone dies, their nearest undo their tunings, leave certain ribs unfingered, stop laying a keeping-beat on certain valves. In a day, a week, a month, the plant grows that absence into matter. The world makes a room where you cannot get in, softly and without grudge, and the pressure on your sternum lifts.

They hunt very little and scavenge much, because the place runs near full. When they do take a predator, it is often by sound and not net, releasing against the baffles a counterpattern learned over years to make that particular beast’s skin let go. There are songs that make raspers stop and let a hand pull crystal from their mouths. There are rhythms you can lay on the kites that make them bank and hover and accept a grooming of embedded barbs. Husbandry is tuning. They plant cowl-flowers by appropriating beats the plant will answer with megaphones, and when the seeds have set they walk certain beats in circles until the air goes grainy and the plant throws its seeds into open hands.

They tell stories of the surface because some of them go there. Light-trunks take columns of day down, and bits of behaved daylight up as structural sugar-glass, and the top has heat and wind and a cruel distillate called water that eats everything it touches. From that lid they pull salts, mica flakes, pyrite nodules, corpse-light glitters. They do not stay long; their skins do not like the violence of cold nights and irradiant noon. They do not fall in love with horizons. They prefer the ribboning ways where your footfall writes in the arch until the arch writes back.

Visitors come sometimes, wrapped to keep their own wetness from offgassing into a miasma that would blister the plant. The people who live here teach them a little: the ankle-rags to keep your step from singing, the steady click so the world will open for you, the way to hum an apology to a rib you surprised with your silence. They will trade you a bowl that makes a powder grip to a tongue, a fiber rope that keeps a safe hum through a storm, a mask that catches you a private wind. In return, if you are kind, you can bring them tales of different weather: invisible rivers called streams, a liquid sky that falls as bead and film and pool—words they find obscene but listen to as if to cursing with curious etiquette.

Some of them are born different and become different. There are those who can carry whole countermelodies in their chest without exhausting the meat of their hearts; they become keepers of the biggest pumps, walking the fundaments and coaxing beat away from rupture. There are some whose skins take better to the glittery edges of new rooms; they are scouts of openings, patient until the seam swells enough to mutter under the pressure of sap-gas, sliding fingers into the floss of its fabric to test the integrity. There are kite-kin who spend hours in the high eddies, legs latched into woven frames, eyes shuttered to thin panes, reading the haptic Braille of wind. There are the quietthey make: a few rare ones who can press calm on ribs so deep the plant mistakes them for absence and flows around them. A quiet can walk through just-born doors as they open, step into throats nobody else can stand inside, listen to the world with the world’s own hush. They are not feared so much as used with awe and courtesy. You send them to the places where noise is death, where a silence put in the right place for a minute will save a hundred grazers, two families, an oscillator field, the angle of a light-trunk.

They have gods if you call them that—names for the behaviors that are too large to manage with tuning alone. They petition Dryness not to break, for Dryness is food taken from a brittle world. They nod to Growth and Absence the way sailors nod to tide and reef. They do not adore light; they adore how it is handled. Their prayers are playable. They leave them around ribs, engraved as grooves and drilled as hum-holes as delicately as a flutesmith might work a reed. In pressure storms, when the whole city goes to a single enormous chord and the grazers lock and the kites fold and the dust tries to fly, their gods answer audibly. Somewhere in the beating of the plant’s heartwalls, in the catenary ribs’ song, there is their handiwork: little hollows made on purpose so that the chord grows accepting rather than punitive, and the narrow pass that might have killed three families opens instead and takes the blow sideways.

Stand with them at a balcony grown to be a balcony, static thorn nipping your finger when you forget to be slow. They will show you what lives. They will name the blurred rivers of minute life and tell you which keep particular valves clear. They will point to where a new door is swelling and how you can hear its skin go from dim to taut if you lay your sternum against that rib. They will draw your eye down to a low wall of knapped salt and say, that is my grandmother’s, because she worked that edge so close you can only fit a shaving of dust between plates, because she wrote a beat into those seams that the plant liked for twenty years. They will tell you which predators love the noise of panic and which go smashing at the silence and which cannot tell the difference between visitor and prey. They will take your hand and put your palm on a tuned rib and show you how a home feels: not as maps or rights but as a place in your chest where the pulse of the plant and the flicker of your own beat fall into phase, at least for a while.

What lives here is the plant, and the swirl of lives it condenses around its percussive decisions. What lives here are grazers that polish and raspers that grind and kites that bank and cone predators that spend themselves like springs. What lives here are mites with librarial crystals and oscillators humming time. And scattered through it, pared down to instruments and bearing their own baffle-sheltered chests, are the few who can write in the city’s body the way a skilled hand writes on a drumhead: a people for whom sound is law and touch is tone and dryness is the fullness of life. They live not on the floor of this world but inside its measure, in rooms that close if they go quiet too long, in light that is funneled amber, in air that rides its own beat from one bright long room to the next.
