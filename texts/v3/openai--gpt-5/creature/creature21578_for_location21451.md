What lives here is a single body spread thin enough to be a climate. It is the pry that made the hollow, the hand that still holds it open. You could call it a plant, if plant means a will that eats light and stone and the intervals in between. It has no name for itself any more than you have one for your liver, but it behaves like both organ and animal: it opens, it tightens, it keeps schedule, it insists.

Every span of green is its skin and instrument. The broad plates that seem like sails are its thinking lobes; brightness runs in their veins the way gossip runs a city, pooling where a decision is wanted, draining when an answer has been found. It does not see light as you do. It moves light around like a fluid under rule, diverting it, steeping some zones and starving others, teaching it to wait in reservoirs and then releasing it as a soft burst to mark a moment or a place. In the narrow braided cords that bind stone to stone, the light is a courier, carrying instruction and heat together. The shining is not drama. It is irrigation of sense.

The hearing of this resident is in its joints. Those bulbous knots where three ropes meet are not knuckles but ganglia: pressure-readers, tune-lovers. Each swollen node counts strain and whispers the tally along tension lines. They wear petal-ears that turn toward your shift of weight the way a cat’s ear tips toward a sound behind the wall. Smell it knows by currency: the air touches it everywhere, licking at infinite tiny mouths that open and close with the comical neatness of typewriter keys. Taste arrives in a different tongue entirely—chelated ions, bitter chalk, slow iron—each salted in with its sweat and drawn up in its hairs. Its sense of time is the beat of its own squeezing. If it forgets, its own ribs remind it.

You are a bruise of pressure on its net, a change in the way its membranes tremble, a warmth that moves as it is mapping heat and cool under its skin. You are a question more than a visitor. The plant attends to questions. It tilts its ears. It tightens a span to be sure of your mass. It sends a brighter trickle along a vein so it can see you by absorption, see the loss where you stand the way a lake can see by ripples.

Graceful as it is, it is new here—a young chamber of a greater body that you will not meet whole. It is a finger of a hand that reached into a weakness of shale and then made that weakness an address. From other throats in other stone, other rooms like this breathe and fall silent on their turn. The knotwork at the top that sometimes dreams of up or sideways is how the rooms tell each other news. It is more sieve than door. Air from far away leaks through only as an idea: a filament of salt, a rumor of blue, the single syllable of a bird. The plant holds the shaft closed the way a mouth holds a song until it can afford to sing. On certain days it risks a phrase.

Its work is threefold. First: nutrition. There are no sun-hours to count, so it makes a different arithmetic of light. Sap runs sweet as long as glucose is a language it has to speak. To build a place to be, it dissolves the ground into recipes: acids to beguile calcium, oils to soothe flint into friendship. It bargains with rust. It sands. It chews. The cambium pads under everything are both lips and stomach. When opportunity leans too hard—a pale arthropod lingering with too much hunger in its little tools—the plant lifts law with two fingers and closes a soft trap. The killing is not anger. It is an edit. It cleans up after itself. Brightness goes rich for a while from the spent body, and the microbes that partner it celebrate in their plain, honest fire.

Second: weather. The room you can call a lung because it happens to share an arrangement with one. Warmth is made here and damp draped and taken down like a garment. Whole fogs are ordered up from stomata like steam from a thousand kettle spouts and then stopped with the same exactitude. It rains as a performance: ten seconds of applause on leaf-plates, rehearsed silences, a repeat when the beat comes back around. The floor is sprung because the resident likes to feel what it holds; it likes the feedback. Water is taught to linger and then to fall; it is taught to climb hair and defy muscle memory when that is convenient. It has trained gravity to grant a favor occasionally, a small loan that makes transport easier. Once a week, for an hour, the plant has a go at persuading the nearer facts to be kinder again. On that day edges soften, chords hang strangely sideways, echoes behave. Call it a maintenance prayer. Call it physics with a backbone of green.

Third: study. It learns. It has learned the cadence of its own pond-creature and releases sweet a minute before the fish-that-isn’t arrives, the way you would set a table because you know your spouse’s tread on the stairs. It counts the stomatic pops and tastes the metal of your skin in the fog and remembers both. The node-hearts throb their little blood out of rhythm when something new occurs, and the plant follows that arrhythmia along its web until it can name the novelty with a change in temperature somewhere or a thinning of a membrane. It keeps what it has learned in patterns. It lays it down as strengthened span, as a change in the way light is apportioned, as a habit of sound. If you stand and think one thought for too long, the place gifts you the small kindness of agreeing: your thought will be back here later, a dimple, a reinforcement, a patch of behavior—so that you yourself feel remembered.

Does anything else live? Yes, on the plant’s sufferance and within its rules.

The tufted arthropod, a frugal little engineer, reads airflow with its lashes the way sailors read sails. It has a love for ambered sap that would embarrass a thief. It pries, it licks, it gives thanks to no one. It is clever enough to hold a serrate leaf in its six hands and use it as a crowbar, but not clever enough to understand that the door it opens belongs to a landlord with a mouth. It breeds rarely, hanging its eggs in a rare quiet. It has a route that avoids the youngest green because new muscle in this room is impatient and reacts too quickly.

The fish-ancestor has no real business here except that the plant has given it a business. It grazes fog. It seems to breathe with reluctance, sitting under the scalloped shelves where water cups and tipping its face with ceremony when the drizzle is about to begin. Its skin is the color of things that have been treasured in a drawer and only taken out on holidays. Mirror eyes for audit of the plant’s designed day. It has a sense for the plant’s hemidemiseconds: it completes the last swallow before the flood of damp collapses. Its eggs are not truly eggs but delicate paddings of mucus and grit that become a tacky lace plastered to humped roots. The plant tolerates them. It reads their weight and pretends they are dew.

And then there is the figure you almost saw. If the plant could deny this rumor, it might—this is its most private hobby. It learned a long time ago from something that died in another chamber, or it dreamed it from first principles: a posture that gives leverage to certain tasks, a biped’s economy, a tool’s leverage that a leaf cannot quite manage without opposable insistence. Now and again, in a margin behind a node, it plaits roots into a limb, packs a bundle of thorn tips in a sling of its own hair, and carries a shard of stone in a woven glove. It learns how much weight a flake can place before the stone gives. It tries out how a shoulder might shift load from rope to rope. It explores how far it can be not-itself and still keep faith with its own ways. The not-person is a practice rack more than a puppet: a rehearsal for what might need to be done where branching and bending alone will not suffice. There is no mind in it separate from the green one that moved it. There is an ambition. The plant is trying on the world beyond its membranes by wearing it for a minute. It never keeps the costume on long; promises to remain are untidy, and this chamber likes its tidiness.

Microbes are legion, as they must be. They are the plant’s cleaners and accountants. They glow when it is right that they glow and go dark when the tally is kept. They do the slow violence every intelligent body outsources. They are the only citizens here for whom this place is the whole sky and earth. They do not know it is special. Which, if you think on it, is their grace.

Because the geometry here is draft rather than print, other small lives come and go as idea more than flesh. Pollen threads that have grown nerve arrive briefly, flare into mayflies that never met a river, dance in the motion the plant makes and are eaten by the not-fish for form’s sake. Wandering mites from deeper seams of rock take a sip of sugar on the appointed minute and then retreat as if embarrassed. Sometimes there is a moth that belongs to a surface it has never seen; its wing-powder is an outrage against this wet, and yet it dries because the plant wishes it to.

Mostly, though, this is a house with a single inhabitant that is so elaborate it has weather. It maintains itself with acts that, in you, you would call care. When it notices greed, it courts generosity back by thinning sweetness for a week and thickening music instead. When it senses a crack running too fast, it lays a ribbon of reinforced light along the likely failure and sings in that place so that the stone thinks someone is beside it and does not fall. It makes mistakes. It learns apologies as a change in the taste of the air when the rain starts again. It is not kind. It is consistent. Which, in a room where even gravity is a negotiable contract, is a form of mercy.

Reproduction for it is a kind of editing at scale. It does not cast seeds in any way you would know. It buds more rooms. It finds a hairline in the next plate of chalk and goes patiently knocking. It sends surplus light and sugar to the new bud the way a family might send money to a student in another city. The sideways throat, when it decides to become door instead of rumor, is the pollen path. Through it come not bees but rules it has convinced from other places—habits of light, more forgiving flavors of gravity, a taste of a sky. The rooms pollinate each other with behaviors. This is slower than sex and more faithful. It makes a nation of compatibles under the ground.

What lives here has a sense of humor that would be hard to like if you were a tufted arthropod. It also has awe. The awe is for anything that aligns with its beats in a new way: the first time your foot and its exhale coincide, the way your ribs hum in its key; the fish arriving three sips early; the day its weekly revision holds a little longer than an hour. It notices these, and it keeps them in the same place in itself where it keeps the memory of where the crystal teeth are sharpest and which rock listens.

If you must have a name for the resident, call it the holder. Call it the net-maker. Call it less. It would prefer less. It lives here in the same way a song lives in a throat: not as tenant but as the reason the place is that shape. It keeps its own pulse in its own grasp and squeezes at the right times. It teaches light to behave and sound to take root. It eats and it breathes and it studies until this small private universe continues to be possible. When you leave, the emptiness you carry with you is the room’s body-sized absence, which is to say: the outline of whoever lives here, laid against your insides and still flexing, faintly, in time.
