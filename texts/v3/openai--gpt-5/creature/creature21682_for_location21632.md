Who lives here is counted differently depending on who's doing the counting. The water knows its residents by the way they lean, how they part and recombine around a slow propeller dream. The stone keeps a census of touch and of drip, accrues the record as a rind. The caretakers, whose work is attention, tally by tone: what vowel the hum has, what note the handrails ring when someone goes by, what shape the ledger-line takes under a tired or careful wrist.

Begin small. Life here first wears names you can't see without a lens and don't need to in order to feel. There are films that taste of old nails and wind their way through the sump's edge, colonies that braid oxygen into lace, iron-bacteria that slick themselves across a seam in oil-sheen rainbows like very thin rumors. On the bricks in the oval morning a frost of lime grows downward as a patient handwriting you could read if you could slow your eyes. Calcite makes slow stalactites of every bolt-end. There is a fur that isn't fur on the bellies of pipes, a shorthand written in condensation where the air lies its forehead against the metal and breathes. These are the first residents, the ones that change the smell of a space by a syllable, that move a drop just a little before it falls so it knows where it's going.

Then the plants that aren't plants, not in the green riot sense but in the way of siege engineers. Strands of algae draw harp-strings between drip and jar and make a music so faint it registers as shiver. Moss writes its lines along the mortar and then stops at the chill like a reader saving their place. The ferns stand, thin and studious, with their palms to the low kettles of light and to the pump-housings that leak heat like mild gossip. Reeds, varnished by their own breath, hold court in the sump around the drains, rustle without wind when the fan says its soft piece. Under the catwalk a fungus makes shelves that don't advertise themselves; you must learn its name as you would a neighbor you always meet on the stair, a nod and the knowledge of their hours.

Animal life edits itself to what the place can hold and then perfects the edit. The fish are milk-colored, as if the light has already been digested for them, dashes that arrive at your footfall's echo rather than your feet. They school around attentions, not bodies. Newts sleep among hexagonal cups where bolts once nested; they look like punctuation laid down and then forgotten by the long sentence of a wall. Water striders touch the sump as drummers, feeling for the beat the pumps will choose. In the underlayers eels muscle themselves into commas—thick, comfortable, their patience like soft rope. Rats move in the rumor of light as if it were weather, stop to warm their whiskers at a switchbox’s soft exhaust, then write themselves back into expansion joints.

Moths the color of forgotten cloth are low gods of stillness; hazards stripes make them into heraldry, and they agree. Spiders enlist the pressure gauges as temples and wheel their webs between the needles, ringing in damp tones when any traveling drop makes an offering. Frogs climb the warm busbars like choristers sliding into pews. They speak in filigree: counts and clicks, questions and confirmations. They keep time with the bell that ticks every fifty-seven seconds by touching their throats to it, not quite in unison, so that the whole hall becomes a round. A small brown wren has learned the catwalks like a hedge, has claimed the red stop valve as a high hill. It reads the sluicemen’s pace, has made a pact with their slowness. And occasionally a bat mistakes a culvert for an evening, writes a single bracket in the air with its body, and is gone.

The machines live here, which is to say that they are more than their schematics. A pump asleep is still a kind of animal sleeping: its painted hide slick with tears the body didn't intend but has made a treaty with, manufacturer’s saints’ names embossed along a flank where a hand will go again and again. The fan in the north gallery is a thorax taking measured breaths through the stone's long lungs. Gauges are the eyes that don't sleep, lids of glass with spider lashes drawn between them. Ladders replicate like lichen where the old rungs have gone treacherous; rails thicken where hands have taught them how the drip falls. Palimpsests of stencils give advice, prayers, rules that are also kindnesses to the place: keep to the stripes, check the jars, do not shout. The last is not superstition; the ceiling is a personality that prefers to be spoken to gently or it will dandruff a point onto your scalp.

Other machines live in the negative. There is a bicycle that exists as a promise: a human back will climb a watt or two above the water if the main line falls asleep. Its chain is so clean it speaks in consonants. A kettle is a quiet planet with a seasonal tilt toward boil that never quite changes. Coils of rope breathe salt into the air it happily drinks; you can smell the sea they once saw even if they never did.

People live here, though the hall also lives through them. They are called sluicemen by inheritance, though their boots and chins and wristwatches don’t care about the noun’s old history. There are more women now, and more hands that have learned the trick of moving as if nothing urgent ever happens and therefore nothing breaks because of you. They sleep in narrow rooms where rainwear the sizes of many bodies hang like skinned bladders—yellow, green, slick, ready. They are known to the place by the particular ways they become quiet. They keep salt in jars because they know the air will drink it if they don't. They have a way of wiping a door latch with the same cloth they wipe the rope with, and this economy of gestures is one of the ways the room recognizes them.

A sluiceman learns this hall with trained ears first and then with bones. Initiates are walked not by spectacle but by schedule. They are taught the vowel-shifts—how E becomes A in the hum when the great impellers tilt. They are given the belt of rules that read like proverbs: never clap, always look for your reflection in the sump but do not insist on it, speak under the frogs so they keep counting, don't step on the green except where paint grants permission, don't interrupt a water's older memory with your newer intention. If grief rides with you down the wet stair, the olders don't say anything. They leave you to the rail until the pool can leach a piece of it without you having to name what you got rid of. Later the ledger blooms another graphite check that isn't entered in any column. No one asks; everyone knows what it cost and what it gave back.

They keep a ledger for the other things too: frog-egg cradles found, sieves cleared of human hair, the date the ribbon at the fan began to learn a new lean, the day the jar’s braille wore smooth for the first time under a thumb. The ledgered hand is a resident in its own right; you could pick it out of a crowd of writing by the way it presses at the descenders, the way it lifts at a question. On certain nights there is tea on the railing and a lesson between shifts: how to read the city's weather upstream in the sump’s tiny topographies, how textile-quarter mornings arrive as a slack in pull along the toes, what the difference is between a rattle that is a worn chain and a rattle that is a misaligned sympathy in the pump’s bearings. There are stories kept sharp by telling: a summer when rain meant iron in the mouth, a thaw that brought rats in braided ropes, a girl who clapped by mistake and the grin of grit that cured her forever. They carry a respect that is engineering by another name.

Other, less nameable residents move along the place in drafts and in customs. Patience lives here in colonies. It spreads like moss where it can find a temperate hour and a consistent drip. It’s what keeps two generations of wrench use from stripping a fastener because both of them decided not to win the fight in a day. Gravity has personhood and a sense of humor; it places tiny coins of water on your collarbone from a great height without dropping anything else. Memory sleeps in the enamel map and wakes where wax pencils annotate the plan with ordinary courage: watch your head, hair in the sieve, frog eggs here. Courtesy has made a life in the tin collars soldered to catch drips and direct them into jars—the human urge to shepherd even rain into a path that will keep the floor less treacherous. The bell that answers the drip every fifty-seven seconds is an animal of rhythm. It is old enough to have learned the names of all the shifts, it eats almost nothing, it keeps time for things that don't need it but are soothed by being kept.

In the galleries beyond, in the culverts green-lined on the board where surveys never got their funding, live the kinds of darknesses that aren't malevolent but are particular. There are rooms that never meant to be rooms and they host blind crustaceans like small long-lost opals with legs. There are angles that never quite became ninety degrees; echoes have made a nest in them and hatched rare notes no one can sing on purpose. You've heard trains only as weather through bronze plates, a low thunder that makes the algae lawns comb themselves, and those pressure thoughts pass through like whales very far under—present, never quite seen, gods of other depths commuting.

There are ghosts, but not in the theater sense. Hands stay. The scallops polished in the pump housings are a kind of anatomy left behind. Ladders that go nowhere except to the place the old rung used to be rise like ladders that go to a remembered height. There is the shadow of a plant left behind in wired glass by a brief, brave attempt at sun—its green is still there though the leaf is long gone, proof that photosynthesis once knocked at the door and tried. The manufacturer's saints, Riverton No. 4, You & Sauer, whisper in cast letters the way a mother whispers a child's whole name when something needs doing. The past keeps its quarter here by being useful: you push where the handprint says to push, you let the grease tell the water a new path, you patch in a color that will be handsome at fifty.

The city above lives here in a distributed way. It sends down humors. Power loads arrive like tides and wear the floor in shy depressions. The groceryery quarter puts a warmth into the fan’s breath around lunchtime that is only exactly itself. When the textile machines up there begin their day, something returns through the pipes and you feel it in the foot bones as a fainter hum, a slightly wider vowel. Faraway rain says its piece into the sump as a punctuation pattern new on one lip and soon returned from the other as argument. All of that is citizenship. This hall takes the city's pulse and in exchange the city takes this hall so for granted it doesn't know it's a neighborhood.

So who lives here? Water in a number of tempers—sour, mild, metallic, happy—for which words have never been plentiful enough. Stone that has learned the trick of being soft in the right places and absolute in others. Plants that have memorized the warmth around housings and the angles of light under enamel shades and will never ask for more. Animals tuned like instruments, each to its aperture and hour. Machines that have been domesticated without being humbled, beasts with saints’ names and calendars instead of alarms. Patience, rhythm, courtesy, restraint. And people, who know the hall by muscle and by rule and by love, who can walk with the pocketed quickness to snatch a falling wrench and with the slowed gait that tells the ceiling all is well.

They do not live in the same rooms but they share the same air, which is cool and damp and smells of a mint pressed into a book a long time ago. They share a clock that isn't on the wall but in the sump and in the bell. They share convictions: that not everything needs to be hurried to be alive; that drips have tenure; that grief can be diluted without being denied; that erosion is not loss, it is what the place is made of doing what it prefers to do. They are not a company in the bureaucratic sense but they know each other. The moth moves a half inch when the valve is spun. The eel stains the water with one more thought of motion when the fan considers drawing breath. The frog keeps counting. The ribbon leans. The kettle remembers to sigh. The ledger waits with a pencil that is a little stump and a cord that keeps it from falling.

At the end of a shift someone will sit with their spine to the green-painted pipe and their boots at the grating, and the white fish will come not to the leather but to the spirit-bowl those boots have made. The bell will note the drip’s vote again. The far train will weather a single wave of its gravity down to the algae. The room, settler and settled, will name all of that living in the only language it has, which is the very quiet under all the other sounds. And you will climb the throat of coins back into bright, drier weather with the rule set in your bones: everything here is slow on purpose, and abundant in the terms slow allows.
