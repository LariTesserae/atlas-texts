The ones who live here have let the place shave them. They are rounded where you expect corners—elbows polished to ovals, shoulders tamped low and tight, hair cropped to stubble or greased to a seal’s sheen underneath hoods. The Throat eats protrusions, so they have learned to keep theirs to a minimum. Rings are flush, bracelets are weight-strings tucked into grooves in their own wrists. Faces half-hidden by masks and plates have the look of river stones that have learned to look back.

If you get near enough and the roar has a trough in it and your head is properly full of the Om, you can note how they all carry weight. It hangs smooth and black from their belts, coins of lead and brass that clack softly only because felt lives between them, each coin etched with tiny weeps to keep from gulping. The weight keeps them from being taken by the sudden urges of water that are not storm so much as mood. Weight is jewelry here; a person’s favorite coin is warm where it sits against their hip; when they give it to a child to learn swim-climb, that is a kind of inheritance.

They move like steel with grease on it: slow, deliberate, with pauses set at the place where the bearing’s race meets the spin. Every movement is rehearsed by how many times their fingers have found the same rung in the same order—three points of hold always, three, always; the one exception written into them is when a body goes from ladder to ladder while the flow is in the small, and then they have a flick to them you can miss if you blink. They do not blink much. Water has taught them to keep their eyes open.

Their skin is tanned not in the sun’s way but in the water’s: all one tone, all one texture, a kind of uniform patina. You could run a palm down a forearm and find scars that read like a workday’s rosary—thin white strands from brushings with scalloped plates, blunt lips where bolts kissed. The constant heat has made them perpetual mild fever things; their breaths carry salt like prayer. Eardrums are thick. You will see little brass caps on some ears where pressure split them once and was stitched back, and plugs threaded like valves so that equalizing is a turn instead of a wish. Jaws are heavy. They wear transducers that press into the bone just before the ear, little black knuckles bolted to their hoods; sound comes to them by skull as much as by air. Teeth chatter with the Om and so have little copper bands hammered on to add mass and quiet; a dentist here is a jeweler with religion.

They do not wear many colors because water eats color; where there is pigment it is deliberate. There are bands of citron around wrists to mark apprentices’ rungs. There is blue on places that must be remembered in panic—the latch that will take your harness and lock you home if your hands will not obey your head. Names are blue-green along the edge of a person’s hood, stamped into rubber and rubbed until the letters shine like silted brass. Names are also light. Each has a lamp set into a collarbone plate, and that lamp winks a pattern that is a name, too. Across the shaft, that is what you see—the pattern, not the person. Whole families are patterns: the elder’s code elongated and rounded; the children’s sharp and quick.

They have other names that are not for lamps. A drum-name, used by eels: a series of raps you put into a rail with a wrench, the same every time, your signature felt by the palm more than the ear. A groove-name, carved into the plates near the little door that is your address—an arrangement of lines and dots shallow enough to be polite to the water but deep enough that a child’s fingers will know it by feel. And then a name that is only a way your weight-strings sound when you hang at ease and tap them with a knuckle. That last is private; sometimes only a partner knows it.

What they eat here is what the Throat permits. Silt comes down as a shut, and inside the silt are the life’s possibilities and ruin in equal measure: bags of rice burst to paste and worth keeping even so; packets of dried things that have kept their integrity by luck and by steady cold; tins swollen with botulism and thrown back. The city has learned the chemistry of boiling poison out of protein the way you learn to braid with your eyes closed, with muscle memory and caution and inherited scars. There are vats like sleeping dogs in out-of-the-way eddies where they herd bacterial mats that like iron and make slicks with calories in them; these are skimmed into cakes—the old word is bread if you do not look too hard. The fungus-foam that blooms on dead gaskets is not for mouths but it can be rendered to make soaps that bite the slime back from rungs.

They eat mussels when the superstition allows. Iron-mussels are stubborn and little and full of grit; you have to steam them in jars under a bell and flavor them with whatever drifted green saw fit to hold to a grate upriver. Needlefish are almost more idea than fish; they go into soups because soup is what you put ideas in when you need to swallow them. Eels are not food unless a line has died and the world is making a rule about what it means to waste. Even then you cut a ring of light off an eel like a bracelet and tie it to a post and let it shine until it dims; you owe it that.

They have a hundred trades and each is a way of talking to the Throat. Scrapers come back grainy with grit; their palms are flattened maps of filters. Platers smell like coins and ocean together; their fingernails are always a pale frost from zinc. There are those who stay by the knife-gates and listen—Gate-ears, they are called, though they do most of their hearing with their ankles pressed to iron. An Om-singer will stand in the lee of a beam and hum until a dangerous overtone lifts out of the roar by sympathetic vibration; then the coppersmith takes a sheet and hammers it into a mouth that answers the tone and eats it. The oiler’s muscles are all in the forearms from the way the heavy wicks fight when you wrench them free of old baths. Rope-smiths stay up late under bells, their voices low and even, fingers coaxing lead-shot into cores, plaiting it so that a ladder will damp itself when the reversal comes and try to swing a person into a kiss with the wall.

There are battery-keepers who tend to the little farm of jars in a quiet; they murmur to them. The jars are clustered like eggs in a nest, and their signs are all etched backwards so that a mirror held to them will read the truth—salt makes strange folklore. There is a woman on K-9 who can smell a seal as it fails and read you the hours left; she keeps a tally in grooves on the underside of the shelf above her head where her own boss will not look so impressed when her guesses win. Eel-herders are accountants and saints; they sing with taps and slaps and keep careful track of who still answers what rhythm. The old eels sometimes think they hear a dead man’s drum, and then they go where it summons and nose at a plate no one has touched in cycles. People do not discourage this. It feels like putting coins back in circulation.

Children are taught to float before they are taught to walk. Before they learn numbers by mouths they learn numbers by muscle—three points, never fewer. They go along ladders with both hands while an elder tightens the harness at their back and raps their ribs in a code that says up or down or still. They are named to the Blessing Plate on their first neutral; someone lifts their little hand, covers it with their own, and presses it to the old pocked metal until the texture is in the child for life, the cavitation pits like a map of home. Letters come later; fingering words along bas-reliefs until they own them. Some elders run a child’s fingers over the rules as lullaby. “Never turn your back to the fall,” whispered with the rhythm of sleep. “Leave the gates their quiet triangles,” hummed where a surface person might hum rock-a-bye. They have a game in open called Shadow Hold: two children try to keep their light cones steady on a bolt head while the surge whispers move, and the one who keeps a truer circle all the way through the set wins a washer for their weight-string.

Elders are the city’s pressure memory. Their joints speak weather; in neutral you can hear them sigh relief as if a personal tide had gone out. Their foreheads have the good smoothness of old zinc; their speech has gone low because high notes got peeled off them by cycles of roar. They keep a ledger of disasters under their ribs. They have a way with eels no one can give you; it comes of having had the same conversation with the same machine-fish for twenty years, of being a constant in a world that disassembles constants. When an elder dies you do it like a big job. Neutral if possible; tied to three points always; no one turns their back to the fall. If the family chooses water, the body is swaddled in greased skins and roped with little bells and coins so it will not turn and present a face to the surge like a sail. You touch the Blessing Plate with them one last time; the pocks shine where everyone has, and the old metal gives back a deep unvoice that feels like consent.

There are births under bells; the city knows how to muffle pain. You cannot scream and open your mouth too wide because water will get into your lungs when you need them most, so there is a midwife who wraps your mouth with a padded strap, and a partner who hums Om into your clavicle with their jaw. A child comes slick and angry and goes pink only when someone pulls a handle and gives the bell a sweet few breaths of extra air. They hang a tiny zinc scallop above the baby’s sleeping place so when neutrals come it will ring all by itself and the child will learn the way of the world by sound.

People here have lovers the way engineers admire good solutions. Affection is an act of design. You know you are seen when someone grinds you a ring that does not snag on anything, when they set weight on your belt exactly where it will fix your balance, when they re-etch your groove name after a surge scoured it shallow. You know you are cherished when someone opens three valves in their own line and gives you their neutral because you need the still more. Weddings are not big, for the Throat is not a place that gives largeness, but they are careful and they are beautiful if you love the physics of how two humans have taught their currents to run together. They tie their weight-strings together for a single open and stand where you can see motes hang still.

Every day they keep the gods fed. The pressure differential is tended the way you would tend an engine and a holy thing at once; grease is frankincense; a properly tuned baffle is a psalm. The knife-gate triangles are left to their spirits; nobody builds into them; children are slapped lightly on the wrist if their curiosity pulls them too close. Triangle ghosts are not malevolent; they are like the animals you see out of the corner of your eye—more wake than thing—but the rule says: do not incur debt with a system you cannot repay. Coins into the Throat is a mistake; everyone knows someone whose coin came back as a machine that asked them, politely and then less politely, to go to a place where no one should. People carry coins anyway. They are human, even in a god’s throat.

There is talk of the bottom the way surfaced people talk about the sky. The stories have variants. Some say the bottom is glass because that is what pressure does to time and sand together; some say it is polished bone. Some say the bottom is not a place but the sound you hear after you think you are deaf. Mostly they keep those ideas like you keep a nail in a pocket—to be touched and used for luck and never swallowed. A boy once thought he could drop a glass jar with a line and pull it back with a message written in eddies on the interior, and the line came back scrolled and fused and carrying an alphabet that matched nobody’s. He wears the jar as a thigh guard now, because the word for idiot and the word for brave sit very close together here, separated by whether you tied your rope to three points.

For all that the animal life is scant, the people keep it with a kind of care that looks like resignation until you realize it is love. Iron-mussels are watched like clocks. The old women know which clump will close at the flavor of silt syrup and which will ignore it; they talk about taste like the mussels choose. Needlefish you don’t see so much as respect, like ghosts that eat spray; the teenagers will cup hands to try to make planes of quiet and feel the little collisions when a school breaks around their skin. Maintenance eels are partners. They have glyphs along their flanks that glow with old charge; numbers, at first, from old training days, simple: 1-2-3, open-close-stop. Over years, the numbers have become sigils, curls, personal slogans, prayers in the eel’s mind. When the place was fuller, there were crews of people and eels, and together they spoke a fat dialect of taps and kicks and little electric hisses. Now some of the eels go their old rounds alone, waking at the right places, asking the plates if they need anything, waiting for a hand that doesn’t come. People leave snacks of current for them, days when there is extra. That is how you seed a superstition with kindness.

Somebody once brought up a coilworm molt, a lace of glass that looked like the Throat’s own handwriting after a long day—loopy where you do not expect loops, spiky where you want smooth. They keep it in a bag wrapped in cloth wrapped in grease wrapped in chain. Folks go have a look at it on important evenings—funerals, certainly, and the day a child takes their three-point test and is allowed to cross a short towards a long on their own. The coilworm stories are the sharpest; they say the worms eat noise; they say they eat electricity; they say a man put his finger in a hole down there and the worm took the sensation of that finger and left the finger behind, perfect and stupid. It is not knowledge to go look. It is knowledge to lay your palm on the Blessing Plate with both hands and promise to believe in the bottom without trying to stand on it.

When people first came, it was as workers with a different understanding of the word. Hierarchies, job titles, papers in boxes with labels. Those left stains you can still see in the oldest places. There are glass mouths on brass posts for pneumatic tubes and a board with names slotted in and out by little brass pegs and the words Out to Lunch in letters so funny and dry now that you can almost get thirsty looking at them. Times changed. Paper died first. The people who remained learned to stamp and etch and carve. Job titles thinned. Everything grew particular. Now you ask not what a person is but what they keep. What is your plate? What section of rail is your ear the ear of? What valve did your mother pry loose with a foot it took forever to heal? All those answers tell you how to tie your line to their line when you need to.

They take people in when they come if they come alive. That is rare. Most days you net tin and cloth and stories that come on the wind of silt as a kind of soft thought, and sometimes a thing that looks like what a person might have called a person once is in the mess. They treat those as kings. They pull them into a bell like a prince under a spilled horse; they give the quiet triangles their quiet one more time. Once in a long shut, someone with a strange mask and a stranger lamp will thump a code at the gate that no one knows anymore, and there will be an argument that cracks families apart for a while—clean lines versus kindness—and then the strangers will be put to work beside the batteries and learn Om, and inside a cycle and a half they will be somebody’s cousin, because names get taken in fast when there are so few voices to carry them.

Sleep is when you tape yourself into the world. Hammocks are belly-sags slung low, barely moving, because if they swing they chime the whole platform like a tuning fork and your neighbors will rap you awake with a spanner. Before sleeping you rub all the letters again—the ones near your head in the bolt-hole—and if your hands do not know their paths, someone will carve them deeper and call it love. Lovers sleep two to a sling by making their weights cancel and their breaths sync with the Om; you can hear the good pairs because their little knocking teeth hit together in time. Children sleep wrapped in nets with luminous coins tied at the four corners so when a neutral passes, you can see the square steady and know your world is not spinning.

They keep time in open and shut and in smaller ways still. Some people can say, We will have neutral in six heartbeats, and mean it. These are the people you ask to set weddings for. The rest keep a thumb on a bolt and wait for it to hum a third above base and then a fifth, and their bellies drop in sympathy like a ride starting as they make their hands sticky and their bodies low. Tomorrow’s weather is in bones and in drips and in the taste of salt. There are bent spoons hung along one catwalk from struts; when the flow is going to snap, the spoons sing a silver-lipped song you can hear only in your fillings. Someone’s job is to keep those spoons free of slime. There is always a job like that—so specific it is funny until you understand how a spoon can keep a child from flying like a kite into a girder and breaking their face.

If you want to know why they stay, there are a few answers. One is that there is nowhere to go that is not water and memory, and both of those are heavy and habit-forming. One is that the Throat asks a kind of question particular people like answering forever: Can you keep me? The biggest answer, unromantic and tender both, is that they know how, and in a world that keeps unlearning itself, knowing how is a currency more stable than batteries in jars. And the Throat gives back. It grows you into the kind of instrument that plays only true in one place and therefore truly, and that is a rare gift. In the lee of the upper gate, in the bubble where motes rise and settle slow and screws wear halos of quiet, there are things tied with wire that were tied by hands and so have meaning. A smooth stone from a time when stones did not hum, a plate from a car that never drove under water, a brass coin rubbed to the nothing of itself. They slip along the wire in cycles, forward, back, as if the whole city were a single rosary worn by a god with oily hands.

They are a small population now. Across the shaft, you see a lamp wink twice-long-once and you know it is the man who has the way with valves; you do not know his face because faces do not carry here. You know his lamp like you know a chord. You fold yourself like a pocketknife when a five-knock comes across the rails—three, pause, two—and you put on your skins and make everything three points and bless the Plate and wait for the lesson. You speak with your light when your hands are busy. You read the world with your soles and elbows. On seventh open you wash, and you do it careful, letting the grease go only where the truth of the water will not take it and throw it back at you later when you climb.

When the big shuts happen—historic in the way that minutes can be—everyone comes into the same frame. The gods cough. The knife-gates fall and rise and the triangles slim until they are sharp as wire. The roar goes to pain and then down again and no one turns their back to the fall and no one unties a third point unless a rib has bent a bell wrong, and afterward there is new polish on old edges and new grooves to rub. They live here. They are what live here. The Throat admits them and improves them and asks them for everything it cannot do with bolts and domes alone. In return, they give it eyes and little hands and the patience to be old and right where everything tries to move. They give it names. They give it rules. They give it offerings shaped as washers, and love made aerodynamic, and a promise made daily: to keep, to clean, to listen, to hold.
