They live here because they learned to drink what stone exhales when it cannot quite cool.

Closest to your ankles, if you had ankles in a place where standing is a mistake, are the grazers—the floor’s tide of hinged scales that read like a word you can’t speak. They have no topside in the way we mean; the outer surface is armor patterned with tiny hexes that pinch and flex, writing and erasing thermal script as they move. Their underbodies are velvet with capillaries, brushed with an electrostatic nap that unfurls when contact is right and lies flat when wrong. They do not chew. They kiss the salt fuzz and tug ions down gradients that run through their bellies like rivers. Their blood—not blood—carries no water; it is a melt of light salts and metals in proportion learned from the rock. When they halt, it hardens into wax; when they knead, it becomes a slow slick, and that is their circulation. They lane and eddy not because they agree to, but because the mountain’s pulse has been running through living bodies for so long it has taught even solitude how to merge without touching.

When they molt, it is thread by thread at the seam the way a zipper unstitches, and the shed seam is a perfect coil like a shaving off a carpenter’s block—only glassy, brittle, singing at the edge of hearing as it cools. They reproduce by case and swarm: a scale will begin to carry an extra plate under its own, ghost-pale and not quite aligned. Over breaths it becomes more itself. At a certain pulse it snaps free and lies still, pressed by all the neighbors; then it wakes, folds, and joins the flow. There is no brood and no pair; reproduction is a matter of weight and timing, of when a body’s stores cross the threshold for doubling and the collective friction will accept the new shape.

Above them cling the bookplates, pale and hinged, that do not travel but do not plant either. They take a long time to decide to close. They shiver first—minute peristalses corrugate their rims as they taste the exudate with thousands of canaliculi like the holes of a flute. When a pulse swells and then drops, they exhale—yes, they have lungs of a kind, a plenum—then drop the covers and make of their edges a tight seal without true pressure, only van der Waals’s patient kiss. Inside, the crystals are scraped by tongues like combs, prizing ions into solution, stealing away charged futures and folding them into their own fins and plates. They open again with the regardless speed of a shutter. Their interiors are stitched with conductive cords that flare for a breath when the air’s recipe changes. Those cords are both sense and muscle. If you watched them for a day you would see their opening schedule is a calendar matched to the wall’s five histories. They avoid the old scar of the comb-tongued season. They prefer the layers that cool in smaller steps.

The ceiling is a quiet orchard of sacks. Glossy seeds hung by glass cords that were once taut lines, routes for someone else’s passage. The sacks inflate when the mountain inhales and sag when it exhales, always relieved by the counter-beat. The beaks at their tips are no mouths but nozzles: they sweat a drop of a salt mix that stays liquid above what rock would call melt because their own bodies cheat it, seeding it with crystals that won’t quite finish. When a grazer flow moves below and a few degrees of wrongness match a template that has meant food in the last hundred pulses, a droplet falls. It is less a weapon than a logic gate. A pucker appears where it lands. The chosen grazers stiffen and fold from the outside inward. Then from the sack unspools a line so fine it is almost a temperature difference braided into a slipknot. Up it comes, without drama, without weight in the world, into a place where heat is stored and spent. The sacks are not individuals as we use the term. They could detach and go, but they have never learned a direction except down, and the air here does not allow down. So they hand their children outward. When one has made a dozen nozzles from itself over many hundreds of pulses, it allows its cord to collapse and is eaten from the outside by the motile frost—the shag.

The shag is a thinking without a center. Call it motile crystals if you need a name; it is a fur of rectilinear growth, hairlike in effect but angular in fact. Each filament fills up with a certain ion until it can no longer carry any more and then fractures along habitual planes. The pieces drift, are carried by the thin gas’s rivering, snag on other pieces, align, anneal, and thicken until they can remember again. Their “thought” is whatever pattern they collectively preserve about where the good leaks are, when a seam tends to open, which groove in the three-columned shrine amplifies the home current best. They show their decisions by migrating, first a film, then a velvet, then a plate of frost that climbs a wall and settles in an old groove and makes it younger. They do not feed like the grazers. They catalyze and are catalyzed, using the residue of creature wastes and exhalations to build more of themselves. If there is a farmer here and if the farmer is gone, it may be that the shag retains that intention like a scar that keeps healing in the same shape.

Predators, yes—there are always those. Not sharp-toothed, not lunging, but geometrically clever. The slag-spindles, black as cooled lava with pits across their plates, each pit a well that knows a fraction of a degree when it changes. They study surfaces without moving. Even when they move they don’t seem to. Their limbs are hinges set into a casing that stores twist the way basalt stores strain before it cracks. Around the notch in the floor, where the rock remembers the old trap, the spindles keep vigil. When the signature is right—the pulse, the hum of a grazers’ flow closing over the notch, a six-pulse dust cloud just settled—they exchange shape with the air; a limb is elsewhere; something under their touch chooses stillness and doesn’t choose again. They don’t eat in the edible sense. They lay along the body and apply little currents through those surface pits. The carapace softens along the seam. The wax blood bleeds. They drink fields. They leave behind shells that are no longer shells, but polished negatives that the gas sharpens into mirrors.

Others comb. Pale cylinders riding in the gas, more buoyed by thickness than supported by legs, heads like ciliary fans that feel for lines of charge. They drift until a patch of grazers stutters in its heat the way the old do, the wounded do; then the fans close like irises and a mild implosion—an absence of something in the field—pinches life still. Their wastes click when they hit the rock: glassy needles that plant and stand and make little islands of poorer conduction. Around those islands condenses—condenses in a way that does not involve wet—an oasis of less. Species grow there that like the longer cooling tail, that like a steadier, lower. The combers, not knowing it, garden for others long after they have gone.

There are engineers if by engineer you mean a body that understands the mountain by pushing into it until the mountain moves slightly differently. Someone or some lineage made the three leaning columns and inscribed them. The grooves are not writing for eyes. They are vector diagrams for gas. They catch the breath and fold it into three standing waves that cross in the shadow. The polish on the inward faces is not only the stroking of passersby. It is the consequence of a million pulses rounding the same turns. Who carved? Imagine a creature long, blunt-headed, covered in plates with tongues like combs and a belly cased in a high-melting glass. It drags itself along a face and rasps not to feed but to tune. It calculates across its whole body, shudder by tiny shudder, because numbers in this place are felt as lengthening or shortening exhalations. It cuts through seasons of hot—those five bands on the arch—in order to make a place where the faint current will stand up when called. It might be gone. If it is not, it is a legend that rarely comes down from deeper seams, from vents whose gas would blind anything that knew water.

Outside the chamber, the shingles. Each is a stony sail that knows only two states: open to the least wind and folded to keep their heat. They flange together like scales on a fish’s flank, and when the mountain sucks in and the lungs of the nave need quiet, they lay over the mouth and the world goes from absolutely dark to denser-than-dark. When the mountain exhales with more vigor, they gap and you can feel a cooler on your face that will not survive to be a breeze. Over years they grow outward; on the cliff their backs are scrolled with minute ridges that shed black dust—their winter’s growth flaking away every once in a while in blizzards small enough you would only know they fell if your skin itched by a whisper. Their children are not them until a long while after hatching. They leave behind husks shaped like raspberries in the parapet’s hollows. Those are not the shingles’ castoffs, though they look like that. Those are empty cradles of another lineage that borrowed a little of everything.

The parapet is a living wall, built from the slow orbit of bodies that refuse the center. The grazers, the combers, even a spindle in drought will travel the rim, teach their children the rim, until the rim is not a route but a habit that has exported so much excretion and shell that a handrail has formed. In its hollows the flyers—call them that, though they will never lift here—pack their eggs. The eggs are bubbled glass, lace of high-silica spun into thimbles that sabotage conduction just enough to keep a pocket of gas cooler than its surrounds. Larvae hatch into the densest pooling, into ponds of air that lie like varnish in depressions. They swim gas like fish do water, not with fins but with little lens-like plates that focus their own heat into microjets. They go in circles. That is not a metaphor. They must circle until the mountain’s breath keeps them in the middle, held the way a ball stays on a string when you whirl it. Between pulses they accrete mineral from the ion fog across their velvet bellies. They become heavier than their nursery; the plates stiffen. Eventually a child walks up out of thick air and cannot swim again. Some will never learn the climb to the mouth; some will, and when they do, the shingles outside recognize them by the way their skins spill a wavelength of chill. They are adopted. They inherit the cliff-face and the night.

Spoors of former minds remain. Glass threads pinned into stone with melted beads mark swinglines for something that moved from anchor to anchor, carrying what? Nets, perhaps, to catch mica-shivers as they fell. Nets to thicken the air in a corner for a season. Nets to baffle a seam and make it exhale more on every third breath, starve it every ninth. The ghosts of those nets are almost nothing. The anchors themselves have been repurposed by the sacks, by the shag, by anyone who needed a hard point around which to tighten. On the floor a scatter of white marbles still holds down an old thought. They are almost perfect spheres because perfection takes very long here; small flaws annealed out over uncounted coughs of the mountain as they were rolled and sat and rolled again under returning tides of grazers. Each marble is pitted on the side that faced the pulse because even perfects can be eaten if you sit in the same place with an attractive difference for too long. Who wove the net they held? Maybe ones like us. Maybe not. There are hands here, but they work in heat and slowness. They would have thought as easily in lines of polished groove as in words.

Their senses make a world we would fail inside of. Eyes are pointless. They wear their eyes out over their skins in countless wells, each one a microcup that knows when a current slackens a hair. Ears are surfaces that remember the last minute’s rhythm and feel when it falls out of exact. Tongues are combs that count missing ions. Noses are arrays of charged hairs that read the room like a graph. Their syntax is simple and vast: a body straightens, a neighbor turns, pressure drops by a whisper, a ripple passes, a hunt resets. They communicate by changing what is upstream for one another. A sack shifts phase; a shag moves a fraction along a groove; a column hums not for sound ears but for skins that can be sung into posture.

They keep time because time is kept for them. Fifteen pulses of the breath is as far as a crack stays fat with food. Six pulses is as long as a good glitter of dust hangs without losing its edge. Every third breath makes the shrine’s shadow kind. Every ninth starves it. A spindle knows how long to wait; a grazer knows how long its seam will treat it; a flyer’s egg knows how many exhalations to endure before thawing just enough to let the larva go. None of them have clocks inside, exactly. The mountain has a clock, and they are parts of it that feel close to the face.

You might want a mind to find among them. The closest thing is distributed and patient: the chorus of bookplates that opened and closed for so long in such an accidently synchronous way that they began to tune one another, the shag settling and resettling in chorus, the comb-tongued shaper carving and returning across eras to keep a small current turned inward where small bodies could not be swept away. Intention, here, is a tone that becomes difficult to tell from design when repeated enough times across rock and pulse. Perhaps there was a singular farmer—a creature that moved along the walls, a not-body with a desire—but even if it is gone, the farm holds. The smallest grazers multiply in the shrine’s hollow and are spared; the combers cull the lamed; the spindles take their certain tithe from the notch; the sacks harvest the pulses of the tide. Self and system braid.

They leave little empty. Even the space where a seam opens and the breath rushes through is occupied by bodies orienting themselves to become less resistance, to become more harvest. Sparks when their filaments brush are registered as notes in a score unreadable to light but legible to heat. Those sparks seed microweather and change who eats whom three pulses later. A child drifts and hardens and steps. A plate opens and closes to lichen something that is not lichen. A sack exudes and retracts. A sail outside sighs shut and the nave deepens into a hush so full the concept of silence becomes silly.

They live without complaint because complaint would be wasted and would read as a signal. They keep accounts in polish and thickness and the way a curve wears. They wear the mountain into a room and then live in it until the next equilibrium makes a new room beside this one. If you stood with a hand out, you would feel them pass like a polite crowd acknowledging you as a contour: a lump in the flow, a bit of shade, an obstruction that will not stay. If you waited long enough, they would use your hand as a scaffold. If you were a story among them, you would be told by the way heat bent around you and by what did not grow where you had been.

They are not lonely. They are numerous and exact. They occupy this hollow the way a word occupies the mouth: fully, then gone, then fully again, the same and not the same, made over and over by breath.
