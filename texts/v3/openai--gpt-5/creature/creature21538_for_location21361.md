What lives here is not one thing, but something like a braid pulled through fingers so often it keeps the memory of the grip. It wears bodies when it has to. Sometimes they are frogs, sometimes they are bats, sometimes they are a person you would have called a custodian if you had a better word for someone who hears the weather in stone. Sometimes it is only a rope shivering, a lip of clay answering a draught. It is easiest to see the human outline, because we share outlines, but even they are porous at the edges where the court’s breath moves through them.

The people who belong to this place do not look like founders. They look like outcomes. Salt has braided itself into their hair until it doesn’t glint so much as dull to the same driftwood tone as the wedges in the floodgates. The skin of their forearms is polished by years of handling wet stone; their thumbs know every rib by blind thought. They walk with the frog-pace you already noticed—slowed not by age but by consideration, each step a consultation with the residents perched in gloss-eyed lines along a jar’s mouth. If a frog startles, it is because a new hand has arrived, not because the keeper moved too fast. Old hands are a temperature the court understands.

They live adjacent rather than within. A slit off the main court gives to a cell where the air is less aggressive, where the benches, because less wet, hold warmth like old bread. Here there are two sleeping rolls and a third that is more a memory than bedding; sometimes there is one person here, sometimes two, and over years there will be a sequence of pairs that is itself a lineage. They do not bring iron. They carry a reed brush bound to a wood haft with gut; a gourd with a shaved lip for scooping; a roll of leather sewn in pockets with wax cakes and a blunt awl of bone; a little sheaf of tiles pre-drilled and sun-baked. Noise is their only loud instrument. When the day is thin and the frogs are lazy they beat the hanging plank in the tunnel with the flat of their hand until the court remembers itself.

You could tell their age by what they hear. The young ones tilt their head to the high whistles at the net-oculi, the flirt of the bright slits, and they grin private when a wall-pipe takes their calling note and smooths it down. The older ones listen to the belly-sounds: the jar chuck resettling as it fills, the under-roar that carries the sea’s pressure in it like a tonic. There is a middle age where you are all pitch, forever tapping the baked throats with knuckles and bone pegs and, if you are bold, your own voice, singing one held tone until the water beads attend to it and quicken. The great keepers—the ones old enough that fingers never tremble except in fog—hum in their sleep and wake with the right sheen already on their tongue.

They eat from this place gently. They lap the jars’ uppermeniscus when they must, scoop crusts of mineral from a boulder to lick for comfort when their bones talk, strike a match of flint and shell for a brief flame no brighter than a moth so they can think near it. They do not poach the throats of the mouths: frogs are customers more valuable alive than in a pot. If they take anything that has looked back at them, it is the little crabs when they mass, and even then it is with thanks and only the soft-shelled recent molts that will not survive the bat-shadows. Mostly the court pays them in breath. After a week inside it, their own lungs learn to shoulder and release in the same refrain as the pipes. They leave less and less breath elsewhere.

You can tell their craft by their hands the way you can tell a beekeeper by their patience. The nails are kept blunt not by cut but by wear across wet rock; the knuckles are healed from split times, white lines that narrow into callus hills. On the pad of the pointer finger, a small pearl of old wax that will never quite leave because they always pinch and smooth with that habit. The wrists show rope-bite not from hauling but from listening—wrists looped in net when they reach overhead and sway to feel if a section listens smoothly or if some strand is taking more than its share of dew and ought to be loosened. The ears are often packed with wool against the raw days, and on feast days they pull it out and sit on the bench niche and let the whole court write its text into their skull until they can’t keep still for the joy of it.

They do not so much fix as encourage. This is not work like repairing a fence. It is dowsing but sideways. They lift the leathers when the air is glutty and lay them when it stings too clean. They adjust the driftwood wedges that pin the heavy brine doors with the seriousness of priests, because if one wedge lies wrong and a storm pushes, all the jars sing a different despair afterwards. They keep the emptiness empty, sweeping the broad middle with the uneven-toothed rake and scolding the newcomer who lays down their pack there, because owning that unowned space is the first ignorance and the court makes no apology when it punishes ignorance with silence.

They raise their children here the way you might have raised yours in a church, except the doctrine is measurement. The first lesson is to feel the dewline on a rib with eyes closed. The second is to whistle until a pipe answers, so the child can feel call and response as something physical. Their games are balancing jars on toes and stepping the sounding stair without raising a bat, naming the eels and being wrong about which is which and being forgiven because the eels are only briefly named. They have a ritual of giving the frogs a day of fright: once a year they all clap, together, on a count only the elders can hear in the underwater of the jars, and the frogs take it as season and throw themselves into riot; after, the court rains so convincingly the elders have to stomp the steps to turn it off.

Do not think they own this place. That is another of the rules, pronounced soft. They are in service to a larger appetite that has learned to be courteous in being relentless. The court itself, if you were speaking as they do, is the animal here, slung low and broad, mouth full of blades and reeds, a body so patient you would make the mistake of calling it inert. It eats wind and excretes water. Its bones are already named in their language, the way a whale’s bones are named by the people who live with whales. This scalloped interior rib is the Breath-Ledge. That long clean plane is the Librarian. The great lump of limecrete in the middle that clubs the draught into tractable tongues is Old Back. They will, in joking, scratch Old Back as you would a dog and tell good days from bad by the sound the jars make when his shadow changes the room’s microweather.

If there is any godwork left in them it is only because cause and effect, practiced enough, acquires a personality—a reliable neighbor. They will say, if you ask them what lives here, that the rain has a nest and this is it. Or, if they are in a scholarly mood as only someone who has never seen a school can be scholarly, they will say that what lives here is a contract that learned to breathe. They do not speak of ghosts. They have no need. Any voice you might be tempted to call supernatural is made by a thin pipe and a certain ratio, and the pleasure is not drained by knowing that but deepened. The miracle here is repetition.

There are other lives here that are not the keepers and not the machine and not the individual animals by themselves but colonies that shrug on a sort of mind when they gather. The frogs, as a body, are a musician of surprising taste; the keepers have been corrected more than once by their harmonics. A thin, cloud-high day, with no heat and no promise, and the frogs will find a pitch three fingers down the palm of your hand and hold it until the pipe behind Old Back voices a fog note and the whole place moves a single degree toward rain. They are not trained in any way you or I would recognize, but they are invited by never being shooed. The bats, overnight, knit maps with their loops; if you were a mathematician instead of a thirsty person you could probably read barometric pressure in the shape of their paths.

The eels are the only things that do not admit of any bargain. They come when they come, long, unspeaking, and lay along a jar lip as if they had known clay longer than people have. They are given names by children and then they are forgotten by the names when they go back under and follow some brick-cold knowledge in the foundation that only eels get to have. If any sacred is kept, it is this: the eels are left alone. The keepers have a superstition they will not defend but do not break, that to trouble an eel is to unremember how to hear.

On the days when the mist comes through every slit and the court goes senseless and bright at once, the keepers crack their knuckles in time with the jar-bellies and move in a square, cleaning what you would not have seen as dirty—slime from a gutter seam, a feather plugging a pore—because in fog the whole world is turned up and anything could seed a failure. On the blank days of windless heat they sit on the far bench where the surface is most dully dry and they pluck little spiders out of the netting one by one and set them outside the door with a note of apology they do not bother to speak. They pack wax into the leathers then, mend the knots that have eaten their own ropes, tell each other stories that are more repairs than they are entertainment.

Those who once drank from these jars and do not come anymore exist as a rime on the cups on that high shelf. If you make the mistake of thinking this is all of the place’s memory, the keepers will show you with a flat palm the mineral writing down the backs of the limecrete boulders, a fluent script the court writes to itself every time a drop dries out of time with its neighbors. They read it the way others read goat shoulder blades pulled from a fire. Last year, here. The year when the door wedges swelled and took three men to move. The season of moths that came warm like bread from the slits. Once, the animal was sick: a hush for a whole fortnight where it should have rained from rope-under. They speak about it with the embarrassment you reserve for a body’s failures that are not the body’s fault. After, someone came who knew a different knot, and the illness did not return.

If they have enemies, they are simple. Salt is the obvious one; silence the subtler. Salt gnaws at leather, takes a set in the fibers you cannot easily soften again. Silence is worse; silence persuades every throat to go to sleep. The keepers have ways against both that are nothing more than discipline. The wedges are pulled and re-oiled with fish fat and tallow made from what the sea gives them that is not good to eat. The silence is beaten out of the walls when it gathers. The court, roused, answers immediately, because it wants to live in the only way a built thing can: by functioning.

And so if you must come to a single pronoun, you could say that what lives here is a it that behaves as a they. A we, if you are fortunate enough to be invited to stand in the center for a long time and feel your own breath add one ugly small layer to the chorus. It is the court as organism built of surfaces and agreements, and it is the keepers, who have traded their independence for a lifetime of listening, and it is the frogs whose massed glitter-throats keep the pitch true at dusk when no one else can reach up there. It is the rain that isn’t rain and the way sound, which should pass without trace, leaves wet fingerprints on stone.

At the quietest hour, when even the jars hesitate between chuck and echo, the thing that lives here is as naked as it ever is: a bare idea surrounded by bodies, an old problem solved so completely it can afford to sleep. A person sits with their back to the wall that does not drip, a bat has made a new crease in its fur with its tiny comb of a thumb, the eel remembers a river it never saw, and every rib in the stone is beaded to the proper line like a well-tuned instrument left on its stand. Outside there is the sea. Inside there is the conviction that you can convince air to let go of what it holds if you learn how to speak to it. That conviction lives here, and it has learned to keep itself wet.
