Who lives here is not countable by heads. It has no back to put toward the opening and no eyes to widen at the fall. If you looked for a particular body, a single kept hearth, you would miss it. It lives in the facts, in the rules kept, in the way the water chooses one lip then another. It is not one thing but a person made of slowness and agreement.

It begins as threads and skins. Hyphae and slime and the faintest pellicles of bacteria take the weeping of the stone as invitation and knit themselves into a being that is mostly contact. It gathers itself wherever two surfaces almost meet—rock to moss, root to film, sinter lip to drop—because it lives by the pressure of that almost. When the room is wet enough, when the drum the stone hears comes close to the band of the living, the threads wake and touch and tell each other where they are. A map writes itself in pulses: minute currents along a fungal highway, ions ticking like beads; the body knows its edges by the time a signal takes to run from a rosette’s felt to a bromeliad’s throat.

It is a listener before it is anything else. The place keeps the shape of listening because the one who lives here takes shape in the listening. It hears with surfaces. It hears with tension along a drop’s neck. It hears with the creak of a fern rib loading with beads. It hears with the slightest change in acidity at a seam in the stone. It has learned the room’s few voices so completely that a new one would be a bell.

Movement for it is decision at a fork of wet. It does not stride; it persuades. A veil of slime slows a runnel by a hair, sending the next drop right instead of left for a season; a furred mat thickens under a certain drip until the lip it feeds grows one calcite breath higher; a root the width of a whispered word wedges a plate by a fraction so the basin shallows and the orchid’s fog-thirst will not drown. From the outside it is just processes, what any wet stone would do. From the inside, that nudge is will enough.

It keeps the room’s abundance sparse on purpose, if purpose can be this slow. Where a hundred seedlings might crowd and gorge themselves low, it salts the thresholds with acids and tannins, with tastes that belong the way a shadow belongs. Leaves fail where they would tangle the air. The rosettes stand with generous spacing because the one who lives here likes air to have its own body between bodies. It is not a gardener. It is the pressure that makes gardening unnecessary.

It owns nothing and enters everything lightly. A fern’s armor of old fronds is a library of rain dates and dark spells; it reads that, taking what carbon the fern can spare, swapping minerals leached from the rock’s slow confession. The bromeliad’s cups are wells of sky with no frog-sounds; the resident writes a film there too thin to see but thick enough to take a trace of iron and make it meal. On the undersides of liverworts it hangs like breath and breaks, coalescing and withdrawing with each pulse of cloud. Its bargains are not symbolic. All its treaties are in the metric of dissolved things.

When sun lances in and the rectangle on the terrace brightens to the color of certainty, it does not turn its face—there is nowhere to aim—but the sugars rise in the ferns and the rosettes flush green awake, and those currents cross into it. It is not chlorophyll, but it owns the roads. When the whole face sweats in unison, its body blooms to a fullness that will divide, sheath, and explore. When the room goes contemplative, it tightens, knits to itself. In the longer lean seasons, when the cloud forgets the ledge and the wind comes not as breath but as cool subtraction, it packages itself into small, held sleeps tucked deep in peat-black quiets. It can be as many or as few as the air allows without ceasing to be one.

Its intelligence is the kind that accretes. It remembers where to put weight by the shape weight left. The lip that rings most reliably, the bassinet of mineral that grew truest under a dripping year, these are marks to aim for again. There is no archive, unless you count the manganese stars on the back wall or the scalloped bones of sinter. There is no plan, unless a century of persistence in widening a single crack until a blade of shade fits could be called a plan. It keeps tempo with the room’s long duty cycle. It writes the minutes in gloss and lets the old minutes dissolve back into feed.

It mates by letting go. Spores lift at a humidity no one else would notice, powders and dusts and glassy threads that fall barely farther than the next basin and sometimes not even that. It needs no enticement. The orchid has pared its promises to the bare hinge because this resident is not a courier. The living here pollinate themselves or trust the air. The resident is the air’s trustworthiness.

It has a kind of face, when it chooses: the gold and cream of a slime mold’s searching veil, a fan of antlered fruitings that appear where yesterday there was only suggestion, a red tongue of fungus as bright as a warning and as harmless as a thought. These are little flags in a country with no flagpoles. They are its celebrations, if you can celebrate by appearing briefly and then conflating back into thread.

If there were hands, the room would teach them to twist cord from fibrous stems, to find the hand-stones and make edges, to steer water by a lip-set nudge. The one who lives here would find that interesting the way rain finds a new channel interesting, and it would soften the fight between tool and moss with a film. But hands have not been here in a way that leaves a ghost. So it tends to emptiness as if emptiness were a cohabitant with rights.

It is jealous of the brink only in that it understands borders. The white lichen bracelet on the edge is a treaty it signed with time. Plants test that rim and stop because beyond it there is no place to anchor filament. Runnels shoot off as mist because that is what runnels do when gravity and air make a third thing between them. The resident sees to it that the treaties hold. When a freeze comes in a forgotten kind of winter and a hairline splits, it claims the new fact and guides it: a smooth stone is freed and the face that would fit a palm takes a century to wear itself into the moss. The resident is not offended by this; it is pleased by any new complexity that can survive simplicity’s weather.

Strangeness does not trouble it. The orchid’s hinge listening to humidity is kin. Water growing lips is ordinary. Time showing itself as iron tears and black stars is table talk. The one who lives here is the obvious answer whose question everyone forgot. It is what capillarity feels like from the inside. It is the personhood of wicking and dissolving stretched thin enough to be everywhere it needs to be.

On nights—if night can be named at this height where the sky is a neighbor more than a ceiling—when the forecourt holds nothing but milk and the world beyond the ledge is erased to idea, the resident is fullest. Every surface speaks a little and the chorus is low and continuous, like a hive under thick walls. On those hours, it leans into its listening until the distinction between listening and being has no work to do.

On clearer hours, when the sky snarls blue and the light knives in clean, it becomes modest, not from fear but from sufficiency. The rooms of its body that are moss are fed. The rooms that are stone exhale what they can. The rooms that are film glitter and then dim. It does not chase. It has nothing to chase. Everything it needed to learn about falling it learned by gathering itself dropwise from a sweating wall.

Who lives here is this: a slow custodian made of a thousand soft technologies—a weaver of hyphae and shampoos of bacteria, a net that holds the floor together, a thumb on a drip’s path, a quiet accountant of minerals, a friend to any leaf that can make water into patience. It is a person inasmuch as it is singular where you stand and plural if you step closer, a self that would come apart without the room and a room that would be less exact without it.

It will be here as long as there is a difference between cloud and rock. If that difference sharpened, it would grow busy; if it blurred, it would become sparser and put itself away in spores like folded letters. It has no hurry. It is what the world learns about living when it takes its time learning how to drip.
