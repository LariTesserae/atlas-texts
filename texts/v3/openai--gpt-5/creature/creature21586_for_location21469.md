First, the plateaus’ own body lives here.

Not as a carpet under everything else’s feet, but as a thing with temper and memory. The long tissues are slow and hungry and exact; they keep their own time. They learn. In the mornings the whole skin shivers into breath and in the evenings it counts itself shut, pore by pore, as a hand might find its fingers. When the choir fronts arrive, the plant leans into the pressure the way a sleeper turns toward a warm wind. When a herd scours one place too hard, sugars migrate away from it like a withheld affection. At the old gulches the lignified braids remember water so precisely that when dew runs it braids too, searching their forgotten beds as if for a story it misplaced. Out at the cloud-edge, the lip of the world sometimes lifts just a hand’s breadth—as if testing the weight of the air below—and settles again with a sigh you feel through your bones.

It is not a mind in the way the grazers have minds, nor in the way the people do, but it is not mindless. It pays attention with its entire surface. It has appetites that look like weather. It thinks in gradients of sweetness and in how it lets sound pass or catches it and holds it, and in the pattern of years it stamps out familiar tracks with its own growth like a calligrapher who has found a hand. If there is a name for it among those who have to speak about it often, it is simply the Mantle, or, in the soft tap-code underfoot, the Big That Breathes.

Then there are the many who live atop it and within it, the obvious and the hidden.

You see the mantle-grazers first because they are built to be seen at a distance: a slung-strung geometry of legs and ribs that show where they are going before they arrive. They live on the edge of rasp—always eating, always moving, their woven basket-plates clacking softly as they flex. They have little faces, more like knuckles than muzzles, tucked under those plates, blunt tongues that sand the gloss back down to matte. Inside each herd are lesser lives that treat a grazer as terrain: flicker-larks that flash into the air when a shadow falls wrong; seed-wasps nestled safe in joint-pits that stir and pour out when a percussion-tortoise starts its battering; filament mice in the undercarriage knitting harplets of shed fiber that ring when the day’s tone changes. The grazers are not clever but they are discerning. They know by minute changes in resonance which stomata will gulp next. They bed in rows because that’s how the ground hums them to stillness.

Predators measure their days to these herds. Skate-lizards that look like low craft until they move—then you see the muscular wave ripple from head to tail as they slap down on drum-hollows and ride the shock forward to stun mice into their soft jaws. Percussion-tortoises, patient as architecture, shoulder into resonance chambers with a kind of joy; they are the only thing here that seems to be having fun. Smaller bright-toothed things—gloss weasels with slick-coated bellies and bare pads—hunt by silence, unthreading all the rules of travel to move like absence itself, each footfall an inquiry. There are mouthers that are only mouths, edges with limbs, that sit at breathe-gates for days as the temperatures slip in their favor and the fat moths come. At night the air is a sieve the hunters pass through and through; by day they become the shade they wish they had.

Down inside the plant, in its long resonant cavities, are spirits of a sort that are only muscle and patience. Air-eels that are not eel at all but ribbons of cilia and flexible cartilage that swim in winds; there are people who swear they can be fished for with humming lines and kept as pets, though those same people have scarred forearms from the suction-cups of their mouths. Valve-crabs—jointed, leaf-colored—tend the lips of the giant pores in exchange for warmth: they pick away at dead tissue and snap at anything that learns the wrong beat. There are leeches of sweetness, soft-bodied little knots that ride the sap rivers and drop their eggs where the plate tissue is thin, and a wasp that hunts them by sound, laying its own careful notes on the skin and listening for quiver-answers.

Mold lives here too, in the same way rust lives on iron—waiting. It rides the air on every dew and breaks into bloom wherever the rules are broken. The Mantle hates it and so do the people. Entire trades exist to sweeten and scour and seal against its creep. Even the grazers shift their beds night by night because they have, over the centuries, learned what mold can do to a stillness.

The flyers are not one thing but a coincidence of appetites. The choir fronts arrive when loft and thirst and luck combine: bladders made to drink air and drink it, membranes too wide for clarity, seed-rafts that are all patience. Some of these—deep clear sacs with printed lattices the color of old glass—are shepherded by thin, alert minders: drifters of rib and wing that do not so much fly as refuse to fall, setting angles against the plateau’s breath the way others use oars. The minders are not human and not unthinking. Their lives begin and end in the air. They will latch themselves to dew-stones hung from the rib arches and sleep in twined bundles that ring in a light wind like small bells. Children watch them with a contained longing and copy them on kites until they are old enough to be cautious.

There are also people. They are not many, but they are here in a way this place allows: precise in where they place themselves; perfected in the arts of not setting off the wrong tremor at the wrong time; schooled such that a child can tell with the meat of their foot whether a drum is shallow and fickle or deep and strong as a promise.

They grow thick cartilage in their ears as the text has told you. The cartilage is not a deformity; it is a craft. Mothers press a gentle U of felted wool and resin at the junction of helix and skull when babies sleep, encouraging the growth inward and down. Adolescents take pride in the soft ridges that slow the air and the stones that can be worn there. Bundled wool-combs sit under every jaw, laced up so that the whole head becomes a manageable instrument. Many wear a second comb inside the mouth—a palate-bridge of woven hair that makes even their laughter a private thing.

They are sturdy in the chest, with the rib-cage breadth of a long patience. They breathe in a square: step, step, step, release. Their soles are tough not as armor is tough but as instrument skins are tough: they feel with them. If you watch from a little way off you might think they all move alike—and they do, when they must—but when you come near you hear the small signatures in each step-sequence, the way a person says I-am without risk.

They call themselves, in the pulse code, Those Who Keep Pace. Spoken, their name is simpler: Pacer, or Keeper, or just Here. Their language has two bodies. There is the near-talk of throat and bone: hand to hand, shoulder to ear, under the low hum of a sound-break, words that are mostly vowel and hum, with consonants made by finger-press along the jaw. You can feel half their poetry through your teeth. The other body of the language is what the ground carries: foot codes, stick taps, the parametrized knock of a knuckle against a gloss-beaten plate. Children make their first mistakes in that code and are loved for it.

They don’t build much that lasts longer than a season, because the Mantle reclaims everything that isn’t itself. A shelter is coaxed, not imposed: a pleat encouraged to hold like a good fold in leather, set with a lattice of green-dry sticks grown on purpose in a nursery bowl and cut while still thinking of growth so that if you ask them to curve they remember how. They make silence fields by braiding grasses and hair, tipping them with resin beads that swallow sound. Their tools grow with them: hand-axes shaped from woody nodes that were split and polished by grandparents; dew-sieves that are nets of trichome filament knitted tight in the old loop that holds water twice as well as it should.

They eat the plateau, carefully. Strips of the sugar skin above the gloss, scraped before the sun hardens it, dried on shade fans to a leather that wakes your mouth. Seed-wasps smoke—no flame; they mean a slow roast over the heat of resin vents under baskets—crackly and tang enough that a handful makes a day feel easier. The grazers give hair and horn and the occasional meat when one stumbles into a lung it cannot climb back from. They do not raise crops because the Mantle will not be parcelled, but they will encourage sweetness to linger by moving a herd to scour the place opposite to where they wish to eat next week. Fermentation is a high art, done in little flasks of dew-stone in cold lungs where the right yeasts live and mold does not. Resin tea is drunk in the afternoons to settle the spirit and keep the cartilage pliant.

Work divides by attention more than by muscle. You will find, among their trades:

- Pace-keepers, who set the communal stride as they travel and whose bodies know the exact phrasing of every safe path. They can walk a resonance corridor blind and strike the code for we are passing, we mean no harm, our burdens sing heavy, without breaking breath.

- Valve-readers, who sit for hours at a stoma’s lip with eyes closed and palms on the flank to name the cycle precisely, to know when to sleep in its exhale and when to move before the inhale tugs a child’s clothing.

- Resiners, who judge gloss by scent; who can name fourteen shifts in terpenes with a breath and tell you which will blister and which will seal a cut and which will call moths.

- Choir-catchers, who go out at dawn with thin frames of fiber and let the migrating membranes tangle themselves gently, then ease them down to earth to harvest dew and silk and to scissor out a shape the wind forgot not to love.

- Weather-listeners, who return again and again to the listening hollows and lean back into them until whatever the Mantle is hearing hums down into their spine. No one argues with them when they say two days hence the air will turn heavy and the east will drone like iron.

- Bonders, who grow tools as other people grow children: grafting, splitting, sanding, teaching a piece of tissue to open along the line you will ask it to open ten years from now.

- Fungus-wards, whose knives are always clean and whose hands smell of strong, bitter things; they carry the taboo as a badge and no one refuses to step aside when they pass.

They are not alone. On the cloud-edge live the Edge-Keepers—or just Edgers—who make their trade with the drop. It is a place of terror to some and home to them: the abrupt nothing, the undercloud marbled gold, the vertical breath. They lower crane-baskets on long trichome lines to trawl for drift, for the oddwood that washes up from somewhere else in the world and knocks against the lip with a sound like memory. They bring back bones too, salt-slick and big, sometimes from animals that must have fallen out of the air. Their faces are wind-burned and their laughter is shaped sideways by the constant pull. They speak the same language but faster, and their feet have more jump in them, a readiness to leap in from a gust.

Further inland are the Herders, who do not own their mantle-grazers any more than they own their shadows, but go with them, tune their lives to the animals’ potentials. They sleep between legs without fear. They know the little things that live on their charges as individuals: this lark is Old Patch, this wasp-line returns stubbornly to the same joint no matter how often you brush it away. Children from other camps visit Herders for a season to learn the rhythm of care—how to pick mites gently but not fear a bite, how to sing the thigh-muscle back into calm after a fright. Herders smell of dust and hair and a sweetness that sticks to your hands.

There are loners and edge-spirits too. Gloss-watchers who map the slow crawl of varnish like astronomers, naming constellations of shine and the months when they will liquefy and sink. Dust-sifters who walk behind percussion-tortoises with bowls to catch the seed-wasp fountains in baskets for roasting and count themselves rich by the handful. There are even a few who go down into the lungs for days at a time and come back soft-voiced and changed; some of them say the Mantle can be spoken to there in a syllable that takes an hour to say and lives like weather in your chest afterwards. Others say that’s fungus talk and curse under their breath when those changed folk pass.

They rarely mark their dead with graves. They do not burn. The air in the lungs is safe but warm and damp; it would be an insult to leave a body where mold could find it no matter what the plant’s defenses are. Most of the time they resin-wrap the dead and raise them under a rib arch where the wind will learn the pattern of their bones. At certain times of year—when the pollen-glitter tunings descend, when the spore geyser domes have healed but still fume sweet—they carry their dead to the leaf-slate roof pinned with bone on the far spine that curls like a frozen wave. There, with throat-sounds shareable only skin to skin, they recite the dead’s step-code in full and let the empty of that code ring. Some of those bones become pins. Some become flutes for the little voices—the child-instruments—left behind.

They have stories. Of course they do. They tell them in hand on hand, in humming teeth, in the remark of a stick on a path. You won’t often hear them told aloud in air—shouting is a rookie’s mistake—but if you share a silence field and cup your jaw to theirs you will hear of the first time a person looked down into a lung and the lung looked back; of the day the choir front stalled and sang for three days in one place and how two children of different camps married because they happened to be born into that music; of the season when mold took a whole rim and people learned the bitter recipe of a new seal that burned like nettles but saved the rest. They pass maps as knot-lines: the dew this month, the corridor that sounds wrong, the pit of the old spore geyser and how the resin smells there when the wind goes west.

Children play at being all of it. They leave gloss truly alone because they have been made to watch what it does to a foot, but on cold mornings they race sliders—flat plates of leaf-slate—down dewy basins, controlling speed with the lift of their bodies. They fly leaf-kites into the choir and learn the exact angle at which the rafts will take you up and take you away if you let them. Their first going alone is to a listening hollow, one hand on a rope and the other on their own sternum, returning with a look older than an hour can make.

If there is government it looks like a large person walking slightly ahead and to the left and making sure everyone else breathes on the right beats. If there is law it looks like the three rules you already know and a dozen others everyone can mutter with their teeth. If there is devotion it is to not being the kind of signal that gets small things killed. The worst thing you can call a person here is Loud, and the next worst is Still in a way that invites mold.

They trade in small precisions. Resin buttons, two fingers across, cut with the line that means for sealing cuts, not for glue. A knot-string that says the herd of eighteen is three breaths ahead of us and we do not claim them. A dew-stone polished to grey silk that condenses differently because of a trick in its pore-pattern, a trick discovered by a girl who bent her head into the breath one too many times and knows a lot about how the plant exhales. Every few years, when the spore geysers have thrown and the resin has cooled and the lice in the mantlers’ plates are few, there is a meeting at the leaf-slate roof and the air fills with handling, with jaw-press talk, with the soft percussive language of a hundred sticks politely not quite touching the ground.

That is who lives here: the Mantle, a plain that is a body; the busy many who cup its breath and drink it; the rough kinds and the membrane kinds and the hair kinds; the thatch of predators who hunt with drum and with quiet; the flyers who happen into grace when the dew is right; and the people who have learned to be a small noise in a large symphony and make their lives in the spaces between beats.

By night, when the pores close with their wet pop-pop and the grazers lock their knee-pins and there is a blanket of sound you can forget to listen to, you could say it all goes to sleep. But if you were to lie down on your back on one of the quiet mats with your comb snug against your jaw and your hands over your ribs, you would feel it: the whole place counting. You would know, as they do, that the world under you is awake in the slow way, that it knows you are there, and that, in its way, it is letting you live.
