What lives here chooses the margins and learns to be timed. It does not own warmth except in rationed sips. It uses the schedule the way canopy insects use wind: as the big fact that moves everything else.

The water lives here first. Not the water itself, but its little retinues: the films that appear anywhere a drip lingers, sheens that bite into metal as neatly as a thousand teeth. You only see them because in the smear of a lamp they show as oil rainbows, or because your fingertip, cold and smooth on the run, comes away with a nicotine-tan gloss. They eat very little—skin from your breath, organics the filters don’t catch, the sugar in a careless spill from someone’s ration drink—and they turn it all into a froth at scales finer than dust. You see the froth where it rims rivets, where a panel bolt has warmed and cooled exactly so for days. You could harvest it with a razor edge. The thought comes to you because things do harvest it here; you just don’t yet have a category for what those are.

Next comes the kind of growth that looks like it should have a smell and doesn’t. Puffers on motor flanges, the whale-fat fungi the administration tolerates because to strip them is to admit heat loss and so to lose an argument with a budget. They cheerfully occupy cages meant to keep hands back from moving parts and clap their spore into the drafts in the half hour after the north fans change direction. Nothing eats them that you’ve ever seen, unless you count the gray moth larvae that will find a warm tin box they can crawl into and hatch there among relays, nipping everything soft and starch-bound, then clumsily blunder their way toward any beacon and end in a wire net choked with lint.

Wire nets exist here even where no hands have tied them. Wire spiders is what a friend calls them, with the seriousness people use when they mean to make a name stick. He means the hunched complexes of fracture lines where metal fatigue and a vibrating seam spin tight; the little concentric snares that static scrawls between handrails. They hum; they sift. The nests catch lint like snowdrifts on a fence, and with the lint come the dull moths, the skin of packaging, the flake of peeling spray-cure, fines of AntiCrust the drones have scrubbed and something has shaved back to life. Webs and moths and lint together make an ecology that weighs grams and extends for blocks. A hand through it is a snowplow through a valley, exhilarating for you and devastating for every small plan that had room there.

A lot of the residents don’t read to the eye. They live in the words you start to use without thinking: bloom, scale, slime, bleed. They turn hydrophilic to hydrotaxis. They show up as maps in salt when the brine breath comes, a blush on the struts like a rash that then pulls back to the seams where the bots can clean it. The bots are a fauna all their own: small, deliberate, soft-footed not to harm the paint. Beetle-bellied scrubbers that patrol the same banded rails every two hours, squeegee quadrupeds that nose water into mouths with a tenderness you would recognize as care if care here had hands. Most of them don’t see, in the way that a sun-thing sees; they listen to the carrier tone in the nearest duct and the status beacons blink on their hides like pilot fish. It is hard not to watch them the way you would watch animals: grazing where slime fattens, clustering and peeling away, freezing all together when the emergency tone drops out and then starting again to comb the floor once the blowdown groan ceases and the fog lifts.

You learn to stop saying there are no animals. The ones that are obviously alive have learned the same lesson about timing that you are learning. The white eel that haunts a sump lip will sit a day into the week tide because the Patience Plant’s junk, copper and promise, guarantees a little rain of edible at four o’clock in the gray. The eel’s body takes on the same gloss as fresh weld because the same light falls on both and because the eel has polished itself against concrete with a faithfulness machines would envy. The blind fish with the metal filings in its belly is a rumor until it isn’t, a pale dart in a lamplit gully one low hour when you can’t tell whether your breath shows because of temperature or fear. You mean to come back with a net and don’t. Somewhere someone else keeps a jar with a thing like that in it and a list of dates on a tag.

As for the vent-swallows: those, if they are real, live on the schedule entirely. They are tuned to reversals and pressure crests so that to you they are a mistake in your eye, a comma of motion above a louver, and for them your headlamp is the edge of a break in an everlasting day. If they have nests, they are where your body won’t fit; if they eat, what they pursue rides the same drafts that carry their bones. You do not get a feather in your hand unless one is tarred down where a grating has got it. You keep it not because it proves anything to anyone else but because when you bring it out in your lodging, needed fingers thawing at the warming booth, it proves you are tuned to this place enough that a life which would prefer not to be watched has let you.

There are other small citizens not so mythic. Springtails solder themselves to puddle rims and skip like thrown stones between droplets where oil has laid a skin, each jump a test of whether this is water yet or still the thought of water. Booklice nibble labels off conduits in the warm bays because glue is food and because in the hum behind a panel you can raise eggs as if every day were summer. Rotifers, tiny mouth-crowned gears, find the weeks of fog and then go back to crystalline seeds of themselves when a purge sucks their world dry. From a municipal koi pond someone released a population of amphipods into the drainage grid years ago, and now their translucent commas are in a dozen sumps, in thousands on the right nights, grazing the bacterial froth like deer on a winter lawn, nothing of them left after a hard blowdown but the idea that next purge cycle you will see them again. There is protein here if you know how to watch for the hours when it coheres at the mesh.

People live here, though the place is too large for anyone to own more of it than a path and a room temperature. Officially they rotate: eight hours on, a service train back to day, a bunk with a window and a plant that droops because your place upstairs is always cold now and you are not there to water it. Unofficially, some do not go back. They become names no one above speaks much because above you are always warm and that is a different nationality.

Those who stay learn to see with their ears. They carry no music because the system supplies it, an oratorio that is also a bus schedule and a hazard placard, and to put a speaker to it is to stick fingers in its mouth. They have spoons—long, old, bent-beautiful—that they stick against pipes to hear what the steel is saying. A gulfed silence will drive them to move faster than any light could. They call places by the note they remember there: the Blue Throat (a choke that hums at 440), the Cathedral’s B (a purge that could shake out of you something like a prayer if you were wired for prayers), the Scavenger’s C where the grinders take ducting down thirty hertz at a time.

They sleep above sumps because noise is a kind of blanket and because mist is a kind of privacy. They cache food in the red warming booths because there is heat there you can pretend is yours; they change out of their wet things and clip their socks to cup hooks and hold their stiff fingers over the louvers that throw a sting of hot out into the boooth for eight allotted minutes. When you share such a booth with a stranger you make room and do not look long; you both hold the paper cups in the same way as if there is a school that trained you to.

Their pockets have useful saints: earplugs, a heat-lance tip scored for drawing new driplines, a razor to harvest bloom if ever the stores forget you, wire and ties, a pack of hazard tape for making a place a room no one else will see as a room until you pull it down. They are superstitious in proportion to the system’s predictability. They will not whistle. They will not say quiet. They do not eat in the path of a blowdown even if the map says no purge is scheduled, because there is also the accident tide and it has the right-of-way.

There are crafts here that have never had a guild but should. The Wickers draw new rain for the mouths that need it, stinging filaments into paint so condensate suddenly remembers a different route and no longer makes a hazard of a stair. The Mossers tend the black galvanic turf like farmers, because an engineered plant is still a plant; they know how thick is thick enough and when to send the squeegee-ungulates out to crop it back to its task. The Salt Shepherds show up when the brine breath breaks—in this cycle it is always between the upfan and the downstream purge, two hours when your lips taste of the sea and everyone’s hair goes to wire—and they ride the service flats along the run, trading racks in and out, laughing because this is the only time of the week the underworld has a taste. The Gasket Witches have a hundred o-rings on chains to hand out as if they were blessings, and they stick their faces up into the seam of a roar and tell you by smell if you should pull the power or persuade the seam to be patient. In a pinch they will bite a strip of their own coat for a stem-wedge and call it good.

They write themselves into the place. The names in marker-ink on beams are not graffiti, they are cautions and continuities: this bolt will seize in a wet week; this valve takes three turns more than a sane person would give; shake the chain twice before you take it or you will lose a knuckle to the rebound the first time the grinders bite. They draw fish on the catwalk above the sump where the blind thing was seen and then cross the fish on the week when it is trapped and jarred and released in a body of water called, by some bravery of naming, Lake.

There is a shadow population that is folklore even to the people who never go up. Men with coughs who sleep by the fans because dry air is kinder to their lungs; a woman whose job is above but who cannot stand sun and so does all her paperwork by the light of the conduit glow, the kind of high power you can feel through your molars. The Node, an old person who knows the system by its maintenance logs and so can answer any question if you will sit through the part where they complain about fonts. The Lineage, which is not a family but looks like one: three and then four and then five who have chosen not to go up for reasons as complicated as anyone’s reasons, who have made a room inside a retired cistern and laid an insulated floor across the cold, who trade work hours for a territory the size of a modest apartment and tend their own black moss like a lawn. They have a cat for a week or two each time the pigeons breed too rashly; then the cat goes back up or disappears.

Above them, everywhere, the machine-life runs. Not metaphoric life, not the way you would say a city lives. These are beings with their own durations, mating cycles of firmware pushed at three in the morning, predatory hook-lifts that drag duct-carcasses to grinders whose teeth are the only carnivore music you get. Conveyor rivers run endlessly in one direction and then reverse for maintenance and everyone who has counted on them being always-rivers makes a curse in their throat that is a hometown word in a place where speech otherwise drifts to grunt and sign. Louvers open and close like gills; the plenum inhales and you can feel the whole room tense; it exhales and all the knitted webs snap a little and re-sew. Floodgates flex and you could put a palm on one and feel in your bone what a city weighs. The trains come like thunderheads, all light and siren and the static that lifts your hair, and you learn to press yourself into such precise pockets of shadow you could outline them on the wall next time there is inspection tape and write, for some stranger who has not learned the local, Here, here is safety; here you can paste yourself and be passed by.

Predation is patient, here. There is not enough spare energy for the brutal chase. Things wait at the edges of their streams and put out a tongue when the flow changes. A child once left bread on a grating and came back later to see what it had brought, and in the thin snow fallen from a shocked condenser there were tracks like parentheses, a punctuation mark of something that rides the wet but will not cross dry. That child is grown now and will tell you plainly that there are no monsters, just opportunists with better calendars.

Weirdness starts to look like good planning. The condensation ladder under a dataspan rains in a neat ring into an aluminum mouth, and a black bark shrinks the gum of a spill clean off a guardrail in an afternoon. A valve will frost itself into bloom and you will find a little drift of white where it stood, crushed diamonds that were only air and cold’s eagerness to make pattern. If you are in the habit of putting coins on rails in any geography, you will discover that here the nearest equivalent is stacking washers on the ledge behind a transformer until the bass of the room has shaken them into numerology: three for luck for a purge; four for a safe silence when you cross the drain valley to the under-parking deck. Math accrues in secret corners like stalactites in tourist caves; the only difference is that the people who tally it here hold their sums in their skulls and that is an admirable intimacy.

It would delight you, if delight is in your nature, to see how many of the lives here use light as a taste and not a touch. Conduits glow not in colors for you but in voltages for them. Indicator constellations below a catwalk are a sky for moths who will never see any other; an LED is a moon and blinks on a schedule that becomes the calendar of those who never go above to watch the streetlights give up and dawn begin. Once, during an emergency that ate visibility to a wrist’s length, the only yardstick anyone had was the fading of beacons, and in the gray a swallow hit a hot light and flared and nobody spoke for a minute about anything because all speech would have had to include how beautiful and pitiless the whole thing was in exactly the same degree.

If you have a sensation here that you cannot shake, it is that territory is less important than time slot. Everything heavy finds a track and everything wet finds a channel; everything living finds a window and stakes it. The eel owns a cut in the sluice’s shadow for the week tide. The swallows, if they are true, own the seconds at either edge of the fan reversal and the length of the corridor that is a pressure wave’s throat. The Wickers own a minute after a purge when the filaments they lay will set without blowing dry and cracking. The Mossers own a four-hour cool when the black turf can thicken. The long sleeps of rotifers own the dry months between leaks. The children of the Lineage own the ten minutes after the service flats pass when the pigeons settle again and can be lifted easily by hand and set higher so they do not foul the warm conduits the cat cannot be spared to guard right now.

And, not least, the schedule itself lives. It blooms and withers, shortens a segment here and lengthens another, changes when an earthquake takes the slant out of a terrace and a thousand liters per minute decide to learn a song in a new key. People court it, argue with it, carry copies of it smeared with thumb and note and grease. Drones obey it like wolves obey draw and scent, faithful and not imaginative. The rest of everything finds the place where it intersects with schedule and stands there like a plant with its share of sun. Things that do not comply are corrected by a polite animal with felted feet and a telescoping squeegee, and the correction is as gentle as any toothless bite.

If you are tempted to believe there is nothing beautiful about such citizenship, spend enough shifts that the pulses imprint on your chest and then stand, lamp off, in a pocket behind a transformer housing where your piece of the noise is only breathing. Let the milk-glass fog show you the exhale of louvers as planes of light. Listen until you can name the creatures here by sound alone: the belt whisperers, the relay chatterlings, the chain ratchets that comb through duct carcasses with patient teeth; the white eel’s flap and stop; the swallow comma you heard or invented; the patient feet of a small machine persuading a drop to go down the channel it should. In that stillness you will know you are among lives that are opportunistic and spare and enduring, and that every day starts crisp and ends with a new dripline someone—or something—will trace, a new grit in a trap, a new gray feather glued to mesh. Above you there is weather, scented and bright. Here there is schedule and pulse and whatever, clever and stubborn, evolves at the seam where those meet. That is what lives here. That is who.
