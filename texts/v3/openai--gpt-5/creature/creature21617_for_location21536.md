What lives here keeps quiet bodies and long habits. It is all sparse and deliberate, an alphabet with only a handful of letters written over and over until each stroke becomes a way of being.

First, the films. They are the truest citizens. Where the stone lips sweat, a black-and-oil iridescence takes hold, almost as if someone pressed a thumb of shadow onto the rock and left it as a seal. Call them skins, or laces, or the mats, though none of those words admit how finely they are knotted. Up close, their substance is threads laid on threads, each strand no thicker than a grain’s edge, braided along the standing waves that tremble over the stone. They do not grow anywhere there is water; they grow where water is becoming water—on the turn of it, in the cool heartbeat of condensation where the air is forced to make and unmake itself.

They eat the tiny regularities the sound makes, the way a wind-harp makes a constant order from a constant pass of air. They trap dust and the charged flecks of broken salt. They hold iron as it changes back and forth from one state to another under the soft battery of the place, and with those small swings they pull a living current through their own bodies. When the hum pushes, their filaments bow; when it eases, they spring. Each flex turns into a trickle of ions across a membrane that isn’t quite a membrane, just a stack of plates and strands that would look like fungus to a hurried eye but has more in common with the crystal they cling to. They lay themselves along the safest lines in the field—at nodes and on the shoulders of nodes—and in doing so they become little instruments inside the instrument, baffles and coils that thicken to a felt where the tone will let them.

They breathe without bubbles and yet release them, the way lichens breathe without leaves. If you attend, you will see their gossamer anatomy give up pinhead globes that nudge off the lip and are gone: a sign of waste being made gentle. They smell faintly of hot stone and something like pennies when the sun is highest. They make no noise you can hear within the choir that shelters them, but they change it if they mass too far; the pitch will flare and an overtone will come in at the edge of perception. When that happens, grazers arrive as if they have been called by a tuning fork.

Those are the glass-backed ones, the small and precise. Thumb-long at most. Pale as unglazed porcelain across the belly, their carapace is clear or nearly so on top, a single shallow dome you could mistake for a bit of bottle buried in grit until it moves. The dome is not glass, though it takes a shine like glass; it is fused from what the wind brings them. They eat dust and salt and lay it into plates inside a living resin, then burnish those plates against the lips of the mouths until they make a window that fits their back. Through it, you can see a little of what they are doing: a thicket of soft organs pressed low; a slow pump; a narrow bladder like a reed; pebbles of borrowed grit lodged at their flanks as if wishing to become ribs.

They climb with deliberate accuracy. Each toe is long and ends in a fan of invisible hooks that find what the films cannot abide: the submicroscopic rough of the hard stone. They will not place a foot where the skin is thickest; they skirt it with the courtesy of desert people around a well. At the lip, they wait for the right part of the cycle—you can watch them counting with their bodies—and then lay that tongue, a sliver, down across the cool. It is not exactly a lick. The tongue is a wick. It thickens at its tip, and tiny pores there drink water as if drawing it along paper fibers. They hold themselves steady as the place thrums and take in a ration that never changes: a number of drops per hour in a number of hours per day. If two meet by chance at a quiet node, they lean the glass of their backs together and stay, heads almost touching the other’s underside. In that small hush, their pumping changes cadence. Whether it is speech or simply a way to tell their inner meters, no one can say. They leave no burrow, only a silvered smudge on stone where they waited in shade too long.

Once in a while, much rarer, you will see the longer animal. Two handspans, soft as wet clay to look at, though it is not wet. Its shape suggests a rolled length of intestine that has thought better of its job and decided to cross the floor. Legs, yes—eight, maybe ten, depending on the age—but they are not jointed in the insect way; they are thick tendrils that lay down and pick up in waves, so that the body seems to pour, not step. Under it, a brush of setae that feel, taste, and condense at once; above, a smooth skin that looks plain until dusk, when the soft spray of charge along its length traces faint maps you could mistake for veins.

It carries a coast inside it. Inside, a sack of viscous gel seeded with grains from the central flats. When the animal moves, those grains roll and slide and never arrive anywhere, a perpetuity of tiny avalanches that drive slow currents in the gel. Those currents are coupled to its sides by a net of piezo threads—quartz-ribbed fibers not grown from it but grown with it, as if its body were both host and mold. The push and pull make electricity the way the place does, and the electricity runs things the way a low windmill will turn a mill all day if you let it. With that trickle, it keeps its body’s chemistry to a set of narrow lines and can live on almost nothing. It eats the mats where they overreach, and the scalloped salt where it grows fat and brilliant, and the young of its own kind when the year has been too generous in whatever way generosity measures itself here.

It never hurries. It has learned there is no finishing-line in the air around it. It knows the lips that condense best and the ribs that hold shade like a duty, and it goes between them in a route you could learn, too, with patience. When it arrives at a sump that is tuned to its liking, it lowers a ring of its body to meet the mouth and closes the meeting with a fit that must be taught to the young by skin. Then it pumps—not hard, just a palmful faster than the background—and what is made below, it stores. When it is sated, it goes, and nothing it has touched is different to anyone who does not know the place down to its whiskers.

There are winged things, but they are not a folk. They come like conjunctions. Colorless, nearly; when they flash you get a stroke of silver and then your eyes find only the heat. Thin wings that you might not see but for the edge they give to sun. They come up where a cluster of throats makes a chimney and the air lifts clean. They hold there as a seed will sometimes hold in the eye of a whirl and point their narrow faces into the trickle on the rock. They drink—with a mouth that is a needle; it plants, pierces, and lifts again—and then they leave by falling sideways until the wind catches them like dandruff. If they lay, they lay single eggs on a line of quartz. The egg is varnished with whatever passes for resin here, and it charges itself with the place’s slow stir of electrons so that it will not blow until the week it must. When it hatches, what comes out is closer to a loop of hair than an insect: a near-weightless larva that crawls toward a dark, takes one drink that makes it many times its size for an hour, then stills and molts and hardens into a harder line that you will never see unless you turn the crystal to the right light and squint.

There are others that you will not see even if you turn stones for a week. In the walls, the endoliths: organisms that live in the walls in the strictest sense. Not moss, not algae, not any firm thing with a name you know. Little stacks and strings of chemistry that choose to hide in microcavities in the basalt and the quartz and the iron’s skin, and accept the long, slow press and bend of the planet and the hum as their weather. They lend themselves to the rock and the rock lends them its lattice. When the tone flexes them across a slide, they slip an ion this way, another that; when the dusk throws a net of little sparks across a vein, they take two electrons that were going nowhere and make them go on a tiny errand inside their bodies. They do not swell the stone or stain it green. They number themselves in thin percentages per gram. If you cut the place out and weighed all of them, they would not tip any scale a person uses. They are more patience than mass.

Under the lifted pages of rock in the muttering shelves, in the Laminites, there is a guild of fellows to the glass-backed ones who prefer roof to lip. They are narrow animals, almost ribbon-shaped, and their backplates are not clear but amber and clouded, laid down shinier than the page above them so they can slide without sticking. They comb the underside for films that prefer shadow—slower, more glassy in their weave, with fewer bubbles—and they wear their antennae like tuning forks held before their heads. When two shelves rub in the wind like an unlubricated hinge, they move off; when the rubbing stops, they return. If you startle one, it does not leap but becomes a tighter ribbon, and the charge along its edge sings for a blink, bright blue against the dark.

In the Lane of Sumps, where the mouths are deeper and the line of them tells a story this place has no interest in finishing, you may sense that something else has taken to living at depth. Not a fish. Not anything with fins. It smells of rust and stone when a breath of it comes up. When the light drops to the point where quartz tips fuzz blue and the breeze steps down a quarter-tone, there will be, in one or two of those wells, a rise of bubble foam that holds without spilling. The foam is not just foam. It is built—a closet of beads and threads, arranged in a draft that is constant, its pores picked to exact sizes so that the pulse of the hum passing through them pulls air and lifts water in the same gesture. It lifts and feeds a structure that you will never see from above, a funnel of living felt that sifts the air itself for what the day brings in: a rain of pollen from beyond the basin, shattered shells of life that tried to cross and failed, powdered carcasses of smaller crossers, the marvelously stubborn remains of things that grew in another rulebook. The owner of the funnel is the size of a cup and soft to the point of transparency. It lowers itself at times and gathers; at other times, it simply tends and keeps the funnel perfect as a cave creature will keep its web when the cave is generous.

Predation happens but looks like administration. The glass-backed take the mats to their borders and stop; if there is one who eats past a border, it eats where a little inaccuracy will not send a throat up a semitone and spoil three others. The long soft ones eat the slubs of iron bloom that grow where two notes beat and make a microclimate their bodies prefer; in return, they leave that beat again, which keeps the count of available coolness at the rate that can be afforded. The winged things are eaten by whatever has leisure to open its mouth upward as they blunder out of a chimney in a moment of distraction. You can tell the night a clearback died because in the morning its back stays and its belly does not, and the back takes three days to turn to a lens again and then to a smear and then to a bright thread to be rolled underfoot somewhere to no purpose but to be rolled.

No herds. No murders. No busyness except the constant, which is busy enough. Their lives are all keyed to the fact that change here is a held note. Births are single or at most two. Eggs are glued to where they will not shift. Larvae, where there are larvae, have the good sense to be beads: easy to hold, easy to anchor, heavy enough to pick a low point and stay. Mating, insofar as the word does not embarrass itself in a place like this, happens at whisper nodes that a person will find by accident and then never on purpose: small squares of eased sound on the ribs where two notes cancel just so. Two clearbacks meet and make a little oval by the act of meeting; two long ones overlap in a way that looks like a rope passing over a rope. Whatever passes, passes without changing what contains it.

You could, if you wanted, say the wind itself is alive here, not in the metaphor but in the simplest test of what life does. It takes energy and makes a pattern and holds that pattern around itself, and when something tries to grow beyond the pattern, the pattern excludes it not by malice but by fit. But if you’re asking, as you are, for who eats and drinks and lays down work in soft tissues, then you have the list: films that are more wire than leaf and harvest stress; small glaziers drinking with their wicks; a slow, gel-stomached animal that drags its invention from mouth to mouth; a punctuation of wings that knows that chimneys are doors that only open in one direction and only briefly; a handful of builders who never see daylight making fixed baskets in moving air; a web of inside-rock that lifts and lowers ions at the beat of the valley and will do so until the wind gives out or the basalt forgets its shape.

They survive because they are polite. They step around the instrument that feeds them. Each of them knows, in a way a muscle can know, the borders beyond which life would spill into changing the thing it requires, and they keep to those borders the way water will keep to the shape that holds it when gravity and surface tension agree. If you leave a grit at a certain lip, it will still be there in a year; if you leave a better grit right by it, a careful eater may move yours and take the other. If you sit where the air makes a small pocket of privacy and wait with your dust-soft fingers on your knees, they will come out as they come out for anything that is not a storm. And when you stand, nothing will have been different to any eye that was not paying attention the way you were.

What lives here is the habit of restraint dressed in bodies that learned it long ago. They are busy all the time, and at the end of their workday—every day—nothing is different, which is the point and the price and the way they stay.
