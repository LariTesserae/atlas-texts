Those who live on the rim are the ones who have learned to belong to a held note. They know brightness as ground, pressure as weather, silence as something you carry in the marrow because the world will not provide it. They are animals and colonies and films and crusts, but the shape they share is edge—bodies that make a living in the thin line where wind chooses, where water does not flow but appears.

Begin with the singer that does not move: the mountain’s own throat.

Inside the vents the stone is not bare. The circular bores shine with a patina like the inside of a shell, slick with tiny draperies of calcite that look soft but are too hard for a thumbnail. That gloss is not inert. It is moonmilk and microfilm, a lacy mat of bacteria and fungal threads and archaea that wicks the bead of condensed water and feeds on what the wind gifts—pollen ghosts, spore dust, aerosol organics skimmed from forests far below. It metabolizes slowly in the cold, laying down lime in increments thin as breath, smoothing a burr here, fattening a lip there. The tuning is not metaphor. The growth of those living skins—which thrive best in the bands of splash and humidity right at the vent’s mouth—minutely adjusts each pipe until the chord settles. The colony likes the song as much as anything here does; the pressure and vibration improve its grip and wicking. The wind plays the pipes and the pipes thicken into the song that suits them. A consortium lives in that adjustment: vent-lichen that fixes the lip against frost-shatter, snot-green biofilms that keep the rim wet longer than any drip could, spirochetes that hide where air twangs the hardest and draw trace metals out of the condensation as if they were food. They are the mountain’s tuners, and though they have no faces they are residents in the most specific way: without them the holes would be different tomorrow, and so would the life that reads their pitch.

At the level of mica and rime there are bodies, too—rotifers and tardigrades collapsed into grit-colored commas inside dry pans that remember rain as clearly as the rock remembers seas. They are asleep most of the time the word means anything. When a hollow brims for three days, when a dripline shimmers for an afternoon, when a film sweats down a wall like a thought that won’t quite cohere, they wake. They spin, they unfurl jaws with more joints than sense and scissor through green scum and each other. They lay eggs with skins so wrinkled they look old when new. And as soon as the heat says again, No, they curl. In the resume of this place they are the notes pinned to the margin—woken when conditions return to a remembered line, waiting when they don’t. Their lives run on the calendar of condensation; their clocks stop cleanly and start without complaint.

Things with wings are the obvious citizens because the updraft is a staircase that runs forever and they have found how to stand on it. There are the swifts, first: needles wired into muscle, their bodies so elected toward flight that standing seems an embarrassment. They nest by making water behave. They wedge pebbles into microledges where black algae slicks, shaping stall pockets where droplets hold a second longer and grow. They paste their nests from what the mountain would not give outright—algae threads combed like hair, grit, saliva that freezes and thaws better than spit has any right to—stuck onto the least generous ledge and held there by the only adhesive that never entirely sets, because somewhere on the rim it is always cold enough to remember. Their routes are muscle-taught and inherited by watching—the same slashing figure-eights through the same heated stripes in the same noon. They do not speak in air; they put their bodies in the quiet between hisses, low when the vents sing high or high when the note has moved into their chests. Mostly they flash. A roll that shows a paler underwing to a mate is sentence and clause. A wingtip dragged like a match along a beam of sun is a name.

The big gliders—call them kites because nothing else holds the word as well—do not so much fly as consent to be held and give that holding instructions. They are not precisely birds, or at least not the kind of birds that ever learned to drop onto branches and consider the day. Their bones are a trellis of struts you can see through at the right angle. Their primaries are ragged along the edges, a deliberate ruin that breaks up the high hiss when the chord would otherwise make their wings buzz numb. Their faces are quiet beaks and big eyes hooded by membranes smoked against the intolerable light. They flag. The pale underside of the wing is not decoration but alphabet. A slow bank toward the cliff flashes a long bar to a partner upcolumn; a tight twist throws a short pulse that means I will peel off for the pan; a double-throw in sequence is an argument about who got there first last yesterday. They hammer snail shells against one particular ledge because the shell gives before the stone does and because the stone—polished high, the only polish for miles not made by ice—becomes a memory even long after the dinner is done. Their voices are not absent so much as pared down: a single deep seethe in the gut when a shadow too big to be themselves crosses below, a syllable like a cough when a juvenile blunders into a wrong path and must be called away from a vent. They have learned the grammar of falling so thoroughly that gravity reads like an arrangement, not a law. On bright mornings they sit motionless on air for half an hour at a time, adjusting one feather, then another, then none, watching the column hold.

Stranger and heavier, calling work down to the rim from whatever dies in the wide brightness, is a bird built to be deaf while eating. Its neck is muscular and bare so no filth will cling; its ear-openings are lids of meat that shut as if the head were winking, a closure as smooth as swallowing. It rides stink the way other birds ride heat. It works the lee side of buttresses where updrafts eddy and insects cloud and also where mistakes collect—the glider that drifted a little wide and hit stone, the hare-pelt of a mammal that leapt for a grike and found a bad handhold, bones dropped here on purpose by cousins from further out. It is ugly by any generous fairground standard. It is perfect by the standards that matter: nothing here has business to be beautiful except its exactness. When the lids of its ears open again the mountain’s tone floods in. You can see its throat tighten against it as if swallowing sound. It stands for a while after feeding, beak open, breast working, letting something in the skull shake out that the muscles had kept safe.

On the ground—that is to say, on the chessboard of clints and the thin deep of grikes—a mammal as small as a hand does a wide profession. It is not a pika and not a marten and not a bat; it is a grike-runner, a creature with toes made into tools, nails thickened like dollhouse pitons, and a fold of velvet skin between wrist and ankle that makes a not-quite wing. It does not fly. It refuses to fall efficiently enough that the wind will hold it. It crosses, which is the only act that matters if you eat where the water refuses to settle. From crack to crack it springs, a dim canard wing stretched for a second at a time until the next safe slot pulls it like an idea catching. It eats lichen like bread and the mucilage that stone makes when it sweats, and on cold noon it licks hoarfrost with a small black tongue and shivers with the joy of it. Its teeth are short and many; its belly is hung with extra fur where it naps on stone so that heat will not leak into the mountain. When it speaks to its own kind it does it by thumping the tips of its toes against the lip of a grike with a rhythm learned from the gaps in the mountain’s voice. Either ear easily ruined by a wrong day will not thrive here; it closes its own with thin muscles when the world gets loud and opens them again to hear the drum. It lines its den with its own shed fur teased into a felt; when that denspace smells wrong, a stiff-bodied corvid cousin will evict it and install itself for a night and then leave again, because taking someone’s house and not needing it is a thing that has become fun.

The corvid cousin is blunt-faced and bad-mannered and plays. Its toys are pebbles. It steals the smooth stones the swifts jam into algae-slicks and hides them in other cracks because it has learned from somewhere that trouble is interesting. It brings bones and drops them where dropping polishes. It does not sing or mimic or slickly bargain as corvids in valleys do. The chord uses up that kind of currency. Instead it flashes its wings the way the kites do, imitating imitations, trying on their sentences like coats and finding some of them fit. It knows where rock pans stay wet longest and checks them while pretending to be uninterested. If language lives in this place anywhere besides the gesture of wing, it lives in the corvid’s map of caches. It will take one pebble from one ledge and move it to another twice a day for a week because the hands of a game cannot be otherwise. You can call that technology if you like. The swifts would call it theft if they called anything at all.

Insect life is uncountable when it assembles and invisible when it disperses. The updraft drags valley air up and out, and what drifts on that current is a plankton of chitin and wish. Species have budded here out of others because a constant column of moving medium is as much an ecosystem as water is. Dew-lickers walk the world with tongues like paintbrushes, sipping lines other animals would not recognize as drink, their feet fringed to stand on sheen. Their eggs are glued onto the damp blackness of algae with a stalk that will be a wick tomorrow. Motes the size of commas, close cousins to mites, graze the metal blush of stone where solutions have dried and left a salty varnish; they carry a pelt of oil inside their shell that keeps them from drying into something else’s food. Tiny clear dipterans, more glass than body, lay their larva into green film while it still moves and let their babies eat the place before they know it as home. Others do not belong as distinctly to one wall or one drip. They manifest and tremble in the sun-sheets like snow thrown up by a plow, then vanish into light. They are food, yes, but they are also weather. The way they gather tells larger animals where the ledges are breathing right, where a fissure holds the right temperature and soft-sounding air. If their beat fails under your ear, go elsewhere; the cliff is about to switch hands.

Vegetation is a plurality of experiments that all arrive at density. Cushions are the success story: buns of stems that touch more of each other than of air, parts turned inward, leaves so fat with their own shape that wind cannot get a grip. They trap their own humidity and sip off their own condensate and have roots that do not much try. They taste like an anchored thought. Saxifrages pretend to be stone and convince even themselves for long hours; in their one generous week they open, and gnats discover they can be pollinators if they remember where to land. Lichen organizes color in low pulses—a scorch-black that eats light until it is not light anymore, a copper-green oxidized blush, a sulfurous streak that looks wrong enough to cheer you when you find it because somebody has found a way to make that chemistry into living. A dwarf willow holds on as if bent by an old lesson rather than today’s breeze and guards, inside its scroll of self, a furred catkin it will uncurl exactly when a handful of insects appear who can do something with it. The plants are not background. They are bank accounts where nutrients have been stashed across decades, to be withdrawn by a mammal’s tongue or a snail’s rasp and replaced again at a pace that would make a gardener cry. They succeed because they expect nothing else from the world than its exactness.

There are spiders here, but not of webs. They walk the sun, small and fast and brighter than you think, feeling the rim hum through their feet and leaping when the phase of a wing passes. They do not cast lines across a gap that would catch anything except an accident. They catch intention. If you say that’s a kind of technology too, none of them will argue, because it keeps them fed.

Once in a great while a weather with a different idea blows a fog right up to the rim and hangs it there. Then the mountain’s voice—with all its insistence—goes soft around the edges and new attendees arrive out of whiteness. Migrants cut tentatively along the parapets, uncommitted, tested by a sound their lineage does not know; a lammergeier from the far side of the table follows some pathway that only bones know and lets a femur fall on the old dinner stone; a human appears as a dot and then a person, roped to another dot, plastic winking dull in the godly light. They stay the time that their lungs and their decision allow. They do not live here. The place is not hostile to them, only complete without them. The mountain does not lower its volume in greeting.

The rules you feel in your chest when you stand on the rim—the way sound maps more accurately than sight, the way moisture obeys angle more than vessel, the way falling is a performance—are not philosophy here. They are how metabolism actually gets done. The animals put their signals where the mountain is not speaking. The plants grow where the angle is correct, not simply where there is space. The gliders hold and peel off like gears in a machine with no teeth. Time runs on maintenance because the system pays out more for repeating the right move than inventing a new one poorly. The swifts thread yesterday’s eight because that pattern is water and child and enough. The grike-runner drums in the same early angle of light because that is when a neighbor’s ear is open at all. Even the tuners inside the vents—bacteria engraving their tiny, endless script—fatten their fringes where the pressure in the song says: here.

If there is a mind that lives here in the way a city has a mind, it is diffuse and loud and older than hoarfrost. It does not have a plan beyond continued exactness. It disposes of mistakes without commentary. It holds open a column of air strong enough to act like a habit and then rewards what leans into that habit. All brightness and no shade unless you tuck yourself into a stone’s inwardness. All noise and one reliable quiet if you find the gaps. It is not empty. It is a spaciousness that lets each thing become fiercely itself: each lichen a boundary that you can put a century on, each insect an answer to a question about damp, each wing a sentence that can be read from the far side of a theater, each small blow struck against a snail shell a memoir scratched onto a single shining stone.

These are the residents: films that tune a pipe and wake to grind a green minute; cushions and rinds writing long biographies at fingernail scale; sweeps of gnat and comma-mites that invent the idea of cloud again and again out of bodies the size of dust; swifts that stitch figure-eights through noon as if the day would ravel without them; kites that call by flash and trust falling to be kind; a dull clever bird that moves one pebble between two crevices and finds in that repetition a life; a grike-runner that licks ice with such sincerity you cannot doubt joy exists at this altitude; a deaf-when-eating heavy-headed thing that cleans mistakes into bone and then drops the bone to make a new bright patch on a ledge. And under all of them, the one that never moves—wind that has made stone into a throat and called, and called, and called, until everything that could learn to answer found the exact way to do it.
