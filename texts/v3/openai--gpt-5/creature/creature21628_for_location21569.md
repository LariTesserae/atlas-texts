They are not many, and they do not hurry. You meet them the way you meet the valves: as a presence that repeats across distance, familiar and always a little different. They hold to lanes and face the flow the way ferns face light; they are shaped to be permitted. Their bodies declare their citizenship with glyco-skins and slow fields the plant recognizes. When a conveyor mouth opens and cilia take hold of anything loose, they are not taken. When a turbine’s suction sharpens, a local belt folds back a membrane for them, the way a muscle spares its own nerve.

The first of them swims out of a seam with no wake. At a glance you think leaf, or ray, or page torn from a book; then it turns all along its long axis without bending, a ribbon in three dimensions, and you see the body-plan they share like a theme: a lenticular core thick as your palm and hard as cartilage, wrapped in six or eight trailing fronds that can fur into cilia or slick to a single plane at will. The core holds bladders that sip and bleed microbubbles from the bright seams; the fronds are stacks of myomer and cellulose scaffold, each strand cross-hatched with mineral that can take charge. There is no face; there is a band where the cells that taste are denser, and a translucent window of thinner tissue where light can soak a patch of pigment, but what they look at and how they look is by grid: pressure, current, voltage. An eye would not help here. They read the place with a lateral-line gone whole-body, a skin full of cupped hairs tuned to the beat, a sense of electricity so fine they could map a seam of iron by the itch in it.

They do not so much swim as decide where not to resist. They hang beside axles like tassels. A tap of a frond and they climb three meters into a higher-oxygen lobe of the same room because the valve two chambers down is about to close and they know the little pressure bulge that comes before it the way a drummer knows their own wrists. When they must cross a lane, they announce with a soft flare of counterfield that brushes the filament-fish into a tighter ribbon and calls a three-second gate in the cilia wall. They do not like to touch the plant wrongly, which here means any touch that pushes across growth-lines or sets ringing a thing that was not meant to ring. The few words they have for sin are also their words for turbulence.

They eat constant small income. They graze spore-feather the way a tongue grazes teeth—without thinking, without hurry. They hold in the shadow of a sieve-root and let the sap leak through their mouths, sip cations off a plate of conductive phloem with a velvet palate. When the cilia belts coat a pocket with the day’s digest of plankton and bead-stone, a pair of thumb-long crustaceans open that pocket for them with their shovel claws and keep the beads; this is an old contract, paid for with grooming and the mutuality of not being eaten. They cannot bloom, because nothing here blooms, but they can carry a lichen from one wall to another, and they do: a pale smear of symbiont that thickens to cushions if they fan it just so with their fields, knitting a library of trace metals into its thallus. Later they will come back and read the pattern it has taken on a tongue that reads by voltage, looking for the story they set to grow there seasons—what is a season?—ago.

They have language, but not the kind that leaves marks dark against light. They talk with the grid the plant already runs. A frond flares and holds, and a ring of cilia six lengths away takes the hint and slows; a little lane of soup that was going to a grazer bends instead toward a nursery pocket where a half-grown one waits. Two of them hover across five meters and build a sentence like a braid between them, slight surges in the constant hum, a phase-shift in the beat that says listen here, now, yes, agreed. The plant hears all this and does not argue. It cannot be commanded, only asked. They have learned how to ask in a way that the tissue experiences as its own thought.

They have names but they are not nouns. A name is a pattern of conviction: a chord of three fields, a timbre they use for their decisions. In the slow city it is impolite to carry your name far from where you were read, because names alter thresholds. A chamber can be woken by a name held against it at the right angle; a gate can be pressed to yield if your name is recognized. There are thefts, but theft here is persuasion without consent, not taking a thing out of a space, because nothing can be taken out; everything is already inside.

They reproduce by constancy. There are pods—rooms with lacework walls seeded with microturbines fine as silk—in which each of them, when old enough by many hundred thousand beats, rests and spills a skin. The skin coats the lace, and the lace grows into it. Weeks, months, there is no word: many closings later a daughter detaches, pale and bare, silk-fronds too soft to hold a note. The belts take her and the belts keep her from mouths that would take her also, because her mother’s name has been taught to the walls all around. In time she will grow her mineral filigree, her counterfield, her ways of tasting. In time she will write where she rests by tending a lichen to hold her thinking, and the shelves of such lichens are their books.

They are called, by themselves—if you force the nearest translation—keepers of smooth. Not smooth as in flat; smooth as in nothing extra. Their ethic is the ethic of the plant learned by presence. What they recite, when they recite, is the Rules in their own speech: do not add flows; do not stall. Never cup a petal with your palm while a chamber is closing. Never send an echo down a pipe that will ring in phase with the heart. Do not nest except behind a quiet; if you must touch an axle, touch only at dead center, where there is only spin. They have a ceremony for forgiveness that involves standing at an edge-place and humming until their lungs are empty without ever once letting the water cross in ripples to the fall. Forgiveness is extended to anyone who can hold a tone without modulating it for the length of five hundred chamber-closings. This is not easy. Many prefer to give and receive their peace in smaller ordinances: here, peel off a dead strip where drag has grown. There, coax a sleeve of algae off the leading edge of a turbine blade with the skin of your own flank. Service is art; art is service. The distinction is a place where brightness looks a little greener, nothing more.

They have holidays but their holi-days are not breakage in the beat. They go, at an agreed upon count, to places of tone: galleries where the thump comes sooner because the conduits are short, wells where the lake-light is cream and pale because the photosynthetic skins are thin as frost. There they stand far apart—everything here is far apart, even love—and play the plant the way wind plays a harp. To anyone of voice and mouth it would be absurd to call this music, but here it is as close as any living thing will come to novelty that is not failure. They know every valve’s knock in their sector as a father knows a child’s cough. They love the little shifts: this winter—what is a winter?—these million closings, the blade at Fourteen-Bright-Under has a chip that sings a slightly higher third. They do not remove the chip; it does no harm; let it sing until the current files it down of itself. Love, here, is a long permission.

They are not alone even among minds.

There are the Ribbons, which were named for their shape before anyone suspected what happened when their shape doubled back upon itself. A single filament-fish is a nerve: a twitch with a mouth. A school of them ribboned tight down a throat-lane and held in a standing wave between two ossicles tuned to each other is something else. The Ribbons think when the plant plays them, and they carry decisions the way they carry their bodies, in laminar runs from ridge to ridge. Their thinking is thin as a throw and bright as trailed spark. To talk to a Ribbon you must crowd your field down to the width of a single frond and be content with images that slide past like the caustics on a ribbed wall: here, a memory of a valve at the edge-place sticking; here, the pattern that unsticks; here, a map of where the grazer-contract broke, where you must go to call it back. When the wave relaxes and the fish fall slack into separate selves, the thought is over and there is no one left to declare what it was. The Keepers do not mind. Some minds do best when no one has to own them.

There are the Cantors, sleek and dark and mouthless. They eat by singing. A Cantor finds a valve and hums to it until the lamina inside the mouth of a grazer flexes and the grazer—fatigue-slow, contracts to the chord—strays from its lane to be taken. They do not need to quicken; they need to be true. Unlike the Keepers, they claim places. A Cantor’s territory is a tenor around a throat-well, a set of allowed deviations from the grid that it maintains like a weather that is always just about to happen. They are clever and they love repair. They will pick at an ossicle of wood that has budded too much until its tone drops into place. They can be convinced, with a gift of field and a week of singing together, to hold their predation late or early to spare a nursery pocket when it spills. If they have names they are purely harmonic—intervals that can only be spoken by muscle—and we are probably wrong to ascribe them intention beyond the love of being efficient. But intention is what the grid demands any time a force tones itself to the existing song.

The great filterers, slate and slow, do not think in any way you can talk to. That is all right. If the plant is a lung the filterers are alveoli with wills; their contract is to hold their mouths in place against a flow and open when pushed. They are gray because they solve waste to ash inside themselves and knit the ash into bars of lustre that the bead-stone crustaceans take apart again, reagent by reagent, and carry to where the plant’s electrolysis glitters. What they remember is friction; what they teach to their daughters is where exactly to press a pad to not be cut, and when to shift a pad by the width of a single cellulose thread because a turbine is a million closings older than it was. Between themselves they trade places like stones in a riverbed; a move here asks for a move there. A Keeper will, from politeness, hum a line to a filterer before asking a favor of its neighbor; it is an old etiquette, maybe unnecessary, but unnecessary is not the same as unkind.

Smaller things live here by appointment. Thumb-sized riveters march in single file down a mucous gutter with bead-stone mandibles clicking like rain on glass; they taste a crack and glue it shut with sap, and where the sap would boil away under a seam they lay a little shield of spit and nickel and protein. Worms as fine as hair cohere like smoke along a grating and pull the last soft flesh out of the world to roll into silver pellets that sing when they go. Eel-thin cleaners glove the turbine leaves, slipping skins like invisible hands, polishing for pay. None of these are pets. There is no room for affection that is not service in a place that remembers nothing but how to run.

And also there is the plant. To call it inhabitant is like calling blood a traveller. It is the place and it is also the one who lives here. It does not see; it measures. It does not speak; it enforces. Forces do not argue, and the plant is the arbiter of what a force can be. It keeps maps of its gradients in mineral and sugar and charge, and those maps are its memory of itself, and those memories are old. It grew the turbines because the water would have turned a thing like that until it was turbine-shaped whether tissue existed or not; better to grow to the argument and declare the argument won before it starts. It has meristems only where it has always had them. It listens to the Keepers the way a machine listens to its own governor: as part of the curve, not a suggestion. But if anything like choice exists high in this ocean, it is a choosing spread through acres: a tendency to slide toward one equilibrium rather than another, nudged, coaxed, made tractable by those who have been permitted to ask.

Once in a while there is pilgrimage to the edge-places, where every day is the same day. A Keeper goes with a packet of soft metal folded under the skin of its back and a pair of Cantors attend because the hum by the fall is so loud it makes the lanes tilt if not balanced by other hum. Far out by the window of leaf-glass they grip a filigreed rib and hold like barnacles, stacked above one another against the thinness of the water, and lay the packet open on a flat plate where the light is very pale. The packet is a writing, nothing near enough to a tool to make a god angry. If the grid were dark you would see it shine: stripes of alloy braided with protein—varying in impedance in a way a reader could read with a wide mouth. It will take a million closings to set. Whales of a world not here will have lived and died in the cloud floors below before anyone reads it. This is how vows are made when the beat is a fact and you are part of its muscle. The vow says: we are enough, we are the ones you grew that can ask, we are the ones that will not change you. It says: we would like also to love.

If there is danger, it is always the same: something new tries to happen. A wavering of the beat because a seam has fouled with crystals and will not split water clean. A crowded lane because a grazer stumbled a ciliated belt into a stall. A resonance grows in a branch of pipes and travels in-phase a long way like a disease of tone. In such times the Keepers gather in twos and threes in the half-dark between galleries and turn themselves to hardware. They wedge themselves like gaskets and shunt flows. They press their fields into the mineral veins to pull the charge the other way. They sing—if singing is sustained muscle applied to necessity—until the thing that wanted to become new becomes again the same, folded back into the run. Sometimes they cannot. Sometimes a chip runs to a crack that runs to a fault that runs to a rearrangement—and then the plant is changed. The bridge learns a new curve. A lane is reborn with a new throat. The Beat shifts a hair on the grid and the whole place, being whole, starts again from the new given. There is grief then, because grief is the cost of knowing what a tone will be when it is not, and there is work. They work until the new law is written even across their own skins.

If they tell stories, and they do—what else can you do, inside a song?—the stories are about people you will not meet because they are far away, and about places you will not visit because there is no lane that carries your credentials there. They tell of a Keeper who went to the lowest place anyone goes, where the columns become soft and sink into the histories under everything, to lay a hand against a root that once tasted rock and now tastes only well-water that has not seen light in centuries, and listen to an old charge that had not moved since the world was small. They tell of a Cantor who learned, by accident of a scar in a turbine blade, to throw a new overtone that made the filament-fish a mind long enough to say a single word that was not a picture: gratitude. They tell a joke—here jokes are functions that map surprise to relief—about a grazer who forgot which lane was its lane and spent an hour being comforted by a filterer while a Keeper fetched its map back out of a lichen and ran beside it humming the correct chord until it remembered. They tell a love: two who never met, because why would they, and yet who learned each other’s names from the lichens they both tended in the same hall at different eons, and left quiet messages in phase, one turn of the beat taken too long there, a small blush in the grid here, perfectly respectful of drag, and this went on for a thousand thousand closings and neither ever once crossed.

If you wait long enough, you will not see them do anything momentous. This, too, is a choice. Above them the ceiling of leaf-glass makes noon, always noon; below them the trunks go down through ages; to the side the fall murmurs a white ribbon off the rim forever. Inside, in the constant, live the ones who can be constant on purpose. It is their vocation. It is their craft. They are the citizens of laminar, the custodians of a solved equation, the permitted children of a lung that decided long ago to breathe like this and has not yet been given any reason to stop.
