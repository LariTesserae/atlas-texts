What lives here first is the thrum itself, a temperament given weight, a habit with a spine. The sound is a resident. It presses into bone and takes up a bed in the lungs and, at shift’s end, walks home with whoever walks out. If you listened with a stethoscope on the columns, you could chart the place like a patient—rate, regularity, the odd ectopic beat when a valve sticks and someone thumps a casting with a rubber mallet and the rhythm rights itself. The thrum knows the tide. It drowses at slack, leans into the flood, settles after relief. It is not companionable, exactly; it is too constant for that. But it is company.

Then there is the tree, which is not a single life so much as a consensus of lignin and memory. It is the native citizen. It was here before the iron, before the rings, back when nets of roots sifted silt from river browns and each season’s flood braided them thicker. It has been taught, yes, into ribs and arches and steps, but the teaching is a conversation more than an order. It keeps its own counsel. It knows where its water wants to go and humors the gutters because they make sense; it grows around a collar not because it must but because the squeeze answers the strain and distributes the pull into something livable. Left alone, it would be messier and taller and louder with birds. Accommodated, it is useful and deep and quiet, and it watches back in the incremental way of a living thing that measures decades in cambium. It lives in brushstrokes of gloss where condensate runs, in that darkening skin at the drip-lines, in the soft eyebrows of bark curving over the edges of plates as if curious about the metal’s taste. It eats the screws. It eats the nails. It keeps what information it wants, in these swallowed tags and numbered pins and faint old scars that—like laugh lines on a human face—are the record of strain relieved and lessons learned.

Machines live here like domesticated animals live in a farmyard—enfolded, named in practice if not in speech, their quirks known. The big pump is the slow bull, gentle by mass, stubborn by momentum. It has old leather and new gaskets, polished places where attention has worn it bright. It complains in tracings: a rise of temperature at one bearing, a tighter breath on the downstroke when the silt is fattier than yesterday. People stand with a hand to its casing and feel for moods. The flywheel is a moon that nobody looks at straight on, a rule of the sky you live under more than an object; you know it will be there, turning, because your body is arranged around its schedule. The belts are snakes that have made peace with being leashed. The governor is a pair of fruits gossiping in the whirring air. Little supplemental machines dart in and out of notice—the fan that scissors the air like a dragonfly, the whistle that startles like a bird when a relief lifts three bays down and its shiver arrives through wood.

People live here, too, in a way almost as placed as the pipes and the living beams. They are not many in the bay at once. It is a working residence, not a village. But over a day the people arrive and depart like tides in miniature, and their names adhere to the things they tend the way moss adheres to shade. They do not wear uniforms so much as accumulate a kinship with certain stains and textures: green-black crescents sunk in the fingerprint whorls, wrists glossed where sap has lacquered them for years, knees powdered with the chalk of limescale, shoulders burnished where straps have ridden the same track along soft shirts a lifetime. They move with the amiable caution of those who have learned the path of rebounding branches, who have taught muscle to expect a certain give from the catwalk and a certain refusal from the brace, who read with their soles all the tiny signals in the timber telling how the weight is arranged today.

They call one group graftwrights. Those are the ones who bring wedges and beeswax and soft straps, who pack clay around a cut to teach a cambium to turn right instead of left. They keep calendars pinned to the living: Release collars before the wet. Move saddle three holes higher at the equinox. Cut scribe on secondary limb first week of month to encourage plane. They carry knives with the kind of edges that respect sap; they do not like steel that throws sparks. Their tools sound like whispers when they work—bone-handled, brass-faced, ceramic-jawed. They prefer verbs like persuade and convince. The tree will not be bullied, and they know it.

Another group are the turners and tenders of machinery. Their talk is in hand signals and the lean of a palm flat on a pipe. They are good with heat. They can tell you the difference by skin alone between eighty and ninety-five degrees on an iron elbow and what each means in context. They listen to belts with their front teeth on a screwdriver shaft, to bearings with the back of a knuckle against the block. They carry a little book in a pocket, or a slate thick with grease that takes a stylus cleanly; the columns remember their marks in brass-pinned drift lines, but the operators remember the rest in cramped notations: 17B: two degrees west of plumb summer-wet. Fan squeal only under load. Relief at 3 bay cross-talk today, check trap for obstruction. On their forearms, a pattern of fine pale scars that are not accidents so much as signatures—the nick where a wire hairpin let go, the shallow crescent from a pipe strap lashing under tension. Their superstition is practical: they do not name the pump out loud before a flood, because if you invite a thing to prove itself it will choose drama.

Children live here, but not as residents of this exact square of space; they belong to the broader web of the city and are brought into Seventeen the way apprentices are brought to an instrument, one careful finger at a time. They learn to put cheek to bark and know who opened which valve three bays away by the note it sets into the structure. They learn to write their names in the bright shafts of motes and then learn not to, because good dust is conserved and the motes are the slow body of the place sloughing itself into air. They carry tins to catch condensate under leaf edges and drink it cold with the reverence of those who understand how clean is made. They tend sprigs of graftwood not because the sprigs matter at scale but because responsibility taught on something that answers back in green is a responsibility that lasts. A boy is given an insulator tooth—porcelain bright, crackle-glazed—and told to keep it white a year. A girl is set to count the rotations of the governor balls at low, mid, and high, and to say them as a song. They are not so much being raised to replace as to continue; the bay does not need many, but it requires understanding, and understanding is slow to make.

Old people live nearby, in drier galleries that border the working wet. They sleep with windows of leaf and cheap screen that open toward the hum and feel wrong if the night goes quiet. They have stories about when a collar froze in a wet year and Seventeen leaned out of true, when four bays along a man ignored a sign and burnt the bark and the next season every rib on that side grew a callus high and wrong and had to be taught back down with straps and songs. They are not nostalgic in the way of telling you it used to be better. They are convinced that better is incremental and proven by the way a gutter shines where it caught and a dry floor where a flood receded. They keep the rules in their mouths the way you keep a pebble in your cheek to stop a nervous tongue—NEVER CUT A BEARER ROOT; RELEASE COLLARS BEFORE THE WET SEASON—and they take personal offense at open flame past the yellow paint.

There are others who live here that you don’t notice at first and then cannot unsee. Lichens are citizens with voting rights. They pearl bolts in tiny mufflers, they give edges back to things that have gone too smooth, they break the shine of dangerous surfaces and make grip. Mosses hold their right to the drip-sides like tenants with old leases; you cannot dislodge them without an argument. Algae make nets in dark water that collect fines out of the flow and then, when someone with the right ladle skims them, turn into feed for fish that are born and die in the same black ribbon of pool. Bacterial films live in a constant negotiation with oil and resin and the mild antiseptic of tannin; they are the quiet chemists of this place, undoing and doing in equal measure. They keep the metal from rusting too quickly by stealing what rust would eat, and the people keep the films from feasting by rinsing, brushing, giving nowhere single to make a fulcrum.

Animals, mostly, do not live here. That is by design, and by surrender. It is too loud, too tremored for wings to love; nothing that needs steady perches believes in these moving edges. The one pale gecko you saw is a marginal soul. It might be a resident, licking condensate off the under-lip of a bracket. It might be a traveler between drips. It knows the hot places and the cold. Its feet are exactly the size of your thumbnail and leave invisible flowers where it passed on a still day. The thin ribbon of fish in the pool is a punctuation mark in a grammar written by slowness; they exist so that someone once in a while can have soup sweetened by something not plant, and so that algae will remember it is loved.

There is also the whole cohort of people whose lives run along Seventeen’s edge more than inside it. Wirehands who wear the clay sleeves on their palms to haul a liana of copper along a path that refuses corners. Insulation-priests who set porcelain like shrines and murmur about clearance the way bakers talk about crust. Canal-watchers with flat eyes who can tell you at a glance if the intake’s shadow means silt or simple cloud and whether the tide is wearing anger today. They are neighbors. When something goes wrong, they arrive at the yellow line and wait with that respectful impatience of those who know they are not the right hands for the job but are the right shoulders to take weight when the right hands ask.

Even rules live here, in the animate way of things that have been believed in long enough to become creatures. They perch where a passerby’s eyes will fall and preen themselves into visibility with stencils and brass pins, with increments and dates. The sign that says NO OPEN FLAME BEYOND THE YELLOW LINE is a territorial animal; it will rattle its tin in your head if your match so much as thinks of sparking. The line that says RELEASE COLLARS BEFORE THE WET SEASON breeds like cats—it appears everywhere, in paint, in chalk, in neat script on a ledger, in the little calendars tattooed into memory. The instruction to never cut a bearer root is a god that accepts no burnt offerings. When a new person comes in green and wide-eyed, older hands do not explain so much as introduce: this is Don’t-Cut, this is Release, this is No-Flame; these three keep their rooms for you, and in exchange you keep the doors closed on bad ideas.

You might say the bay itself lives, as a composite animal, at about the speed of sap. It knows where its edges are, not by walls but by what is inside its care. It knows the names of its own accidents: the Squeal Last Monsoon, the Long Lean, the day a belt parted but nobody bled. It times everything against itself: the slow flood that cleans the grating, the afternoon when the pump runs longer, the hour of fog when the leaves open their uncountable mouths and drink the air. It learns, too, by instruments, because people love a dial. The tide tells itself to the bay through a brackish tang; the bay answers with a comfortable settling. The whistle three bays down announces a relief and the bay takes note in wood-vibration and passes the feeling along the columns like thin gossip.

There are nights when no one is in the immediate circle, when Seventeen goes on with only the watch-lights caged and the drip counting itself into sleep, and on those nights what lives here is night’s version of day—a privacy that is still full, a solitude that is not empty. The flywheel’s patience, the sizzle of water finding its path, the small fan’s sharp eagerness every time a valve lifts—these are presences. They do not need supervision in the active sense; they need being lived with, which is a different verb. The city around this bay, with its clipped squares of silt island and its rails and its other bays, breathes through spaces like this, and what lives here is that breath taken care of, made regular.

If you look for home, you will find it in peripheral ways. Hammocks slung high in annex lofts where the belts whisper past in their carved leaf windows. A tin cup hung on a nail that the tree is in the process of forgetting, dented just so from a fall through two levels a dozen years ago. A shirt left with its shoulders turned inside the sleeves so the mildew has less to get a purchase on. A child’s chalk mark low on a column at knee height, a portrait of a pump that is all wheel and no casing. A porcelain tooth threaded on a string at someone’s throat, luck heavy and bright. A ledger with a dried blade of leaf tucked to mark the page of the last drift entered.

What lives here, in the end, is not one thing. It is a braided life. It is sap and oil and heat and the habits of hands. It is thresholds taught to a tree and a tree teaching thresholds back to people. It is the confidence of slowness in a world where most things hurry. It is the discipline of never cutting what bears and of releasing what binds in season. It is a pump that moves brown water sideways just enough to matter. It is a room-sized heartbeat that other hearts learn to sync to. It is a city’s idea about how to be alive, given columns and belts and leaf and rule, and the few who tend it not as masters but as neighbors to something big and patient that will outlive them all.
