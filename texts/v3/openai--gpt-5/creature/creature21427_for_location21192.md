Who lives here have learned to move like mist—present, definite, and never still in one place long enough to be called fixed. They are cloud-trained people whose first memory is of water speaking, and whose last act will be to hand their body back to the slow green mouths that held them all their lives.

They are not tall by any average that needs a field to measure it. The ridge asks for balance more than stride, and they grow into it. Calves knotted from laddering, shoulders threaded with the ropey muscle that comes from pulling their own weight and the weight of a basket up a vertical street. Their feet are broad and quick, toes with minds of their own, knowing the exact bow of a wet plank by feel. The skin on their palms is polished with fiber, resin, leaf. The skin of their faces shines because everything here breathes on them; they blink politely at the air the way city folk elsewhere blink at grit.

Hair is managed rather than worn—tied up in coils that fit under leaf caps, bound into ropes that wick water down past the neck. Jewelry is lighter than the humidity allows: twists of blue-struck thread around the wrist, a flared band of hammered copper at a tendon where a rope passes often, a seed-case bead the color of old honey in an ear. Their clothes are what the ridge can stand: barkcloth softened with time and sweat, skirts and wraps stitched from leaves pinned with thorns, shoulder mantles lacquered to shed drip. You can tell the season by the cut of a sleeve; you can tell the trade by the stains. Dyers have wrists that hold a permanent lichen dusk. Resin-workers smell like rain’s cousin. The sluicemaster wears sound like a badge—the head tilting when a new note enters, a listening crease around the mouth.

Their voices are pitched with the place. Loud enough to carry through a runnel of fern and splash, soft enough not to wake the frogs to protest. They have whistles that fold into a hand, made from the pulled ribs of a palm leaf, and clicking languages for when the fog sits on the ridge and everything else is muffled—tongue-to-tooth sounds that travel through wood when yelling would just come back wet. You’ll hear trades called by their instruments. A market caller thumps a hollowed elbow-knot with the heel of a fist. The boy marking hour by a basin’s filling taps a gong when the weighted float kisses a notch, and everyone hears the kiss and looks up without stopping work.

They are not many—enough to memorize by faces in a week, enough to keep you learning their variations for the rest of your life. Families are less horizontal than vertical: aunt above, cousin below, a nest of hammocks hung like fruit in a shaft of space between a breadfruit arbor and a buttress wall. Babies are wrapped to bodies and learn the place by heartbeat, by sway. Children are given lines before they are given letters—first a tether line for a careful grandmother’s peace of mind; then a real line, a builder’s line, to hold and feel how much give lives in a knot. The first lessons are rules: tie twice; trust living weight, not naked rock; step on what wants you. They learn animals by sound because they cannot see them most days. They learn rain by its angle of arrival—how a new fogbank touches hair differently from a wind shift.

Work is every vertebra of the day. Vinewrights and root-bridge nurses go out with the morning drip, hands wrapped, shoulders braced against the gentle animal push of green. They coax thickness and direction, plead with sap to run where weight needs to be borne, hang bundles of compost to feed the span. Lacquerers sit in dims and paint skin into leaves so water will break on it. Dye-makers stir blue into vats that look like broken shadows, testing with fingers more honest than a stick; when enough has taken, they lift cloth that holds a memory of orchids without being an orchid. Cisternwrights shave stone into cups that will last a generation and then be allowed to fuzz and soften into the next thing. A weaver’s thigh gleams with fiber burnish. A ropeway keeper leans into his own breath and waits for the wind that needs a hand to pass.

Cooking is small and sure. Leaf bundles snug with sweet roots and small fish caught from high streams on the far side of the ridge—if a cousin there has owed you something this season—or with the fat green grubs everyone pretends not to love when there is a stranger visiting. Stone is heated among fists of charcoal whose fire is not a spectacle but a skill, and the steam turns everything inside out, making sweets out of starch and tenderness out of stubbornness. Smoke is a tool, soaked into moss beds to clean them and keep the ruinous debuts of molds and flies at bay. Honey comes stingless, jarred from clay tubes where black bees keep their civility and their wax. Their wax dries in long hanging cones, pale as fish bellies, clean and warm in the hand. Salt is slow-earned and wears the scent of stone when it is good. They collect water’s attention wherever it lingers, carry it where it is not.

They make their deals up and down. From the south, traders with bark that laughs at rot answer the ridge’s need for beams that can swallow a rain and keep their shape. In return the ridge people sell the mountain’s tears turned solid—salt coaxed from rock seep and patient evaporations, the sort of commodity that turns an argument into an agreement. To the other ridges, where white and white and sudden green make a language of distance, they send resin that sets even when all weather argues against hardness. They send blue because somebody else’s valley can always use a color that doesn’t come out.

They have keepers of times and of times-not. When the birds go quiet all at once, shutters lift in hands that have done it a thousand times without looking up. When frogs clatter before noon, bright cloths come down and faces tilt up: wet is coming. There is a person whose whole work is to carry the city’s ear around its back, to listen to what the dripping means and to tell the gutters whether to pucker or let go. Everyone stops for the army ants. The place makes way in a practiced flurry. Barriers hinged from split canes fold down into a black river, and a hundred birds that do not live here live here for a handful of hours. There is an aunt who knows which snakes will ride that wave and which ones will strike at a pet, and she tells the children and none of them argue.

Their dead do not go into the ground because there is none to go into. They are unmade into usefulness by patience. A platform up under the forgiving shade of a fig holds the weight for a time, wrapped in leaves that break down at the exact speed of story. In the end, the spun-out bones are washed in bromeliad water and nested in a tufa pocket smoothed by the backs of many hands. A braid of their hair and a scoop of the compost from below their last bed go to feed a bridge, and no one calls that romantic; they call it courtesy.

They keep bees and let bats alone. They do not own coatis, but there are coatis who have learned how to be adoptable. A girl at a stall will call a particular one by the scar on its ear and hold a peel just out of reach so it will have to stand up and show its ridiculous clever hands. Monkeys are mornings only and never family. Hummingbirds do not love anything but themselves and the idea of red, and nobody expects them to. Salamanders live where they want and are left to it, because they carry their rivers inside them and it would be impolite to interfere. Children keep mantises on the ends of twigs the way other villages keep kites. When the ants are in ordinary numbers, they are allowed their law; when they threaten to undo a nursery, a line of pepper ash is drawn and the ants turn like water.

The gods here, if the word even applies, are procedures and consistencies given the courtesy of names. The mountain has a pronoun. So do the weeping walls. The white that comes and goes is addressed in the second person singular and thanked, whether or not it did what you asked it to. There is a story told about a woman who angered a buttress and rolled an ankle that made weather of her for the rest of her life. No one will point at a tree and ask it to be what it is not for long. If you say please and the sap does not oblige, you say thank you to the refusal as well and find a different geometry. A child will be named for a sound—a Purl or a Hiss-in-the-Gut, a Quick-Drip, a Whole-Tree-Sigh—and when that child’s voice finally cracks into what it is, they will be renamed with something rounder and more theirs, and no one will find this strange.

They have festivals because life does. A certain beetle lifts out of a dying limb all at once and turns the air to sapphire. Guylines are strung with them in the afternoon light and then released because color is not for keeping. The first day that there is more light than drip in a three-day run, everyone cuts their hair and repairs the edges that have been waiting. In high fog a pair of young people will stand on two facing roots and throw a line across not because a bridge is needed but because a bridge feels good to make. Nights, when the bats creak and the frogs draw their rope of sound tight around the ridge, there are stories told that are not about bravery but about not slipping. Laughs land light. The city is not solemn about its competence; it is relieved by it, like any body that goes a day without falling.

They are tended as much as tending. The vine handrails are fed and in return they grow a grip as polite as a palm. The gutters comply like muscles because they are treated like parts of a living creature and not like servile tubes. A walk is not an inert surface—it breathes and bows and returns you, and so you step with your knees understanding that this is partnership. The rules that pretend to be engineering are also manners. Tie twice because you are not the only thing using what you tied. Avoid naked rock because it is the past that wants to pull you backward. Step on what wants you and you will be invited back tomorrow.

Under the fig that is not quite indoors, the market rolls and unrolls with the lungs of the day. There is a woman with a tufa brick in her lap and a hammerstone, shaving the soft to hold hard water later. There is a man with a leaf canoe that will never see a sea, wiping resin in strokes that are as tender as if the boat were a child. There is a boy with a gong and a look of fierce attendance because timing is a public office. There is a girl who has made a friend of a thief. There are baskets that smell like fingernails and sharp roots; there are ropes that smell like sleep; there is a kind of blue that, if it were a fruit, would be a fruit that makes the mouth water with the idea of coolness.

They are not living against this ridge in defiance. They are living with it in precise agreement. Even their errors are woven—the watchdog that went hungry because someone forgot a shutter is forgiven by a neighbor who heard the silence of birds and lifted it, and the apology is a rope coiled and left where a hand will find it. Visitors feel their bodies calibrate without permission. The place pays you back what you put in, with interest, but not in coin. It pays in weight taken and given back, in board underfoot that rises as you rise, in names you did not know you could hear until you can.

Other lives share it in honest plurality. Ant tides, bird flares, bat leather, frog bells, coati hands, the invisible biochemistry of moss, the slow arithmetic of trees. Humans are not the prime noise but a reliable one. The city’s wealth is motion held just long enough to be useful, then allowed to go. The people here have learned to be a motion of their own—dripping, pooling, sluicing, and gone—leaving the place better braced than they found it and more itself for having carried them. If you asked them what they are, they might shrug, tilt an ear at the sound of a fresh runlet finding a bark seam, and say, like anyone who lives in a verb, that they are busy being here.
