They live by heat and hearing. They live in the place where day thins to a rumor and stone remembers water. They are plural, most of them small, and together they are the chamber’s metabolism.

The ones you notice last, if you enter clumsy and lit, are the ones who own the ceiling. They have made a pelt of the cave and given it a pulse. Each one is the size of a handful, soft-mouthed, with the soft, leathered sails of a complicated thought. They have old maps written in the membranes of their ears and on their tongues. The old hang deeper; the new like a restless fringe, learning the exacting geography of grip, memorizing the spine of each cornice by tooth and foot. Mothers carry the algorithm of their single pups between ribs; they leave, eat the night, and return to a precise coordinate of fur and smell in the dark braid of thousands. A colony reads the cave the way a choir reads breath: they measure together, launch together, cross each other’s wakes without touching, and pour themselves through the cave’s throat by remembering a shape of air. They season the stone with oil and tiny bones, and their warmth is not a simile: it is a weather that rises and stratifies and stays.

Around them and on them lives their second colony, the city of attendants and thieves. Wingless flies as flat as coins and as quick as fingers go about their business in the fur, tasting blood and old milk, hiding from the gusts of departure in the folds of a shoulder. Mites like red commas and pale commas move their punctuation along the lines of veins. Pseudoscorpions grip hair like resolute punctuation marks and ride whichever body will take them to the next seam of luck. When a bat falls, the city goes to ground and becomes the floor’s breakfast.

The floor’s people are the bookkeepers. They account for what falls in the dark. There are armies of click and brush, dry-bodied and made for this soft geology of waste: beetles whose heads are tools; rove-beetles that are knives; tiny scarabs with fans for smelling and rakes for pushing; isopods jointed like thoughts, slow and busy; harvestmen, the leg-collective, each one walking a whole weather with the tip of a foot. They live by taste and tremor. They read the cave’s sentences in ammonia and the faint rumble of a bat’s adjustment overhead. Their work is patience and reduction. They take the soft things back apart. They pull a pellet to pieces and turn it into a mat that gives under your weight and then recovers. Millipedes with the patient armor of beads furrow their way through the damp, secreting a chemistry that keeps the mold from getting ideas. Springtails spring, a glitter of tiny refusals, and drink from beads of water with the neat mouths of siphons. Cave crickets come and go like commas, appearing near the drip-line, all knees and fear, tasting the one spot where water made a clean circle, and launching across a country of odor according to rules they do not tell.

Between rock and air the thread-hangers post their nets. A thousand pale soprano glows maintain that what looks like nothing is still a kind of light. Glowworms drop silk into the flight-path of things with fewer guesses than bats; their threads are beaded with spit-sugar, with the elegant meanness of a trap that works because the world is humid. They light their way of eating with a steady ghost-color and take in, by minute wicks, the city of midges that wanders the warm air left behind each exodus.

In the pools, the blind republic. White amphipods with hands like lilies do tight little oarings through the mineral tea. Planaria, translucent but proud, slide their small moons from one meniscus to another. Small salamanders with the patience of stalactites emerge when the drip is generous; they do not need much. They are made of the gesture of lung and fin that the mountain forgot to complete. Here lives a crust of bacteria and a softness of algae that feed on rock and exhale a film you can taste if your mouth is open: nitre, iron, the ghost of distant leaves, rain that has been translated twice.

On the stone itself, other books are being written. Thread-fungi lace the guano and make of it a sponge so fine it feels like language under your hand. In wetter ages of the mat, foxfire shows a low hill of constellations, but even in its darks the hyphae are everywhere, the accountants’ accountants, unlocking locked meals and paying the world its carbon back. In pale streaks along the wall, where drip and breath have conspired for a century, feathery calcite blooms called moonmilk keep their cool, the signature of microbes spelling patiently across a vertical field.

At the very edges, where the last weak blade of sun in a dry week spills itself along a lip, the photosynthesizers keep their threadbare treaties with day. A few stripped-down mosses, a fern with courtly manners toward that brief ceremony, algae thin as a rumor. Out by the cleft’s teeth, seeds brought in a bat’s gut, or shaken loose from an owl’s soft fist, take opportunity and become small, deserving grasses. They stand where the world of pressure and the world of gravity sign papers.

Predators here are tight as stitched seams. A gecko has the size of a hand and the conquest of precisely three stone shelves. It knows the flaws in the varnish by taste; it knows where a cricket will land because that is where water dies into itself. An owl of the entrance makes its whole living from one geometry: the angle at which a bat-arrow will graze the dusk, the sound that opens when a body turns itself thin to slip a tight. The owl’s tenure is written in ruins that go to powder when you look hard and in the green of that opportunist grass. Snakes climb with the patience of letters and bank their eggs in the cave’s heat as if tucking small suns into a sleeping animal’s ribs. Scorpions are the punctuation at the end of the drip. They have made of a circle of spread water a thesis, holding their claws open at exactly the radius a thirsty leg will enter, and then closing the proposition.

A minority of birds live here as if they were bats who learned daylight and then forgot it. Swiftlets thread themselves into the high pockets and dimpled domes near the stone’s thinned memory of sun. They learned to see by click. Their calls are the handmade braille of air. They paste their cups together from spit and salvage and the memory of return, and they inherit these coordinates the way a colony inherits a smell. They share the middle band of breath with the bats by an old treaty no one remembers signing.

Now and again, a cameo from outside: a moth bewildered by ammonias, a mouse that runs all its races at the wrong altitude and lies down suddenly as if listening to stone and does not rise, a pair of rope-skilled people who come down counting softly and depart carrying the concentrate of a season. Their edges are quickly eaten into the local script.

The ones who keep the chamber’s small economies also keep its poems. They time themselves to fog and to the strange pressure-waves that walk up from storms far off. They know what a rockfall means, and when an old ledge unbuttons and the ceiling sheds, they turn shock into routine: the bats move a meter and a decade; spiders redraw a corner; lizards inherit a shelf. They live in offsets and nested windows: the things that hang do not fly when the things that stalk are hungry; the things that drink do it when the air at their height permits; the things that breed do it in the season when the cave breathes inward and the heat is kept.

There are residents so small that you find them by metaphor before you find them by sight. Archaea in the guano films that strike bargains with nitrogen and leave crystals where they have spoken; bacteria whose feasts make the sting at the back of the nose; yeasts whose fizz is no less alive for being inaudible. They make a diet from the wetness of stone and the leftovers of speech. They are the cave’s quiet combustion. They are the reason the air is warmer than its latitude and elevation have any right to be, and the floor remembers a thousand bats by being a thousand bats rewritten.

If there is a single creature that can be said to live here, it is the composite body made from all of them and from the chamber itself. The mountain has an animal at its throat. It has a pelt and a bowel. It keeps time by pour and by evaporation; its lungs are stitched with sonar and threadlight; its pulse is two rushes a day, out and in, and a thousand small tappings that do not agree but harmonize. It learns a little every season: which niche is now a room, which corridor a slipstream, where the salt dries into a sweet. It forgets a little, too: a niche collapses; a long-courted drip turns its mind elsewhere; a gecko grows old and a new one inherits the border and moves it a claw’s-width toward the pool.

What lives here are specialists of small certainties. Hangers, gleaners, threaders, raspers, coilers, waiters, clickers, lappers, riders, counters, and the ones who keep the heat. None of them has built anything you would recognize as a house and yet the place is furnished: by stains and ledges and threads and flows and a thousand repeated returns. They live by sound more than by sight. They live by agreement more than by power. They live by the weather of their own bodies, and the chamber, pleased by this arrangement, goes on warming itself with them.
