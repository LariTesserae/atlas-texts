What lives here are the keepers of intervals. Not of meadows or streams or deep woods, but of the small places between—between warm and cold, wet and dry, sun and shadow, stone and sky. Each has found a way to make a life out of short windows and hard edges. If you hold still long enough, they declare themselves.

The first, oldest residents are the ones that do not leave. The ledge’s skin is a country of lichens and thin algaes, pioneers gloving the raw rock in patient, old alphabets. They take a century to write a line. Each letter is a community: crusts tight as paint, rosettes with raveling rims that only show their colors—yolk, flame, bruise—when the afternoon light flattens. A soft weather lifts from them when the ledge wets; when it dries they close like fists and ride it out, neither alive nor dead in any way a warm-blood would recognize. They feed slowly on wind, dust, and the breath of stone. They are the book the rest of the lives are written into.

The cushions build up from that book. Moss in domes, not lawns—emerald valves in an instrument that only the evening knows how to play. In their close greenery is a weather of its own: vapors that stay when the ledge sheds, a watered air in miniature. Every day those islands wake under a bead-glittered net and put their entire city in motion. Between the stems, in rooms the size of raindrops, a nation eats. Springtails—soot with springs—sling themselves when the light warms. Black beetles the size of seeds walk like slow ambulances. Mites go about it with seriousness. Slender, pearl-pale worms backstitch the substrate and leave behind thin brightness you only see when you lie down to look.

Within those moss-cities are strangers who arrive by bottle and sleep by the year. A tardigrade, curled like a nut for ten dry days or ten dry winters, returns to itself on the first real wet and walks, a bear the length of an eyelash. A rotifer turns its cilia and is suddenly all appetite; a nematode un-wires from the salt of its own antifreeze and threads into new tunnels. The only clock here that keeps perfect time is freeze and thaw, and these small ones have made a pact with it: stop everything when the tick stops, begin again when it begins.

The plants are residents by decision and architecture. Saxifrages that have volunteered their roots into rock fissures and hold the tiny worlds under lean boulders together with their knuckles. Alpine sorrels that taste like sour sun and have learned to become seed if the day turns too quickly. The unnamed blue that could only be brewed in height and thinness, opening as if shy even at noon; a gentian, maybe, or a bellflower that has ground its pigments dark to catch more of the light. The glare-silver grasses streaming like ground-fur in the afternoon wind are not simple: each blade is ridged and hair-lined to trap beads and slow the air, and each clump is a city-state, the roots gossiping in the only wet lines there are—cracks. They have made a livelihood of the ledge’s habits. Edges bring water, so they make themselves into edges upon edges: hairs like dew-hooks, leaf-rims that drag cloud into drops.

A few conifers have come as far as ambition will push a tree. They are all elbows and decisions, burnished steel where a thousand needle-tips gather frost and hold it in sculptures until sun instructs them to let go. Their roots read the rock like veins; whole generations have felt their way along a single fault, the slowest possible pursuit of a trickle. They do not grow up so much as over, measuring their success by how many winters they can fold themselves into without breaking. The sap in them is not fluid but instrument, tuned to the exact cold of this balcony in the sky.

The insects, like the plants, have learned to be local. There are bees, heavier and furred than your memory of bees—queens that spend their mornings shivering to warm the flight muscles, drinking the ledge’s bead-threaded nets like cups. They keep their colonies small in a crack that faces sun and has at least one good drip in late day. They fly short, high-wages routes from blue to blue, making more heat than honey. There are day moths in velvet vests, emissaries of the night who have found that day is colder here and therefore safer from bats; their tongues can find a flower that only opens when a shadow-angle is right. There are flies that arrive as soon as the rill turns from frost to water; within minutes the shelf is lifted by a sheet of them, a visible breath of midges and gnats. They write with their bodies where the sun is strongest. They know how to be born fast.

Spiders lay claim to the human ropes and nets, making the ledge’s deliberate lines into their own fisheries. They live on tide tables made of wind. The hair-and-fiber catchments a long-ago person strung become, every evening, a beach for their prey: droplets bead the threads, flies come to drink, spiders lift their little boats and haul in catch. When the nets go slack in a dry spell, the spiders move to the carved basins and ambush what gathers there. Their lives have learned the knots; their shelters are threaded into the old mends. The human hand has made niches as surely as any glacier.

Birds have residency status of various kinds. The wallcreeper is the ledge’s card trick: stone until not-stone, mouse-gray until it opens and declares itself with velvet wings slashed in blush and black. It lives as a gravity artist, picking insects from sheer faces the way other birds glean leaves, and is always exactly where the slant turns to vertical. If you watch long enough you can tell the old ones from the young by the efficiency of their flights between cracks and ripples.

Swifts—black commas of impossible grammar—arrive not because it is quiet here but because the air, all at once, is written with insects. They do not belong to any one place and yet the ledge seems built for their punctuation: ribs of rock to thread, an empty middle to carve through. They make no nest here that you could find. They spend their lives in air except for the short obligation to lay and feed, and even that they try to do on the wing when they can. They sleep as a kind of glide. They are citizens of a larger country the ledge happens to be a capital of at noon.

Hummingbirds, if the map allows, are day’s small furnaces, green coins minting themselves in front of the blue flowers. If not hummingbirds, then bumblebees in the act of becoming birds: big-bodied sphinx moths that hum by day, hovering with furred thoraxes and tongues like a question they can answer only by unrolling. The ledge has a taste for visitors who can drink standing still in air.

Finches or accentors or snowbirds come as a family to the carved bowl. They have learned a specific flange, a safe perch, a safe angle of approach. They have a schedule for drinking that interleaves with a stoat’s route and a hawk’s patrol. If you sit with your back against a rib of rock you will hear the tiny taps of their feet on stone, little marks of presence that freeze into hieroglyphs at night.

The hawk is not always a hawk; sometimes it is a falcon shape, sometimes only the subtraction of light that says something is hunting. It travels as if sliding along glass, reading the contour from above, dropped only a little, never effortful. The silence when it passes is not silence at all but all the other noises tightening, expectation going through the ledge as evenly as a wind goes through the ribs. It is less an animal than a way of asking the question: who is inattentive now?

Mammals leave tracks like footnotes. Pikas make their announcement like a toy pressed and released, then vanish into the ledger of slabs. They carry the mountain’s hair—glare-grass, moss-drift, single leaves of saxifrage—into haypiles tucked under stones, drying their harvest on hot rock when the sun allows. Their pantry is their masterpiece and their argument with winter. There’s a map in the way each pika arranges it, a way of placing bitter outside sweet because the bitter keeps better. They are also the ledge’s librarians; you can read the season in what is in their mouths.

Stoats braid themselves into this place. Think of a dropped rope that has come alive, a white switch in winter, a tea-stain russet in summer. They look like waste heat made visible. They know every threshold and traverse—rope, seam, shallow—and they move by rules you do not know you’ve learned until one passes and the air seems to thicken a little. They take pikas when they can, and sometimes a finch at the bowl if the timing is wrong. They den where a human once hid from wind, using old soot as a medicine against lice. They wait at the rope over the cleft because others must sometimes use it.

Goats, or whatever this range chooses for goats—ibex, tahr, chamois—are occasional but not rare. Their tracks write the mornings after clear nights, then fade by noon. They come not to feed so much as to look, to rub their horns and remind the rock of bone. Their hooves make a soft talk with stone that you hear only if you have been quiet long enough to be part of the conversation: tk-tk, tk. Their bodies are large and their effect is small; they pass through as if the place had asked them to and they were keeping a promise.

Lizards live here only in time. The sun-minute lizards—rock dragons no longer than a hand—have exactly fifteen minutes in the pocket where a certain angle between walls focuses heat into a shallow nap. They materialize, digest, breed if there’s a chance, vanish when cloud removes the pocket. They are gone at the brush of a hawk’s shadow. They are not evangelical; they make no claim on the whole ledge, only on the moment’s island of warmth that appears in the same place every day forever until a flake falls and changes the map.

The air and water are residents as well, not in the poetic sense but in the practical one that all the others live by their routines. Upslope wind is a person here, punctual in the afternoons, carrying valley wet like cargo and laying it out in the catchments. It knows the ribs and makes an instrument of them, a faint hum you can hear if you turn your head. Its music changes with the weather not because the wind decides and undecides but because the space between things grows wider or thinner as frost writes and unwrites its letters on the ledge. The rill that runs only in sunlight is a creature of plan and failure; it is alive when it is alive, it has a spine and a gait, it has a habit of stopping with dignity. The bowls are filled by a sky that is forgetful; the nets pearl, the beads fall, stone sips, plants drink.

People come the way goats come: rarely, correctly. The ones who have come have been herders, monks, surveyors, fugitives, lovers of stones, whatever names you give to those who walk without imagining they can change a place by being in it. They leave small things. A beehive shelter of stacked slabs with smoke kiss on its ceiling no thicker than your finger. Windbreaks set on edge that do not oppose the wind so much as show it how to flow. A gutter of hollowed branch, mended with rawhide where it split years ago by a freeze. A rope across the rill’s mischief wound with knots that teach you the hands that tied them knew what wet and cold do to fiber. Nets woven from hair and plant stems, a web for water, that catch and hold a night’s fog and drip almost like thought.

Those things have become citizens. Moss grows greener under nets; spiders farm there; finches drink from the bowls. The stones used for shelter shade a garden that would not otherwise find shade. A sliver of chalcedony left from sharpening sits on a ledge and attracts sun like candy, and a lizard uses it once a day for a fireplace. Hair caught in a knot becomes nest lining for a bird that hatches before a dry week and needs extra warmth. Humans as a species are not residents of this shelf, but humans as hands have made residences that the ledge has adopted.

Everything that feeds feeds twice. Droppings and leavings are not wasted in a place like this; a single pellet papered against a slab is a feast. A spider drinks water from a goat hair trapped in a knot. A lichen grows around a flake of obsidian because it holds heat a second longer at dusk. A beetle cleans pollen from a dead bee’s leg and becomes a link that would not exist in a valley. The nutrient cycle is not a circle but a braid that tightens when the wind knocks and unlooses a little when the nets hang dry.

Predation is intimate and fast. The hawk’s slide over the ledge tightens the air; the swifts’ sudden upholstering of the middle distance says the rill has woken. Stoats open a seam and silence the pika’s toy sound. A lizard miscalculates a minute. A bee drinks at the bowl in a cold hour and must be turned gently onto warm stone by the sun to reanimate; sometimes the sun is late, and a wallcreeper takes it before it can hum again. The life that loses is given to life that carries on; no sermon is preached about it but the rules are plain.

There are residents so small you will not meet them unless you grant a full day to a square hand of shelf. Fungal hyphae knit dead moss into bread for the next colony. A single dark-capped sedge sends up a seed head twice and is punished twice by the dry year, then learns to cheat by making new plants from itself along the crack. A scarab ten grains of rice heavy carries a green jewel of goat pellet as if it were a planet. The frost that grows from the windward side of a stalk—petaled ice that reaches toward light—is a plant in the sense that it grows by a rule and dies by sun.

To say who lives here is also to say how time lives here. Morning’s residents are the ones who can build heat inside themselves or borrow it from black stone: bees, pikas turning last-summer hay into breath, lichens opening their photos to the linear and cold light. Noon brings the brash air-feeders: swifts, flies in sheets, lizards on their hearths. Early afternoon is everyone at once, a market that never becomes a riot. Evening folds it back down along known lines: dew-formers, rope-fishers, plants knitting sugar as the sun leaves and the ledge pulls its cloak of wet up around its shoulders. Night belongs to things quiet even by this place’s standards: moths crossing windless hours on feet that star the thin snow, bats that appear only if the wind fails entirely, stoats on their terrible private errands, the stone’s own settling and tea-kettle pops. Stars take up residence so close they seem domestic.

On a dry day—the rare day when the sky keeps its water and the ledge cannot coax a skin from it—the residents do what they do best: they trust their stores. The moss closes its palisade leaves about its center and rides the lack. The bees stay home and share heat shoulder to shoulder. The finches change their route to a trickle at the traverse that only forms every fifth day and waste an hour flying there and back because thirst is dangerous here. The wallcreeper works the shade where bugs hide from brightness because all the windows have narrowed at once. The ledge holds its breath the way an animal can if it has to.

If you are part of this place for a while, you learn faces. You learn the stoat whose right ear is nicked into a little dagger; the pika that favors sorrel early in the season and grasses late; the finch that steps twice and then drinks; the soap-sheen beetle that always tries the sunflake on the obsidian first and is almost always too early. You learn that an old lizard misses more flies than a young one because it is not hungry enough to make big mistakes. You learn that the smallest conifer, the one with three elbows like a crooked finger, has been setting cones for ten years and losing them to dry wind for nine and may, this one time, make a tree from itself if no goat manages to pass and browse the tip. You learn the song the ribs of rock make on the windiest afternoons, a thin fluted note you can only hear if you stop counting your own breaths.

The residents are not a list; they are the grammar of a place where the subject is edge. Among them are names you know and many you won’t. Their common project is to take the negative of water, the lent coin of warmth, the brief rents in glare and blue, and spend them like geniuses. The ledge gives them almost nothing and exactly enough. They live where the world shows how much life can grow around a few good lines. In a room whose walls are kicked out into sky, they have learned how to be both close and far, how to make a parish out of particles, how to begin again in the next clear hour.
