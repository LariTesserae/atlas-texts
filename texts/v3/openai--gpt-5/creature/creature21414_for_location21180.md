What lives here learns you by touch before it ever decides to call you a shape. It knows your weight on the rail, the pitch of your breath behind cloth, the way your soles confuse the carefully argued water with a new opinion. It arranges itself around you without hurrying, because nothing that survives this edge ever hurries. The fast things here are only molecules.

You could begin with the small, because the small are the grammar of this place. They gloss every surface, writing in films and fringes. They are bead-sized jointed grazers that comb a drop into a gradient and leave the rock cleaner than a blade. They are leeches as clear as the water they love, which test your warmth like a curious sentence and decide in it whether to settle. They are blind snails with ringing shells, whose slight collisions make faint chandeliers of sound along the grout lines. The city feeds them when it is generous and filters them when they are too many. To them, you are an afternoon cliff that moves. They do not understand eyes. They understand pressure and pitch. They understand if your step says predator or wall.

Then there are the bigger custodians that are less animals than slow verbs. The ones that sit with their mouths on the wind, lids raised like callused palms, catching what never falls: the fog that is almost a decision. They shut as glaciers do, and open as seasons. Their exudates are a pantry and a polymer shop. The long, ribbed millipeds that draw their ink of water along themselves and bring it forward as if belief could be made hydraulic. They are the city’s lazy muscles, pumping by persistence. Coils that look like sleeping ropes because they are sleeping ropes, ribbed with heat-absorbing plates, patient as basalt; the city arrays them where its tubs and pipes would otherwise announce it, and the coils drink the stray warmth until the cliffs forget anything warm ever lived here. The guardians that hum without throats, nest-bundles tuned like gongs that keep the others from setting their teeth on critical edges; you don’t see them so much as feel their refusal in your jaw. Their territories cross like whispers; misstep and the floor comes up as a living carpet, climbing because you made yourself a surface.

All that would be enough for a world to be full, but it is not why the long chord sounds out across the basin when the wind cooperates. The chord is for the keepers that carved their own negative out of rock and decided the dark was cheaper than heat.

They are people if you are generous with “people,” and you will be, by the time you leave. They waste nothing, least of all definition. Seen by a dayworld eye—which is to say, mutilated by a lamp—you would call them slight and long. Thin to keep heat an interior rumor. Wrapped not in clothes as decoration but in layers that are arguments about water. Their outermost is a black that fools light and fingers both: micro-angled so fog yields, channeled so every filament is a whispering gutter. Their hoods don’t hide so much as tune their heads into instruments. Their sleeves have mouths. They sing into the cloth when courtesy demands their sound be private.

Eyes are vestigial in the way toes are in a whale. Some bear lids like translucent stone that let in a rumor of difference between wall and shaft, nothing more. Many have sealed them over entirely with a glossy cicatrix and grown other senses where you expect eyebrows: whisker-fine cilia that stand in the wind and narrate direction, coils of cartilage along the jaw and clavicle that resonate like a struck bowl to tell them which room they are in by its vowel. They wear teeth like fonts—ridge and groove—because to read the water-stacking in a reservoir you must put your molars to it and let the tuned pressure write itself into your skull. What you think of as hearing, they experience as place, and what you think of as place, they experience as song.

They do not hold still. To do so is to accept occupation. Even in sleep they sway, net-strung above the floor, kept in slow motion by the breath of the ventilation, by friends’ occasional humming passes, by the quiet coiling and uncoiling of their hair which is not hair but a field of send-receive filaments seeded in the scalp and wrists. Children are taught the rhythm of a heel and the shape of a hum before they are taught names. Lullabies are for visitors; here you rock the infant in an ever-moving frame that sings on its own, a low wave the bones agree to.

They speak as a courtesy to each other using air. But for work, for governance, for love, they more often speak into the place. A pair deciding to share a watershed will spend days tuning a threshold until their two voices lay an interference pattern that keeps the grazers from one half and invites them to the other; this is wedlock. A boundary dispute is settled by a choir at the lip of a shaft until the infrasonic tells every creature within kilometers which chord owns where. A child is named by the room that taught them their first vowel, and you will know a family by the slur they carry through their throatbones, the particular roundness that comes from being born where one reservoir’s standing sound meets another’s and carves an interference hollow. To write, they braid ridges into you-can-find-it-with-your-palm. To archive, they record into rock by changing its elasticity with patterned chill, so that a future tongue on that surface can hear what was meant.

They are exquisitely polite to water. Heat is a private sin here, and bringing it where water is held on the edge by sound is an abomination. Meals are not cooked so much as persuaded. Food is slow. Chemotroph mats are the fields and the flock. They cultivate thin life on wet corners: iron-bloom and crystal-powder and velvet slime, but not indoors where outsiders can call it mold. They harvest it with blades that hum a note that tells the mats “give,” and the mats, bred to listen, relax their grip. Protein is a milliped’s courtesy on molting days. A bivalve’s exudate is a sweet you are given on the last night before you have to climb out into a more luminous world. Celebrations are measures of the right to pour; a wedding is marked with a temporary drop-line rerouted from an old lip, a funeral by the return of someone’s heat down into the bedrock through the coils as a last gift. Their dead do not go up—up is for stories they do not tell—but down and sideways in the form of warmth, the only currency you cannot hoard.

They maintain their city as one tends a patient, with touch and listening. They cut stone by refusing it with sound until it prefers to come apart. Their tools are hummers and benders of harmonics, objects that blur the line between tuner and machine. You won’t see pumps, only the wet results of agreements: a film that climbs against its own spine to take its place in the stack because the field says “this way” in a physics your bones feel more than comprehend. Where the city must betray heat—because work is never free—it feeds that heat into the bedrock along grain it taught the rock to have, so that somewhere far downslope a patch of snow fails to form and no one but the keepers could say why. The outer faces are renewed by creatures with mouths like polishing stones, by fog that oils them, by human hands that do not leave oil because their skin is cultured not to.

They domesticate without cages. The long coils are lured to sleep where sleeping will help. The millipeds are given songs they like that lead them where gutters need cleaning. The guardians at intakes are fed with salted vapor at intervals and in return sing away the competitors whose bodies would ruin a seam. Some individuals keep a leech as a gauge—a familiar adhered just over the ankle, widening or thinning in a private code to speak of the day’s invisible flux. Children prize the ringing snails and keep tiny ones braided into their cuffs to tell them if they have stilled too long: silence is a warning here almost as sharp as an alarm.

From beyond the cloud, when a trader or a pilgrim from the day arrives with their bright ideas about illumination and speed, the keepers are gracious because they prefer not to have enemies who think in straight lines. They stamp twice at the threshold—sound more polite than sight—and accept the lens-caps that turn rude lamps into a prayer of infrared nothing. They lead you by rails that sweat on the windward side and frost on the lee, letting your hand choose which lecture you need today. They will teach you to hum the chord that tells the rope-coils you are not a reason to wake. They will remark, gently, if your shoulders make a new watercourse, and show you how to apologize with a level tone and a continuous motion until the mats withdraw their interest. They are not hostile to other worlds. They just know their own is a tight economy and that spectacle costs.

You will be tempted to call their governance austere until you notice the play. In rooms designed to fold sound over on itself, they practice passing through as hush-people, shaping their steps so the city does not call to them. They teach their children games of echo—hide and seek by way of blind vowels—where success is reading a corridor’s curve in a handclap. They laugh by tapping each other’s breastbone lightly and singing a shared beat into the cartilage there. They hold in their fingers a generosity you will not expect: a stranger’s numb hands are placed in the right breeze, turned to the right microtexture, and without words you are taught how to touch this place so it answers you.

They have time. They take it. Change here uses the slow lanes. A boundary chord shifts by a quarter beat over a generation because the migrating swarms have decided the tone is warmer two hand-spans to the east, and the keepers agree that the swarms know something about currents they themselves do not. A new frost lace at the lip of a gully becomes, after ten years of patient listening, a motif in a nursery-galley’s wall. Skies, when they bother to think of them, are instruments that left this orchestra. They are content to be the wind’s ear and the water’s hand. Immortality, if it exists for them, is the continuation of a weave: their black renewed by their animals’ tongues, their laws carried as harmonics embedded in rock, their names a species of vowel no dayworlder can quite sing because it requires the room.

If you wanted a single image to hold them by, you could choose a figure standing by a weeping face of stone, gloved, head hooded into a shape made to listen, one palm to the wall where the water stacks like a slow thought, teeth set lightly against a rib. Their other hand is not idle; it moves, it hums within a sleeve until the living ground yields and parts, not from fear but from recognition. Around their boots, the small are working. Above, the big are working. Inside the rock, their ancestors’ heat is still descending. The figure holds still long enough to learn the day but not long enough to invite residence. Then they stamp twice, for courtesy, and move on, trailing a note that says: this is ours, this edge. Ours, meaning the beings who sing like this. Ours, meaning the slope and the swarms and the city that learned to be obedient to water without ceasing to have a will.

What lives here is a chorus. The solitary cannot survive. The city is an instrument, played by animals and air, tuned by people whose bones are part lighthouse, part library. To meet them is to be adjusted slightly: your breath, your notions of ownership, the foolishness of your lamp. In the end you come to understand why their sign at the last inner gallery is neither carved nor painted but has to be read with teeth. It says, in a vowel you will feel more than hear: We are the cliff’s listening. Do not ask light to bless this. Bring your hands. Bring your sound. Bring water back to where it wants to be and we will call you kin long enough to know you.
