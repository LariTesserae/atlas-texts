They come out of the glare already making heat, already making music.

The largest of them move in lines the way wind moves between organ pipes: long-bodied grazers with black, oiled hides and the square steadiness of machines. Their backs carry houses of mineral: latticed honeycombs of iron and manganese salts cradling blood as if in crucibles. They feed the boilers constantly—mouths low to the upholstery, cropping the sweet from mats, licking rime, grinding a ration of grit that keeps the chemistry awake. Their ribs barely lift. Heat happens inside them as a project, not a byproduct, and you can hear it where the body narrows to a muzzle: the slow hiss of a combustor that has been learned, not inherited. When they are content their steam whispers straight; when they are anxious the slotted lips squeeze and the whistle goes up through the cliffs, pitched to carry along stone.

They are tender with each other in ways that look like engineering. Calves—smaller boilers with thin, translucent racks of salt not yet blacked—are carried inside a skirt of elders. The adults angle their radiators to make a scaffold that steals light from the young and trades it for heat; they set their feet a minute apart in tread so the ground hum becomes even and the calves’ springs do not jangle. You might call them names and family if you had names that could take a year to say. Their social grammar is long pulses riding the plateau’s rails. An old line hums to mark a mineral lead they’ve found, and somewhere a day’s march along, a sister line hears that hum come in the right register through its bones and turns left like a train at a junction.

In the wake of every boiler-line is a second population that looks like weather given hunger. Condenser flocks flicker as bands of jointed vanes, all underside and intake. Their soft tubes comb dew-beads out of the air—the air that has just been warmed, fractionally, in the lee of a herd; the air that flowers where cushion combs kick dew down in a silver. They drink by hundreds, a bristled uplift that you feel in your enamel. When the pressure changes announce noon they take wing as one book being shut and opened; the sound has a hinge in it. They are as social as wind can be. Flocks break and braid, streaming into the densest lanes of vapor like smoke finding a leak. They store water in bladders banded to shed sparks when they are nearly full, and in storms the whole flock flashes like a swarm of paper being rubbed together.

The fixed feeders build by adhering to one another until their own bodies turn into wall. A cliff face that on one day is bare will, on the next, develop a gloss of mouths pressed against the flow: backplate on frontplate, each with its veil of radiators and its lips salted bright by the fog. They do not build fast. They slow the air and take from it what mineral and wet it will give, growing glaze, growing weight, glowing slightly at their edges on blue days when the world ticks. When their bellies pass a threshold, you see a ripple rise along their length as the fullness moves like a command. They detach one from another with quiet clicks and peel away to lie down in a darker seam to digest, leaving behind a faint varnish that makes the cliff sing sharper when the wind returns.

Under the overhangs and in the knuckle-caves live the first soil-makers, pale things that carry winter against their skins. Milky slugs with ice gloves take the mountain into their mouths and push it out the other end as broth-dark silt. You can follow them by the taste of copper in the air; their tongues are toughened nets of conductive tissue, and when they flex them against a magnetite seam you can smell a push of charge like the breath before lightning. Their bodies are riddled with galleries where whole cities of smaller things pack up and move when the slug moves, so the same caravan of microbes and mites and filamentous farmers stitches seam to seam for a lifetime. These slugs house libraries. The bacteria lining their guts remember the difference between one basalt and the next, and the slugs will overlay one rock with another’s smell until the cushion above it changes its mineral draw and its color goes from oxblood to bruised green in a week.

The cushions themselves are not one thing but a treaty. From a distance they look simple—domes, felt, velvet knobs that shed spark if you are rude—but press your fingers in (careful; you will be punished if you are clumsy) and you feel muscles. The outer hair is all rake and hook to pull down wet. Beneath, sheets of cells slide on each other like a miniature piston, spreading moisture across a woven lung. Inside, galleries run bright as yolk in a broken egg, and the syrup that beads there is a currency. Minute mouths crowd the eaves and sip. The cushion feeds them and the mites and rib-worms groom its combs and peel any frost-burned plate and sew the rents and—most crucially—raid the first soil in loops and bring the sourness back in mouthfuls. If the cushion is an organism, it is also a city-state with a weather industry and a police: tiny charge-loving predators that electrify themselves along axial belts and spring toward any lip that sucks too long without paying into the syrup. It is all neat and it is all violent, and most of the story of this place happens too close to see.

Predators move here like tools raised out of a bench—vises, presses, clamps. The land and the prey do not allow a sprint. Pressers are folds of muscle and bladder under felt that look like cushion until they do not. They thin themselves against a rock face and wait out a week, or two, or a chunk of the season, tuned to their prey’s hum. When a grazer’s steam steps into their quiet, the pressers flood their skin with ions, their flanks stiffen along crystal spines, and the fold comes closed. The caught animal is not sliced. It is held and vented. The press bleeds heat first, whistling it through tuned slots that make other pressers hum in sympathy. Only when the body has gone amber-warm do the jaws harden into plates and the eating begins. Smaller pressers hunt the flocks, launching when a chord crosses a rock seam at just the right frequency to load their springs. Their attacks are less kills than blottings: a thinness of flesh thrown like a sheet that irons a dozen vanes into stillness. The violence ends slow and then very fast when the charges migrate and an arc jumps. There is a lot of smoke here for how little fire ever truly burns.

There are also thieves. Furnace-leeches, the size of a palm and hard to see until they turn their sides and their dorsal ridges strobe in the aurora, hook heat and iron from the folds of a grazer. They do not draw blood; they draw flux. They cling for a day, or a season if they find a crease where two plates meet and the hum is rich. Grazers perform hygiene on each other at rest. They shoulder against obsidian like violists against the backs of chairs, sliding until the sparks run thick—zzzz, tick, tick—and the leech loses purchase. Younger animals practice this together until they can tune the friction-sparks into chords that make spiders fall from their webs. It is music and medicine at once.

Not everything earns water by taking it raw. There are milkers—beings that coax it out of other bodies. Between a cushion’s condensers you will sometimes see a green-black scrawl like handwriting: a creeping composite of fungus and animal that lays down a tiny field to be a cloud for itself. It circles a comb’s tip and kisses it, and the comb steams. The milker gathers the brief rain into its own fringe and then touches the cushion again, paying. It carries both dew and small mineral tastes from comb to comb in a route there is no map for in your kind of page. If you pry one up (again: careful; all things here will defend), the underside is scaled with mouths that are somewhat aware, and if you hold it too long the scales will line themselves along your pulse and your bones will taste like a struck fork.

The plateau has singers that are not walls and not boilers. Thin as ropes, dark as bladewater, they live in cracks too small for fingers, strand-body anchored by fanned feet, head held in the cold. They stream out only when the sky goes hard blue and the big currents set. Their sides shimmer with mica and frozen oils; they pick up charge as a leaf picks up rime. When they are half full they begin to hum. It is not a sound so much as a hand laid on your chest. The pitch climbs with the wind and with their load. If you wait until just before all the heat drains from the face of the day you can see their hum make lines in the aurora, thin green rivulets bending toward them. These are the cartographers. The grazers take their headings from rope-singers; the flocks lay their courses along their drawn notes; filter-walls align their plates to be tuned by them because it feeds better when the song is right. If you touch a rope-singer when it hums, every hair on your body remembers how old it is to be a root.

Scale has diplomats. Between the level where a larva’s best trick is to stiffen itself like a snapped spring with a stripe of quartz, and the level where a herd steps one foot and then waits for an afternoon before it sets another, there are not many animals—but the ones there broker everything. They are the wide-headed ones with bones like pot rims: plate-headed listeners that live where the ground’s talk is loudest. They have no boilers. Their mouths have no interest in dew. They eat only rhythm. They lounge in rib-lands with their skulls pressed to dikes and feel who is coming and whose track crosses whose and what hum has been laid over what hum for the last forty days. They keep no territory you would see. The plateau feeds them a tax of information and they pay it back by laying their bodies across seams the way you would lay a hand across a string to mute it. In famine-time they do more: they lever themselves to block a tremor and send a line of grazers away from an area where pressers have learned too much. There is a sort of thinking in them that looks like giving up fat to make the music right.

When reproduction happens, it happens to schedule, because everything here has learned what schedule is. Boiler-calves are brick-warm the hour they are born because their mothers feed the salts early, nursing them not with milk but with a drip of syrup spiked to change iron into something that will hold heat. Flocculent sheets torn from a filter-wall will take root on any clean cliff lip but only in the three-day window after a crust-brine pool has blinked open and exhaled a certain sulphide. Condenser flocks have a moltphase that looks like weather: a fallout of microvanes that litter lee-slopes like beetle elytra and then vanish into cushions, adopted and made into extra comb. Pressers send out coin-thin buds from the mouths of their traps and glue them, headfirst, to rock, where they drink charge until they get a dark, decisive eye and tear themselves free.

Everything here composes with electricity because the rock does. A mite will sting and leave behind not itch, but a bright aftertaste of lemon on the tongue where an ion exchange has printed a map of where not to step. A cushion will strike you with a crack that jumps a knuckle’s width, just enough to alarm the tender muzzles of a grazer and keep the boiler-line from eating the mat to puddle. And there is language where corona meets breath. You have seen at dusk how the aurora grinds low like a slow river. In that shallowed river, the long-bellied grazers stand as if stalled and then turn one by one and line up as needles. They hum, not to mark their place this time but to say their names. Herds are made of names; a name is a history that can be sung in pieces every evening for the months it takes for the sun’s angle to change and then regrouped into something else when the sky says it can. They do not remember by storing. They remember by passing the song around the line until each body knows a different fifth of it. If a predator kills one, the song has a hole for a season. If a calf is well, the song grows a new edge, a bright novice note that does not settle into the chord for years.

There is, under all this, a deeper citizenship that looks nothing like anyone you could touch. The whole crest, with its panpipes and boilers and sheets and mufflers and slugs and thieves and milkers, uses light and frost and iron to lift a continent’s worth of water up to its own shoulder and then pass it inward. Living things here do work for the plateau and in return the plateau keeps a climate for them, a sort of cruel homeostasis where most days look like the inside of a blade and most nights feel like metal. The inhabitants are artisans in that factory. They tune and are tuned. They seldom hurry because the metronome that runs through them is older than running; because stepping fast breaks rails of tone that need years to lay; because even the smallest of them can get a day’s profit from a rim of rime if it has the right comb and the patience to let the dew walk down into it rather than be drunk in a gulp.

Sometimes a traveler, if there were travelers that high, would look for faces and find none. But if you stand long enough until your own breath has fallen into the slow of the place, you start to see how much of a mind there is in a boiler’s decision not to whistle, in a flock’s swell across a denser seam of haze, in a filter wall’s choice to peel today and not tomorrow when the fog is richer in the glitter that scalps their lips, in a mute-eater’s stillness stretched across a seam so the hum bends. You would see that everyone here counts—to a hundred, to a million, to the length it takes a stone to get a first skin: counting steps, counting sparks, counting the time between dew coming up and dew going down and making a life out of waiting between those numbers for the right rhythm to arrive.

So who lives here? Engines that grew heart and habit. Moss that became a city with police and waterworks. Walls that feed and then leave and then return to the same lip like monks. Slugs that carry farms under an ice-glove. Flocks of membrane that churn the air until the air answers back. Predators like presses. Mites that lick voltage. Listeners that starve themselves to tune the ground. And over all of them rides a sense that the plateau is not simply where they live, but the instrument they play: a black, shining violin bowing its own weather while everything with a pulse adds its note to the slow, intense cordage of the world.
