The first resident is the note. Before anything with legs, before any worker learned the catwalk by feel in the soles, the drone moved in and made a territory of air. It breeds in the towers, sheds skins over the plates, puts down roots in the bones of whoever stands long enough. It claims and yields at once. The animals are born into it the way fish are born into pressure. The people tune and feed it; it keeps the herd from scattering and, just as faithfully, it keeps the people small and precise, walking their lattice a man’s height above the ground, a flock on perches.

Below the lattice, on and between the paths written in sound, the little grazers thicken wherever a surface is briefly alive. They are thumb-length and beetle-plain, a spill of gray and dull silver that moves as one organism made of a thousand tricks. Their legs are hair-fine; each foot tests the tone of a surface as much as its slickness. They do not chew so much as lap and comb—rasping veils of mineral bloom, teasing water to the lip, drinking with their skins. They arrive early in the sequence: first touch after a wet. They leave tiny scalloped tracks in the rime when they cross a plate the workers have set to quiet, and a matte path in the shine where they have gently eaten air’s fall back to metal and stone.

Behind them come the scullers, palm-broad, smooth-belied. They move like thinking mops, wide paddles flexing in alternating strokes, each pull spreading what the grazers gathered into an even film. Where the grazers freckle, the scullers feather. They are fond of seams, of gentle slopes that end in a lip. Their senses are the senses of damp: gradients of cold felt through their bellies, a hum at the edge of hearing that tells them which plate is speaking and which is merely resonating. If you look from a catwalk at the right dawn, you can see a whole sheet of them tilt in the same instant, aligning to a new whisper from a tower louver opened half a hand’s breadth. Their patience looks like intelligence because it shares a posture.

Sutured into the yard’s own sutures are the seam-worms, thin as a drawn thread, long as an unrolled sleeve. They sleep like seams in fact: indistinguishable until the yard asks them to wake. A pulse shivers through a row of plates, almost subsonic, and a hundred black stitches flex and peristalse. They are the yard’s wringers. Inside them are sponges and cords; when they contract, they draw water down to gutters and sumps the way a fist draws blood through a cloth. Their skins are a little luminous with whatever salt they have handled last. On quiet fogless days they do not move at all. On rain-days they draw silver lines over everything they have been told to call down.

The center of presence, though, is with the bellies. The workers call them that because any other word would make them smaller than they are. A belly is the size of a milking stool, layered and slow, its shell like dry paper that has been wet and dried and oiled a hundred times. They are not armor so much as storage; each lamina can take on held damp, held salt, held heat, and give it back on a timetable older than any foreman in the sheds. Their faces—if they have such a thing—are indistinct; they are all underside and edge. Lips at the margins, unseen mouths along the skirts. They move in lanes as true as painted lines, but the lanes can be remade by a new standing wave pulled across the basin, and the bellies will bow and re-sort with the dignity of tides.

A belly eats with its whole perimeter. The grazers and scullers prepare the meal; the belly lifts what remains, teases out the fraction that will bind, extrudes what the sheds need. Exudates bead along hidden ducts; pellets settle in a wake, chalk to the eye, soapstone to the hand, each denser than you expect with a mineral logic coaxed from common wet. Their leavings are the yard’s crop, and the bellies leave them by ritual as much as metabolism. Some lanes are for laying; others are for resting; others are for molting—when a belly shucks a sheet and the workers carry it with tongs to hang in the dark and teach it to be useful.

They do not breed seasonally, not here; their cycles are keyed to the year’s small shifts in the sky’s pitch. When the longest null opens across the basin in late cool, the bellies turn slow circles and the innermost ones flatten, the way slow dough settles. Under them, under their long plates, are little bells: soft, pearly capsules that will be pried up later by hands in thick gloves and set in cradles of fine net over humming plates that will tell them, by tone alone, what to become. If the keeper sings the wrong note at the wrong day, the bellies do not die; they simply become other. The yard keeps a vocabulary of wrongs that turned out not to be. Some of those are prized, kept aside for readier storm-work, or for laying extra-hard pellets, or for living at the rim where the watch-birds keep their patrols.

The watch-birds are not birds; they are a long hinge and two long legs and a sail of ear, a skeleton made lyric. They do not fly. They run and stop and run and stop along the alleys between houses, hopping up onto a fence to make a little bowl of quiet around a step or a threshold. They live on tone, by which is meant they eat what tone makes accessible: the moth-weak scraps of fungal mistake in a gutter corner, the skins of worms when the worms have outgrown them, the stray sculler who lost the beat and drifted under an eave. Their ears are membranes stretched bronze-thin; when they open them fully they shape a hush the shape of their gaze. They sleep with their ears furled like sailcloth, head tucked against the mast of their spine, one foot resting lightly on the other. They are tended by the yard’s children, who comb the dust out of their ears with copper pins and carry tiny trays of warmed brine to keep their throats flexible.

Smaller presences, countless and without bones, make the thin skins over everything that can be called a face. They are iridescence taken personally. They are rules following themselves: this much creep and no more, this much sugar pulled from a trickle before the hum tells them to halt. Under a louvers’ drip the films are bold; under a catwalk’s shadow they are shy. They are eaten as soon as they declare what they are. They do not mind. Their life is boundary: to make one; to be one; to be erased and be the memory of a margin in the grazers’ bellies.

Inside the sheds live the worker’s other familiars: the dynamos with their sleepy eyes of mica, the mills that roll a mash of pellets into bricks. The machines do not live as beasts live, but they have tempers and appetites. There is the black-bottomed vat that makes a soft plate out of exudate and time: if you hurry it, it weeps and will not cure; if you leave it into the third bell of a fogless night it firms with a ghost of gloss that means some property no one can name will never leach from it again. Old motors vibrate a certain way that the herd has learned; when those fail, a new motor humming purer can bewilder an entire flank of bellies and make them walk through a fence that has no posts. The sheds keep the old ones burning not for nostalgia but because the animals know their dialect.

People live here, but you do not see them much on the ground. They are lean with steps and sure with ladders. They wear their boots as if they were extra bones. Children learn to walk the lattice before they learn to fear falling off it. Old ones walk it with a metronome in their hips. There are lineages and trades, but they run together like braids. You will hear them call each other by the tools they tend: Plate, Baffle, Gauge, Sump, Stringer, Brick. If you stay you will learn that those are not families but offices, and that a person can be given a new name when they take a new tone into their care.

The tone-keepers are a small priesthood without blessing or scold. They are technicians who sing. Their job is to know the yard not as a map but as a memory of vibration. Each can hum the yard into being with lips closed, so a child sleeping on their chest will dream of plates thrumming and bellies turning and will not cry at thunder because thunder here is a cousin, not an invasion. When a tone must change, the keepers adjust nothing quickly. They call a meeting and bring out the boards with chalk lines where someone long dead once traced the first safety. They wait for the cool to be right, for the damp to be ordinary. They hang cloth over certain faces so that if a lane wants to slide it will slide into a safety the animals already half-believe is there. Then they move a shutter one fin’s width and stand very still until their own ribs forget the difference. Governance here is a hand on a louver and a promise in a hum.

They do not farm in the way you expect. Their food comes with the traders down the polite channel from the slow river: sacks of grain, barrels of pickles, nets of fish that do not taste of weed because there is no weed to taste. In their own kitchens they ferment and stew. They keep a fungus in dark cabinets that they feed with a thin extract from the vats; it blooms into pale sheets that they layer and grill and call meat with a shrug. In the mornings, someone warms flat bread on a platen that was never meant to cook and spreads it with a salt jam boiled down from the mistake films. On feast nights they crisp a cured belly-plate and eat it like crackling. No one outside believes this when told; no one inside can quite explain why it is good.

No one plants trees. No one weaves wreaths of green. They decorate with shine and seize. Children make necklaces of spent washers and shards of mica. Lovers press their fingers into a copper sheet while it is warm and keep the print until it greens despite everything. Funerals are quiet. They place the body on a bier and pass under the watch-birds and into the yard at night, when the dome of light is soft and the tone is a little lower so that the worms will not wake. They lift the body to a certain plate held long in reserve for this, a plate whose hum is tuned to carry sorrow away as slowly as dew, and they stand on the lattice while the condensing air gives the dead back their moisture and the grazers take the bloom and the bellies lay a little extra, not because they are told but because the yard knows how to be kind.

Visitors from the flats come sometimes with spindly boats slid across the old mud furrows. They stand at the rim and squint as if they could see what the sound is doing. They cannot. They notice only that the people here hold themselves as if laden with an invisible tray they will not spill. The traders leave with bricks that will end up as filters in cities that do not believe in animals as machines, or in vats where a reaction will only behave if it is persuaded by a remembered note baked into a fired salt. They leave with stories of watch-birds that hush a barking dog from a courtyard away, of children who walk rails with buckets and never slosh, of a place that is louder than any fair and yet makes your hands slow instead of fidget.

Other things live here that are not quite countable. There are ghosts of old machines, which is to say there is the way a replaced plate will hum with a remnant of a pitch it no longer has; people lay hands on those and call them grandfathered. There is the smell of lionized iron the first minute after a rain, which is a resident in the same way the daily shade pattern is a resident: it shows up for itself and everyone else learns to incorporate it. There is the way the rail shivers into your wrist if you lean on it longer than sense; you carry that tremor out with you and will wake later to find your own pulse and the yard’s having synchronized without permission.

And there are absences that live here with the insistence of presences. There are no leaves. There is no rustle except of a thousand legs moving like cloth rubbed briskly. There is no dog that will follow you across the ground; even the riverfolk shy their animals at the rim. There is no day when the song stops. If you imagine what a silence here would sound like, you do not imagine well; it would be the opposite of quiet, it would be panic wearing vacuum.

So who lives here? The herd that is also a factory. The people who are also an ear. The watch-birds who are tympani on stilts. The seam-worms and the grazers and the scullers, the bellies who take what the air gives and make what distant engines will trust. The films that do not last and thus participate. The machines with their habitual shivers that have entered the animals as inheritance. The tone itself, older than your visit and immune to your leaving, that holds all of them in shapes that are not cages but customs. Together they are the yard’s creature: not a chimera, not a colony animal, but an arrangement—change braided so fine it behaves as stillness, patience made audible, life taught to move where a foundation of sound has drawn the lines.
