What lives here has chosen the seam between intentions. It is not a kingdom but a practice, a thin economy, and its citizens are the ones who can afford to be slow.

First are the films that eat iron and breathes in gradients. They lie across gaskets like pale nap, like milk skim, hair-fine where seepage feeds them. They pull their food from the places where two states meet: where warm laps at cold, where steel gives up its first oranges, where a wire’s skin nudges the water to be slightly other on one side than the other. Some of them grow as threads, chalky or translucent, not plants and not exactly fur, extruding mineral as a byproduct of their living. They make tiny spirals where a drip is steady, delicate screws of iron that are all appetite, and they lace the black silt under the ladders with cables of themselves, a quiet circuitry that runs rust to oxygen grain by grain. They do not bloom; they occupy. They are the steady hands that curl an edge, soften a corner, convert, in a permanent whisper, the built into the spreadable.

In the silt live the writers. Nematodes fine as stray hairs. Bristled worms that draw and redraw their proofs overnight and then ghost away from their work so completely that only the script stays. Isopods that go as if punctuation, little commas prying. Amphipods that spring with surprise, hunchbacked and incredulous, flipping up an arc of dust when the pump wakes. They graze on the film the way someone absentmindedly cleans their fingernails: gently, thoroughly, with attention that never needs to think of itself. They keep the surface legible and keep themselves in it. The small trails between the sunken tools are paths and also sentences spoken over and over, till the hall knows them by heart.

On metal that remembers warmth there are sleepers. The eelpouts chose those lines and learned them the way old trades learn their streets: not by seeing them but by feeling where the faintest draft of heat runs. They are pallid and patient and seem to have been poured there, cheeks to the motor casings, eyelids shuttered to a sliver. They rise only when they are sure. They bite without hurry, and rarely twice, and then coil themselves back into the slight comfort. Their lives are arpeggios of maintenance cycles.

A single conger has taken on the drain at Vessel E-12 and is the hall’s slowest gesture of violence. He is a drawn-out muscle, a certainty shaped like uncertainty, black as the bracket shadows and not much quicker. He has stories in his skin—fine scars, pale thorns healed in—and he has made a map of the borders that matter: the distances between heat and not-heat, the spaces that go quiet and bring food loose. He eats dusk as if dusk were not a time but a pressure relieved, and if the pump steals dusk away he eats the pressure instead.

Others move on the catwalks as if they knew they were street and not shelf. Shrimp translucent as chipped glass run their errands along the cable trays. They learned the ribs of the tray as a pattern that fits their legs—six, six, six, step across the gap, and the world divides itself neatly into crosspieces and crossings. They harvest what the film gives back as crumbs when it is grazed. They will, if you wait, stand still and comb their antennae in the electric breath that leaks from the cables, pausing as if before a mirror.

There are bells along the crane rail, small and clear and stationed, that open faint light when the current feels like a river. They are not jellies as we had them, though they learned the jelly’s trick of being mostly form and willingness. Each makes a mouth that is also a window and keeps itself by a pedicle so fine you could floss a molar with it. They find their food in the minute lifting of the hall—plankton debris when there is any, bacteria-slurred flecks when there is not—and they sip charge, too, as if sipping were a kind of listening. When the far generator tips its values and sets the aisle to its slow drift, the bells answer with a hospital-pale bloom, and what passes for diurnal in this place clicks one notch onward.

In the forest of brinicles live the cold itself and a few that take their living from its cruelty. The brine that trails down them is denser than the rest and unkind; it freezes what it touches or merely makes it so salty that nothing wishes to be that any longer. But in the sheaths it lays, on their brittle skins, there is sometimes a film as light and brief as frost breath, haloing the outer edge where heavy and not-heavy water touch and argue. Here, for a season, will be the ones who can stand to be brushed by catastrophe: archaea with membranes like rules, halophiles that take their salt straight through their ribs, a bravery of microbes for whom winter is a warm word. When a brinicle meets a rail and glasses it over, the living leave a faint hem where the metal steams its loss back into the chill—an outline kept as if with a crayon.

There are creatures that understand light by scarcity. They are not always animals. Diatoms hold to the crack in the pane where blue sky sometimes remembers to ache all the way down. They are few, and they do their work in slow thin, and when the ice goes white for a month they are a faded carpet waiting for spring like a debt forgiven in advance. There are also eyes. The fish keep their places in the amber of the emergency lamps like worshippers around an ember, an unspoken truce that says, if there is warmth there is not prey now. The LEDs make pinpricks that draw amphipods to quarrel in silhouette. The lamps raise no algae of consequence, but they make behavior. In this city-room, light is a minor god whose only sacrament is congregation.

The metal itself practices a kind of life with seasons and rooms. Around bolts the rust has flowered into tight-edged rosettes that are the hall’s dry gardens: not organisms but forever having been shaped by flowing fields as if by breezes. You read them backward and know the seasons of the current that is no longer current—what hummed for a time, what slept for a year. There is a fracture where hydrogen lifts in small pearls every day and never learns not to, a field of bubbles like a stand of short saplings. Touch them and you taste iron in your teeth. They feed an invisible stampede of microbes that live on gas and salt; in their haze tiny ciliated things gather, combing the airlessness as if air were there.

The machines are animals under an oath to keep being machines. A motor makes a hearth, a transformer makes a weather, a relay makes a heart beat you can only hear through your bones. The pump that wakes now and then is a whale at a vast remove, and its rumble forestalls and then permits. It draws little, enough to tilt and never to slosh, and the whole hall admits to its rule with the pliancy of kelp. There is, somewhere behind a panel you cannot reach, a set of capacitors that breathe in and let out on the old timetable; they are monkish about it. All of this lives in the sense the word live learns when biology becomes only one way to say “goes on.”

Plastics have their own tenants. Membranes stretched  over absent gratings, skin-taught and skin-tough, cradle eddies beneath them like cats curled under blankets. Silt heaps in the corners they choose and pours slowly, a long sandglass with a mind. When the draft comes, the sheets belly and slough a new grain of the world into a new slight hill. On their underfaces cling heterotrophs that like the faint outgassing of old polymers, a diaspora of feasts too meager to name, and spiders of the water—the stolons of hydroids, almost theoretical, that nonetheless fish in micro for what the cold-thinned soup offers.

There are also mistakes that learned to be residents. An EXIT sign lights the darkness down a dead corridor and so creates a migration ritual: when it is on, the shrimp move that way and then back in a loop that persists for no reason but a reason once believed. A locker bank makes an alcove that an eel thought was a cave once and so is his cave forever, even when the silt changes the floor so much that it would be simpler to unlearn. The wrench in the ice is an object lesson in how to be patient and helpless and holy; its warped, amplified shadow on the floor is watched by nothing that can call it shadow, but still there is watching.

Predators are few and have good manners forced on them by cold. The conger is one. There is sometimes the sense of a dogfish that is only ever the same hint of dark appearing twice months apart, or else the coils of a strap learning how to be a fish by reinspecting itself in flow. Mostly death happens not as a drama but as a rearrangement. A coat of silt over a slack body is a curtain fall. The amphipods are the applause. The eelpout eats a little of the gill and then goes back to sleep. Bacteria have the rest to finish and never hurry. In a place that has made itself from patience, hunger is a kind of slow arithmetic and the sum is always exact.

If there is a spirit of the place, it lives in consent and habit. It is not a ghost of people but a manner of being objects that have agreed to host lives and so have become not only objects. The beams creak to the hymn from the ice and flex in time; the brinicles read the creak as seasons and grow; the films meet the brine halfway and become white hems around it; the shrimp thread news through the trays. The schedule of the far generator is a sky you cannot see. Dusk is a lull in vibration. Morning is when the amber warms one shade. Storm is a shiver in a sheet of old plastic. The city above is winter; inside the chamber, winter is a tool among others.

What lives here also includes the reading of the room by the small ones. They know names, though not as writing. The word INTAKE makes a pillar that makes a drift that makes a territory. The enamel map is a texture different from the paint and so is a territory too. The pegboard is a field of punctures slow-furred in manganese and the amphipods go there to feed like cattle at a fence. The checklist that became a nacreous membrane holds a fish’s bones against the mesh, and so is a shrine. When the draft rolls through sometimes the bones tremble and what lives in the hall receives it as uncomplicated weather: now the bones move; now they rest.

Up close the citizens are austere: straplike, threadlike, peltlike, transparent. Pull back, and the resident becomes the braid of all these metastable habits. The hall’s creature is the combination: the pump’s low pulse, the ice’s drip, the films’ feathered appropriations, the grit’s writing hand, the bells’ minor light, the eel’s late mathematics, and the neat little committees of shrimp. Its food is the difference between things. Its breath is a minute current. Its sleep is the long hush between relays. Its work is continuity carried through materials that rust and spores that will not green and lights that are not supposed to be lights anymore and yet are.

So you ask what lives here. An accumulation of small obediences. Things that keep to edges and become edges by keeping. Iron-eaters that lay down lace and silt-eaters that read it. An eel with dusk for a calendar. Shrimp with grids for streets. Bells that drink charge the way moss drinks mist. Ice that grows fingers and heavy water that acts—without knowing the word—as a hunter of haste. Machines paced down into animals. Light with the social gravity of a hearth. The room itself, a citizen of the city that outlived its people, going on, and through going on, alive.
