They live in the intervals, in every between. They do not occupy the rooms so much as become their seams, and their first appetite is for difference—of pressure against pressure, salt against salt, iron that wants a little oxygen and oxygen that wants a place to lose itself. They are not bodies the way a fish is a body. They are fabrics of appetite and patience that drape everything you can touch.

Call them mats and films and veils and thalli if you like. The words are poor, but they serve. The elders among them are brown and iron-quiet, breathing rust the way a bellows breathes ash: inhaling the least solvable of states and exhaling a lace that stiffens the day to stone. They make lawns that are more like lungs, rising and assentful with chemistry. Nearby, paler veils thread from seam to seam, each strand powdering its own sleeve with the little stones it calls out of water. These are lace-makers, seam-keepers; if a crack forms between slabs, they arrive not with urgency but with inevitability, and by the time you notice them they have long since made a living throat out of a once-blunt line.

From overhangs descend the ribbons: long leafless leaves that never learned noon and carry, in their glassy tissues, entire cities of partners. The ribbons themselves are almost a civics class in gradient—slightly richer inside than out, a surface that reads the ions flowing over it and shuffles energy sideways to those more clever at spending it. The partners within are the small accountants of the place: nickel-tasters, sulfur-whisperers, little merchants of hydrogen who keep columns of electron and proton the way a monk might keep a book. In some reaches the ribbons thin into tassels where the current slips just fast enough to comb them. In other reaches they thicken, sheet against sheet, until the hanging gardens touch ledge and make shelved alcoves that drink silt as if it were weather.

They are builders, but only as a consequence of eating. A basket once woven of sapling becomes an organ when their slow coats thicken it to rinds; the wood within is a relic, dark marrow in a stone bone. Rope blooms a white crust that is somehow not white but the idea of white translated into lime: you can split it and find inside not rot but a record of twist and fiber, still supple in outline, living in its impression. Across the floors that are not floors, between the wedges of old brick, they lay down one layer and then another, and you can read the seasons in the tightness of the laminations: colder brine, closer stripes; a warmer year, more loosen in the grain. In the bellies of drowned courtyards, their patience rounds up into nodules that rise like slow bread. Cut one and you will see that they do not grow so much as remember; the first memories at the center are gray and flattened like a page, the newest are olive and quick, inscribing the present with the least hurry in the world.

They have no faces, but they are not faceless. They have preferences so steady they might as well be personalities. Some court the faintest fizz, congregating where gas creeps out of clay. You can tell them by the way they hum along the lips of an old kiln, the way a dome will catch a current and give it back as sound; inside that hum, inside the drapery that answers it, lives a cohort that prefers the negotiable energy of the in-between—never fire, always ember. Some choose the pressure-naps at the lee of a vane. You can run your hand across those bays and feel the nap thicken the same way a fox’s path thickens a meadow, except here the fox is shear stress and the meadow is chemistry.

There are the Rust-Breathers, flat and mahogany, stippled with what looks for all the world like berry-seed but is iron fixed out of restlessness. There are the Ivory-Lacers, who lay webs in cracks and salt thin threads of themselves with gypsum and lime so that a seam becomes a colonnade to a cell. There are the Blue Rememberers, who do not glow so much as concede that light is a word that can be very faint; they outline the lips of ancient wells with a reluctance that can be read if you have been in the dark long enough. There are the Ribbon-Groves, the Hanging Census, each frond not a frond but a census of partners counting and sharing and counting again. There are the Cushioners, green-brown and convincingly solid until you press them and discover they are neither sponge nor mud but the slow compromise between the two—distinct life entrusted to a common skin.

They move in the way the place moves. Reproduction is not an event but a longer solution. A film slackens and slumps and in that one, generous shrug tears a dozen tongues loose; those tongues root in the next stillness and become dialects, and you would not know they were kin until you pierced them and tasted the same iron at the same depth. Gene is gossip here, passed along plasmid by plasmid, whispered through pores and occasional currents, viruses like weather bringing a new way to breathe to a corner that had been breathing acceptably for a century already. There is no alarm in this; nothing rushes; the novelty becomes a new habit about the time your grandchildren would have forgotten you if you had grandchildren.

They sense in gradients: they feel a tenth of a degree as a child feels a door open in winter; they know a change in shear the way a blind swimmer knows a stone vane from the far room of sound. Where a terrace edge begins to cant and offer a gentler slope, they thicken their thighs—if something without thigh can be said to have thigh—so that particles fall out of water where catching is most useful. They are not shepherds of current, but current acknowledges them; the vanes break and split and the living nap makes a thousand small edits to flow, and what falls out of water is as deliberate as anything gets down here.

They tend their dead with a kind of geologic piety. When a film has spent itself to ash-gray, it is not scoured away by mouths because there are no mouths. It is roofed over. It is turned into foundation. The newest layers treat the oldest as holy, not from reverence but from convenience: old is flat and stable; old is a ledger that has already balanced its columns. In a place without teeth, memory makes the scaffolding.

They are house-proud, but the house is the continent. The grooves polished by rope are now micro-harborage; each depression left by a craftsman’s thumb in soft plaster is a cup where a new colony began and still repeats the hand that made it. They live in the brick’s straw print, in the tiny caves where plant fiber charred out. They live on the faces of the long retaining meshes that have fossilized; the mesh’s old regularity carries forward into the ridged arrangements of colonies that prefer rhythm over accident. Toolmark becomes bedrock becomes custom.

They have seasons, the measured ones this place admits. When colder brine slides like a silk unfurling at ankle height, some tighten and take less, others open pores to drink clarity and let mineral play through. When the older, warmer layer returns, they relax, and the world blurs and they breathe longer words. They are all lungs for different verbs: to settle, to bind, to coax, to stipple, to sheet, to make shelf.

You could argue they are architects. You could argue they are only opportunists with excellent follow-through. Either way, the bowing reed lattices that have slowly sagged into arches were only coaxed toward their last gesture by the weight of life agreeing to sit there long enough. The narrow channels that discovered themselves to be cloisters were taught that by sleeves of growth stiffening the lintels just enough. The basin has become a grammar in which they compose themselves; repetition is how they read and how they write.

If there are minds here, they are the size of meters and decades. A mat spread across a bench and down its lip and into a runnel and up the far face into an overhang means something as a single sentence, even though it is made of a thousand mouths too simple to have intention. Some sentences down here are entire neighborhoods wide. The sum of feeling flow, choosing thickness, opening and closing pores, sharing sugar and electron—over such distances, over such spans—becomes something like thought, the kind that has given up on nouns and prefers habits.

No whiskers, no fins, no single animal custody of fear or appetite. The nearest thing to an animal is the way a curtain peels away from an overhang when its own weight has out-argued its adhesion: a slow creature leaving a slow bed. The nearest thing to flight is the brief shake of phosphor when your hand brushes a lip of blue and the place remembers lightning long enough to answer back. The nearest thing to speech is the shift in sound when a dome begins to hum because the living inside have flocked to the new tune, rearranging porosity until the water sings in a lower key.

Who lives here? The patient algebra that turns difference into food and food into stone. The roughened veils that coax mineral to declare itself and then keep the declaration as archive. The hanging gardens who never learned to bear their own weight because they never had to. The rust-breathers, the lace-makers, the ribbon-groves, the cushioners. The plank of blue that marks a well lip like a remembered promise. The colony that inhabits the negative of a reed in plaster as if taking the measure of an absent hand.

Also the hand, in a way. Not as bone or name, but as style. The builders are gone and their vows have gone on living, taken up by organisms that cannot spell vow but are perfectly able to keep one: to settle what drifts, to make stillness where motion is immoderate, to turn short energies into long continuities. The place’s soul—if a place this wet can be said to have one—is not a ghost but a commitment that has learned to breathe rust and salt.

And you, if you stayed. First as warmth the mats would come to recognize, then as patience. Your fingers would learn which cushions are the kind points in a cruel pressure; your ears would learn the hums; your body would slow its seasons to match the brine’s; your breath would fog a visor that shows you less than black and you would wipe it not from annoyance but ritual. You would not become one of them, but you would be taken up into their practice the way a stone ledge is taken up: as surface, as channel, as something to settle on. Down here everything that can be used is biography. The residents are not organisms alone; they are the use of time, the craft turned into growth, the growth turned into geology, the geology turned into home.
