What lives here is the agreement.

Before you catch any limb or face, you feel the inhabitant as a pressure, a habit, a kept pitch. It is in the way a seam of lime has been stitched to coax a sheet of seepage into thin lines. It is in the patient lay of clay at the lip of a catch-basin, in the bias of a rope that swells itself taut. The first life is a cadence — the room’s own chorus, maintained. It has instruments and stewards.

If you watch the places between the places — the seam where ceiling hoods down over wall, the back of a shelf, the little galleries just under the drip-line — you will sometimes see the stewards themselves. Not quickly. They prefer to arrive by degrees, like condensation. A dark becomes a pale. A pale becomes a pair of shoulders and a knotted forearm and the thin shine of a wrist wrapped in gut. They do not cross the middle space of the room if they can help it. They keep to the edges, the ladders, the old routes that were carved to be half-sound. They step where all the other noises are already strong so that their footfalls can be lost within the sentence the room is saying.

They are small the way precise things are small. Not dwarfed, but thinned by custom and by ration. You can count them if you count fingers on a ledge and the number of breath-plumes held near the stone to keep them from dispersing into drafts that do not exist. On some days there are three, on some only the one shadow that repeats in more than one place. Once there were enough to seed every shelf with hands, and the city remembers that with the density of its making. Now it is a family of maintenance gestures, wearing people.

They are pale. Their skin reads as wet chalk until you see the sheen is not sweat, but a varnish of thin clay slip smoothed over flesh to blunt the bite of the air and give water a place to collect without going where it should not. The clay is gathered from a high jar, dry as flour, then kissed onto the body in a single palm’s breadth at a time, because dryness is a treasury and must not be spilled. Where the slip has cracked from an elbow or knee and been re-wet by the room, the pattern on the skin looks like a plain of drought remembered and remedied.

Their hair is short or bound — not for fashion, but for capillaries. They plait it with wires of limed gut so that it wicks without dripping. Droplets collect and are touched with the tip of a finger and given to the nearest spoon-sequence or thirsty seam. Ears have grown generous and busy; not larger, exactly, but fanned by cartilage folds the way a bat’s ear is, tuned to the chamber’s standing hum. Eyelids are heavy and mostly closed; the whites seen under them are the lightest part of the person, a pearl-gray rather than white. They do not suffer the little fox-fire bowls. Their eyes have become listeners too.

Their teeth take on a house’s wear. Edge by edge the enamel frosts with minerals, then is abraded by the grit of meals that are not quite food in the upper-world sense: simmerings of biofilm scraped with patience; a broth made from isopods and the occasional chirpless cricket ground in mortars with fungus-crumb; shavings of blind things from deeper channels that drift here after floods no one has witnessed, and therefore must be believed as taste. Salt, measured as religion. The tenderest pieces of crust from the desiccant caches, rehydrated with ceremony and shared between those whose hands did the cleanest work that day. When a pale salamander looses a clutch of eggs to the lower eddies and the room sweeps some to a shelf, the stewards will thank the witness and take one, not two, and carry it between tongue and palate like a bead, head bowed to the hum.

They do not clothe themselves against cold; they clothe the cold. A strip of gut is not a garment; it is a damper hung from a collarbone, a valve that can be lifted or set down on a leak-line with a shoulder shrug. An apron is a traveling baffle. Their belts are cords studded with slotted pebbles, each pierced at a slightly different skew, so that when a knuckle brushes they knock a pattern no stranger would mistake for accident. Those chords speak quickly to the next pair of ears wherever they are pressed to stone. The stewards’ wrists remember the tempo: three heartbeats per sluice, one breath between each press.

Their tools are quiet, and, even when metal, dull. A copper awl greened slick; a chert blade that is a little too worn to alarm the rock; a flat of bone for smoothing comb-lines into a seep-wall. The most personal tool is the weight of their own hands. To smooth a lip, to obscure a resonance that is making too much of itself, to set a braided rope’s twist, to teach a spoon-ladder to catch a more ambitious drip — these are all prayers done with fingers.

They sleep in the warmest grammar of the room. Not by the fox-fire — light wastes. They sleep where a channel full of water throb-throb-throbs a steady bass and the cavity that hums in solved chords all night thrums through their ribs. There is a shelf that has known so many backs that it has learned the language of shoulderblades and rounded itself to speak comfort to the seventh vertebra. When they lie there they do not so much rest as agree again to be porous. If you had the nerve to brush their cheek while they slept you would leave a dry streak and the cheek would ache for being too defined.

They raise children without changing their volume. The young learn the room with their mouths — everything is first a taste. Salt is taught second only to distance. They are given wands of bone with knots braided through holes, the cords the color of old water so that the knots must be learned by feel. A game: stand at one lip, close eyes, and walk the measure embedded in the wand to a safe step on the far shelf. Miss and you do not fall, because everyone missed a thousand times before you and taught the room a sill to catch your shin. Another lesson: count aloud each drip from a named mouth, then stop counting and continue knowing. The wrong note is more dangerous than the misstep.

They keep their dead. “Keep” does not mean store. It means use. The room’s rules leave little place for bodies to be simply absent. Bones are needles and then, when cracked, are awls, and then when too short, are beads on a code-cord. Sinew is braid. Skin, when there is enough of it, is strip and veil, stitched into the choir that sings over the pool. This is not desecration because it has no theater. The people here have no appetite for story that makes of return a drama. They have craft. A dead hand teaches by how long its tendons hold a knot the living cannot yet keep. The fox-fire bowls glow brighter on days someone is taken apart, and nobody thinks that is coincidence; the room does not do coin.

In this way their presence is not obvious even to themselves. They have perfected the art of being part of an environment that would continue without them, and yet is better with them: tuned, steadied, taught away from stupid collapse and toward lasting pattern. They tune the hoots that the veil’s punctures make, not to their taste but to the old notation carved into slate. They shift a knot on a cord because the season above has changed its long lungs without telling them. They retouch the combing on a wall where the water has grown uncharacteristically bold. These are not creative acts in the sense that painters would use the word. They are restorations of a score.

When they do pronounce sound as speaking, they do it into each other’s faces. Words are tools that can only be trusted hand to hand. Even anger is a private metal, smelted quietly within the mouth and dropped back into silence to cool. There is little anger; not because they are saintly, but because too much difference in a place like this becomes catastrophe. They have exiles the way the surface has weather. If someone cannot keep from introducing a new rhythm — if his laugh breaks a count, if her feet always fall on the up-beat — they send that person outward, along a dry tube, to carry messages up to a dry season and return when the ribs of the city can take a jolt. Even the exiled are stewards. No one is not a steward here, because there is nothing else to be.

What else lives here lives by permission of the rules the people learned to love. Blind isopods. A slow cricket that forgot its voice because the room already had a song and did not require another. The porcelain salamander who is not theirs but happens also to be their neighbor. That creature is older than their line here and, they think, will be present when the last word in their knots means nothing to anyone. The salamander floats in the lens like an em dash. It opens its mouth and the room moves through, which pleases the watchers. They have named it often and then stopped naming it because the names felt like pins that would rust. They keep a shelf for it, a warmth shelf cut under an overhang where it can bring its belly to a cleaned place and someone — with great patience, in case this is blasphemy, in case it is medicine — can rinse its gills with a little clay-sweetened water if a season runs silt-heavy. This is their hospitality extended beyond species: a steady hand, a wash, a nod. No altar. They will not keep a god.

Life also inhabits the devices themselves. The low wheel of slotted clay that turns with priestly steadiness and ticks a strung cord of pebbles is not watched merely as proof that time moves; it is addressed. It is scolded very gently when it lags and thanked with a touch when it keeps its mind. In the bowl of spoons carved from bone, each cup is known like a child’s birthmark. The second from the bottom has a chip that makes its drip a little fuller and therefore a hair later. They have left that chip as a signature of a season, and when the next drought returns, they will remove it with a compassion that makes no sound.

Who lives here can be counted as atoms if you insist, but you will be poorer for it. There is an old woman whose job it is to keep the slate board true — to re-carve the lines ruined by the slow sweat of the wall. She smears the carved scribe-marks with lime and oils them with the fat of her own hair because other fats do not exist here. There is a thin man whose wrists know the humidity by ache and who can tell, with his back pressed to the rock and his jaw on the damp, if a connector-crack ten rooms away has begun to tell untidier stories than it did last week. There is a child who has never stood in sunlight, who will never need to, because there is enough exploration in learning every throat and belly of this one small room the earth kept by accident and then on purpose. He wants to fix the hoot that is an eighth-note too eager, and is not yet old enough to know that someday his wanting will be called a danger and he will be sent to the dry to carry a message and come back kinder.

There is also the city, which lives like a fungus lives — by being more network than body. It eats difference and gives back sameness. It eats gravity’s urge to pour and gives back a careful ladder of spoons. It eats noise and gives it back disciplined. It digests the gestures of its people and returns a shape that suggests a person ought to stand here, or put a shoulder there, or breathe against this veil at this moment so that the hole of a certain size makes the correct third. If you are polite and wait long enough, the city will teach you to place your hand where the others do when they arrive or leave, a pressed palm that will say: I am with you, and I agree.

Sometimes — rarely — a draft from somewhere unreachable brings the white ghost of ash into the room. It is not ash; ash is a surface word. What the draft carries is a rumor of burning and a grit that has not belonged here for so long that the stewards feel it in their throats like a memory of a song they were not alive to hear. They keep a lidded jar of that grit. They will never pour it anywhere on purpose. It is a word the room does not speak, and therefore cannot yet metabolize.

So: who lives here? The chorus and its caretakers. The salamander that witnesses because witness is its way of drawing breath. The unvoiced cricket and the blind things that graze on glimmers you can only see by not seeing them. The wheel that ticks and is therefore animate. The cords with their learned knots, which are books that taught themselves to be handled. The habit of exactness. The craft of preventing difference. A people so nearly vanished that they have become more like verbs than nouns.

If you were to come on a day of adjustment, you might see one of them climb to the singing veil. He or she would touch a strip of gut, slide its knot a thumb’s width, not to change the song, only to keep it. The hand would then rest for a second on the rock above as if smoothing hair, and a very small, very plain sentence would be spoken mouth to stone to ear invisible in the wall — a sentence that exists because they do:

Keep the drip slow. Let the rope swell. Give the noise its room.
