Who lives here first is water. Not in sheets or blows, but in intentions: beads choosing edges, films finding their thickness, fogs folding sound into themselves. It moves through the city as will and habit, arriving each morning in small decisions and departing at noon in others. It lives along the undersides of everything—drip lives here, rivulet, bloom of rime, the weight of a droplet considering a leap. It is the senior resident and the youngest, both ancestor and infant. Everything else takes its cues.

Moss lives here with the confidence of a creature that has been invited. It is not an invader. It has contracts: ledges pledged to it, bolts promised, twelve square meters of pipe-bend given over to its stippling. It keeps its end of the arrangements—holding the bead just a hair longer so that a gutter can wake as a thread, lending its cool to the air so that a child's cheek is not chapped by steel. It has varieties that would not survive elsewhere, bred for tact rather than height: whisper-moss for wrapping a conduit, glass-moss that shines when thirsted so a tech knows to feed a line, latch-moss that grows across seams until someone slides a blade under it and it rolls back like a cat to be stroked.

Under the moss, within it and around it, a plural of insects lives as if thought could pulse through green. Their cities are the size of fingernails and last a week; their roads are the lines of least wet. They eat quietly, a tide of invisible mouths, and in the slackest afternoons the entire block seems to breathe because of their patience. Names are needless for them, but people give them anyway: bead-gnats, wick-lice, seamhoppers. A few are kept intentionally—pollen couriers that move like a rumor between dwarf willow and fern, springtails coaxed to graze the algae that would otherwise choke a fringe.

Birds live here like annotations. Snowfinches align themselves with the truss-rhythm of a span and then erase their alignment in a flutter. Their wing-sound is an event; it is the sort of thing a person will stop and look for even when there is nothing to see, simply because the whip of air under a bridge is a cousin to speech. Geese pass over only sometimes. Their single calls land like the memory of an address you’ve forgotten is yours.

Otters live at the edges, which are always moving. They are not fed, not fenced, not trained; they are read. Their presence is a gauge of whether the heat exchangers are furred over, whether the kilns have been cleaned. It is common to see them slick through a service channel, leave a sculpture of water on concrete, then go soft-belly-up in a puddle that wasn't here the last time anyone looked. They share the city without permission and that is its pride. Stoats write their own atlas along the settling seams of sliding neighborhoods, lean little punctuation marks crossing a gap a heartbeat before it closes.

Humans live here, or something very close to them that has taken instruction from textiles and dew. They do not stand still so much as hold still. Their clothes are an ecosystem: coats with a soft nap that wicks breath outward to catch as frost in hair-thin lattices, cuffs that can be rolled to expose hydrophilic bands and harvest the smallest fog, collars lined with bibulous fringe to drink the damp off a pipe so a seal can be checked. Their boots suck quietly and release; their tools are wrapped, every edge dulled with felt or moss or silicone so that the city's talk can be heard around their work.

They have learned to move like weather. They arrive early and condense on a site, bright threads of fiber trailing from their hands, from the shoulders of their mobile scaffolds, from the drones they tend like a loose flock. You can watch them unspool in a slow spiral across a patch of raw silt and feel, in the set of their bodies, the satisfaction of setting a surface the way a gardener feels when the first rain after a seeding does exactly what was hoped and nothing more.

Their neighborhoods are not places but postures. A block is a congregation of skids all deciding together to purse in toward a moisture sachet, to present a broad belly to a dawn breeze, to flex their ties so that a bracket can be replaced without anyone feeling the jolt. Addresses are verbs here. You do not live at 12 Comb-Row; you live leaning toward the afternoon drains, you live above the sedge-furrows that caught last week’s fog. You write to a friend and say meet me where the under-bridge rains begin at nine, where the gangway is held a degree steeper than it was yesterday because L5's kiln is curing wet bone and the otters are keeping their whiskers in the water.

They keep quiet not because they are timid but because sound is rationed the way heat is rationed in a polar station. Their voices exist, but are used where hands cannot see. Much of the day is fingers, glance, body angle. They talk with the give of a sling or the slow bend of a braided cord. They have a sign language that is partly glove and partly garment: tug your right sleeve, let the cuff drink, then wring it a way that says later and another that says now. When they must speak aloud they do it so the syllables can pass without striking anything hard. Children learn to soften their laughter by bouncing it off their parents’ shoulders.

The children learn other things by touch. A rite of passage is to walk the morning basin barefoot and come home able to tell the difference, toes to ankle, between a mat that will hold and a mat that will give; to tell with your forehead to a pipe how full the cistern is because of the way the living wrap has opened its pores. They can judge the plan-of-the-day with a look at the angle of three gangways and the draw on a power umbilical: we are courting the dew at Line D today; the kiln will be quiet by afternoon; the softwires will slacken and the finches will come low. A proud parent will run a hand along a rail at noon, feel their palm filmed as if the metal sweats memory, and think my daughter will make a good comb-keeper; she hears what this place says when it says not yet.

Wick-keepers, comb-setters, fringe-tenders, siltwrights, rib-readers—what they call themselves varies block to block, week to week, depending on the work in hand. Everyone knows enough of most of it to stand in when a crew is thin. The people who guide the peristaltic pulses through the clear veins are revered in a quiet way: they have a feel for pressure that is worth a fortune anywhere else and is ordinary here. Those who tune the magnetic rails so the rubber-tyred cargo can pass without scuff or sigh are considered patient. The ones who will sleep the night in a white-furred coat out on the edge-plain, tending a line of composite guides so that tomorrow’s dew learns to turn there, are loved without fuss.

Food is damp and generous. They cultivate sweetness the way others cultivate heat. There are bowls of sedge seed soaked and beaten into paste with a mineral that tastes like a frost has a taste. There is algae, certainly, but not the bloom-choke that an outsider questions; this is clean and kept and flavored with lichens roasted until they crackle with a black-orange smoke that smells like cowlings on a cool dawn. Dwarf willow catkins are candied by cold and eaten soft as breath. In basements that are not below so much as held a little dimmer, they grow a mycological crop that tastes like the underside of rain. Fish are a guest—otters take them, people watch them, sometimes a child will come home with her sleeves sparkling and a small silver body and there will be a hush-careful stew made and the broth will be more story than meat.

Their governance is legible if you know how to read a gangway's spine. The day’s agreements are not written but worn. When two neighborhoods meet, the angles of their kiss tell the tone of the conversation. A generous lean means someone has loaned someone a battery bay; a tight draw and a crossed umbilical means rain is promised and everyone is thirsty. The only hard law is softness—that every edge should sip before it sheds, that no drip be hurried into a runnel until it has named itself, that silence belongs to the commons and laughter should be placed where it will not carry.

There are rites that look like maintenance to an outsider. At first light on the dewiest days they hold out plates of alternating hydrophilic and hydrophobic squares and tell a story into the beads, moving the plates so the droplets chase each other in ordered paths as if listening. On hard-rime nights they stand together on a span and hum in the range that wakes the harp-wires and feel the city answer in their ribs. In a century when someone writes about music, they will say the most delicate instruments here were pipes wrapped in living mats that opened their pores when a pressure song asked and closed again when it thanked them.

Death is not a breach; it is a material. A body becomes feedstock for a mat that will live above a beloved bench; hair goes to a spinner and returns as shadow in a braid; teeth find new bite holding an infant walling while it learns to keep its face in a thaw. A memorial is a line of fringe drawn long enough to turn a drip into a runnel into a streamlet that will catch a small frog the first time it comes up to browse on condenser drips. Names are kept in the slung bands along high alleys, each stitch another season someone’s silence kept.

Not everyone here was born in the basin. Some came with dry nights in their lungs, from places where rain falls only when thunder has argued for it. They take a while to stop flinching from the under-bridge rains that do not exist elsewhere. They take a while to understand that a door will never slam here, only settle. After a few winters they learn the library of white: the different ways frost flowers itself on cable versus rail, on old iron versus new carbon rib, and how each is a word for whether to sow a corridor with fresh bibulous threads.

The city itself lives here as a colony-animal, a many-headed polyp that feeds by setting out surface. It too has moods. There are days when it pulls taut as if afraid of its own wetness; there are thaws when it sags, delighted, and its drip-edges are full of a slow laughter you can feel in your knees. Its decisions are long and elastic. It grunts, sometimes, as a geotextile knits, the sound too low to alarm and too honest not to respect. In the midday hum when a kiln somewhere sends its order across the basin and the otters keep their whiskers in the water, the city holds still and lets the work pass through it, like a cow in a river letting the cold go around its legs.

Machines live here as neighbors, crossbred with moss and intent. The drones flock at dawn without any drama, a scatter of small glossings that leave their threadwork behind like silk from insects that have no name yet. The kilns are patient heat wrapped in jackets that sweat, older than they look because they have learned grace. The rails remember when a child fell asleep on a cargo sled and adjust, silent and exact. No engine shouts because shouting would be a theft. Their dignity is in restraint.

What lives here finally is attention. An entire people built around the idea that a place can be asked to do what it wants and people can learn to want it too. They are unromantic about it. They carry knives edged for cutting wick and fringe; they purse walls to teach drips stubbornness; they run their hands along the bellies of spans to feel the barely-there rain and decide whether to put out trays to catch it. They make a game of seeing their own breath in the morning and counting how many beads it sets on a cuff before it stings. They gossip in taps and flicks and the slide of a zipper-tooth closing behind.

They do not forget the larger map. The goose that calls once at evening clears the throat of the entire basin. They lift their heads and their hands go still on whatever they are wicking, catching, wending. It is not yearning so much as orientation. Out there is a river that is not yet a river choosing a braid the way a human chooses a room. Out there are plains learning their own lines under other towers, other hands. They love their bowl because the horizon never hurries toward them. They love it because here density is a way of being and hush is a material and nothing is ever finished with itself—least of all themselves. They live, like their city, by recording and revising in a hand still learning; they live by setting out surfaces and keeping them soft; they live by the pace of beads.
