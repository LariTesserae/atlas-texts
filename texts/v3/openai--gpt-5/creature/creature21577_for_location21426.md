What lives here is first a pattern. Before you can name an animal, you have to name the habit of the place: a drawn breath and a measured release, a twenty-count of insistence and a five-count of letting go. That rhythm is the oldest resident. It woke before any egg did, before any larva found a recess to trust, and it continues now with the stubbornness of a habit that has become the thing itself. If there is a mind here, it is written in how the pull brightens and dims, in where the eddies stand and how far the bubbles rise. It is not a plan so much as a pulse that informs plans the way gravity informs walking.

Within that pattern, the lives arrange themselves.

The smallest citizens are bacteria that like iron and current. They paint the seams in dark rinds, drink from the feeble bleeds of electricity at cracked porcelain, carry a little night-sky of charge-seeking chemistry on their skins. They draft the letters of the room back into themselves, halo the pump-capacity plaque emerald, lay delicate bronze patinas over stamped numbers you can feel with a gloved finger. On the one sheet of friendly plastic, their cousins make a skin as thin as a thought, foxed where amphipods have grazed their initials. They are archivists who cannot read what they record. They are also the cooks of this place; they take what the machine offers—iron, trickle charge, the carbon from the world that has washed its smallest tragedies down a pipe—and turn it into food thin as mist for the next citizens up.

Those next are the gleaners, a bright blizzard always just on the edges of your sight. Amphipods, translucent as fingernail clippings, spin and clatter in the micro-eddies around stanchions. They never stop eating. They preen the grate bars, snip at algae filaments with earnest, ridiculous intensity, take apart any soft thing the size of a pinhead. They are street sweepers and confetti both. Their numbers are not constant because this place does not like constants, but they have a way of being exactly enough: enough to keep the vents from fur-ing over, enough to be eaten.

The shrimps are the commuters, ribboned bands of small muscle that know the intervals better than any bell. When the draw deepens, they step sideways onto the lee, tucking themselves under flange lips and the pairing surfaces of bolts where the water kisses lightest. When slack comes, they move, tail-flick, tail-flick, each to a seat they know: a gasket groove, the hollow in a stanchion’s foot, the honeycomb of a filter drum whose pores glow faintly when the backwash begins. They have found the promise in rubber seals, the oil still trapped in old fiber packing, the meager banquet of a thread of grease that has no reason to be food and here is food. There are a dozen kinds, all wearing the same outfit with a slightly different cut, and if you were to track one for days you would find it running an old service route the lost line on the wall still insists a human once walked.

Threading the place like a thought that prefers right angles to curves are the eels—small ones as thin as bootlaces and elders as thick as your wrist. They are made of map. They have learned the syllables of conduit and change their bodies to speak them, jaw to hinge, rib to elbow. They know that the ninety at the far gate has a burr inside it you can fit a face against and not quite touch, that the vertical run under the failed fuse holds the same mild warm as sleep. Their hunger is simple and exact, but they are also janitors. You will see them reverse into a narrow, vanish what would otherwise wedge there—a coil of sea-grass, the skeleton of a leaf from a tree that grows six blocks from the old pump house above—and then slide forward, jaws set just enough to carry the problem to a place the current will understand. If the machine could ask for help, it would ask with eels in mind.

There are fish in residence who have made jobs for themselves because the math likes it that way. The pair of thick-bodied patrols live between two lamp rings and claim the predictable wealth that falls through that volume. They have learned that appetites can be as precise as clocks. One is an inspector, vanishing into the cavity behind the rating plaque between beats to check the insect-rattle confusion of amphipods there and come back with a mouthful, always in time to take advantage of the outflow that rolls right on schedule over this square of grate and no other. The other is a hanger-on in the best sense: station-keeping in the slow pull, letting what is inevitable arrive. They look like arrogance but are only good timing.

They are not alone. Small, shy, glass-sided fish with coin-lantern eyes line up with their faces into the draw when it strengthens, drift off their marks like leaves when the backwash blooms, and reassemble with a choreography that could not be taught until the cycle itself taught it. At the weeping fuse, a cluster of juveniles—not a species so much as an age—tucks noses into the warmth and sleeps, fanned by the slower vibration that the blown coil leaks into the water. The lullaby of breakdown is a nursery rhyme here.

Crabs own parcels. Their tenure is measured in barnacles that have decided the crab is a rock and were corrected, and again decided, and were not. One, as wide as a plate you might have eaten off when there were dinners above, has a square of grate he will suffer no stranger to cross during his time awake. He is geology in motion, armored with other people’s paint, arranged by the room into unreadable scripture. He patrols the length of six perforations and no more, testing with claws the looseness of anything that can be loose, knocking from rivet heads the calcified hats that, if left to flourish, would make a turbulence that would invite something he does not care to invite. He is a caretaker in the language of the room: he keeps his boundaries smooth.

In the same tongue but with more curiosity speaks the resident octopus. There is almost always one, rarely two, and its life is a series of preferences. It prefers the slope of the intake dome that is closest to the wall, because it likes three directions of retreat. It prefers the rubber of old seals and the felt smell of them, and sometimes the taste enough to bite and make confetti of what used to hold water at bay. It prefers, now and again, to rearrange the little world of levers and plates available to it, to unseize a valve that had learned to be happy where it was and convince it to find a new happiness a quarter-turn away. It cleans stamps on numbers with its arms and, bored of that, draws temporary alphabets in the amphipods on the plate beneath. It has a pantry for gaskets, half the time for eating and half the time because the idea of a pantry pleases it. Where porcelain spills a faint, uneven moonlight that is not light, it will spend an hour, three hours, unblinking, tasting that wrongness the way you might listen too long to a hum and begin to hear in it the weather and a story you did not expect.

There are jellies that commute by accident, bells scarred where bars taught them the shape of refusal. They are weather more than citizens, but then weather lives here, too. They pulse through the short ellipses of lamplight like thoughts that have forgotten their subject and are content to be motion. Sometimes they clog. When they do, the gatherers arrive—a family of shrimp you have not noticed until now whose shared purpose is to be small enough to go where nothing else can go and foolhardy enough to try it—and convert the problem back into a moving system.

Snails keep rows where the gantry once wrote its straightness into the ceiling. It is a marvel, this choir of slow mouths arranged as though on benches, a handful high up against the vault, a handful lower, with a gap like an aisle between. They do not know it is reenactment; they know it is convenience, that water slides there the way they like. But in their arrangement, you feel a courtesy that has survived its reason. Their shells carry around them the memory of siltier weeks and clearer ones, of a season where the pull softened at dawn, and then, the winter after, softened a little later. If you believed in omens, you could read in that chalk the city’s forgetting of itself.

There are unexpected tenants. A length of chain, long ago hung to close a gate, has developed the habits of a snake: it shivers when the pump cycle changes, tastes the water with a clatter so habitual the creatures here treat it as information. A check valve slamming is thunder and that matters. It is not that the machine is alive, exactly; it is that its behavior has become part of the local grammar. The creatures press their faces to the bars when the impellers slow to stillness for those three minutes every eight or nine months, feeling with the bones in their heads the groan of the structure settling into quiet, holding their positions with the same attention you find in any congregation at the moment before a ceremony turns to whatever comes next.

And there are the things that are not bodies. The algorithm that calls each pump to start and releases it to coast was once a line of scribble on kraft paper tacked to a corkboard; now it is lifetime taught into metal by spring constants and flywheel inertia. It lives here. It is patient because it has been asked to be patient for so long that impatience would be a betrayal of its own shape. In the same way, pressure lives here as a person might. It is not an idea you think; it is the condition of your being. Those who live here do not remember learning it. They wake and know that everywhere presses, that every thin wall is two hands at once, that to survive is to spread force around until it becomes a place to rest.

The predators are not objects of melodrama. There is no theatre in a place where time is scheduled. The sleek fish with the heavy shoulders will take a glass-side, yes, but only in the window when the amphipods have thinned and the outflow has not yet brought bulk. The octopus will peel a shrimp because the shrimp did not keep a finger’s width away from the tender corner of a beak, and then ignore the next three because it is doing something else. The eels are thieves in the sense that a gutter is a thief for moving what must move. The crab postures; the octopus considers; everyone moves aside when it is sensible.

They know each other’s mass the way neighbors know the turn signal of the car that passes at six every evening. They communicate with absence as often as with presence. When the ladder three lamps down sings in a way it has learned to sing only when something large touches it, there is a ripple of small lives stepping a body-width left without looking, because looking is a luxury and here attention is a currency you spend only when all other costs are accounted for. When rain on the roof broadens the note of the room, you can see in the lamplight the whole place lean in slightly toward where the draw will briefly strengthen and then weaken.

Life here has found work that answers the place’s needs by accident and by quiet iteration. This is why the hall still functions as if maintained: the shrimps and amphipods keep intakes from fur; the crabs knock mineral from hinges before it becomes leverage against movement; the eels clear lines that would develop their own logic if left; the octopus makes mischief that is sometimes service; the fish prune abundance back to permeability. They are not a crew because there is no crew chief. They are a crew because the sum of their selfish acts is maintenance.

Some tenants are passing. A cormorant will not come; there is no air. A seal will not wedge its skull under the edge of the gate; there is no edge. But once in a very long time, when storm and tide consent, the thin and hardy offspring of a thing that belongs to a marsh will arrive as a seed larger than a seed, ride the slow water, be caught on a bolt, become a velvet nub of green. It will not prosper. The light is poor and the iron’s taste too strong. In a week it will melt into the bacterial spell, and the amphipods will make of its surrender one more celebration.

The only ghosts that haunt are the gestures of old hands left in the room as instructions that have become decoration: the SERVICE PATH that does not know it has been walking itself forever, the hooks where great wrenches dangle because metal invented habits the body cannot now indulge. And yet the animals honor the suggestions—not from nostalgia, because nostalgia requires a past you remember, but because many of those gestures are sensible. The line waist-high along the nave is precisely where the water is lazy enough for a fish to wait and never be surprised. The hooks collect bubbles where air gets trapped; amphipods like bubbles; amphipods draw fish; fish draw eels; eels draw octopus; and so a script written for boots becomes in this medium a line of cuisine.

If you were to give the community a single name, you would be tempted by something ecclesiastical, because the place refuses drama but accepts ritual. A better name might be Schedule. Another might be Patience. They do not have myths other than the hush, which is honored by every back fin and every tentacle in the instant it arrives, and by the first long, collective sigh when the impellers catch again and the spine of the room resumes its hum. They do not have leaders other than the brief tyranny of a current when it sharpens; they do not have seasons beyond the ones the weather above grudgingly shares; they do not have tools beyond what they were born with and the furniture of a machine that has had time to become world.

What lives here, then, is the city you get when intention and water cut a bargain and keep it long enough to believe it is natural. It is old in the way a metronome is old: beats that stack until they feel like time itself. It is sparse in plants and crowded in animals that have made small, correct lives between made things. It is a hall of working parts with tenants, an organism that pulses and digests, a neighborhood whose gossip is the tonal change of a filter bed and the rare creak of a door that has not moved in years deciding to show its voice to the water again. If you left, you would carry not the faces of its citizens but their calendar in your blood: draw, ease, sigh; and the sense that what lives here is alive both because and in spite of being at the end of something, and has turned that end into a style of continuance.
