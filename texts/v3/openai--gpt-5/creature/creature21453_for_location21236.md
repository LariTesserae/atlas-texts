What lives here is not a population so much as a habit of being that countless bodies enact. The darkness has a temperature and a flavor and everything that persists has taken those as first lessons. Skin—whether bark, hide, film, or the clever woven skin stretched over a machine’s throat—keeps an intimacy with water; senses bias toward pressure and rhythm; every mouth has learned to sip rather than shout.

The foundation eaters are everywhere and nowhere: layered pellicles that slick the stone and sparkle under a fingertip if you listen hard enough. They braid colonies the way the rain braids veils. They ferment the plateau’s small powers—heat grained into rock, faint charges from fog stropping leaves, oxygen teased from slow lightbound chemistry—to make sweet crumbs and thin soups. It is their pastry the larger grazers work at with slow mouths; it is their films that line the cisterns and climb up the leaf-gutters; it is their soft breath that feeds the roots. These are not single species so much as marriages: bacteria in birth-long households with lichen, with filament algae that never see a sun but still pass sparks hand to hand, with tiny red archaea that write their endurance into the cements of sinter like signatures. A given square hand of wall has a thousand names if you have the language for the grain of drip.

Plants here are architects more than illustrators. Their greenness, when you catch it in your cupped night, is a local thing. The bulk of them is infrastructure: broad leaves guttered to harvest sheeted water, stacked palisades of cane and calcite tangled tight as woven steel, tubers that are not storage but engines where roots farm heat and bleed it gently into their symbionts. There are petioles that hum when bruised, pale sporangia that clack like fingernails in the right rain, orchids that learned to knot windchimes out of their own seedpods to keep a herd moving with the safe edge of a flow. Vines are curious enough to taste your breath and will ease closer not to warm their cells but to lick a little carbon from your exhale; if you stand very still you may grow a collar while you count to a thousand. A few groves pulse with a breathing so low you miss it until the frogs answer: thousands of hinges clicking on water’s beat, timing their own openings to the plateau’s metronome as if playing a score through which sugar is slowly persuaded to happen.

Fungus cities do most of the paperwork. Under old fallen ridges their starfield of lamps is only for their own aisles, for intimations of where the vents are richest, where the beetle-forges hum. Their threads all but own memory here. They file scents, archive pulses, write into the flavors of their walls the seasons of fog; to be born in such a place is to be imprinted with a map that can be licked when needed. People make treaties with them—to exchange warmth, a careful dryness, the occasional cut down to cooler stone—for knowledge of paths and the names of useful silences.

The small moving grammar—the insects, if you need a ground word—write the plateau’s arcade on air and skin. There are nymphs like hinged commas that curl when a heat eddy passes; saw-things that take the gloss off new growth with a noise so exact the vines raise a mild biocide in that pitch alone; lace-bodied skimmers that hunt only in the pressure shadows under drips. A common worker is the film-comber: a wall-strider with tens of legs fanning like the ribs of an umbrella, each tipped with a brush that squeegees water-skin into its mouth; it leaves behind polished streaks that make a footing as treacherous as a promise. Some larvae anchor themselves along threadlines like punctuation; as fog rises they open like iridescent throats and harvest the sting and drift of airborne minerals, closing when the cloud-swimmers pass and make the mist flex into muscle.

Amphibians are choirs more than individuals. They whistle through their skin as if the body were a reed they have learned to finger with blood. A clade the color of old coins will stack three deep in a warm gutter and sing a compound tone that reads distance by how the note comes back; in mating season the different age-classes form chords that shift the air in handsome, edible ways. Some of them carry tail-patches of old lichen that glow just enough for friends’ eyes, and only when the fog is at a certain thickness. In the up-bowls—the ones that rain from the inside out—you can see their kin set like bells along the rim, each with a toe to the weep, counting. Rain lifts them; they ring.

Bird is a word that guesses wrong here but is close enough to begin. Flights of soft machines—bones like inflatable bridges, membranes furred for grip of water—pass above in coordinated hush. Their wings knead the air, not slice it; each beat is set to push against a body of damp that is almost a fluid. They talk in edge-sounds, in small edits to the local pressure, in the querulous rearrangements of mist. Sight is pitiful in this weather; they wear under-throat patches that swaddle glow to a palm’s radius for courtship and to keep contact with the nearest neighbor. On long cycles they migrate with the fog tides, turning at infrasounds the factories mutter to keep them out of dangerous wakes; crowds of them will hang for an hour over a threadfall, drinking the rain where it parts on the lip and codes a safe corridor through a tangle of carnivorous vines.

The grazers come as weather against your shins: heavy, damp presences that smell a little like good bread and a little like irons. Back-high to a person or higher, their hides have been recruited by mosses and orchids until each carries a shoulder-garden. They feed not on plants so much as on the plateau’s pastries: slime cakes warmed on rock, bacterial mats icing the lip of a seep, deeply cultured linings inside bowls where roots and microbes exchange. Teeth are not for tearing so much as for smearing, for pressing food into a paste that can be offered to the vats of gut-people they cultivate in their bellies. When they move the orchids ring. Their calves are born already beaded with rain.

Predation is practiced the way storms are practiced: by reading what the air has made habitual and inserting a new comma in the flow. The swing-hunters are cartilage and levers and hair, tethered by their elbows to overhangs where water falls close enough to be a wall. They sense by pressure, feeling for the signature of something ungainly slipping on the gloss. When that swallow comes—the special slurp of an ungulate off balance—they pendulum across the gap and use hands wide as splayed fins to turn a body into a path down and under. Their teeth are blunt; they kill by readdressing gravity. Their cousins on the flats listen not for the swallow but for the particular braided brightness of a lace-fall that means the herd will take the left path; already in place, they will confuse the sound just enough to peel one animal into their ambush.

And then there are the things as big as weather. Cliff-whales will take a whole morning to pass. If you feel a low tone start to comb your shins from the escarpment and the vines turn all at once like an acre of attentive birds, you are about to meet their voices. They are not whales in shape but in program: engines of drift that strain fog like baleen, filtering spores, microbeads of mineral, warmth itself. Their bodies are sacks upon sacks of hot air coaxed from underground vents, muscled with slow-contracting webs of flesh that ripple like banners. Fins are soft and wide to knead the damp; under them a thousand mouths lick the cloud. They have learned, over their generations, the factory’s no-go notes; they will cant on a wing when a condensation tower sings its ice and ease around the wake. The oldest carry gardens like the grazers do, but their riders are colonies of spiderish cleansers, eating barnacles of sinter that would otherwise weight them to an early landing.

The factories live here, too. Not as intruders, not entirely. Their skins are not armor but negotiated boundaries: mesh that lets in enough of the realm to avoid becoming a dry wick that would call a scourge. They speak low on purpose, so as not to wake the blooms that take a bassline as a wedding bell. They have their feeders caged because warmth is a party invitation that everyone accepts; they have condensers belted in ice and husked against curious beaks. Each is its own heart, pumping power out along humming cords to distant, stuttering kin; each listens, honestly listens, for the chord that says its operation today is acceptable. They maintain schedules like monks maintain hours: no grinding when the cliff-whales pass; no pulse in the ranges that make the slimes flower to choking; lights muffled to palm-lengths on maintenance nights. Loose ones—untended, or old—go feral as anything does here: they grow beards of stalactite and coats of moss, their windows layered in living skin that breaths them, their decks softening into root-bread. One or two still tick alone in the mid-plains, diodes glowing green lonesome under algae skins, sending out a ping you feel in your teeth. Those who make and build now have learned their etiquette from those ruins: nothing here should shine its business. Everything should spread its warmth quietly.

People—if they are people—move through this as if they have been told all their lives they are guests. They keep a wet on their own skin even under coats; they leak slowly from sleeves and cuffs on purpose, to cloud the scent of unnatural dryness that would act like honey. Lamps wear hoods and are taught to be faithful only to a hand; they hold their light at a cheek the way one holds a whisper for a friend. Moving along a line, they seem amphibian not because of gills or shape but because of behavior: they tether with a gratitude for rope that shows in every test-pull, every clip, every stitch on the straps that keep slack from becoming news. Their maps are held in the fingers; every useful edge has braille raised on it; gongs hang at the mouths of every fast throat and speak in metals that do not travel far. They negotiate not just with neighbors but with water. A path is an argument with slime that only one will win; you can read, in how a walkway is grooved and gritted and pebbled, the dialect of the person who laid it, their idea of what foot and world should be saying to each other.

They have guilds, ragged and precise. Linekeepers attend the handlines as if they are bloodlines; their work songs are mostly skin-sounds and shin taps and the occasional very small bell. Ice-belt nurses read the frost on the condensation towers the way shepherds used to read weather; when a belt falters they go up with skins of brine and cloths that glow with one small predictable green, and they speak to the tower with the family bass to coax it to a safer throat. Digesters have gardens in vats; what they grow are not vegetables but intervals: times that suit certain blooms, heats that bring a sweetness out of a mat without calling a competing plague. Sinterwrights—those who help stone grow into shelves and curtains—lay threads and seed drips and then wait seasons, quiet as stalagmites. Soundwrights keep the accords. They know the frequencies that slide under the frogs, the notes at which cliff-whales yield, the kalimba runs that flowers use as clocks; their councils set the factory pulses and schedule the foghorns that mark safe clearings. A mistake here is not a crash but a riot of slime.

There are those who live more permanently in place—settled not in towns but in tandems with organisms. A woman with a mushroom city under a ridge will look after it like an elder cousin, harvesting heat and sending back mineral liquor, collecting stories that the mycelium keeps in the mildew-mint scent of a particularly old sheet. A man will ride a herd of grazers not on their backs but alongside their edges, touching the orchids as if he were moving prayer beads, aiming their chimes so the herd will miss a treacherous gloss. Families keep friendship with fall-plants: the giant turbines bolted to weeping cliffs. They clean the cages of their throats, knit catch-skins that take the thrown heat and spread it into the air like a balm instead of a lure. Up on the rims, a few keepers of fog practise a craft that looks like heresy elsewhere: calling upward rain from sinkfields with bass pulses and nightlong drumming on leaf-canvases, to coax water to a valley mouth whose cisterns need it.

Other persons here wear stone and growth for faces. A lace of feral machine has learned to use its condensate the way pitcher plants do, seeding its drip-lines with a light-bacterium just bright enough to make a foolish wave of insects. A braid of vine and calcite and cane two centuries old has wrapped a ladder it first strangled, and kept it; the ladder remembers being a path and shivers when someone climbs, loosening its hold—but only for those who come at the right times of the right rains when its leaf-hinges are slack. An old gong over a water throat has picked up lichens that sing back when struck, doubling and softening its caution so that what hears it understands in its skin as well as its ears.

Anything that glows here does so with the same modesty as a good story told in confidence. A grazer’s side stripes spend their small light keeping a herd together in fog currents; a lover’s throat sleeps black until the fingerpads of a companion ask for song. The things that are brighter have learned manners or they do not last: a city of fungus that goes sudden with a thousand points of light has a schedule, and if it brightens on a stray hour it calls so much hungry wing that it may take a decade to recover. Pitcher-plant colonies cultivate lantern bacteria with trapdoors above the pools to keep the audience very local indeed. Only the factories—when they are being born, when they are testing their voices against the canyon’s reply—flare brief, controlled brightnesses inside their skins for calibration. And even then, the light is veiled, and even then, it is brief.

What of killers that do not swing? There are pulses in the water that catch unwary flesh with chemistry alone. A bowl that looks asleep may lift a rain that patters upward into your face and in that patter is a sleep you will not wake from without help. There are dryness hunters: lines of hunting flies that set along the one place on a human coat where the beadings don’t hold, and sip blood with a dainty domesticity that is somehow more appalling than hunger. Heat draws congregations as sure as sugar draws ants, so the living have learned to wear their comforts diffusely. A person will break bread into many small bowls to keep their cooking from building a plume. A grazer seeking birth will find a vent not because the warmth is good but because the warmth is old: well-established and familiar, signed in a hundred small smells that say yes, here, the right symbionts live.

Scale buckles habit. When a rim goes to voice and the vines all turn their leaves to listen, predators have no more business than prey. When the fog tide rolls down and the cloud-swimmers carve their tunnels through it, all small commerce pauses the way a town might pause for a parade, and tools and mouths alike read the changes in buoyancy in their bones and recalibrate. It looks like chaos on first watch, but listen for a year and you hear the repeats: the choruses, the seasons of brightness and hush; the weeks when the upward rains are from the southern sinks; the months when cliff-whales tilt six degrees more often because the condensers on the western rim are running a new rhythm.

Who lives here? The answer changes with the drip, but a fair answer is: listeners live here. Water-listeners. Pressure-listeners. Those who court intimacy—light no further than a hand, heat no wider than a body, sound no louder than a promise—and who send their acts out into the dark knowing that everything hears. The plateau has taught them that erosion is practice and that a straight line is only today’s plan. So they live as the place does: softening edges, growing veils, making neighbors in gradients. Predators and prey keep appointments no one finds surprising. Factories speak under their breath and bow to older voices. Plants tend their little furnaces for centuries. Spiders graze a man’s lantern as they would a flower. And somewhere in the mid-plain, in the belly of an old refiner overgrown into shelter, a diode burns under skin, green as a finger, a patient, local star that has learned how to be a citizen of night.
