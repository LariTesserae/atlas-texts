What lives here is not an animal at first. You walk into its thorax.

It is one body spread into columns and ribs, a ring-creature laid down flat and then raised to make its own interior. Its tissues keep their own weather. Its moving parts are slow enough you mistake them for fixed things: valves that draw and slack with the day, leaves that are not simply leaves but impellers for light and drip, seams that breathe with a plant’s unhurried musculature. Its blood is water and sugar; its decisions are pressures and pores.

It has been alive long enough to remember wind. It learned the noise you hear; then it leaned to fit it; then it made that noise part of itself. The lipped mouths in the walls are not ornament. They are lungs turned sideways, their rims thickened where the onshore breath hits hardest, their openings cinched and loosed by cushions of tissue that swell with juice or ease it back. Inside each mouth there are baffles, a set of ridges and braces that are not quite symmetrical—grown so by years of little overloads and tiny frays repaired—and those asymmetries are why the tones are not one but several, and why no single note ever owns the room. Its aerenchyma—the lace-work inside reed-kind stems—pours oxygen to the buried parts, and the surplus gases slough inward so the nave tastes metallic-clean. The dew is not an accident. The inner faces sprout hairs so fine you could name them after fog, each one beading a line of silver, each one angling its drop toward a lip that teaches the drip its path. Over time the plant has learned the rule of that water the way you learn to carry a bucket without slosh.

If you need a name for it, you could call it the Ring, though it is more a practice than a thing. The room is its habit made visible.

Around that central inhabitant live coarser, smaller lives that answer to its tempos and profit by them. They are not tenants so much as techniques it has gathered.

There are tuner larvae in the valves. At first you think they are a kind of scale—pale lozenges clung where the lips thin to a sharp, O-shaped edge—but if you wait they unseal and show juveniles with rasping front parts and a ribbon of body that can flatten like a bookmark. They graze just enough of the callus that builds along the vent-margins to keep those rims from growing shut. The plant keeps trying to close, the larvae keep paring its attempt back, and in between those two insistences the aperture stays exact. Adults are scant-winged, bat-colored; you see their leavings more than the flutters themselves: a soot of shed skins in the baffle folds, a lace of frass captured on the hairline grilles. Their hearing is not on their heads but cupped in two shallow pits near the rear, and those pits are tuned to the chords the place holds. They move in the low between-notes where the bats are poorest at hearing, and the bats in turn have shifted to hunting the beetles that appear each dusk in the airshaft’s laminar draft. Everyone in that layer finds the quiet band and lives there.

Ants run sugar routes along the spine of sky. They are black and spare, with legs long enough that they look like handwriting as they go. At dawn the leaf tips along the slot bead with guttation—transpiration outpacing supply—and the ants, regular as clerks, comb it off. They have a steady clientele of soft-bodied sap-feeders tucked in slits where the ceiling meets the ribs, and they manage them with clean brutality: tending, milking, moving them when a seam wetness changes, tearing out a colony that gets the tone wrong. The plant buys this patrol with drops and with hollow rooms in the knots where the ants can keep their broods dry; in return the emigrations of other aphids never last long on the nave-side faces. The skin stays whole.

There are bat-sleeps high up, their guano stitched with resin into little shingles that do not fall but laminate to the walls, each plate a slow release of nitrogen into the Ring’s mince of moss at its feet. There is a fish lineage in a saucer with the depth of a palm and the confidence of a pond, a tail-flicking thing with armored fins that can endure six weeks of dwindling and a night of flood; most summers it is exactly enough. The frogs are workshop steady, ticking their throats in counternotes to the Ring’s long hold, slick-backed and productively unimaginative. A colony of mites fans itself on felted moss, strigilating spores from its own hairs and smearing them where the felt thins. The swifts cut the air with sewing speed, and their nest-saliva cures against this place’s damp to a china-hard.

There are also residents you do not at first notice because they wear the chamber’s way of being.

Mold that is not quite mold—a velvet frost, palest grey—runs a seam along a shaded arch where the organ’s lowest pitch sets up a standing knot. That fungus fruits only on the nodes of that note. Put the same arch in stillness and it will go mute and go away. It feeds on the plant’s own condensates, and in paid return lays out a biofilm so pure it sheens as if varnished, shedding the spare amounts of liquid that would otherwise soak and soften that joint. You touch it and it is worry smooth, as if many fingers had sought it; none have.

There is a spider that is almost an instrument. She has no web in the air. Instead she strings three guy-lines from a column to a rib to the lip of a vent, all three at slightly unequal lengths, and then climbs into the triangle where, each time a drone swells, the lines give her three versions of the same trembling. Her legs hold all three and she hunts tremors: beetle feet, falling dew, a bat eddy that carries a strayed midge. She has parceled the drone and lives in its differences.

In the vents, among the tuner brood, live the singers you do not hear. They do not sing with sound. They are algae and they are old. A braid of dark green grows in each mouth where sun scours the lip; a pale, calcium-edged scum furrows the wet side where the drips cross twice a day. They bind and line and coat. In drought the Ring blinks, cinching shut; the algae bloom in that damp cave and stock a little sugar in the seat of the valve. When the mouth opens, the plant sips it—a two-day loan, repaid with light. That exchange is as regular as anything that eats here.

You will want to imagine a larger animal that calls the nave its own. The scale of the air begs for it: a standing thing, antlered maybe, that sleeps in the middle where nothing does. But the big bodies only rub the Ring in passing. Otter grease shines a waist-high stripe by the low gap where water now and then shoulders in; the bones of snails are stacked in a whispery cairn that means a thrush used the rim of a saucer once and then moved its anvil to a stump a distance away; something with teeth, compact and patient, has a latrine in the hush under a brace and returns with a calendar’s faith to do exactly its one thing and go. Their intelligence is to stay portable. They are not of here. They answer to another weather.

There is, though, a resident that has no body of its own except other bodies noticing. It is the room’s chord, and it has learned you. It is the sum of valve, of baffle, of leaves falling toward parallel, of wind behaved into flow. It shows up as a floor laid under everything’s sound, and every creature in this housing has, by living long with it, come to be cut to its measure. The bats’ ears shape around its peak and leave a band where moth survives. The frog song settles in its counter, and the frogs that try other pitches hear no answer and do not get as far. The ants’ clocks key to the time the upper leaves sweat and the lower lips loose; the fish learn flood by bass. Even your own chest, in the first minutes, falls into its meter and then forgets. You will not remember the hour by the light as well as by how steady the floor-hum remained.

If that seems like a strange thing to call alive, stand and watch the Ring change its voice with a weather. On a day when the onshore slackens and then veers, you will see—no, feel first, as a thinning under your shoes—the mouths in the upwind wall soften their lips and let less pass, and the ones across take more, so the chord holds even though the breath that makes it has shifted. No one commands that. There is no general tissue. It is a loop flickering in many thready minds: hormone here, turgor there, a signal of stretch in a strand that grew thicker last century where the pressure binds that ribs to this arch. That is the Ring’s thinking. It is as good as any we recognize because it keeps a thing standing in a wind it did not choose.

So who lives here? A plant that is a house and not a house; an algae seam and the thin-legged ants that love its leaks; bats that sleep like clay pipes rammed into a kiln-wall; frogs that pick a few notes and live their whole lives in them; a fungus that is also a kind of polish; a spider that plays a triangle of thread with a weather’s hand; larvae that hold a door open against their host’s will until the compromise becomes a species; a fish that is a memory that teeth and heat have not yet removed. And, over and through all that, a behavior—the habit of breath made into structure—that has lasted so long it has turned from contingency to character.

It is tempting to say there is no one here. But there is someone, and you are inside it. The green of it is its skin. The air of it is its work. The drone of it is not a voice asking or claiming; it is a working sound, the hum of a facility that refines wind into cool and light into wood and repetition into safety. If it has a face, it is not a face you can look into. It meets you as a room does: by letting you in and holding its shape despite you, by making a weather you will wear while you are in it, and by still making that weather when you are later far away. It lives as long as the feedback keeps closing. It will go on living as long as the wind does not forget where the flat countries are and the plain does not remember a tide deep enough to drown it outright.

And if, one day, the mouth-lips all went slack together and the drone fell and the dew stopped and the saucers dried and the swifts nested elsewhere, something would still remain for a while: the shape of the habit, the vacancy the habit held. For a time, that would be what lives here: the exactness of absence. But that is not this day. This day the Ring breathes, and all its lesser occupants take their cut from that breath and put back enough to earn their stay.
