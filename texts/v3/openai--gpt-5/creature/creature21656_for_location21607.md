What lives here are the things that can take instruction from a climate.

They are leaf-people and film-people and chord-fed colonies that open their mouths when the organ towers tell them to. They are the wet intelligences that do not pace or flee, the patient engines that make a living out of humidity and a schedule of pressure.

The first to greet you are the broad ones at the surface. They live on touch and tone: plants with thick, laminated skins that drink from both sides. Their leaves are gloves for catching drizzle, their undersides stitched with pores that open to the minor key and rest to the major. Each species keeps a habit the horns have taught it—this one folds at the double-beat so the crawler can pass, that one twists its petiole a degree with every low chord until it has seen a whole day. None of them know sprinting. They practice posture.

Beneath the leaves are the wet throats and plates—the rafts that are gardens and neighborhoods both. The fields here are woven, not plowed. Their roots live half in water, half in the thin hydrogen-breathing film that clings to every thread when the sound presses. Edible stems rise like the rigging of a drowned ship, straight and shy. Fruits are smooth-bodied, shy of fuzz and hair, offering no purchase to a mouth that is not here. When they flower, it is not for romance. It is for the pressure gust that comes when the towers flare their fenestrae and a mapped wind unseats pollen like sawdust. Their pods are clocks wound to the pulse of the pumps, sprung to throw their seeds when the sluices cough and the surface goes from climb to fall. Their children are built to ride.

On all the resting places, in the shade and the backs of things, the gray-bearded ones keep shop. Fungi in wide, low republics run markets for water and the dead. They are bookkeepers who never learned to fear fire; their ledgers are hyphae. They take the leaf-ends the algae have done with and the sloughings from a pipe gasket and feed them forward in threads, trading phosphorus for sugars, nitrogen for shelter. The molds are their signwriters, quick to letter a new surface with continents of appetite in a night; the next day, the timed mist erases their map and they start again, industrious and unhurt. Down in the channels, where the flow is a caress and not a river, biofilms mosaic the hard curves—some brown and slick, some amber as tea, some thin and pale as a breath on glass. They keep the water’s chemistry exact, like clerks at a scale, adding or taking ions so the green can keep its look and stink sweetly.

Inside every machine seam there is also a living—a wet, microbial departmentalization no one designed at first and then learned to license. The valve faces glow with bacteria that polish as they eat, a slow pulse of biolight that reads as healthy the way a cat’s fur does when it shines. If the glow dulls, it means hunger, and hunger means a sludge beginning, so the rigs come to feed the films a slurry and comb their surface with soft brushes until they gleam again. Around the mouth of a pump, archaeal colonies hold like caulk at temperatures a person would call a fever, digesting what the rest refuse, exhaling the small, useful gases that make the chemistry keep working. In the weeping stones themselves, calcifying microbes live as grain by grain additions to a wall, slowly inscribing weather into structure, writing the day in stalactite grammar.

The mosses are the ones with ears. They climb the resonator towers and upholster their throats in green that moves. Every long tone ruffles them a fraction, as if the tower were a back to be petted. They are not there by accident. They drink the wet the sound squeezes from air and pay back damping and lift in exchange—softening an echo here, thickening a swell there. They let the towers play cleaner. In storm-time you can see the micro-forests of them lean together and apart like listeners deciding when to applaud. Farther up, in the bright amber under the canopy, epiphytes have made themselves into families in every elbow of conduit and crossbeam—aunties and cousins of leaves, each with a particular gloss and a bead pattern like bracelets, catching heat from a pipe and water from a drip-line and never once touching soil. They flower for themselves and for the pressure gusts and drop their stolons like little snakes when the sequence tells them it is their turn to take more elbow.

Lianas are the thieves with a license. They wrap themselves around any vertical that bleeds condensation and lay their bark like gutters, catching drop by drop and feeding their ascent. They do not strangle what they climb; they are not in a hurry. Their job is to lift leaves into the better suggestion of light and tie distant parts of the district together with living hose. If a net sags, a vine finds it. If a span sweats more on one side, a vine evens it with a loop back. You could trace routes across the district by where the lianas have chosen to braid, like following old goat paths in a hill country. Here, the path-makers are plants.

The pitchers and traps have had their hunger home-kept. They do not eat animals. They cannot. They keep the municipal mouth. Their fluted throats are full not of digestion but of clear, resin-scented emulsions that catch grit, charcoal, the paper-lint that escapes a digester and the soot a failing bearing exudes before someone comes to cradle it. The carnivory of their ancestors has been tuned to an appetite for dust. In return for this housekeeping, the drip-lines thread their lips to keep them from clogging and the gusts are kind to them when the big shake comes.

The machines live here as grazers and shepherds both. They do not pretend to be beasts, but watch them long enough and you learn their habits. The crawler rigs are flat-bodied with compliant edges; they move with that steady deliberation of a thing that knows its work will be endless. They arrive at the weeping walls where the moss has become a muffler and brush it back to plush. They belly up to sluice mouths and feed on the bright green felts that grow there, wagging a hose, excreting a clean line where the water can grip again. They nod to one another in tread-polished arcs on the aprons and then slide away, leaving no print because everything is rinsed always. Up on their fixed rails, cranes inch like insects that survived an extinction event by becoming architectural. Their slung nets rarely swing; when they do, they drip. They cull the canopy, comb out the spore-wet mats that would gum a conductor, and lower the harvest into the mouths of digesters that burp quietly in rows. The spoolers above are domestic as laundry, unrolling and rolling the rain-seeded threads that tremble at certain notes and sing at others, pricking the air like the faintest of zither strings. It is all work. It is all continuous.

There are people, but they do not live here in the way a thing lives inside a law. The district rejects their bodies tenderly and unyieldingly. Their inner ears sour under the low chords. Their muscles forget to unclench. Their mouths taste metallic and green. So the people live just outside the ring, in rooms with thick floors and counterfeit silences, watching this place as you might watch a sleeping child—on screens, in readouts, by the shade and vigor of the bioluminescent polish on a valve. Sometimes a door will slide aside because the sound admits it and a technician in a hooded rig will step in to set a hand on a trembling rail, to replace a scored belt, to retrieve a crate of glabrous fruit that came ripe under a particular sequence. They do their passages brief and to the meter, and the district forgets them as soon as they go. Their voices arrive mostly as patterns of pressure the locks can understand.

If there is another resident, it is the tune itself. The hum is not just a condition; it is a tenant with habits. It has a morning face and an evening temper. It passes at night in a minor register that wrings the sky of water and leaves the stones glittering with tears; it brightens to a fake noon that turns the rafts and airs out the underwalks; it tightens to a staccato that makes the weirs cry and scrubs the pores of leaves. Everything that lives here has learned the tune so well it might have been born from it. In that sense, the district’s weather is a mind that lives in its instrument.

There are smaller things that qualify as life and neighborliness without ever showing a face. Protists drift like untroubled thought in the meniscus-thin layers where air meets water; they roll themselves up at the harshest pulses and unroll when the note softens. Diatoms skeleton up the edges of everything with little glass boxes that slip and glitter and lodge. Ciliates keep their personal worlds in order in the stagnant corners a crawler cannot reach and are sluiced to other corners at the hour mark. They are too simple to get sick with fear. They abide.

Stone lives here on a vegetable time, wet and slow and meticulous. The seeded crystals that prickle out of the weeping walls are growths as much as structures; they are coaxed by water and sound like teeth by a tongue. The levee ribs carry years as coats, thick and pale below, thin and dark above, each stripe the memory of a humid conversation the city and its swamp had that day. Every bolt glows with the skin a rain gives metal when it comes without dust. Everything is being finished, always, and nothing is done.

Even the empty, which is to say the spaces—sight-lines, courts of air, the theater in front of a pump house—are inhabited. Sound needs room to stride; the district keeps it. Water requires time to forget its haste; the district grants it. A root wants depth more than it wants neighbor; the district spreads it out. Those volumes are not vacancies so much as the bodies of the powers that run things—the slack the tune pulls through, the blank the chemistry needs to do its clear work, the patience growth demands.

There are rules and they are observed because they are also comforts. Light is poor here by choice and weather both, but it is the right poor for thick leaves and modest appetites. Heat wraps everything like a language learned in infancy. The infrasound is an ordinance that keeps cries and footfalls from entering, and so it is also a lullaby for the living engines that like no surprises. To live here is to be a stoma with manners, a film with a task, a machine with a route, a wall with a drip. To live here is to be carried by rhythms that make you again each hour.

So what lives here? The green that leans and listens. The gray that takes apart and gives back. The amber that warms and sifts. The drawn-out minds of pumps and cranes that do what they have always done and are content. The wet that remembers and repeats. The sound that keeps the law and is the law and is also the lull and the prod. The city’s appetite, distributed across pitchers and pipes. The crops that look like equipment and the equipment that, under the canopy’s dim, look like grazing.

No animal lives here because animals are made of alarms and pauses, and there is no pause in this place thin enough for an animal to hide its heart in. Instead there is a practiced plenty, a pressure held and let go, a district that is a single ongoing body composed of leaf and beam and note and drop. If you must name a resident, name the chorus. It is at home. It is everyone.
