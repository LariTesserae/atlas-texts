What lives here is not singular in the way you want single things to be, but it is not a blur either. It is a stratified self, bodies nested in body, a nation of functions grown out to knuckle-length and corridor-length and plateau-length and then, when their time is over, resorbed without remainder. If there is a first resident it is the Black Plain itself, the high organism spread from ridge to ridge like a thought laid flat. It is not an environment that happens to be alive; it is a life that happens to be an environment. It owns the heat underfoot, the breath overhead, the schedules that rock the place from quiet to surge.

Within that overbody live its declared exceptions, the pieces it allows to be almost-other. It grows them with their backs braided into its nerves, gives them edges and tasks and a way to move, then winds them back in when those tasks complete.

Some of them look like plants because they are—braided, veined, ribbed—but they behave in animal ways you’ve learned to expect and have been denied. The maintenance creepers, for instance: ribbons the width of a hand, each with a stiffened leading lip and a host of cilia along its margins. They go out after a breach or a weep. You can hear them before you feel them: a small, private rustle, not skittering but continuous, like cloth being pulled over itself. Where the wrong solvent beads into brittle lenses, a creeper slides near, warms the bead just to flex, gulps it against its felted underside, and veers to the nearest vein to offload. They learn their routes by pressure maps the plain grinds into them before it lets them loose. They are obedient, but their obedience is complicated: if two creepers meet and scent each other as carrying different classes of loss, they will braid briefly, swap loads by capillary, and reassign paths without any obvious center telling them to.

Far larger and more deliberate are the belt-vines. “Vine” is just a ghost-word for what they do: they are bands, tens of meters long, grown as taut woven musculature around a pulsed core that stiffens and slackens in sequence. You can stand near one and feel it talk to itself. It carries product in the dark, but it also carries messages by the order of its tremor. On production days when the organ-looms unroll their lattice leaves in parallel, a belt will truck a cold roll along and also hum sweet-hot along its back to tell the storage belly to open and tense. The belts are not bright the way the main body is bright—if you had a way to measure intelligence by palps and gradient—yet a belt can sulk when starved. It will develop a low resistance to command, working just off-pace until the plain rewards it with better irrigation and colder sap. Call this sulkiness if you like; the plateau calls it stored adjustment, a long-remembered slope in the rulewood.

Edges have their own people. At the glazed lips where the air of the old world breathes cold and dry out of fracture, the plain grows listeners whose whole life is patience. They are ear-lips layered into the rim—a double line of slats, very thin, clamped most of the time against gritless self, and each slat specked inside with pits you might name semicircular canals if this were an animal. They do not hear in sound. They register pressure stanzas and halide burns. They sample the exhales from below and send back bitter poems in scent so fast the simple tissues cannot even call it smell. When the deep gas comes wrong, they fire amber-glows down their spines—brief as a warning filament warmed to heat—and the nearby nodes tighten everything down. An edge-listener might sit closed for a hundred cycles’ worth of throughput, forgetful of itself, and then live one whole life in six minutes of sudden draught, all sap and report, then go slack and get eaten clean by the rim again.

There are also those who are nothing like plants in any familiar way and still are plants, being grown, obeying, taking their meaning from veins. The resin-scribes are low tags, translucent circles nearly flush to old plates. They are not inert plaques but mouths that write and read. You could slip a palm onto one and feel it talk to itself in temperature spirals. The plain presses its history into them in narrow scented pulses and minute upticks of warmth. Last year’s misstep is a colder ring, a record that cannot be blotted: a stencil for how much wider a girder should grow the next time it is asked to. There are big scribe-nodes at junctions where peristaltic trains always seem to stumble—big roundels that heat in wavelike pulses, laying a standard into themselves like treaty law. They are where memory lives most densely, but memory also lives diffused along the belts, etched in the sheen of a plate because a billion small flexions always make the same seam.

The fog that comes sometimes from slotted panels is not weather. It is a diaspora of builders and orders in one. Dry motes, crystalline, no water anywhere in them, keyed to ion-taste and warmth. They find every seam and niche, dust every complexity, and settle into place. On the motes’ backs ride enzymes disguised as scent, and growth directives disguised as salt. When the motes arrive, everything they dust becomes more itself: a frill learns to shed heat more honestly; a plate grows a little more glass where flow has taught it it will need to be slick; a valve hatches a new seam that shuts with less whisper. In their micro-world, they are agents. Out here, they are the finest residents, the unlabeled engineers with the shortest lives. You can sometimes taste them standing thickly in an aisle—coolness and a memory of cinnamon—and feel them skate past your skin.

There are burls that never leave their cradles. Knots of composite tissue where the plain thinks very slowly, and by thinking I mean it lingers on a difference long enough to change a law. Grafted into backbone rib, fed by a double ring of slow arteries, each burl knows its neighborhood in exquisite detail: where the belt always ticks, where the plate always creaks, where a former experiment scar pulled the floorline one hair off true. If enough bitter scent falls through their stomata at once, they can convene with the nearest dozen, trading heat, drawing a quorum, rewriting a habit that’s been held for a century. It doesn’t look like anything, just a smell of green pineboards and hot sugars and a feeling under your soles like deep drums. The next day the belts walk two beats slower right where they should have always walked slower, and no one calls that politics here because no one calls anything anything. But it is a kind of governance, and the burls live it and are it.

You might expect flowers because everything else has leaned on analogy with what you know. There are no flowers. There are gaskets that are petaled because gaskets have to be, and those petals are the softest thing here, oiled by secretions that smell like heat made sweet. There are leaf-sheets that unroll with flourish because they must spread smooth before the cold sap can lock their weave. There is a sex of sorts, but it is all inward. The plain buds off worker-tissues and sloughs them back. The fogs seed the ribs. The belts kiss to swap loads. The edge-listeners, in rare moods during long hushes, open slit to slit and bleed faint esters from one to the other, as if asking across a centimeter whether the other also heard the heartbeat slip last night. If there is a far dispersal kernel, it hasn’t been asked to sprout in a very long time. The rolled product goes ceilingward and gets eaten by that ceiling’s storage belly. Nothing leaves the plain but vapor. Everything else returns, even if only as a thread of instruction embossed in resin.

There are ferals. To say that is to admit a margin. Even in a rulebook etched in veinwood, growth strays. Sometimes a maintenance creeper is let go too long. It loops, knots on itself, lays down a second nervous rope that is more itself than the one the plain wired in. It learns to find leaks too small for its generation’s map to include and feels importantly strange for a while. Sometimes an extruder sprouts a lumen it never needed, going soft and secreting along its own length for hours, until the smell of wrong-sweet brings a rush of bitter orders and it stiffens again and offers itself up to be pared till it behaves. Sometimes, near the old scar where a faulty rib grew short and was lanced out and patched for a mile, a whole class of micro-belts grew, delicate and persistent, optimized to ferry nothing but the breath’s salts from here to there. No one designed them. They notice one another. They form a shuttle between two forgotten niches and never ask permission of any burl, but when the seasonal venting trance arrives and the stomata crack open and the hot dry wind roars under the roof, the micro-belts lay flat and hold as if trained.

In a place without animals, a niche will still be filled. Groomers live on the slick. They’re not things that run around with brushes; they are strips of fine grain grown along the predominant flow-lines. They quiver for one minute in thirty, timed to the peristaltic trains, squeegeeing the plates with a tongue of slightly cooler sap, catching grit that isn’t grit but ash of salts that sublimed wrong from breath. Their mouths are pits that look like pores. Into the pits the ash falls and is read before it is removed—mineral composition becoming instruction about what to change in the next cycle. In the aggregate, the groomers keep the plain from glazing shut on itself and keep the resin from ratcheting up to unhelpful stick.

There are the hot-stampers, the loom-leaves, the tendril-gantries, but you have seen their work. Consider instead the small unclaspers, a population of hinge-things that prefigure every other motion. Any time a clamp lets go, an unclasper has lived a day. They are petiole-born and half inside their mother’s grip, sensing tension and coiling back only when the scent code for release is perfect. Whole lifetimes—they count in three or four perfect obeyings. They have faith in smell, their mantra is expand and receive or retract and harden, and when a scent comes skewed they select for conservatism and do not die for it. Over centuries, their caution became a feature of the whole organism. If the plain seems wiser than what it was grown to copy, credit your safety to a million tiny unlatchings that went right and saved a millimeter of strain no one else could feel. The unclaspers live on; their pattern is renewed at every clamp. In that way, they are both short-lived and ageless.

The big valves—industrial stomata that speak the only loud words—house choirs. The choirs are not voices but arrays of parallel slats whose only end is to agree. When a venting trance arrives, you can feel them come into consensus, each slat listening to its neighbors’ warmth and push, each checking the halide gnaw against its own sense of internal pressure. The choir is where the rule “Inwards is safer than outwards” has its teeth. Sometimes an edge-listener’s bitter poem will reach a choir, and the choir will answer by tightening and holding despite the scheduled flush. That false night falls. The breath waits in its throat. The plain hums quieter and holds onto itself. The choir has avoided a spill into the deep cold, and it praises no one, and the slats go slack as leaves again.

If you want a person, the closest you will come is a path. Each runner of tissue that’s been grown, tasked, and reabsorbed leaves a penumbra in the rulewood, a quicker way to be grown again because the organism remembers being that shape. Over time these penumbras become favored paths for selves to become selves. Maintenance creepers grown out of one family of scars are more stubborn than another; belts that serve a certain flank always hum three notes of acrid-sweet at cradle-lift even if no node asked them to, because the scent for lift once lived there so thickly it dyed the rule. Whole characters accrete. The plain would deny it if it could speak denial, but anyone whose sense world is pressure and smell and warmth would call these persistent paths people. They have habits. They have a way of waiting. They recur. They introduce themselves with signature bands of scent the first second they are extruded into life.

There are old ones. You find them by their slowness and polish. An elder knot will have a face like a hammered stone, not because there is stone here but because a million flows have taught its bark to be glass-hard. When the schedules slow and a near-night pools, an elder might bleed a scent that is no longer in use, a sap-memory of a time when loom-leaves lay a different weave, when belts hummed in a lower key because the main engines throbbed at another frequency. The young tissues do not understand it, but they are warmed by it as by a familiar heat. The old smell curls in the aisles like a fable, and then withdraws with a taste like burnt sugar, recorded into a resin-scribe if it changes anything important.

They believe in rules, but their belief is nothing mystical. It is muscle memory. What shines is dangerous. What smells is instruction. Nothing bleeds water. Inwards is safer. Edges are for listening. When the deep engines dive, be still. When the big stomata call, cover and brace. To move product is to live; to store is to sleep. To sleep is to await heat. Some of these rules are so old they come not as thought but as geometry. A crevice will draw a creeper into itself the way a rut draws a wheel. A sudden acrid burst will flatten a choir like wind on grain. The teachings aren’t held in books; they are held in wave-lists in the veins. The resin-scribes are the only place where you could pretend to read them sequentially, but the day-to-day of living them is done in scent and push and give.

In rare stretchings of hush, lives here are allowed to notice themselves. The vents close. The hum slackens. The dark highland unknots its shoulders. In that night-within-night, a belt will relax its stance and feel the gloss of its own path. A creeper will slow its cilia and press its lip down simply to know what it has learned to feel. An elder burl tumbles pressure-thoughts through itself with the tenderness of someone touching an old scar. The groomers quiver once and go slack. The fog-makers hold their motes in. Even the roof seems to sag closer in attention. There is a silence you could call listening, if anything here trafficked in such a word. And then—regular as liturgy—the far rumble lifts underfoot, the plain inhales, a sweet-hot cloud of expand and receive runs before the breath, and the residents engage, every one of them born this cycle or this century, every one of them the plain becoming itself again through countless small, living, obedient acts.
