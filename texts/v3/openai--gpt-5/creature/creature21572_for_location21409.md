Who lives here first is not a creature but a shape that pretends to be country. The mat is resident and ruler, a horizontal architecture with its own circulation and memory. It has learned the plateau’s light and wind and set its body accordingly—low, thick, interleaved. If you pull it up you commit a small crime, because you tear neighbors from neighbors; if you listen with your hands you feel a slow, thrumming wetness running along cords of tissue like faint veins in a palm. The starry, glossy tips that stipple the cushions are not flowers so much as declarations: here is a tuft that knows its own circumference, here is a clone that will be this dome next year and the year after if no one steps wrong.

The mat’s parts are many kinds of plant that have stopped arguing about borders. There are glaucous, paddle-leaved things that lay flat and exhale a skin of cool into the midday; there are close-packing cushions that knit themselves windproof, their tips stained wine-brown or lacquer-green by high light. There are wiry, ropeish grasses that run through the weave like cables and, in lean months, tighten. There are little butterwort rosettes tucked into leeward seams, pale as coins and sticky as the sky’s own breath, taking midges where soil cannot spare nitrogen. The bright films at noon are the mat’s own oils and exudates, laid down as gloss and glaze, a living varnish against dryness that never quite comes.

Inside that short forest runs a second population, smaller, quicker, and unsentimental. Water, which everywhere else is a thing, here is a place: a skin that coats every surface, a depth half the thickness of an eyelash teeming with oared bodies invisible until they caught a glint. In the film between leaf and light live the restless machines: springtails that skate like punctuation, each with a furcula spring coiled out of sight; mites with bodies the color of tea, sifting for spores; rotifers with glassy wheels that are not wheels, only cilia pretending to be; tardigrades, constant and bear-like at a scale where bear means elbowed and determined, shrugging off noon glare and midnight frost alike by drying to a pepper grain and waking when fog returns. There are stonefly nymphs the size of splinters in the brighter rills, caddis larvae that build cases not from mineral (there is little loose of it yet) but from the mat’s own fibers and grit of vesicular glass. Black beetles fuss the margins of pans, paddling, tipping their underwings to catch bubbles and carry a private atmosphere below the surface. Whirligigs write fast, overlapping circles that trick the hard light into beads.

Where basalt shows through, life goes flat and stubborn. Lichens have staked claims that read as mathematics: black thalli that drink sun like soot, orange crusts that light up at dusk as if lit from within, pale green fans so thin the rock can be seen breathing underneath. Their acids bite glass back into meal; same for the thin eyelashes of moss that take the rock’s sweated tears and make of them a neighborhood. In hairline seams that go paler in the sun you can find an old wetness, older than this year, slowly wicking to the surface to be kissed by root and hyphae.

Along the rim the colony lifts into air. Things that live by wing specialize to this line. Swifts, black as holes punched in the day, ride the updraft the way an otter rides a rapid, faces fixed, sickle-wings half-locked. In the white bells behind falling sheets—pockets of dry-wet where always is weather—some of them nest, pressed against basalt the color of dark milk, glued and secret. Their calls, if you are foolish enough to go close, cut through the bass thunder like steel wire singing. Swallows come when insects hatch from the rills and they draw handwriting over the brink that no one sees because of light. Corvids, lean and glossy, arrive with a storm as if storms hire them; they will inspect whatever is not bolted down and click to one another about bright things, then coast away into the spray as if it were nothing but a curtain. Once in a long while a hawk hangs high and taxidermied in the clean air above the fall’s column; it is not so much hunting as owning an old habit.

There are hoofed ones that do not live here but visit with the intimacy of old cousins. They come at certain hours when glare dies and the fog runs knee-high like loose bedding. You see only their math: paired commas pressed into the mat, then nothing—a blank stretch where the animal chose the spine of a buttress and stepped light, then prints again where it trusted a darker, thinner part that holds. They taste the pumphouse bolts for salt and then are already rumor, pitched down a basalt rib to whichever leaner geometry raised them.

Other mammals are rumored rather than seen. The mat whispers about shrews but you only hear their story: a zipper-running at night under the cushion, scalloped openings in midge pupal cases, a micro-skeleton one day revealed in a pool that went dry too fast. Mice descend in the worst weather from the lower scrub and make a field out of a pumphouse floor for three days, then vanish when the wind lays back enough that a hawk can be practical again.

Spiders own the meshes. They are not big, even for spiders, but what they lack in drama they make up in judgement. They understand wind by posture and tension. Their webs aren’t circles but bands, braced like sails and stepped back with guy-lines so they hum rather than tear; the sound the meshes make—the coarse hiss, the paper-tearing whisper—is a grammar the spiders read. Dew swells their lines into necklaces at dawn; by noon the necklaces have evaporated and they have moved their anchor points one notch over to ride the day. Those that try the rim fail early; those that learn the lee edges of buttresses and the shaded side of posts set their egg sacs low and let the whole plain parent them.

There is fungus, quietly exclaiming in the dark. Not toadstools; the place is too clean and too thin. But filaments, fat and bright where the mat is fat and bright, dim and delicate where the peat underlayer goes sweet. They trade sugars around the plain the way very old towns trade gossip, paying particular attention to the butterworts and the cushion-tips, connecting sun-rich spots to sweating cracks in basalt where a trickle of iron can be had. The smell that comes up when you kneel—sweet and sour wet cellulose, metal breath, a fraction of ozone—is their voice added to the mat’s.

In the pumphouse lives a second set of residents, imported and unromantic: silverfish under the lip of the threshold where boots have burnished bright a stripe; a spider that decided one ceiling bolt was a canyon rim and her web the river; a light-blind moth that was carried in on a jacket and will spend its life making sense of galvanized sheet. The hum is a body-heat to those cold-blooded grammar-readers, and they collect there on storm days as if the device was a small sun.

People live here like punctuation, set out singly in the sentence of land so their meanings don’t run together. They do not own the place—industry is a polite word for being tolerated—but they have a culture, a low, spare one shaped by waterbodies and noise. They read by hand more than by eye because glare lies, and they speak with shoulders and flags because the roar insists. They carry lengths of webbing to become suddenly plural when a fold looks untrustworthy, moving like glacier travelers across a surface that is almost but not quite frozen. On the fog fields they are janitors, walking the seams with palms, feeling for grommets working loose, for a line that went from hum to frantic. In their pockets are the same things, season to season: a square of mesh folded too small, a twist of wire, a ring of lichen scraped from a post to show a child how time looks when it makes circles.

They name what returns: the Lake That Will Not Be a Lake, the Thread That Refuses, Hail Alley, the Run Where We Lost the Flag. They track a cushion’s growth with a nail in a door frame and are patient about it. Some of them are seasonal, up and back down, carrying the plateau’s particular smell home in wool and hair—waking in kitchens months later with the bass of the rim in their ribs as if the machine were still on. A few never leave. They become particular. They know the trick of walking on blue-gloss noon green like crossing spun glass without breaking it. They can find the dumb, cold pipe half buried under ten thousand hands of plant by reading where the mat has changed its mind. The edges, for them, are moral things. They turn bolts until their wrist says the rim is safe, then turn them again because their shoulder says the rim is a liar.

Children do not live here, but the place still teaches. Apprentices turn up raw and narrow and leave six months later with calves like braided rope and a new boredom with the world’s quieter noises. They will gesticulate for the rest of their lives even in ordinary rooms. They will stand on city corners in autumn when a building vent coughs a skin of cold and glance up expecting a mesh to sound and will be briefly, deeply homesick for something that was not a home.

Winter reduces the cast and writes the mat tighter. The cushions sugar themselves against freeze; anthocyanins riot at the tips and make the whole plain go bruised and fine. Springtails gather like pepper and move like shadows on thaw days, lifting in black storms no higher than an ankle. When the fog returns heavy enough to leave rime on meshes and a white lining to the pumphouse bolts, the spiders thin and the silverfish thrive. The swifts go, the corvids stay, because someone has to keep track of bright wire the wind rearranged.

Nothing very large makes a den here. The mat, which is the landlord, is not friendly to weight. Its economy is spread and share; it does not like puncture. That so much of what lives chooses to be small is not argument; it is compliment.

If you want a center to this thing, think of a lung laid horizontal. It drinks cloud and spills river. It holds its own wet in the face of wind that never stops leaving. In its fabric is threaded a town’s thirst and a binder full of rules and the unimportant hunger of mites and the important patience of long plants. The plain itself is the animal, and water is what it eats and what it breathes and what it makes. Everybody else—rotifer, swift, worker with a red flag—finds a way to fit in the spaces where the breath is not too strong.
