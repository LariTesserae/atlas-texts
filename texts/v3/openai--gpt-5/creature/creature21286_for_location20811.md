Low things live here first. The small authors of the place. They keep their bodies close to the film and make their arguments in friction and breath: insects sawing at the heat until the whole noon hums like a single wire; snails writing the stone with tongues fine as paintbrushes, leaving cursive that only dampness can read; crabs testing the border between out and in with an anxious arithmetic of claws. You don’t meet them by looking for faces. You meet them by stepping wrong and being convinced, instantly, that the ground has opinions. They attend to every inch as if each were a separate country.

Threaded through that busy smallness are the long animals of sound and water—the frogs and the fish—who do not mind being felt before they are seen. The frogs count the hours in pulses and change the weather more than clouds do. The fish draw their maps in rushes and whiskings, then take them back again. Shallows keep their own schools: glass minnows that choose light over secrecy, drab-settled sleepers that have retired from speed, silver bodies whose idea of flight is to break the surface, get a handful of breathless notes, and fall with the same surprise every time. Little crocodilians keep the edge honest. They lie as if chopped from shade until shade moves.

But if you stand with your hand against the sweating seam of rock long enough, if you let the chill of buried rivers persuade your skin, then another presence arrives. Not an animal with a name that fits in the mouth. A people who do not introduce themselves all at once. They are a way of keeping to the low rule without surrendering to it. The plain has made them ankle-minded. They have eyes that have learned the grammar of glare. Their stride is not long but it is exact, their weight distributed like a heron’s idea of walking—down through the heel, then the flat of the foot, then a patience before the toes trust the film. They solve mud with sandals that look like nothing, just thin mesh, but they spread a person into something the water can argue with instead of swallow. Their skins shine with the ordinary oils of survival and are salted by the place into a finish that discourages both sun and biting things. They are not tall. That is the first courtesy they ever paid this country and they have not broken it.

They live in separations because distance is honesty here. Households hold their own hammocks like thoughts held in separate rooms. They step toward one another only when the sound says it is safe to be close—at dusk, when the insects loosen their grip, or in the dry when the sky has more nerve. They speak across water not with voices but with contrivances of reed and shell that the breeze can carry. A string of snail shells on a cypress knee says I am here. A board thumped three times on calcrete and then twice more says I need a lift. They never shout; shouting belongs to thunder, and thunder is a visitor they treat with the formality due a guest who often forgets to show up.

The seam in the limestone is their school and their tap. They visit the bands—iron-orange, black-green, the chalk of new tufa—the way uplanders visit altars. You can see the history of a year in the mineral: where the weeping strengthened or thinned, where a dry spell asked the aquifer to be more generous than habit allows. They read it with their palms. They taste it at the wrist. They take what drips, always, but they also nudge it. Low walls of marl shepherd the rills into curves the land will accept. They carve nothing straight. Straight is a promise the water will use against you.

Height is currency, so they spend it slowly. Every inch they can lift is a year they buy for a root, a cradle, a cache of fish dried to leather. Old shell makes new lens; old boards of calcrete are coaxed out from the hard and shored together until a platform holds the idea of shelter above the practice of flood. Their shelters are humbly outrageous—not houses so much as admissions that houses are a kind of joke out here. Frames of cypress and mangrove, roofs threaded from sedge and palm, legs raised on piles with lichen that counts their age in faint rings, a knee of bench-rock borrowed from a friendly ledge. From a distance you might take them for part of the vegetation’s strategy. That is the point. What rises above reed-height belongs to birds. They have no desire to argue that law.

Birds tutor them anyway. Ibises inspect their gardens as if reading. A line of white heads bobbing in judgment over a new bed where lilies have been persuaded into a clump that will feed a household for a season—chopped corms boiled to a sweetness that remembers the water and does not resent the pot. Herons show them the use of stillness and how to be a wall that fish mistake for luck. Gallinules teach them how to run lightly on reflections for three or four miraculous steps—their children practice it on mornings when the pans are gridded with a breath-thin crust of salt and you can almost stand on a rumor. When raptors write the sky with their old urgencies, the place listens and so do they. Jobs pause. Hands open and empty. Heads learn the angle of a correct shadow.

Their tools are the aftermaths of other creatures and the polite thefts from the rock. Hooks carved to a left-handed curve, because that is the way a snail’s last argument turns inside its shell. Nets with meshes fine enough for minnows when the flood is persuading them up and down the old river ghosts. Tridents for the slow fish that believe in camouflage more than consequences. Dull wedges for calcrete, sharp spatulas of limestone for prying tufa that can be ground with herbs into a paste that takes the sting out of sedge cuts. Their best knives are not knives. They are ideas about pressure and patience.

They eat without hurry because here nothing honest moves fast. Apple snails pried with those left-bending hooks, slid back into their own shells with spices, and warmed at the edge of a low fire while the wind edits the smell. Crabs argued out of their punctuation and set to crack under stones. The occasional rash of silver when fish have chosen panic—then smoke, then rope, then a string of white bellies shining on a line stretched between two dwarf cypress that have tolerated the company. In a good year a boar is insulted into carelessness near a hammock and the winter has meat that pushes back against the salt. They love the white hearts of lilies and the sheen of sedge seeds under a thumb and the bitter, mint-sharp teas that make the heavy air fall more kindly into a chest.

They reckon time in long measures you can stand inside. Flood is not a date. Flood is a persuasion. They watch it come up the way you watch a child grow or a promise kept too slowly to make a scene about. Camps rise with it. Racks of smoke shift. Ladders add a rung. The youngest walk out to the benches, tow their feet in the pans, learn the exact day a hand-deep rill becomes a knee-deep hazard. Their stories take afternoons. They have no patience for climax because thunder has trained them to expect it and be content when it rescinds itself. The plain’s oldest joke is the dark anvil glowing on three horizons, the mouth already tasting rain that will not arrive. They laugh and pour another cup of tea.

Secrets belong to the mud, so they keep theirs there. Caches of salt crystals wrapped in grass and slid into a seam only a hand knows. The track to a sweet seepage disguised not with brush but with the absence of disturbance. The quiet bargains with snakes. You almost never see the snakes. That is fine. Seeing is a noisy habit and they are proud of their listening. A woman can tell you, hand to the air, whether the frogs have decided to shift their song a mile west; a man with his ear to calcrete can count a school he will never lay eyes on.

They do not gather often and never in the center because there is no center, only a set of agreements about edges. But when the dry opens its long throat and the seam in the rock forgets less, they will walk from their separate households and meet on a bench that is generous enough to seat the patient. They bring their distances with them—gifts taken from where they stand apart. Slabs of weed-stiff rope, baskets that smell of the last flood’s top foot, a bone pick rubbed to a darker shine by the same hand for twenty years, a child named for a weather. They excuse themselves from work for an evening and lay their wrists against the cold bands of the rock to measure how much river is still ancient under their feet. The birds permit them the height. That, too, feels like a feast.

They have laws but no lawmakers. They have rules that grew by being true. If there is rock, it will weep; do not waste that grief. If there is a hollow, it will brim; do not promise a dry bed to anyone but the sky. An inch of rise is not a little thing; count it like you count a birth. Stand apart so you can hear each other. Leave the lanes of reflection clear; the sky practices on them and you are not the sky. Nothing you say with your mouth matters if the cranes are not also saying it with their legs.

Children test the borders of these sayings like everything young does. They run on salt the morning after a fierce blue noon and fall through the crust with both legs and come home laughing, white-sugared and bleeding, and no one scolds them until dusk, when the gallinules begin their scandals on the far pans. Then someone tells a story about a woman who tried to stand where the place had written open water and how the water took her welts and gave her a scar so tidy it looked like a decision.

Do they believe in gods? They believe in the old rivers. They believe in whatever it is inside a crocodilian’s skull that lets it hold the shape of the surface in its mind while the rest of it is in another world. They believe in the looseness of lightning and in the good manners of height. They believe in the kind of patience that is a pleasure, not a penance. If you ask them for a name for themselves, they will give you one that means “those who keep the edges legible.” Or they will shrug and ask you what the frogs were saying on your way in. It is the same answer.

The other inhabitants accept them the way the place accepted the old curve of a river and then forgot to erase it. Herons will use the roofs as pause. Deer will stand at the spill of a platform and work the taste of mint from the water a human just lifted a bucket out of, both parties at ease with the exchange. A boar will clean a root under a rack that still remembers fish. Dragonflies will take a wrist for a reed. Snakes will write their slow cursives in the shadow of a sleeping child who scents like salt and herbs and nothing vertical.

If you stay long enough you start to see that what lives here is not a roster but a way—low, warm, articulate with water. The animals, the rock’s reluctant generosity, the weathers that announce themselves as if from far inside your own chest, and the people who have learned to live as lightly as a reflection and as lastingly as a ledge—they make a mind that has already decided what it wants to be. Edges read to the end of their own sentences. Pauses that are full, not empty. A place kept true by those who understand that to survive here is not to build a tower but to put your hand to the weeping stone and feel a river that does not know your name and love it anyway.
