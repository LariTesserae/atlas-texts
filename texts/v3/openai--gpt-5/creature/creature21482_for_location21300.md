The first thing that lives here is the breath itself. It goes and comes like a tide no one sees, a body-sized river pulled through stone by weather a horizon away. It is the sovereign of this country—cool, exact, punctual—teaching everything else its hours. Where it squeezes, it makes voice. Where it slows, it lays down water as if paying rent: bead by bead along a seam, a gloss on a rung of rock. It remembers the size of each crack and plays them like a set of pipes; the inhabitants learn to read these notes the way others read light.

Light lives here only in narrow, stingy pieces. It is an immigrant with papers stamped “temporary,” arriving down the wells and chimneys, stopping where the angle refuses it. It keeps small gardens for itself—rings of ferns gripping a damp bracket, a hairy ribbon of algae where the rock bleeds a thread of water, lichens salted on stone that still remembers sun. It gives some animals leave to see and keeps the rest blind. It marks territories with its edges: beyond this drip, no green; beyond this boulder, only memory of color. It is a landlord who doesn’t walk his whole estate.

The rock lives in a way you can mistake for stoicism. It is young here by cave standards, full of new cuts. Its faces are honest and unsanded, the fractures like fresh teeth. It has not yet grown the soft whiskers of stalactites except in certain moods—milk-drips stalled to tips, a curtain that has not learned a ripple. It eats and exhales air, trades grains across a room, prints dew maps on its skin. It accepts every nest and footprint, all the small crafts that cling to it, and in exchange offers shelves, cradles, arches that turn wind to music. It holds a grammar; new pieces slot in as if always written.

Plants live, but not as owners: they live as punctuation. Ferns in the skylight halos write a bristling fringe along cracks. Lichens make constellations where the weather can still reach. Algae paints thin green commas down a wall that never dries. Everything herbaceous is in retreat the instant the light bends away. Dried grasses blow in and take up residence as relics, not as citizens: crackle-drifts at the foot of the wells, a brittle archive that notes the seasons of the surface without belonging to them anymore.

Animals are the loudest inhabitants and the most numerous by desire. The swifts own the holes to the sky. They have built the same nests for longer than any single bird has lived—lattices of twigs glued and re-glued so often you can’t tell yesterday’s work from a decade’s. Their lineages are knotted into the ledges. They read the moving air the way sailors read tide lines on a piling, and they take the updraft like an elevator. They have a use for the hard things: a block’s sharp shoulder remembers the knock of beak and shell where a quick gray shape bangs a snail dry in the draft. Their young learn a ceiling as a neighborhood learns cross streets: take the flute that sings a high E, glide to the violet patch of lichen, bank at the shelf that clinks when you tap it. To the swifts, the slits of light are doors that never shut, and the whole house is one enormous hallway.

The bats own what the light doesn’t. They are the dark made mobile. They hang by the tens of thousands in black hanging orchards, a fruit that never ripens and never quite stills. They warm as a city warms, by crowd—body on body, mothers holding a weather for pups that can’t make their own. Their voices add up to a single seam of sound that threads the system end to end: each colony a ripple, the sum a held note that changes hands but not volume. They leave in pulses that feel like the thought of a sea crossing itself, and they return the same way, an endless pulse that has only crests. They map the stone by return-time, and what they teach their children is not where things are exactly but how fast answers come back. You could change a room by a hand’s width and no bat would notice for years because the note would take the same breath to answer.

Under and after them, in the dim where the air sets a dew, live the long-legged tribes that look like ornament from a distance and hunger close up. The cave crickets make frosting out of themselves along the humid seams, jeweled sheets breathing in soft accord. When they move, they move like grass shifting in wind you can’t feel, turning as one, ticking their dry rain. They leave the rooms at night sometimes, up the chimneys, to browse at the edges of the world you can see, and then they rain back down along the cool walls, bellies holding the surface’s scrapings into the cave economy. They mate in the corners where plinks have carved bowls you could drink from with a fingertip, and their egg clutches sit like a thought kept for later.

Spiders live at the thresholds, as if law demanded they declare themselves at every door. They draw triangles where air converges, rigs delicate trip-lines across mouths of corridors turned to wind tunnels. They are engineers of the slightest pull. When a fluttering thing mistakes a breeze for a river and lets itself be delivered, the spider is already at the knot, home and trap being one. Pseudoscorpions ride other bodies to new territories, stowaways on cricket backs and moth wings, pincers held up like toasts. Harvestmen browse the edges of guano dunes in their slow, many-legged way, keeping books with their little mouths. Whip scorpions flatten into thin rumors of themselves under shelves and sample the passing air with their wire-long feelers, angling the world into an equation of scents and minuscule breaths.

In the cradles of black between colonies—on the soft hills that smell like salt and sweet and sting—the pale janitors move. Bright isopods like thumbnails of moonlight labor in small committees. Cockroaches the color of milk with eyes set like garnets go out and return in jerks, tasting, refusing, taking. Millipedes ringed in lantern-amber coil and uncoil like new cursive. Beetles with elytra that catch stray light in a jewel flash drill their forelegs into the powder and bring up news: here a seed husk, there a moth’s papered wing. Mites, too small to be counted without counting the idea of them, bloom and recede on whatever will let them happen. On the guano itself a different country flowers—filaments and films, a white beard of fungi that drinks nitrogen and gives the air its astringent bite; bacteria that write their chemical alphabets in crystals crusting along the drier edges. The dunes breathe slower than the rooms but they, too, are alive with decisions.

Along the rims of light, the edge hunters pass. Ringtails with their banded compromise between cat and raccoon stitch the circumference of the skylights, two bright coins for eyes held in lamps where no lamps should be. They know the hour of the swift return and the spillways of inattentive. Foxes come like modest aristocrats, velvet-footed, consenting to take from the cave but not to be of it, their whiskers thrilled by the low thunder of wings. They never go far. They do not enter the language of the inner rooms where sound replaces line of sight; they eat at the border as if not to be seen reading another country’s mail.

In the pools, the monks keep vows. The fish are white as porcelain and as breakable, with no use for eyes they do not have. Their heads carry the long reach of their other senses: taste fanned out on faces, pressure mapped in a continuous attention to what changes. They live slowly and practice omission—no fast movements, no fat wasted, no day to divide into parts. Amphipods tumble in their tiny parabolas like snow shaken in paperweight glass, quick as thought when a light touches them. They are poppy seeds in quantity and ghosts in texture, and the pool is as full of them as a winter sky is of small noises. Flatworms slide like signatures nobody but them can read. If there are salamanders, they are the dissolved kind that forget legs when legs aren’t needed, pale filaments of appetite with the echo of a face.

In the rock there are lives you can’t call animals without insulting rocks. Iron bacteria draw whiskers of rust where a drip brings them oxygen to spare. Films of life you might mistake for dew rim the pools with skins so thin you are not sure whether you broke them or your breath did. Nitrifiers cycle the ammonia of the bat cities into something the air can carry away. Sulfur-lovers take the scraps of this chemistry and knit a lacework on gypsum needles, crystal fur that grows where the sums work out. They are a kingdom of quiet in a nation of noise, and they are as decisive as any hunter with fangs.

Visitors come and go, and they, too, live here in the way a migrating bird lives in its sky. Owls sometimes hover over a chimney, yellow eyes turned down as if reading the fine print on the dark, and drop to lift a bat from the stream as a hand might pluck a straw from current. A snake might anchor its loops in some crag and surprise the night with a fast mouth, but if it does it leaves almost no sign because stones here keep secrets well. Beetles from the surface fall down the light wells and die, their lacquered shells brilliant as a thought of summer. Moths paste themselves to the stone in paralyzed dawn and later are reduced to faint cobweb syllables.

There are people of time, too—inheritances that are not genes but maps. A swift chick hatches under a ledge polished by the feathers of its great-great-grandparents and asks the air what it has always answered. A bat pup grows its ears into the room’s long measures, the way a child in a bell tower grows to know the bell as a neighbor. A cricket’s path across a wall is a palimpsest of past moltings, tiny pale jackets hung behind it like coats in a mudroom; the thread-thin fungus on the seam will write over these and write over its own writing, until a piece of rock the size of a hand contains a library of decisions nobody but a spider reads.

Everything that belongs permanently knows how to be many in almost nothing. They budget. They bank moisture in noon-cool hours and spend it where dew pearls. They queue for corridors of air the way commuters learn a city’s traffic, stepping out into the flow to be carried, crabbing sideways when a faster body comes. Even the blind fish save: they will mouth a crumb of something for a while to be sure of it. A fox learns that this chimney is for bats and that one for birds and that between them is a corridor of smell that empties at midnight. An isopod population waxes and wanes with maternity season fixed above it like a moon.

They all obey the laws of the dark without writing them. No one makes a light. No one paints a sign. They know rooms by how voices die in them and find each other by tune. If a pressure change says storm, the whole place shifts its calendar. If the draft thickens, the bats use it as if towed; the swifts offload cargo of insects into it; the spiders reset their triangles a finger-width to the left. A drop plinks in the same cup for years and, unheard by anything with ears tuned to higher dramas, a certain bacterium uses that constancy the way a monk uses a bell. Order is practiced, not imposed.

What lives here is a parliament convened by stone and air. It is a choir without a conductor that knows the note and renews it. It is the presence of hunger wearing ten thousand shapes and the presence of patience wearing even more. It is twigs turned to architecture, guano turned to bread, breath turned to river, darkness turned to map. It is bright eyes at the rim and none within. It is young rock that looks freshly broken and, because of that youth, is honest about what it is hosting: a trial balance of life drawn at small margins that nevertheless adds up to wealth.

You can give them names—swift, bat, cricket, cockroach, isopod, millipede, spider, whip scorpion, ringtail, fox, fish, amphipod—and be right. But the names that matter here are verbs: hang, pour, cling, drink, ride, listen, wait. That is their citizenship. That is who they are. If you strip the proper nouns off, what remains still lives: a community of breathers arranged by a machine of stone and temperature, making from condensed weather and wind-borne crumbs a loud, durable life you can feel humming in your bones long after you climb back into the thin, careless light.
