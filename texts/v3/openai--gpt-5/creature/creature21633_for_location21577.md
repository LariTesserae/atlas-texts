What lives here begins with those who can bear the noise and take it inside without flinching. They are animals with lateral lines that read the sea’s handwriting across their flanks; with inner ears weighted by bone dust; with whiskers that turn pressure into fact; with bodies that are more shrug than stance. They are plants that are not trees but something more like lungs on stalks, built to inhale sway and exhale slack, anchoring themselves to old stone with palms of living root. They are machines that have learned not to announce themselves, that hold their breath and hum in the kelp’s key. They are colonies you could break with a fingernail and lives longer than empires braided together, all of them doing the same thing in a thousand ways: gripping and letting go in time to weather.

The forest here is not wood, but it has trunks and canopies and a floor. Bull kelp rises as if rehearsing standing over and over, each stipe threaded with gas and patience. The holdfasts feel like carved knuckles, splayed hands that have found the one right purchase on old basalt and will not let it go until a winter decides otherwise. Laminaria spreads thick blades like pennants. Between them, reds branch and cudgel and web, an architecture of soft antlers and lace that drinks pale light. The coralline encrusters paint every honest surface old-rose and brittle pink; tap them and they knock back softly, like coral remembering something warmer in its sleep. In the hollows of the plateau where surge unwinds into shiver, eelgrass makes valleys of hair. Even the barest slab only looks naked; you kneel to it and it is alive—films and skins and fur of things too small to name, a library of appetite a millimeter deep.

There are cities at the kelp’s feet. In among the holdfast roots you find a borough of small fugitives: snails with shields for roofs creeping in deliberate arcs, chitons buttoned down so tightly they might be iron. Hermit crabs change their addresses in a flutter of waiting claws. Urchins mow—patient as slow rain—the sugar off coralline and leave the rock rasped clean in purple hedgehog crawls. Their teeth don’t sleep. Where the urchins bunched and swarmed and all that was soft became skeleton, a pair of wolf-eels moved in, faces like old men and jaws made for quarry that fights back. The two live side by side for years, a domestic scene that happens to be fanged, taking apart shells as if unfastening coats. When the long-armed stars were plentiful they hunted here too, slow bright disasters slung low; these days their cousins—leather, blood, a handful of ochre—work the night shift and leave shy five-toed prints in silt no one will see.

Rockfish suspend like punctuation just where kelp shade meets open—a row of commas, each with the right to one rocking parcel of surge. Kelp greenlings keep the punctuation from turning into a period; they come and go in amounts that say maybe and not yet. Lingcod lie as if they were the dark underside of stone and then explode into assertion, blue-marbled mouths making quick grammar of anything they can swallow. In the cusp months, a male will claim a cave the color of bottled rain and fan his eggs with the sober gravity of a lighthouse keeper, beating down fungi and thieves with a fin until his energy turns thin. Sculpins look like something the bottom invented to think with—expressions that are really camouflage, fins that are really hands.

There is more water than floor, so much of what lives here wears a decision about speed. The herring are switchblades opening and closing in unison, silver hinged to light. Sandlance are gone, then part of the sand, then a sheet of fish again, taking the sentence of a shadow to heart. Juveniles in thicknesses of glass and breath practice agreement and apostasy, plus the skill of becoming line instead of dot if a cormorant lances past. Jellies go by like thought—their bells translating heartbeat into travel, their trailing veils tricked with stinging grammar. Comb jellies are the quiet brilliance of a piano in another room; the row of cilia stepping light turns the little traffic of blue-green along their ribs into something you can taste if you close your mouth around it and pay attention.

Out where the plateau no longer bothers to be floor and simply stops, the drop glosses and glows and then grows serious about blue. Along that tidy line the sea does a trick with density; cold rises out of the deep as if it were silk in a well. That is where big backs roll through: porpoises that look like punctuation without the sentence, surfacing in commas to breathe and to say “and.” A gray whale lifts a slice of itself heavy as habit, breathes a plume, breathes again, goes down hauling the shape of the place over its broad head like a hood. Humpbacks drag the rain apart with their fins and close it behind them; when they sing, the escarpment takes the sound into it and hands it back longer. Those songs don’t entertain; they measure distance and possibility the way a carpenter lays out a room. Farther below and off the shelf edge, sablefish patrol their favorite isopleths, taking advantage of gravity’s politeness, and shrimp move in slow red countries over benches that arrived one stone at a time.

Birds do not so much live here as happen in the exact places needed. Gulls set themselves like kites on the shoulders of wind and use each other for direction. Cormorants enter the water as arguments and come out heavy as sticks, green eyes full of fish decisions. Murres don’t believe in land if they can help it; they choose to be bullets and shoot into the wrinkled skin for a minute of hunger’s relief. Jaegers and skuas do their thievery without shame. On storm days the long-distance fliers, dusky and disinterested, draw runes across the weather—the albatrosses that follow fronts in patient, tireless geometry and land only if something has gone wrong with sky. A foggy day crowns everyone in wet; gulls are ringed in saints’ light they don’t need.

The mammals find edges—the leeward, the lee of another body, the lee inside a kelp’s heavy skirt. Seals congregate where the plateau hunches up and gives them speaking rights above break; their talk is all elbows, splash, disapproval, the humor of the impatient. Sea lions own whatever they can climb. On rare weak-water days there will be an otter stretched like a thick belt around a bed of kelp, a face like a monk snapped out of prayer by a mussel. She will comb her cub with wet hands that look human until you remember she has more ribs than you and carries a toolkit in the drape of her own fur. Take the otters away and the urchins will ask to own the walls. Let the otters thrive and the kelp rises into a forest that stays forest after storms. Either way the rules come first: bend or travel flat, or be remade as substrate.

People are a kind of seasonal weather. You don’t see them often, but you can trace their presence in the quiet decisions the place wears. The broken tower on the rocks is stone with a square memory and lichen working maps of splash zones across it. The weather mast stands as if someone had correctly calculated the number of insults the wind can deliver and taught it to shrug. It sings in crosswinds instead of shouting into them, blades ribbed to bite gust and spend it, a polite reed in a relentless mouth. Farther out, orange drums sit like buoys with intentions. Below each a piston takes each swell and unrolls it into something that can run a freezer and a desk lamp and a kettle miles away; at the bottom of the escarpment a cable, armored and bearded over, lays a cold hum along the stone. Sharks and skates taste it at the edge of their thoughts, an extra line on a ledger of currents and prey.

That humming is company for a machine that lives closer to the ground than some fish. It has tracked feet instead of fins, a low roof the color of a storm and a long intake that is not really a mouth—more of a rake that understands mercy. It harvests not like a herd but like a gardener—passes it makes are careful and prearranged, leaving the anchors, opening lanes so the waves can spool out without tearing the anchorage up by its hair. During weather it squats and becomes geology for the length of a gale, wearing eiderdown of bubbleweed and the badges of its own patience: barnacles beside dents, scrapes that everyone else has translated into habitat. Sometimes an octopus stops by and interrogates it with the attention due to a strange, deaf neighbor. The machine does not try to learn the sea; it tries not to make the sea learn it.

Between these larger presences are lives so numerous that their sum is atmosphere. Diatoms rock steady in their glass houses, worshiping the part of day the rest of the world wastes. Dinoflagellates take their evenings personally. Copepods throb in clouds like a rumor; krill draw dusks down and push dawns up, the migration that resets life for all the hungry. Tube worms write the same sentence in chalk in a hundred thousand places you never looked. Nudibranchs—little churches in candy colors—eat churches, hydroids reduced to belief and jelly. A crab swings between two kelp blades like a clumsy punctuation mark and flattens itself when a shadow kneads the surface. The snapping shrimp keep score with noise, and the urchins rasp along in their beads-and-bone way, and the fish flicker, and the place turns over its own light into food and back again.

Below where the light resigns you can enter rooms built of sponge and politics. Glass sponges pipe minute rivers through themselves and turn stray bits of life into less frantic futures. Sea pens write and erase themselves all day long in currents, bright quills with bioluminescent ink for emergencies. On benches built by years of very slow avalanches, thorny snails and sea cucumbers keep a kind of civic order by eating whatever sacredness drops out of the lit world above. It is not empty down here; it is rigorous. Sound moves through like law.

Life lives by law and by loophole. Everything tall is a flexible conversation between stiffness and surrender; kelp stipes that yield exactly enough, the spines of fish that fold and cut, wings that in other places would be for air being here appointed to flight among slabs of water. Everything that lingers is anchored by more than friction—by glue, by suction, by architecture, by strategy—and even so is ready to go. Schooling is joining, defense by choir. Solitude is a math you do with your gill beats. Sight is a ration; hearing is a province. The ones that make their own light do not waste it; you flash when touched, or when eaten, or when sex would be more efficient with punctuation. Nothing starts at the surface; the bright skin is breakfast for someone else’s long thinking.

People come in careful weather on boats that wear the same humility their machines have learned. Divers appear as extra fish with more bubbles and worse manners, their eyes wide out of necessity. They touch the rail bolted to the rock and become again the kind of animal that likes a handhold; they check the barnacle load on the kelp-rake, scrape, listen, leave. In a little building upwind on land somewhere you cannot see, the hum of the array becomes the whir of a freezer full of bait and a lamp over a kitchen table and a charge slipped quietly into a battery. Nobody here throws trash on the water because it will only come back and argue.

There are drifters and there are strays. Logs arrive out of river mouths and accumulate colonies of goosenecks that open and close in unison like a row of tiny churches practicing vespers. A plastic drum from some other hunger bobs at the edge of the trench for a week, collects a skin of green, earns a temporary neighborhood of juvenile fish that use its quivering shadow as a fake island until a true storm demonstrates truths about fakes. Kelp rafts themselves, dislodged forest sections braided by surge, ferry amphipods and hitchhikers out to where the blue deepens to no-never-mind and become at last food as well.

If you had to name the resident citizens, you might be tempted to point—a wolf-eel here, a harbor seal there, an octopus behind a lip of stone making a midden of someone else’s mistakes, a brittle star writing cursive you will not learn to read. But it would be truer to say what lives here is that whole arrangement: the patient skeleton of an older world holding shape under a younger one that refuses ever to be finished, and in the narrow argument between those two certainties a population of improvisers. The parishioners of this place are not devout to one image. They are devout to rules that make them function:

- Find quiet by balancing motions that have no interest in you.
- Yield like someone who intends to stay for centuries.
- Announce yourself, if you must, in a voice the house accepts.
- Pay for bright with cold and time, and know your credit will be called in.
- Distrust the easy day; work close to the long slope, not the single promise of calm.

What lives here is the kelp that breathes and the machinery that learned to breathe with it; fish that write sentences you can only see from the corner of your eye; mammals that make a family out of weather; birds that read the water’s handwriting from the moving ceiling; sponges that build rooms in the dark and keep them swept; a thousand small clackings and raspings and opinions; a cable that runs like a vein of other people’s warmth; and you, if you stay still long enough in a lee to be mistaken for rock, bone-deep aware of the weight above you and the old lie under it, hearing everything that lives here go about the complicated work of being busy as weather.
