What lives here practices continuance. Not one thing at a time, and not with the sharp silhouettes you expect from a drier world. The residents are hung in the same medium, breathing the same warm sentence of air; they cohere out of it like meaning out of murmur.

Begin at the level that pretends to be ground and isn’t. The rooted people—if “rooted” is the right word for creatures whose bodies are as much wick as wall—are busy with transactions no other biome would let them attempt so boldly. Broad-fanned palms and palmettos, their ovals lacquered dark to drink dusk, have learned to make bowls of themselves. Every rosette is a lensed eye, and each eye holds a republic: mosquito rafts angled at the skin to siphon the atmosphere, red shrimp that swim like commas, dragonfly nymphs so thin you miss them until they hinge and reduce a neighbor. Tiny snails with eggglass shells patrol those personal lakes. Rotifers scrape the slopes where the leaf meets its waterline. Tardigrades trundle, indestructible as punctuation. The tank-keepers secrete sugars around their rims to feed ants, and the ants, in turn, defend the watery state and drag in grit to thicken the meniscus and foster roots. To call these epiphytes “plants” is to underspeak them. They are built rooms that never hardened, nurseries that never close.

Between the tanks hang strands and ladders: lianas, cross-stitches and guy-lines that hold the whole floating world together. Some of them host gardens of their own, ant-tended epiphyte farms sprouting from dampened bark and leaf-scar. Ants cut tiny windows in the woven dirt and bark, air the nurseries, fertilize with the condensed arithmetic of their wastes. Elsewhere, vines keep a slower commerce with beetles and moths. Thermogenic spadices—meat-colored, their breath faintly of ferment and iron—give off measured warmth that draws cold-blooded messengers and then feeds them to the hour of meeting. The message is not scent alone but gradient, a hill of temperature in a land where wind forgot to carry news. Beetles come and feast and sleep in the flower’s heated chambers; they depart, powdered with someone else’s plan.

The buttressed trees—the elders—are not pillars so much as sails driven by capillaries. They make shadow nets of themselves, broad to spread weight over the patient raft and deep to wade into the unseen water table that is also their body. Along their boles, sheets of condensation slip, and lichens taste and tint the runnels with slow chemistry. Their skins sweat and so shelter: paper nests of stingless bees paste themselves to the under-curves, a city of clay and honey that hums its own convection. The bees manufacture wind, fanning in shifts; the hive drafts a weather that both cools its brew and sends small breezes into this windless parish. Carpenter beetles knock—wood chimes—through hollowed stems, tapping to mates a map of where the food decays and how quickly.

Between trunks and among the roots are lawns of generosity: kraken-armed ferns that lay down shade-room after shade-room, and bryophytes so sure of this place’s promise that they colonize vertical faces, up-cupping each drop until the world agrees to grow them. Bracket fungi laminate the dead and dying, gilded fans turning logs into staircases for finer citizens. Stinkhorns rehearse their same evergreen joke: perfume of carrion in a land where almost nothing distinctly rots, and the joke works every time, bringing in flies with green-metal faces and tongues like paintbrushes.

The chalk-lumps that weep—a mineral perspiration, a steady saline whisper—are anchorages for a slower nation. Snails, patient teachers of pace, rasp the crust and store it under their own names as shell. The weeping feeds more than shells: calcium goes uncompromised into tiny spines and egg coats, into the balance-sheets of water fleas and the plates of minute shrimp, into the yolks that will provincialize a whole new world inside one bromeliad cup.

In and under the mat, the invention of fish continues. They are not all of a piece. Some have learned the trick of air, raising their muzzles to the film beneath a leaf and tasting the heavier oxygen there, shingling their gill arches into a labyrinth that handles breaths one at a time like coins. Others run on electricity, whispering with weak fields that stutter against roots and find out the next inch’s truth without ever needing a sightline. Many are bodies made into oars and levers, built to sift blackwater without haste: since water here is movement declared by consensus rather than slope, they tune to pressure-change and the faint pull of a stomata field opening on a hot patch of leaf. At the edges of open pans, bubble-nesters stitch a froth roof under sheltering fronds, foam houses that cradle eggs at that perfect interim between air and drink. The nests last not because weather spares them but because so many small lives agree to work where each drop would otherwise fall alone.

Amphibians thrive like the very idea of amphibian was invented for this. There are frogs that are drums—each body a rubber kettle ringing a note that partners memorize over ferns’ hum. There are frogs that are threads, narrow and quick as the suture on a surgeon’s needle, using capillarity as architecture to cling to any vertical damp. Glass bellies glide among leaves and press their children to the underside of the world, eggs visible like pearls in jelly rosaries. Poison-colored parents that reared their poisons from the pantry of ants pace up to tank after tank and drop a single larva where the water is freshened by a beetle’s mistake. Later they bring unfertilized eggs, yolk as meal, visiting like a poem revised: again and again, similar and utterly necessary. In the peat itself, blind caecilians ratchet forward, ringed with ridges like a sentence inviting fingers to read it. Some of them grow a mother-skin for their young to eat, a living swaddle shed in scheduled generosity.

Reptiles manage heat and heft with the confidence of long practice. Small crocodilians ease beneath the lattice of roots and become itinerant forces, all rumor and wake. Turtles heavier than they seem by their absence learn the trick of weight distribution, their feet splayed and padded to lie on the raft and be accepted by it. There are snakes so aquatic they are green again from algae, and snakes that keep to rafters of the under-canopy and watch with chastened patience for the moment a frog’s machine falters. Lizards here shine as if varnished, their skins slick to foil saturation, their bellies polished to slide on leaf and root. Anoles that used to believe in day and night have become bilingual, their throats’ dewlaps glowing ember-red in an evening that doesn’t end, signage bright enough to read in pewter.

Birds are the grammar of breath made visible. Long-legged religionists stand in the mirror-water and practice stillness until it becomes a technology; then their head writes a stroke into the page, and a life ends without comment. Rails thread the palmetto tunnels, leaving polished seams of mud you recognize not by figure but by feel. Bitterns and herons, streaked for verticality, spend sunlight like misers. There are frugivores that know the calendar of figs and the warm arithmetic of ferment where fruit disintegrates by decision rather than disaster. High up—there, where the gray presses to green—the wing that rearranges air is both prophecy and punctuation. Sometimes it is the big bat, fox-faced and sable, shouldering through a lane it keeps the same way dragonflies do, a vast animal of intention gliding on membranes that gather heat and radiate it to steer. Sometimes it is a raptor, a cathedral of pinions that never bother with drumming when pressure and posture will say the same without noise.

Mammals lean light. Heavy things end up written in, slow and single, like the margins of a codex. There are sloths that are mossy as the logs they do not hurry to match, their fur combed to host lichens and moths whose larvae feast on their dung and so make fertilizer to green them further. There are small browsers with toes like spread hands and bellies that never cool, going palmetto to palmetto along the sugar-dark commute, pruning nothing too hard and in so doing shaping the tunnel for their descendants’ bellies too. Opossums unhook their feet only to tilt their faces into flowers warmed by politics and smell of old meat; their pink hands and clever snouts pull beetles from the warm rooms and carry pollen as side effect. Rats with webbed toes swim the platter-pools under fernrooms and surface under shelves of bracket fungus with whiskers beaded like rosaries. Bats—always—draw lines in the traffic of gnats, seamers who tailor the boundless insect cloth into garments their tiny skeletons can wear. Fruit bats nip a fig and let its sugar light a hidden galaxy in their bellies; insect bats lace ultrasound through the buttresses and stitch the place to their own moving maps.

Insect life is the majority opinion here. Dragonflies hold patents on sky-lanes; their routes are as practiced as signatures and as territorial. Damselflies sit like enamel pins on open water and tremble their abdomens to throw a standing ripple that says this square foot is mine, rest elsewhere. Katydids, violins with legs, warm music out of themselves in an octave the air was waiting for; they twang and answer, their wires running through the whole place by way of leaves and hair-rough stems. Weevils and stem-borers feel that rhythm through their own mandibles. There are bees that don’t sting but would die for home, wax-potted honeys made in rooms too soft to measure. There are wasps that paste their paper on the underside of a shelf of fungus and build an upside-down city tight as coral. Termites, for their part, construct in the negative, never a tower against gravity but a dark extension of tree into carton, an infiltration of building into living skin. They farm fungus inside a house you can mistake for bark, and the house breathes with the tree’s breath, and both bleed humidity that feeds the other.

Even spiders seem sanctioned into consensus. Orb webs slung between palms shine perpetually—as if strung with dew when it hasn’t “dewed” here since memory—and they hold because the air stays, because vibration is message not threat. Big fishing spiders test every black platter and decide whether to be reflection or disturbance. Amblypygids, the hand-shaped ghosts with soft-piano strides, tap their feelers against wood and speak in seismic whispers to mates who answer from around the curvature of a trunk.

Leeches, earthcolored and perfume-neutral, keep their appointments at the banks of pools that are not quite pools. They are patient accountants of red. Treehoppers footdrum in sedge corridors and shepherd their sticky children under the fluid attention of ants. The ants themselves are bead-bright lines of law, reconciling every sugar and protein to the queen’s quiet ledger. When they falter in the damp’s democracy, a fungus edits the colony’s behavior into a final sentence: one ant climbs under a leaf, clenches and flowers a stalk from her head—a tragic, local clarity that leaves the surrounding system no more and no less than abundant.

What passes for predation is conduct rather than event. A caiman mouths the current a little, thinking in water the way a cow thinks in cud; an anhinga learns where the mat gives enough and punts itself forward with a foot that knows each root by heart; a hunting snake tastes the same two inches of air three times and says yes to the pattern. What looks like mercy is only budget. You see none of the starving that makes a desert honest. You see only ritualized exchange, appetite portioned to leave interest on the principal.

Disease lives here too, but disease is not an invader—it is another vowel. Parasites ply the traffic lanes: flukes waiting in snail meat, larvae coiled in the tender underbark of frogs’ toes, nematodes threaded like hair through the tangle of peat. There are fungus blooms on moth wings that do not kill so much as color, and algae on turtles that do not drown so much as annotate. Rot is an organ employed, not a failure suffered.

Children occur like weather. There are egg clutches glazed to the underside of leaves at exactly the pitch of water they will drink when they crack. There are live-borns in mud corridors, pink as the mineral leak and just as inevitable. There are brood pouches in shrimp under leaf by leaf, tiny palms clasping. There are ballooning spiderlings releasing silk from the doorway of a bromeliad and sailing nowhere because wind is a story told elsewhere; they simply float in place until gravity tells them to wrap this buttress instead. There are tadpoles herded by a parent’s belly skin to better puddles and fed with her own body remade into milk. Around each young thing is an audience: fungi setting their appetites aside for an hour, ants patrolling a cleaned ring, beetles pausing their tapping in the log’s lungs.

Who lives here drinks and exhales heat the way desert things drink and exhale water. Bodies have been made into radiators and mufflers, fins and fans and lattices that wick. Gular pouches are manual bellows, and scales are engines of shine that throw sun from skin as necessary. Leaves flex with slight clicks you can feel on your forearm like coins tapped on glass, showing fresh sides to the air they are busily settling. Wings are as much for stirring as for flying; you can stand under a bat road and feel the cool the way you would feel a thought.

There are no buildings not because nothing constructs but because everything constructs in a tongue that declines corners. Nests are softened into their hosts and disappear until touch finds the change in temperature, the marginal silkiness where fungus has been convinced to be house. Paths exist, but they are bodies’ intentions written into peat, repeated until the land itself leans that way. The only straight line belongs to ants, and even they round their edges.

So: who lives here? The leaf that is also a well; the root that is also a net; the flower that is also a heater; the beetle that is also a knock; the fish that is also an ear; the frog that is also a fan; the snake that is also a tongue laid along a sentence of scent; the bat that is also a seam in continuous cloth; the bird that is also a weightless plumb. Lives here, too, the slowest thing you will ever know: accumulation. It is a citizen not of a species but of an agreement. It wears peat for skin and microbe for hair and moves upward an imperceptible width each unanxious day.

If you insist on a crown, on some resident to call sovereign, you will not find it with teeth. The ruler is sameness, but not the kind that deadens. It is sameness as craft, a finished pattern copied until the whole field gleams with it. What lives here are decisions repeated, bodies perfected to the point of quiet, mouths whose hunger hums instead of cries. And because the light stays gray and the air stays wet and the chorus neither swells nor heals, they do as they have learned to do: heat and cool one another; thread one another with sugars; push one another’s water uphill; stir the stillness until it counts as breeze; and in so doing, make a place that keeps becoming more itself.
