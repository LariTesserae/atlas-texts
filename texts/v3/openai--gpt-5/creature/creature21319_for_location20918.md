Begin with what you would miss if you were looking for eyes: the work of films, veils, and lawns. The first citizens are thin. They live where contrasts sharpen—at the lip of a brine fall, on the windward face of a stone, in the hairline seam where meltwater threads under salt. They are makers of texture in a place bent on smoothing itself. Their cities are microns tall, elastic and slick. A palm laid to one would know them as nap and slipperiness, as a faint resistance like breath caught against your skin.

They feed where chemistry gets interesting. Brine, sulfidic here; iron, soluble there; a cough of methane in winter that prints carbonate like frost on a cup’s rim. Their names, if we named them, would be long and Latin, and they would argue among themselves by swapping electrons along strands of conductive slime. When daylight comes thinly through a crack, a green wakes that has slept a year. It writes with photosystems for a week, maybe two, then goes back to sleeping by smell—oxidizing iron, nibbling sulfur, rearranging nitrogen, squeezing energy out of redox in the dark. If there is mind here, it begins with the sense of gradients: up (less salt), down (more), wind (current), lee (stillness), and the thousand little slopes of possibility between.

They build with exudate. To the touch, it is a mucilage that binds silt into fabrics. To the eye, it is a faint gloss, a film that takes a bruise of light and makes it last one more second. They sew pebbles to stones by accident. They farm, if you allow that word, by laying stickier mats where brine fingers rain, catching the continual fall of fine, dead world and working it into their own. They make the first sentences of food in a grammar that needs no sun.

Around and on them move the keepers—small bodies jointed like tools. Amphipods, carapaced and deliberate, pass over the lawns with the glooming focus of custodians. Their mouthparts are brooms. Their forelegs, what you might first call hands, are not for reaching in joy; they are for pinching and scraping, for persuading a bit of mat to let go. Theirs is a life of raking and tucking. They know the taste of everything. They walk with a steady, windless patience through a snow of silt that never lands the same way twice.

Isopods—ragwort-colored, slab-backed, the aesthetic of a tanker barge—hold behind stones in a clump like fallen leaves, the arrangement only they feel, each mouth turned to the same direction so that what arrives on the current arrives first to the most awake. When the water stiffens, they stiffen, their little rhythms slowing to the patience of ice. When it softens, they unthicken and move, changing stones as if changing addresses for the season.

There are sea spiders here too, not spiders, but joint after joint ending in a proboscis, adjustable and polite. You will not see them until a lacework of legs resolves from the mud and steps around the emptiness you are making. They visit sponges the way someone goes to a neighbor for sugar: formal, brief, intent. No hurry except the kind defined by chemistry—the prey’s particular slow, the predator’s only fractionally faster.

The nets that fish lay here are not woven of fiber but of sensing. Consider the fish that is less a color than an approach to transparency, the body glassed in antifreeze. Its blood carries little pigment; it does not need to carry much oxygen where cold loads water well. It is gravity, muscle, and a lateral line tuned finer than you thought nerves could be tuned. Its thought is turbulence. It lives on the knife-edges where currents slide and stall, hunts at sills where flow condenses into power, ambushes at the crush points. To see it is to feel a muscle pass you in the dark.

Jellies are the punctuation that marks out silence from silence. They hang their bells like faint punctuation and listen with threads for what the dark brings. There is nothing dramatic in their flash—no surface phosphorus, no green corona—only a readiness for happenstance. Their stinging is enough for the small; it does not aspire to bring down the large.

Siphons out of cracks—anemones, maybe, or small stalked things—stay closed most of the time; when a freshet of oxygen comes along the grounding line, they lift and open to it like tongues. Suckers on bivalves handshake their boulders and do not let go. When the tide breathes out and the pressure lessens by a whisper you would not think a creature could detect, they hinge their shell to a different angle and sleep in the bias.

Glass sponges stand in a long low country along the clean-water tongues, frilled and filigreed. They are receivers, and they remember. In dragon-bone lattices of silica, they write down the shape of currents, the taste of oxygen, the gossip of distant storms. A thick year in the above-world marks thickening there: spicule upon spicule stitched to bring more of the sweet through. If you want a record of time here, you read them as you would tree lines, not by color but by the wariness of their windows. Within their glass, microbes shelter, a repetition of the alliance that is life’s first trick: we catch and move; you eat and feed us back.

There are tunnels in the ice, thread-thin, that are brine and pipe together the underside like postal routes. In those columns live creatures whose lives are almost entirely about salt. They are drawn to more of it and to less of it both, different families, different sympathies. One makes its living at a salinity that would freeze your tongue to stone; another needs the relief of fresh to keep its proteins from folding in the wrong directions. They grow slowly and make little of themselves, but wherever a brine finger reaches, there they are, a halo around each cold descending thread.

At the methane seeps in the trench beyond the plain, life lifts a little, enters into the luxury of being many. There are white beards of bacteria strung like pearls along the faint upwelling, ridges of carbonate stacked like thin lips, a thicket of red worms advancing their bristles into the leak and making of a cough a climate. Snails with radulae like files rasp at the chimneys’ skirts. Small fish learn the roads that lead to these tables and add a new route to their maps of pressure.

Hunger has made an artisan here. Someone with chelae—call them hands for the way they go about it—works stones and shells against boulder and hinge. They choose a wedge of shell not only for thickness but also for the slight give that will keep it from popping free when the bivalve spasms. They brace their back legs against a groove left by a long-gone keel scrape, make of their body a lever, and will the world to pivot around them. When the hinge opens by any increment, they measure that opening with the same organ for pressure that tells them which tide this is and whether the sill ahead will be passable. They are not clever in the way we flatter; they are precise. You can watch them test the same wedge on three cracks. You can see them wait for the internal wave to change the lift on the shell by a fingernail’s weight. They are patience practicing physics.

No one school of bodies gathers here except where energy does. When the rare gift of a drowned thing comes down through a crevasse—a bird, a seal that lost the hole it meant to rise through—the plain remembers what abundance feels like. The amphipods discover the windfall first, because they are everywhere. Then the isopods, then worms from seams in the mud, then stars that you did not think this country had until they came on jointed feet from far sills, moving like machines that have considered every step before taking it. In two days the carcass is a perforated idea of itself. In two weeks it is mostly bone. In six months the bones hum with a new penumbra of life: filamentous worms in the marrow, bacteria that work fat into salts. Osedax, bone-eaters, do not need to be named here; they are a way hunger has learned to wait for the very rare by being ready for it always.

There are children here, but they are not tender. They are packages of spring, of rhythm. They hatch as if the calendar were in their blood. Some stumble immediately onto a road of pressure and are delivered to a crest they could never have reached under their own power. Some stay near, taking the neighborhood as a whole life, and set themselves down into the first good grit-filled crack and anchor their adulthood on the same stone their parent used. Being young is risk and drift in a world that dislikes both. Most that survive do so by becoming indistinguishable from their place before they have learned any other trick.

Bodies here are instruments more than containers. The setae along a limb, the hairs of an isopod’s mouth, the pores of a fish’s lateral line, the membranous bells of a jelly are tuned to different notes of the same song: pressure, speed, density, taste. They hear infrasonics as a steady thesis in the bones. They can tell in their cartilage whether the ice above them is thinking about cracking. They can feel with their feet the fricative vocabulary of silt—whether it is creeping because the internal tide has arrived, whether an incoming brine fall has cooled the mud a degree and stiffened it. Eyes are common only insofar as a light rumor sometimes matters. Many of them have turned perception to listening and touch, to the syntax that water writes around each object.

What would you call their social, their way of being with others? It is not congregation. It is not solo either. Imagine the long slack of a neap tide when a sill sleeps and a child thing could drift across; in that hour, five species share a lee as if it were a porch. No one touches unless touching is feeding, but they inflect themselves to one another. The amphipod rakes the mat more politely than usual. The isopod leaves a half-chewed tuft as if in a kind of coin. The jelly drifts higher so its shadow will not disturb the chelae-handed being doing its lever work below. Manners, if you like; arrangements reached over long repetition until they felt grown rather than chosen. They have no language but they are very good at reading what the water has already said.

Nothing persists in profile here. The ones that would be dramatic elsewhere are trimmed to low and round, to hugging and adhering, because the tides are knives for anything that stands and declares itself. You will not find architecture except in bone lattice and silica window, and even those are edited by woolly currents into forms that shelter flow instead of stopping it. If an individual makes a structure—as one of the handed ones does with a little wall of silt behind which to work a hinge—it is erased in a pulse, and the knowledge that made it resides not in a structure but in a habit of timing.

They keep time differently. Years are not rings alone; they are sets of practiced passages. When the moon is in a certain place, the sill near the trench will sing a little, felt in the legs. That is the week to try for the seeps. When the brine falls along the ceiling above the dull ridge are heavier in taste by just so much, you do not cross under them because the convective fan will make you tumble and wear your carapace thin. When a polynya opens somewhere out in the world and cold air scours the new skin of sea, the infrasonic vowel overhead deepens a half-step and the pressure roads all change their angles for a day. The old in this place know that in the way of knowing how to breathe.

Do not mistake quiet for lack of appetite. Predation is an agreement struck sparsely and with exactitude. A fish opens its mouth once in an hour and is correct about the hour. A sea spider chooses a sponge’s window so that withdrawing will cost the sponge more than opening. The wedge-bearer levers exactly when an internal wave lifts one hinge a whisper and lets the other bear weight. There are almost no chases. What speed there is belongs to the medium.

At the edge where the basin narrows into throats, there are athletes—small bodies, quick by local standards—that ride the jets the way surfers ride standing rivers. They slide along the undersides of internal waves, slip into slipstreams, change their angle a fingerwidth and find themselves a kilometer away when the tide changes. They do not leap; leaping is a surface habit. They flow. They are those for whom the map of this place is written directly onto their nervous system, for whom the body is not carried by currents but inserted into them as a vane is inserted into wind. If there is culture here beyond feeding and being fed, it lives in the way these riders teach without teaching—the way the young watch the old hold a certain tilt at chest-height while the knees go the opposite and learn to make a crisscrossing world a road.

And then there is the medium itself, the oldest resident, which is not alive in the way we use the word and yet is the grammar in which life here writes itself. The water is a companion mind that thinks in gradients and folds. It remembers, not with neurons but with layers: a fresher lens trapped under salt, a cold tongue under that, a slow swirl over the trench that is still finishing a turn it began last winter. It is the distributed touch among every inhabitant—pressed alike to sponge window and amphipod hair, passing equally through a fish’s thin gill and a brine filament. If there is anyone here, there is also this, always this, holding them to itself by pressure and being held to the planet by weight.

You will want to know what they fear. They do not, most of them, fear light. Light is a rumor, a holiday. They fear change that does not announce itself in a language they speak. A shelf’s underside fractures and the ceiling rises a meter: the roads of pressure rewrite in an hour what has been steady for a century. A freshwater flood pours under the lid late in a season because the above-world has its own strange year: the chemistries that made a living halt for a day for some and for a month for others. But even these are not disasters so much as altered biographies. This is an old sea. It has seen the lid higher and lower, warmer and colder. It remembers continents shaving past its door.

So who lives here? The thin ones that write their hunger directly into salt and iron. The low ones that anchor and sip. The patient rakers and levers. The listeners more than lookers. Riders of invisible waves and hangers in lee. Bone-keepers waiting a decade to bloom. Glass towers that collect oxygen as if collecting stories. Fish that feel your passing as a sentence in pressure and decide, or do not, to make it their meat. Creatures that are excellent at staying exactly where they are without being still, at reading what the water says before it says it, at surviving on rules any child could learn if a child could taste density with its teeth.

They are not many. They are very much themselves. They have perfected the art of being small in a country that flattens the tall. They live by agreements: with tide, with cold, with time. If you float there and listen for long enough, you will hear them only as edits in the hiss, tiny takings-out in the great continuity. And if you press your palm to a stone and feel the world’s hand press back through it, that pressure is also them—every sponge window, every bristle hair, every jelly thread sharing the same companion, writing themselves on it and letting it write them back.
