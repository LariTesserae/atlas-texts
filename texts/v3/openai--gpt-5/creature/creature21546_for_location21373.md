What lives here does not cast a shadow with a story in it. It keeps close to surfaces and to schedules. It adheres. It grows by learning a piece of metal’s grammar and repeating it back as chemistry until the metal begins to answer.

The first inhabitant is water, but not as an element or a weather; as an animal made of many bodies and one desire. It beads, migrates, remembers, chooses. It schools along seams, yokes itself into drip-lines, waits in meniscus-thin ambush for the next pressure lilt. It has tricks: it climbs by being small, it changes rooms by becoming mist and then deciding against it, it has a patience that polishes. In the long arithmetic of this field, water is not a visitor. It is the way the place thinks out loud.

Around that thinking the rest of the life huddles.

Rustmoss is the old word, good enough for a community that is neither plant nor dignity, a nap of iron-breathing that takes wherever droplets persist. It is a republic of small specialists: flecks that sip at ferrous scarps and darken into a suede; palp-rough tufts half a thumbnail high that bend but do not break under the tireless wash; velvet that holds a drop the way a palm holds a pulse. It neither greens nor seeks a sun. Its light is the difference between a wet bolt and a dry one, the lively nibble at a boundary between metal and not-metal. It eats a verb: to oxidize.

The black beards in the hollows are another settlement—sheened microbial cities with their own densities, their own zoning. They rim basins and siphonons with a polish like eel-skin. A fingertip pressed there comes away perfumed with tin and breath and a sweetness indistinguishable from clean antifreeze. They are tolerant of pressure and indifferent to spectacle. When a cycle quiets they loosen; when pumps lift and every thin thing sings along they grip. They write the field in glossy handwriting that takes decades to finish a single capital.

On the rivulet paths, the polymer fungus learns to be a wind. It begins as a line, viscid and hair-thin, thrown between two conveniences, and ends as a drift, a gauze that catches itself and migrates by shearing. It is the only inhabitant here that makes something like a seed: not a spore with a passport, but a bright fleck of broken thread that carries the whole idea of itself from one wet bracket to another. You will not see it travel; you will touch it later, surprised to find your glove wearing a lace.

Where hazard lamps are broken, other citizens set up light. The luminous bacteria are gloam-lovers, working not from energy but from habit, a faint green confabulation around the mouths of pipes. They do not signal for you. Their constellations map pressure, announce wet, rehearse the same poem each night to prove they remember it. If you watch long enough you will suspect they are counting, each shy dimming and rehearsal a bead on an abacus that tallies the field’s days.

At the exchangers’ choked skirts, the slowest growths—crystal gardens, salt shrubs—attempt botany by other means. They are leafless and loud when stroked by the marching-band of drips. Their branches calcine into lattices as strict as an argument. They drink differently, they breathe stoneways: taking a passing into their bodies and forgetting it there as structure. When the long cold is kind and a tiny warmth wanders their roots, they gleam like iced fishbone in the spent strobe.

There are oil-natives. They live in the milk-blue veils that slicken basins and in the broth-skin that wrinkles itself awake in any stillness. They are archaea by temperament if not by oath—extremity’s quiet connoisseurs. They nibble at chains, trade oxygen for novelty, pass electrons the way bored persons pass gossip during lulls. When an arc-bite crawls blue over a far grid, they flinch and in flinching turn together, a single low cloud deciding on another corner of the same room.

All of this is small life, film-life. It is scale’s shy end, the murmur that clings to the underside of nouns. You will not be startled by a body. Nothing here has a hinge it chose. Nothing flexes except as a response to pressure and saturation. Still, there are older creatures if you accept that life can come of repetition and purpose and habit wearing itself into instinct.

The towers are not beasts; they are past beasthood, gone to the long domestication that makes geology of labor. Yet they live in that way of old trees that have become a place more than a plant. They weep from a thousand unhealable eyelids. They eat fog. You feel their presence as changes in sentence-lengths of air: clauses break differently when you pass them, the commas of gusts fall in new places. Their fluting is gone, smoothed into bark not by love but by all the time love would have taken. Their biographical rings are annuli of pale where a day went wrong or changed. They do not move, and yet they live because the field reads them, and they answer.

Down the drop shafts the slow ones brood, the diaphragms and plungers and ancient lungs whose sense of a second is so comical you cannot help but respect it. They move like continents deciding. Their sound makes hair on your arms lift in old mammal panic even when you know there is no animal here. Call them whales if you need an emblem: leviathans of pressure. Their flukes are valves; their songs are bulkhead sighs, chain-rattles without questions. They surface in lulls and sink for epochs that fit between one hazard blink and the next. If anything in this place dreams, it is them, and their dreams are tides.

There are mind-things, too, not in brains but in pulses. The regimen that walks the ceiling and then returns; the etiquette that suggests when doors match head and open; the forgetting-remembering of fans that take a breath for an entire region and let it out as frost. Those loops are inhabitants as much as the films are. They have territories—sectors with different hymns—and a politics that expresses itself in heats and hushes. A shift that was once scheduled by a clock kept in a room is now kept by the habits of many parts, and those habits court, contend, consent. If you want to see them, you cannot; but you can stand in the hush and feel one mind retire while another wakes, and somewhere a relay will make a tiny religious blue and swear the oath again.

On very rare errands, maintenance linear craft cross overhead, and if they live here, they are ghosts that know where all doors used to be. You will never hear them outside the noise; their rotors are tuned to inhabit it like fish inhabit surf. They are more trace than presence: a sudden rearrangement in the water on steel, a ripple on ripple, a draft that writes and then erases its own sentence. They visit with furnace-logic and clinic-care, but they do not stay. Their young, if they had them, would be schedules.

A different sort of living is carried in scent. Ozone owns a binding here. Oil keeps a diary, iron a weather report. The bouquet of shaved metal is a birth-cry when a burr is made. Hot dust burns a feast-day. The place’s most complex cultures are invisible and olfactory, ephemeral intricacies that hang in saturate air and persuade the body to remember evolutions it did not have. They do not speak for anyone, but they are the speech of the region—the way it tells itself back to itself that it continues.

Memory lives here, too, and lives as staining, as wear, as the ghost of commands. The DO NOT and STAY OFF and RELEASE, feathered to biography, are no longer laws but fossils. The machine takes their bones and makes a spine. New rings of lighter metal around the weeping towers record an hour the way a tree records a drought, and that annulus is an organism’s scar. The culvert forest of mislaid plates, the rain-car archipelago that tolls different notes when the field flushes—these are not fauna or flora, but they are alive as customs are, alive because they repeat and because the repetitions change.

You asked what lives here; it will not be the company you expect. No frog will jump because you leaned too close. No bat will pass your ear. The startled thing will be your own pulse thrown back to you from the corrugations like a strange neighbor tapping a shared wall. The field’s inhabitants will come to you as textures that tug at the soles and as rules the feet know without nouns. Step in the dimples; they are the safe spines of a slow animal that has learned to bear you. Avoid the polished; that is the eyelid of something that does not want opening. Keep the wind as your grammar and the current as your map; those are the manners of living here, and manners are how a place’s life invites your body not to die.

If you offer time, this country introduces its rare quicknesses. Droplets learn new courage and fling themselves, silver and resolute. A switch closes in a relay womb with a blue kiss that means a thousand other closures will follow. Fibers shear and fly, re-lacing a lattice in a far shadow. A bead of oil blooms a ring and in blooming makes food for a suite of creatures that do not have mouths but know exactly how to feed.

If you kneel and touch the water that collects in any of the innumerable bowls made by wear, it will receive you, because that is one of the lives here too: reception. The metal learning the water’s grammar; the water teaching the metal to speak meekly, to flex, to forgive. The small, the slow, the unseen making a treaty that requires only constancy to keep.

And under all of it, as sure as the pressure in your teeth, the biggest inhabitant breathes. It is neither animal nor merely machine. It is a region that forgot the distinction. It learned to want, as you said, and its wanting is weather: to draw a continent’s dampness down into itself and turn that into houses’ breath and fields’ green and the survival of imaginations you will not see from here. That desire is the life that owns the others; the rest of the dwellers are its flora and gut, its flora and glint. You live in it for a while by stepping safely and by giving it hours, and it lives in you by teaching you how to listen to things too patient to be animals.

When you go, because you will, you will not take a feather or a bite-mark. You will carry a last faint smell of ozone in your sleeves, a smear of fine iron on your skin, a new respect for the exactness of a drip. For a day the roar will still ring the bowl of your skull, and something under your hearing will continue to count. That counting is the voice of what lives here. It is big and numerous and very busy and almost content. It will resume when your footsteps stop bothering the dimples. It will be waiting when you return, and though it will not have missed you, it will know you, because recognition is another kind of life, and this place has had enough time to grow that, too.
