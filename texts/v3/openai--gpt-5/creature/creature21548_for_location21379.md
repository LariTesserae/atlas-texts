The ones who live here arrive with their lungs ready to learn a single sentence and repeat it forever. They do not so much inhabit as attend. They take their sleep and tea and arguments in the aisles of a long machine and pretend a town into the interstices, or they take a town and pretend a machine around it so water will keep happening. Either way, they live by the rules the place has written for them in condensate on every flat.

They’re small at first, the ones who will become the place’s proper citizens. They learn the sump by foot: toes curled over the edges of catwalk grating, soles memorizing which runnels are slickest and which drip lines salt-crust just enough to bite. They count by valves and by shift horns. They know that the “day” is the length between horn-blast and horn-blast and that the light is never going to get better than this. Their nursery rhymes are work orders sung back and forth: keep the flow, keep the flow, clean to the line and no further; a jump-rope chant that matches the thrum of the fans. They play games of balance on the low shoulders of the Pipe Forest, hands on painted steel that is always a soft-bleached warm; they trade pressed leaves from the permitted saltbush hedge like money, the leaves brittle and tasting like brine. They fall asleep to a white hiss and wake to a white hiss and in between someone carries them from hammock hook to hammock hook while the condensers rain.

The elders are tuned like instruments. The oldest ears can hear a half-semitone shift in the south-line pump from across the Pan Yard and, without looking up from their tea, lift a finger toward the right wheel. Bodies take the shape that work demands: forearms laddered by old rake handles, thighs polished by the friction of oilskins, a band of paler skin where the respirator straps always lie. Their hair never dries all the way; salt frosts the wisps at the nape when the fans grit down in the cool hour. They learn to lift their feet as they walk so the wet doesn’t claim their heels with rot; they rub lanolin into the cracks and pray in the shrine for new boots before the last season gives way. Their skin is a map of what metal does to human flesh when the metal sweats: every smear of iron leaves a ghost; copper makes a green coin on the wrist where the bracelet rests during lunch.

They come in shifts, many of them. Day shift and night shift are lies they tell one another so that someone else will take the next block of sameness. There are Dripkeepers, who tend the heat-exchanger stacks and make the rain fall in the Drip Cloister. They move like monks in a garden, eyes for the way algae climbs fan blades, fingers quick to lift a clumping frond and scrape it to the floor sieves. There are Comb Women and Comb Men who take the broad-toothed rakes to the catchment pans every morning you can name and draw their delicate channels so mosquitoes have nowhere to think. Their combs are carved with notches from all the mornings before. There are Panhands in the Yard, who read the glass-skin when it sloughs and slide the edge of a squeegee under and peel it back on itself like a lizard shedding; they can tell at a glance whether the cake stands are ready to take another layer in the Salt Court. There are Linewalkers who go from pipe to pipe with touch and ear, finding the little bruises the place takes—the start of a leak on a union, a clamp that walks toward its threads. There are Painters, all brightness and patience, who keep rust in its season and no further, walking the perimeter with their wands and speaking softly to the rails as if addressing an old man’s joints.

There is a woman who sells tea from behind a counter always beaded with its own climate. Her cups keep the warmth of the diesel plant like children keep vowels; when you put your mouth to their edge you taste iron and tannin and the stray sugar the kids found in a bag once and have been eking out since. There is a cobbler who presses the soles back onto boots that the sump eats one layer at a time; his resin tin never hardens because the air will not let it and he takes it as a blessing. There is a nurse with damp hair who wears stainless at her throat and knows the salt-sting rashes by sight and which blistered heel needs a rest day and which needs a thinner sock. She catches babies with hands that smell like antiseptic and brine, and she wraps the dead in canvas and keeps them in the cool shadow of the louvered walls until the families gather. The families gather under fans. Everything gathers under fans.

They name their children for the place. Not by law, just by logic. There is Louver, little and serious, who can already tell a midge cloud from a fruit fly halo. There is Rill, who swings her comb across the wet with too much joy and has to be called back to straighten the lines. There is Semitone, because he was born the afternoon the south line found its tune. There is Baffle, who sleeps only when someone is moving air near his face. There is Pearl, not because of the stones but because of the way the rails will sometimes string themselves with big drops. There is a boy named Valve who will one day grow into his name and catch, in an off moment, the slap that would have knocked another boy into the Pan and under.

They eat what the place gives them and what they can trade out. That means fish, mostly, the quick silver of mullet and the stripe of killifish seined from the channels when the light edges up and otherwise inattentive. It means crabs from the damp margin with a watchful eye for their large opinions. It means greens coaxed where a pipe leaks, a handful of leaves snipped from a sedge bed the engineers tolerate because it is small and because it hides the gecko clutching the wall just there. It means rice that arrives by truck and salt that arrives by conveyor and sugar that arrives with the trader who likes to stand under the biggest fin and pretend he knows rain. It means tea stronger than needed, to cut the heat that isn’t heat but the slow hand of a bath no one drained.

The animals permitted by the rules do not read the rules but keep them well. Dragonflies live here, not as jewels on reeds but as maintenance staff, passing inspection over the same rectangles a person just combed, faster and with no clipboard. They know where to stand—invisibly, on air—and how to tremble their wings to stay. Swallows have written their home address into the rafters and the thin dark places where beam meets beam; they scribble down into the Pan Yard and back up again, hemming the air with tiny stitches, and their threads are hunger and the way the warm pipes will always lift midges. Fiddler crabs quarrel along the margins as if argument were function and not entertainment. You can tell the hour by the quality of their clatter if you live here long enough.

The herons came on their own and brought patience with them. They stood, necks telescoped into themselves, when the first evaporator trays slid into place, and they stand still now in the big basins and measure the calculus of how far a fish will dart on a ninety-degree turn. Egrets flare bright and then fold because nothing here burns but everything shines. Nightjars own the clock’s night and the light’s day at once; their throats hold a different white noise and they drape it over the machine’s tone at the edges of everyone’s sleep.

Geckos are new and constant. Each wall that sheds water is a road for them; each bead a hope. They flick, copper eyes like coins in a palm, licking the mineral film from their own toes. Under the biggest fins the frogs breed always—bottles clinking, a banquet none of them will finish. They live because the water lives; they live because nothing is allowed to be still except the living things themselves. Rats attempt the edges and find cats waiting, lean and salt-quiet, whose coats never fluff because the air will not permit fluff. The cats learn the catwalks like the children and never look down.

Smaller lives make the color shifts and leave their prints only when light hits slant. The algal film that maps every angle of this place is not one color but several, each one a different appetite at a different salinity. The green-sheened thinness that makes the rake marks look like writing. The iridescent smear that sets in where the condensate gathers and the fans don’t reach. The powder-pink stutter on a pan edge where the heat lasts longest. If you kneel down and put your nose close you can smell each one’s hunger. If you live here you do not kneel down unless you have a reason and a rope under your waist.

Machines live here the way trees live in other towns: as structures that grow by being the same. The diesel plant hums behind louvers and takes the ripe air as food. Pumps do not stop; they wear fingerprints into the hands of the people who have learned the trick of coaxing them through their tired hours. The conveyors drum a heartbeat and the valves clap their hands to set the rhythm. The heat exchangers drink the sky’s wet and sweat it out again; the sumps pull everything down just enough that you can feel it in the tendons in your feet. The town tends its machines and the machines, in return, keep the air breathable and the cups full. If there is a god here, it is work, but the people make smaller gods they can love: a saint of shade you can hang a scrap for at the clinic door; a grandmother of rakes whose little altar holds a comb tooth and a brass penny; a patron of boots with one bright blue lacet coiled around a nail. Someone always stops to thrum the fans’ safety grilles with their fingers as they pass, not a prayer exactly, but a conversation in the language everyone hears.

Visitors come rarely and do not stay long. They stand on the causeway and squint at the ceiling of vapor as if trying to see the world through a sickness. They taste their own shirts afterward and wonder why they taste like coins. If they stay they learn to speak with their hands because the hum eats the ends of sentences. A flat palm, angled, means make it shed. Two fingers tapping a rail mean is that yours humming wrong or mine. A roll of the wrist means swap the filter and try again. Even the softest talkers here are good at the scalar low: words nested low enough to carry under the hiss, vowels spread enough to stand up. Children learn this first—how to be heard inside a vowel.

The place itself makes people into a kind too. It changes their idea of time from sun to cycle. It replaces afternoons with maintenance windows and pairs their holidays with flush-days and restarts. It teaches them to move the shade with them wherever they go, to prize a swath of shadow as if it were meat, to applaud a breeze the way other towns applaud a good rain. It takes their tempers and salts them down. You can have a fight in this town, but by the time you have found a corner dry enough to slam something into, you will have thought of the Paint Crew and their tired wands and then there’s nothing for your anger to cling to. You tell the person you are mad at to meet you under the seventh fin and talk instead. You call it even and trade half a sugar packet and a salt cake edge and promise to keep the flow.

If there is loneliness, it’s a quiet kind. The horizon is another basin never started. The levee is the only curve big enough to read as sky. Lovers court by walking the high line side by side, one stepping on the gabion wire, one stepping off, hands not quite touching until the windless air picks a direction and gives them the excuse. Babies are named for sounds and surfaces. Funerals move along the catwalk in a single file with a flag that will not lift. The dead go out under the same humming, and someone counts valves while they go because a valve chose that moment to complain and the habit is the habit and the place survives because the habit is more important than the story.

What else lives here are the rules. They’re not painted on signs much because paint has better work, but they are present in the way every surface is arranged to drain and every courtyard is a pan and every sill is a lip. Keep the flow. Soften light, harden surfaces. Trade heat for water and water for order. Invite animals that eat other animals and design for their routes. Give shade first; comfort follows. Clean to the line and no further. Let the rest grow where it’s allowed. The rules are alive in the way a rule is when a culture breathes it in and breathes it out, not as commandments but as muscle memory. If you’ve ever watched a person unthinkingly set their mug on a slope so the condensation doesn’t pool—well, you’ve seen one of the rules living its small life in a hand.

Because the place obeys itself, because its mood is steady as a held note, plenty of small dramas have room to play at the edge. A cat carries a gecko and is talked down by an old man with a pocket full of fish skin and an understanding of necessary predation and mercy. A girl sticks her arm too far into a mesh to show a friend the pink of the algae and does not pull away fast enough; the nurse teaches the friend the pressure trick so the blood learns its new path and the two are bound together by the telling forever. A boy knocks a valve closed and catches the slap and leans into it and the whole south line sighs happily and the Pan Yard lightens by some fraction you can taste on your teeth. A heron misses a fish and makes eye contact with a Panhand across a stretch of bright. They both fail at what they are trying to do and both try again, because the note holds and the water moves and the air moves only when made to.

And the place grows, which is another way to say it keeps making itself new edges to keep still. There are always fresh gabions waiting, cages of wire filled with stone bright enough to cut fingers. There is a backhoe idling beyond the levee, its diesel a cousin to the plant’s low song. Someone is always unfolding the map of the next basin and tapping it where the channels will run. Someone else is tracing with a finger where the reed will be permitted, how much saltbush will hold the wind, which sedges will survive the drip. The cats will walk that way and the swallows will rewrite their scrawl onto those rafters and the dragonflies will expand their jurisdiction and the work will change only in the way that a river changes when you give it another bank. The river is the same because it is always leaving.

What lives here are people who have decided, or been assigned, to live inside a machine without pretending it is not a machine. They make it kind by the degree to which they can, which is not much and is enough. They never get silence and have made of that lack a comfort they can carry into sleep. They do not get borders made by hills or trees; their borders are tilt and the feel of clay under a boot. They are not rich with variety; they are rich with detail. They hold their babies up to the drizzle under the biggest fins on hot days and call it blessing. They teach their kids to cup their hands under the condensate streams and drink from their own roof and call it luck.

They live here: the frogs under the rain that is not rain. The swallows in their stitched sky. The herons balancing patience like a tray. The cats writing figures on the catwalk. The geckos eating the walls clean. The rats on the boundary thinking better. The pumps at the low note that never shifts more than half a semitone if anyone can help it. The diesel plant that hums a lullaby because it does not know any others. The algae counting days in shades we do not have names for. The children who know the rules like a song and grow taller inside the white hiss. The elders reading the sump with their feet. The town itself, pretending to be a machine or the machine pretending to be a town, holding its breath and refusing change, a gentle animal made of pipes and shade and promise that the flow will be kept.
