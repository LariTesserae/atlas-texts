Those who live here learn to arrive by ear. Infants are marked not with ink but with a chord, the household’s interval hummed against their soft skull so bone will remember it. By the time they can stand, they can tell the high-seven moan that means the western notch is waking from the low-two tremor that says the rope to the granary will need relashing by noon. They are people who greet with a rattle, who keep door-hangs that answer when the cliff sings so the home can announce itself back. They do not call out across open space; they let the chimes carry their names in pieces and collect each other by overlapping them.

They smell of oil and warmed resin and the ghost of fires that do not char. Skin is rubbed daily with seed-pod fat and powdered amber to keep it from cracking. Hair is kept long enough to braid and heavy with resin beads, each bead a small bell that tells you how a head turns. Their clothing listens too: woven from pale fog-fibers and tough leaf-strips, it clicks quietly when they move, a polite percussion that gliders mind and mites dislike. Their gloves are leafglass—thin as bark and flexible like remembered sap—worn until they take the shape of the work that is done in them.

They are ropewalkers before they are scribes, because every errand is a stride between ledges. The first rite is length: a child, roped with three lines, crossing the small courtyard gap while their household hums the crossing note and the youngest glider perches near to reassure them with clicks. The second rite is listening: a dusk spent alone under the market promontory, back to stone, until they can draw the simplest map of the day with their finger on their palm—the wind’s three rises, the neighbor’s new chimes, the stutter that means a hinge has split somewhere in earshot.

Households are braided rather than stacked. People marry across ledges not to move belongings but to combine rights: a strip of garden-shadow here, a run of rope there, a window that looks into a sound-lobed alley where the seventh harmonic is full and kind. Property is responsibility. If you inherit a section of scaffold, you inherit its tending and its share of shade; if you adopt a length of rope, you adopt the knots that have held it, and the duty to teach those knots to the next hands that will hold it. Men and women work side by side, but the ones who decide where the rare wet will be poured are always the driest-handed elders, knuckles lacquered in a lifetime of oil, voices polished down to a rasp that the wind respects.

They keep three names, because wind erases one. There is the hum-name, a chord or a click-pattern that any doorway chime can answer when asked; there is the hand-name, shaped in finger and wrist where no gust can steal it; and there is the shadow-name, kept for the closeness of kitchens and beds, spoken like a sealant over troubles. Strangers get only the hum of the house and the trade-hand; lovers and children get the shadow that glues them into a family. Outsiders, when they come along the top and lean down in astonishment at this scab of life, go away imitating the chimes and never know what the oiled walls say.

There are occupations as narrow as ledges and as old as the last good overhang. Rope-makers spend their days at long looms where resin thread is drawn and doubled and twisted until it is supple as skin. You can tell them by the pads on their wrists and the grooves in their forearms where the thread has taught them its passage. Resinwrights are the chemists and priests of glue. They tend braziers at the hush-lull, watching oils take on exact degrees of blue flame, reading the thickness of smoke the way a smith might read a heat-shadow. They are the ones who temper plant-glass into something a hinge can trust and coax brittle green to grow into a curve that will bear a household.

Gardeners rule by patience. They do not water. They brush, they coax, they cull, they wait. They keep cords with knots to find the right angle of leaf-plates. They carry brushes with bristles cut from stiff hedges, soft for pollen, stiff for mites, stiffer still for brushing glues that have skinned over. They sing—not to plants that do not hear in our register, but to each other, a slow uninflected tone that keeps hands moving at the same speed hour after hour. Their gloves are thicker than anyone’s; the leafglass curls at the fingertips with the heat of their bodies and the friction of their work. The good ones have never cracked a frond in anger. They know which succulents, once they have glassed themselves to the wall and begun to tunnel, can be taught to leave a little more room, a little more doorway, a shelf where you can keep a pan.

Glider-herds are misnamed. No one truly herds a thing made to ride wind. But there are families that the gliders consider part of their air, and those households hang out bowls of ground seed and oil and let the birds—if birds they can be called—come and clatter and fold themselves under the eaves when dust-storms rise. Children learn the gliders’ castanet language before they learn their letters: snap for go-away, quiet click for come-closer, the rolling rattle that means a thief is looking you in the eye. For a fee, a trained glider with a message weighted in its lamina can be coaxed to take a shortcut across an impossible space and tap it twice against the window-lintel you intended, but they also have their own ideas, and sometimes they deliver a dead moth instead because it is shinier.

Whistler-keepers are as close as anyone here comes to shepherds. The whistlers are pale and tube-bodied, gentle and alarming by turns, with a dozen ringed mouths under their snouts that can be pressed into any small crack to drink a note out of the cliff. The keepers are small people with leathered shoulders and scars on their shins where the creatures’ mouths have found them when frightened. They keep carved boards of stone with holes drilled in them, learning which hole gives which sound with which wind; they carry cords of dried seed-pod that, struck, calm the snakes when a sudden harsh harmonic sets them wriggling. No one slaughters a whistler. When one dies, they are placed in a deep crack and sealed there with resin and ash, and the crack is left to sing through them. Some alleys are said to hold the tone of a family this way, a long ghostly line of sound that is more like a conscience than a haunting.

Mite-sweepers go bare-armed and carry brushes like cudgels. The chalk-blind mites eat anything left still long enough to feel like bone, which is to say almost everything. The sweepers patrol the ropeways and under-rails, whacking at clusters of whiteness before they can chew a fibre from round to blade. They are paid in dry coin, which is to say in beads of temper and jars of old oil, because everyone knows what a single missed patch can cost. They do not take apprentices; they take conspirators. They have the unpleasant task, when a neighbor dies alone, of going in first and brushing the mites back from the oiled body so it can be mourned without scouring.

Between the people and the plants and the animals there is a thickness of habit that acts like a second hide. Grazers no bigger than a knee curl under the scaffold shadows, nibbling the edges of tough leaves and passing dust pellets harder than seeds, which the gardeners collect and pry open and use to pin the beginnings of the next hedge. The moths that bump your cheeks at dusk are like understudies for the wind: sugar-white and big as saucers, thudding into elbows and chipped cups, bold as neighbors. Children catch them by closing both hands over their applause and release them into sheltered rooms when the fog rises, urging pollination as if they were pushing paint.

There are things here that would be predators elsewhere and are merely disciplines. A web that tastes of vinegar appears in a corner? Everyone knows it eats copper as readily as leaf, and so you move your precious strip of blade-edge and brush the corner down with a wetness of oil. Filaments come, mushrooms of cloth-lint and resin-dust that throw acids thin as breath; they are discouraged by sound and dislike the eighth harmonic. The wall-walkers beat their chimes against them at certain hours, and that is as effective as teeth. Death has no spectacle here. When someone falls, which happens rarely, the ledge learns that person’s weight in absence and everyone carries it differently. When someone goes at home, the household unhooks the chimes from the lintel and paints them with ash and resin and hangs them in the market once, so everyone can carry them with a look and then forget to hear them again.

Their law feels older than it is because it has been polished down to necessity. Wetness is the greatest crime not because it is impious, but because it is immediately expensive. An outsider once laughed at a story of a man made to pour his water into another family’s vault and found herself fined in rope for the insult. “Water unmakes the home,” a dry-mother told her, and had her run her finger along a shelf where salt crystals had once sprung out of air and pried stone apart like teeth. There are other laws just as simple. Do not leave a charge of resin unattended near a rope. Do not hang a chime where it will cancel another household’s notes. Do not touch a growing beam unless you have tended it long enough to know where it bites. Do not sleep on the roofs of the gardens, no matter how sweet the pollen dust, because there are edges under there that even childhood can fall through.

The market is two rushes of closeness at evening when the air fattens and the gliders circle more lazily and everything oil-scented rises out of its vessels. People do not shout prices; they tell you what a rope can remember—how many winds it has held through, how many rescues it has been in. A copper strip used as a knife edge moves slowly down a table like an heir. It passes from a family that has decided to do without slicing to a family that has been invited to move their hearth and wants to take with them something that cuts. You can buy a set of chimes tuned to the seventh and eighth, yes, but you can also sit and hear the maker brush the resin with a wetted thumb (oil-wet, not water-wet; purity lies in terms) and show you how the note can be coaxed a finger’s breadth, if your doorway will have it.

Stories do not leave the wind alone. The eldest tell the child that the cliff is not a cliff but a throat, and once it sang to a sky so wet that nothing ever hardened, and the people were slugs who did not have bones to teach them names. Then the water went down into the stone and the stone stopped changing and learned to talk back; it got a voice, and where there is a voice, there must be ears. So the people grew ears. Other tellings say the wall is a drumhead stretched across the memory of a mountain and the people are nothing but calluses on a good drummer’s fingers. In either case the prayer is the same, if you can call maintenance a prayer: pick, check, sweep, rub, retie.

Few go onto the top. The heat there hums under your feet and the flatness makes the body feel like a dropped stitch. Those who do are called Flat-Feet when they return, and they are allowed to sit in the back of the market until the wall has tuned them back. Fewer still go down. There are stories of baskets riding buoyant pockets of wind a long way, getting caught in the featherfall below where gliders shed and fog-nets that have slipped their moorings make a white country. People of this place do not envy the idea. The ledge is enough country for a life, with its alleys between lintels and the rise and fall of its own time.

Here, the dead go into objects that will be touched: into the lip of a bowl that a granddaughter will wrap her palm around, into the rocker of a leafglass cradle that will sing when nudged. Ash is not scattered on the wind, because the wind is already full. Instead ash is mixed with resin and pushed into simple carvings on the thresholds of rooms, a difference in sheen that the fingertips of the living will find when they go in and out. The belief is not elaborate. If you have oiled and brushed and checked all your life, you have made a shape that can go on taking care when you stop exhaling.

Plants live here as neighbors rather than background. They are busy bodies. They turn their plates and present their lacquer edges at differing angles, play the dim light like gamblers play their opponent’s gaze. The big ones, with mineral hearts and stacks of paddles, sip wind and balance themselves like spokes. They grow their own stairs and hallways and forget, sometimes, that they did not mean them for us. Left alone, they will knit a lattice as dense as retention and leave small rooms like dropped thoughts. Those are the rooms the first settlers found and forced no door into, setting a mat across an opening the plant had left and asking nothing but to stand inside an intention other than their own. Over years, the plants learned that people would brush and praise instead of tearing. If asked on an evening with a damp name, the old gardeners will tell you that the green here is proud. It wears varnish like a badge; it tolls like wood when you thump it. It sulks if overhandled. It loves a song without melody.

And there are lives nobody quite claims. The dust itself is alive with slow trades. Old sunlight taken into resinous pods becomes oil which becomes heat which becomes cured glue which becomes a hinge on which a market opens and closes. Sound goes everywhere and stays; it salts the bone; it is taken into breath and exhaled with a different shape, like an animal being encouraged from one ledge to another. The city is a creature composed of rooms and ropes and rituals that digest work and excrete safety. It tightens in scarcity and remembers after, like an organ learners have learned to contract. If you stand at the promontory on a wet evening and close your eyes and spread your fingers to feel the light dust fall, you will not be alone even if no one touches you. You will be kept by the hum.

Nothing very strange lives here until you leave, but say anyway that the particulars are the strangeness. That a person can listen to a wall and know if their neighbor’s cradle has cracked. That a child can talk a glider down or up with clicks. That a worm can be a flute and a roof can grow itself and a city can make a home mostly of what it has preserved from unmaking. Who lives here? People who do not trust water but trust each other to check the ropes. Plants that do not love the wet but drink the wind and fix rooms into being. Animals that fly by folding into sound and eat the edges of hardness into pellets that can begin a hedge. Mites, moths, webs, acids—a thousand tiny appetites that are held in the palm by maintenance. Stone, which thinks in pitch and hum, and air, which thinks in pressure and lets itself be measured by chimes. All of it held together by a habit of care that is older than any law: to resist and to embrace, to take what passes by and make it stay, to make a life extravagant at the edge.
