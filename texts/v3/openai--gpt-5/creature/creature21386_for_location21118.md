They are a people who have learned to be loud with their bones and quiet with their mouths. The constant shudder from above has tuned their skeletons; they carry the rain’s law in their ribs the way land-breathers carry air in lungs. When you meet them, you feel the hum through a wristshake before you catch the outline of a face: a calm oval, hair roped into cords that stay put, eyes with second lids that slide like patience across the cornea. They move by agreeing with the water. Every motion is a negotiation that never becomes a fight.

Their bodies have settled into this medium the way the plants have. Fingers and toes are webbed to the first knuckle—not flippers, never helpless, but a set of generous membranes that make a palm into a fin with a thought. Shoulders are broad in the way of swimmers; ribs are sprung like the frames of boats. The skin goes a little darker than the water in shade and a little paler than it in sun, a quiet camouflage of olive and blued gray. Many wear a bloom not of paint but of life: faint films of symbiotic algae in latticework patterns on shoulder and back where the skin is happiest with light. It is not a requirement. Some households encourage it, paint their children in leaf-shadow until the algae take; other households polish their skin smooth and wear light-catching beadwork instead. Difference is not doctrine. It is flavor.

They breathe in a way that is kin to everything else here: by partnership. The reed masks are not masks in the land sense; they are cages of woven stem lined with bright green, a velvet of microbiology they cultivate the way you might keep a sourdough or a good vinegar. They call them gardens-of-breath. They cradle them to the face; as water passes, the living lining trades out what a body no longer wants for what it must have. When a mask is young, exhalations pearl fast and silver, little chains of bubbles like bracelets loosed; when it is mature and balanced, the pearls slow to almost nothing. The best singers among them can hum into a mask and feel it answer, a faint coolness under the cheekbones, a tone that means the garden is happy. Children learn this before they learn to prune.

Some bodies can go without a garden for long minutes. The elders tell stories of the Good Hold—breath held so softly that it does not protest, the way a kelp holds tension in a long rope of itself. They practice in games that are not called games: the drift-still, the slow-knot, the pass-through with a single exhale. No one makes endurance a virtue. It is a courtesy, like trimming a neighbor’s vine where it crosses your window and leaving the cut fresh and neat.

If you could take all of them at once and draw them on a wall, you would see a wide family of shapes. Some are long and wire-tough from the outer circuits where the currents go leaner; some are thick in the middle, insulated with a slow layer of stored oils from a winter of dark rains. Hair tends to be kept, not cut: long to be tied to bladders, braided with seedspines for ballast, woven through with snail-shell spacers that click when a head nods—music as ID. Teeth are even, not sharp; they are good at stripping soft plant, good at cracking a young shell with a stone’s help. There is a scar language for those who keep the eels—the watch-chains do not bite deep, but they are abrupt—and a parallel language of deliberately trained callus, a knuckle that says rope-worker, a heel that says baffle-tender, the pale crescent on the palm that says someone once took a blade to a root and did not fear the rebound.

They do not name themselves as a block any more than the forest names itself. Outsiders might call them the Tenders. Among themselves it is always more specific: the House that Holds the East Span, the Splicers of Third Hall, the Rain-Readers, the See-to-the-Comb, the ones who carry the law of pruning for the west moorings. Names string and unstring like beads on a cord of work. A child’s personal name sits between a rhythm and a plant: Pore-Singer, Glassing-Lark, Fifth-Rung, Soft-Kelp. When you meet someone formally, you press brow to brow and let your jawbone vibrate a little: that is the moment where private voice becomes shared pressure, and you each learn how the other hears.

They speak, but not for reach. A hand is faster here, a shoulder more precise. Their language is a braid of tools. One strand is signing with fingers and whole body, a grammar that knows how a wrist floating up is different from a wrist held up, that maps urgency into the tautness of forearm and the angle of a palm to water. Another strand is drumming: heel of hand to trumpet-pore, two sticks on a leaf tympanum, the knuckled roll on a stretched bladder that says come, says wait, says danger going east. A third strand is the patient songs strung across infrastructure: the flutes facing the currents, the shell-chimes stitched along the floor so sleepers wake by thigh and spine, not by ear. Words exist, old and good. To use them, you come close, mouth to mask, mouth to cheek, speak into the bone and the cartilage. Lovers murmur against each other’s ribcages. Chiefs make law with a palm on a hall’s grown beam and their mouth pressed to it, so the timber may carry the sense on.

They govern themselves the way they build: by persuading living things to hold certain shapes. Property is not a fence but an agreement about where you may prune. The family’s inheritance is a topology of permission. Conflicts are pruned. You will see it: two heads close together in midwater, hands gentle and serious, and one person’s forefinger makes a snip two inches over an imaginary line, and the other nods, and a third—an old auntie with hair like a lifted net—touches both wrists and then a nearby tendril, and later, when the plant is ready, the snip will be made. There are written records, but they are not on paper. The law is rows of vines with the story of a boundary trained into them. Each notch is a season, each diverging fork a clause. If a clause must be argued, there are knives, yes, but you will not see them in a fist. They gleam in a circle on a table of living root while the right part of a plant is indicated, stroked, discussed. Losing an argument sometimes means losing the right to make a pruning in a favorite place. It hurts. It heals. It is visited and touched—this is where we decided to be neighbors.

They remember their dead by planting them into the text. When a body ends, it is returned with ceremony to an anchor—not to feed it like meat, but to rest in a place where root and bone can learn each other’s shapes. In a year, a white arc will show in a fan’s core, and people will stroke it in passing. In ten years, a new spine of green will have learned to take a curve it once did not have, and the household will gather there on the day the fan throws its flamboyant leaf and sign a name as if to a relative. Sometimes a hall’s curve is everyone’s dead at once, a chorus of agreements over a century.

Children grow into the city the way kelp grows into light. They start in the hairy margins where mites cloud like dust and everything is quick and small. They learn to explode like the shrimps when brushed by a fin, not in panic but in joy. Their games are currents, angle of fingers, who can ride a rope-lift without touching it more than twice with a toe. They hunt a particular snail that loves the shaded north sides of trunks, not for meat but for the way the beaks make the best needles. There is a year when they all wear the same blue shell bangle because it is what this ridge’s south slope is giving up. In that year’s stories, heroes have blue on their wrists without explanation. Apprenticeship is a long, soft thing, like a tide coming in. A child sits with a fermenter and learns how warmth tastes in a jar; a child sits with a mooring and feels with a palm what slack means; a child watches an auntie talk a fan-fern into a new habit with nothing but pegs, patience, and a lullaby sung to its tissues with a drum.

They eat as the place instructs. The word for hunger is the same as the word for cold, and the cure for both is oil. They press it from fat pods. They coax it from the flank of a grazer in a way the grazer does not mind, cutting no deeper than the rind of fat will forgive and letting it seal again. There is meat, but not often. When a big one dies natural in the blue—one of the wide-fluked elders who roll along the ridge lanes singing—there is a quiet call sent down the tuned halls and the city comes, light and solemn. They take enough. They leave the rest to the browsers and the hosts of small deliberate mouths that are the ocean’s own economy. Most days it is plant: sheets of kelp leaf fermented under bladder for warmth, threads of a sweet water-fern steamed chemically in pockets that feel like someone else’s hand, pulses threshed on reed weirs and packed into skins to become something spreadable. A household’s wealth smells faintly of tannin and sun-baked sap. There is salt in everything and no ash at all.

They keep animals the way they keep themselves: by making room, not cages. Grazers pass and are shepherded by absence, by offering clear alleys of better food and leaving other lanes thick with unpalatable spine. The ribboned not-quite-fish are allowed to knot in schools in certain bays where the current suits them; children float there with faces close to patterns their grandparents also watched. Eels live in the oldest buttresses, and there is always a woman with a scar under her wrist who knows them like jewelry, who can reach into a coil and come out with a creature wound her elbow and a laugh about how this family always bites on the second nudge. Predators are given their corridors. That too is law. If someone is taken—and it happens, though it is rare and does not become legend—they are named in the same ceremonies as those who die in sleep. Loss is a current like any other; to rage against it forever is to tear your own hall.

If there is religion here, it is in three beliefs so ordinary they are not named. First: that sound is a thing you tend. Not just listen to, but keep. There are households whose charge is only to the flutes that stand against the current. They polish the mouths. They mark the day a pitch changed and why. They teach children a scale that means wind thickening above-door, a scale that means loosen the west ties, a single note that means check on your neighbor. Second: that time is a garden. You do not own it. You take cuttings. You do not ask a hall to become what it cannot; you do not make a plant carry your will across the line where it cores back into itself. Third: that height is not dryness. It is another way to be slow. The old stories say there was wind once like this water and snow like blank fish, and that people then had fire they named and stroked and kept in bowls. Children are told these as bedtime, with wonder and the good ache of distance, not hunger. Sometimes an elder taps a wrist scar and says, I saw a lightning come down into the water and make a sheet like the sun walking, and for an instant it was like the fire in the story. No one expects it to be theirs.

The polite vices are also slow. A fondness for certain fats that glaze the tongue and make a brain feel warm from inside. A superstition about never tightening a rope until it sings exactly the note your grandmother liked. A habit among the west-slope folk of throwing an extra half-peg into any joint that will bear it; their halls are famous for lasting and for creaking like humor. There are not many things to hoard besides permission and oil. What people count privately are favors—who taught you to listen to this current, who lent land to root your experiment in, who sent you a sleeper-chime carved with a joke only you would hear.

Some go out. There is a calling that pulls along the ridge toward the blue that feels like a wall. They are called Sounders, or sometimes just the Down-Folk. They carry more stone at belt and ankle, and more breath-garden at cheek and neck. Their hair is shaved closer; their skins are polished. They learn the manners of distance: how to let a big one’s song pass through you without jarring your joints, how to hang like an ornament from a falling edge until the current softens enough to let go. Now and then they return with a tale of another place like theirs, far along the ridge, a shadow of carved growth on a different knuckle, chimes tuned to a slightly other scale. Exchanges happen slowly if at all—detached satchels of seedpods sent on a migration’s back, property marked in knots and color, a signed greeting taught to a fluke-broad elder who will repeat it to strangers whenever it passes their lane. In this way the forest knows itself across distances no one swims in a day.

They have art, but it does not hang on walls because walls are leaves. It is in pruning—there are houses whose taste is famous, whose halls bend like poetry. It is in music—the big drums of the commons that are only played when the rain’s roar has gone thin and high, so their bass can occupy what the sky has left available. It is in weaving, but not just of fibers: braiding a rope that answers the hand as if alive, stitching a fan into a roof for thirty years that will keep its curve after you are gone. It is in jewelry easily shed, pearls of air caught in cages of glasswort stem worn for a day and then let go.

There is humor, dry as anything can be here. Jokes travel like little ripples, signs bent slightly, a wiggle of pinky that transforms “tighten that line” into “tighten that line if you like sleeping wet.” There is irony about the high places, where thunder walks very near. People decorate the uppermost halls outrageously when the storms season comes on—bright weed-flowers, tassels of reed that shiver like laughter in the hammering from above. They do this not to defy the rain but to greet it, to be seen by it. The rain does not look down. It hammers. They look up anyway.

They are most themselves in the commons: a web of living root grown broad as a room, crowded and full and not crowded at all because you can pass through a stall with a flick and a push and a swirl. The air pearls constantly upward: a whole community breathing in company with its gardens. They set out bowls and beads, satchels slick with sap. They argue kindly about pruning rights and sharply about music. They bring a new plant into the ring when it is time—a slender thing with promise—and they sing around it, drums go quiet, hands sign a story about shapes, and the young ones step forward with pegs like blessings.

If you ask them what they are, after you have learned to ask questions underwater, they will press your wrist and show you. A house that has learned to change its mind. A hall that lets thunder pass through. A rope that spoke weeks before it failed. A plant that forgave a cut and became beautiful in the forgiving. A child’s silver exhale from a reed garden. A road kept open for a thing with a wide fluke and old bones that sings at a frequency only stone can hear. They will say, if they have to say it in words: we are the ones who hold things gently until they hold themselves. We are the ones who make room. We are the ones who keep listening.
