What lives here is mostly spread, not stacked. It prefers its body to be film, not volume. It takes the shape the chamber offers: thin meniscus, glaze, rime.

First, the first makers of food—if you can call it that in a place where the word herb is nonsense. They are not leaves but a grammar of cells that learns this room’s temperature and light like a second alphabet. They lie exactly where the heat loosens its grip and water lingers: the drip line, the breast of hollows that do not flash-boil. White not because they are bleached but because they dress themselves in their own work: a sheath of silica pulled out of the water molecule by molecule, laid over their skins until they read as old bone. They feed on what the stone gives up and what the drips collect—dissolved carbon, a little nitrogen, whatever whispers down the cracks. Some of them keep a trick up their membranes: light-driven pumps that run on scattered photons no plant would stoop to collect. Here, light is geometry, not summer, and so they do not wear green. They carry pigments if they must, but bury them where you can’t see. Mostly they are the color of habit.

A hand’s breadth away, where the bright focus walks like a knife and then moves on, another caste waits it out. They take the heat like a tide—retreat to pores in the rock when the spot is white, venture out when it wanders and the iron’s glow dims to suggestion. They nibble on ferrous leavings and exhale different rusts. They do not build mats; they spangle, a beard you can’t quite see unless a droplet condenses and trembles on them. If you had a microscope and very slow breath you would see them slicken the surfaces where later condensation will prefer to gather. They are engineers only in the sense that survival is architecture.

The pearl sheens that flare in broad, weak light are less community than habit of physics learned by something alive. A slick of extracellular slime with cells anchored inside it throws colors the way soap does when sun hits at an angle. It looks like living nacre, flexing as the film thickens and thins. The colony breathes with the room: its metabolism swells as the temperature falls and the dissolved air climbs, then dozes while the bright ropes press white onto stone elsewhere. It does not crawl. The room carries it.

In the water itself live the minute bodies that take their days in singular events. They have more verbs than nouns.

Rotifers, tiny wheels inside them, gluing themselves down with a foot you can lose track of, then loosening again when the little weather sets up and rain moves across the ceiling by handfulls. They eat what sloughs from the films—flocs and scrapings lifted into their ciliated mouths. When the heat rises too long, they close like books and wait. If you scrape gently at night you can lift them off in quiescent flecks that look like nothing at all. In the morning they will open in a puddle the size of a thumbprint and begin turning again, old machines cheerfully re-started.

Thread-thin nematodes ghost in the thickness of a single bead rolling down a wall. They navigate by gradient—salt to sweet, cool to warm—bending like hair in a slow current you didn’t know existed. They ride the room’s minor rivers like punctuation, appearing in numbers after a day that made it rain, nearly absent after one that only hissed. If there are more complex desires in them than this, the room has planed those down.

Tardigrades are here because everywhere that water can be briefly, they are eventually. They arrive by accident—on a mote, in a drop that traveled down through cracks from some patch of moss you will never find at the surface. They wake, graze delicately at the film’s margin, and when the focus strays too near or a hot hour goes long, they pull in and become monuments as small as a dust grain and nearly as patient. When the chamber returns to breath-warm and then wrist-warm, they plump enough to go back to their slow, satisfied bite.

Under the lip of black glass, the only thing here that counts as animal in a way your bones recognize. It is almost clear, a length of the light itself extruded and jointed. The plates of its body catch a little shine like stacked lenses because they are, in a way, exactly that—cuticle laid smooth by the diet of nothing but slime. Its legs are fine wires that walk on the tight drum of the water skin. It does not break the meniscus; it plays it. The underside of it is dressed in tiny hydrophobic hairs that make a kind of raft, so when the film thins it’s still enough and when it thickens it never drowns. It turns its belly into an instrument and reads the room: where the pull is stronger, where a drop will go.

It eats the white—scrapes at the bone-pale line with mouthparts so small they’re more inference than sight. It leaves behind a tidy stripe the width of its head, a path as legible to itself as a trail is to a deer. It walks that line until a hiss writes warning: a bead strays toward a hot spot and flashes to nothing with a sharp whisper. The walker flattens beneath a shadow seam without thinking the word. Its antennae are long and unadorned, not for scent—there is not much to smell here—but for heat and the micro-shakes of the chamber’s thinking. Sight is mostly vanity in a place like this; what eyes it has are pale and probably honest enough to admit that light here is a matter of pain and pattern rather than view. Its schedule is the chamber’s. It comes out strong when the afternoon gold begins to dial the room down. It is not common. The room feeds only a number equal to its ability to insist on not being many.

Once a week, once a month, or not at all in a season, the hunter arrives, and you would not know it by web. The air is too wet for snares. It comes flat and patient along the undersides where dripping is less, a thing like a spider that never bothered to own a loom, or a pseudoscorpion no wider than a lupine seed with bookish claws and no sting. It can be white as the biofilm or the color of fogged glass. It waits where the walker likes to pass, feeling in its palms through the glass for vibration in the music of the meniscus. It eats on trust: that in a room this small, small hunger will be paid. It will take a rotifer if it must, pluck a nematode from the side of a drop by its tail, go hungry for the many days in between when none of those oblige. It breeds in the fissures where the light makes lace of itself and the heat comes late.

There are mites too—specks with legs and quiet appetites—scraping at salt, tasting iron, working a blister’s crust with masons’ seriousness. They are moved around the chamber by accident: riding the walker’s belly hair like burrs, then stepping off where the film tastes better. Their lives are small cycles inside the bigger one that the light runs; they are part of how the room edits itself without hands.

Guests fall in: a fungus spore that swells then gives up, a pollen that swells and does not, a micrococcus that never meant to live in glasslight and finds it can. These are not residents, but they fund the residents, and the residents web them into themselves and become less easy to name.

Eggs live here without being seen: the walker glues them under a luminous seam where the sun-knives never quite park. They are the same honesty as the room’s droplets—clear until they aren’t. When they open, the young are miniature, exact, hungry, and go find the pale. If they miss the first week and the first week is a hot one, there will not be a second.

The work of breathing belongs to no single thing. Even the rock does it—takes in heat, gives out water—and the lives tucked in its armpits hitch their timing to that. They keep a calendar that is nothing but a memory of edges: when the white focus sits here, move there; when the drip starts to stutter into rain, let go and eat; when the smell of wet iron comes up harder, wait. If you stayed long enough you could recognize the patterns as personalities, but they would remain correct in their churlishness: nobody here is generous because the room is not.

So what lives here is a roster you could count on two fingers and then many more you would need a lens to love: film that builds its own glass skin and erases the idea of green; heat-timid chemists that browse iron when the room looks away; rotifers that turn wheels in prayer; nematodes that write cursive in water too thin to see; tardigrades that choose stone-sleep and then wake with their mouths already moving; mites that scrape salt with a kind of patience that only makes sense here; a thin, clear walker that reads in the meniscus when to go and when to stop; and occasionally, a hunter made of waiting. Everything else is water deciding what to be next, and the stone lending it places to make that decision. The chamber is not generous, but it is exact, and the lives it hosts are exact in kind. They do not decorate it. They complete its motion.
