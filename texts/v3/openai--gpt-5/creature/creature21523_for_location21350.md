Who lives here is first of all the world itself.

The rind that spans horizons is not merely backdrop. It is the resident: a single, distributed plant whose name, if it had one, would be something like the sum of its forces. It does not have trunks or leaves in any way that maps to wetter botanies; it has investments. It has lungs where canyons open like organ mouths; it has bones where the wind has abraded muscle down to spar; it has milling-plates where the flex of its own breadth grinds the sky’s fall for its bread. It has no core you can point to and say, This is it. It is rim and brace and pistonfield, a body stretched thin to meet a pressure and make a life out of it.

It has memory, of a sort. The memory is in tune more than in tissue—old balances that remain because only those balances could last. It remembers in thresholds: when the air reaches this speed, close there; when the particulate flux tastes of iron and stone, vent here and lay a brace. The very fact of those braces holds the recipe for the next: geometry as heredity, song as governance. Reproduction is fracture and sail; whole arches loosen when there is a lane of calm, and the freed pieces go as seeds do, gliding on a frequency shepherded by old ridges that still sing. What it keeps to itself—the soft catalyst, the hot oil within the cold bone—never shows to weather. Everything tender is turned inward.

Around and in it live those the plant can afford to host and those it can’t refuse. There is a citizenry like any flesh has: symbionts, parasites, gleaners, hunters, engineers by instinct.

The kites are the first the eye finds, because the wind finds them. They fly like nothing else because to them “fly” is only another way to say “eat.” Their bodies are planar and hemmed with microfins that hold precise angles to pressure gradients the way a chisel holds its line to grain. Anchored, they are flags: scales laid smooth to take the scour; cords like whiskers conduct them to the electrostatic mastheads, and they sip current and ion drift and the little half-reactions of dust onto oil as easily as brooks once did oxygen. Let one release and it becomes a blade, geometry reconfiguring from a drag-capturing pennant to a wing that knifes for quiet lanes. A kite that has fed long carries a sheen at its edges where intake membranes have extruded ceramic for armor; when they die, those edges outlive them, little scimitars scoured into the spalls.

The vent-things are smaller, sift-dust made intention. They live where noise is richest, because what is called “sound” here is the most reliable pump. They are shells and cups and lattices that learn the seams of eddy and shock; they open to pressure as seedheads open to rain, and every compression that rattles their mechanophores ratchets an inner shuttle. They graze on aerosol like whales once grazed on krill, but their appetite includes events: a burst of turbulence is a feast, a calm is a famine. They seed by riding on peaks of standing waves until an organ detunes, then settle where the beat is kind enough to do work but not tear.

What passes for herbivores—although herb is the wrong root in oil—are the mill grazers, the clip-beak ones that come into lee-oases where grit piles up and the world grinds. They have clamps not legs, dozens of small jaws distributed along a belly that can flatten and lift with the laminae’s flex. They trap themselves to the plant’s knuckles with ten thousand cilia-hooklets, and their beaks are plasma-sprayed with fluorinated enamel that laughs at cold friction. They eat the plant the way a bee eats a flower: not the bone, which is too integrated to be thefted without violence, but the extrusives, the already-paid-for powders and resins the plant abandons between cycles. They leave behind little wind-stones, desiccated pellets that the belts later take and crack apart for metals.

Hunters are edges personified. Their names here would be written with thin lines if there were script. They look like blades because what is not blade either shelters or is erased. They have a midline of muscle that is more spring than meat and cutwater flares they can fold and unfold to tune drag. Their senses are not eyes but arrays of pits and reefs of piezoelectric ceramic grown under skin; they read the world as lengths of force. They listen to silence: to the absence behind an outstretched rib where a kite must be, to the little disruption when a grasper moves out of phase with a sweep. They do not pounce so much as fall correctly into a channel; in the right channel speed happens to you, and you only must keep your edge true.

There are those who climb and keep. The striders walk the outer faces the way beetles walk bark, yes, but they do not only go in ones and twos. They are a whole way of being the plant has made room for: bone-bodied, resonance-eared, with two great hearts that are drums. In dry seasons and in between gales they gather where the ribs’ tones travel farthest and set their hearts to a common meter. They speak in beats the plant hears.

It is not kindness; it is necessity. The continent’s organs do what they were built to do, but the sky is not perfectly faithful. Sometimes a brace hums wrong, or a vent is fouled by a fallen filament, or charge gathers to a dangerous degree where the plates are tired. The striders’ language began as alarm and maintenance: three quick beats, one long—seal that mouth; a low doubleknock spread along a ridge—dump plates soon or you will arc unpleasantly. Feet and hearts and handled mass define phrases; they lay their bodies along spans to damp bad modes, stand in vent-lips with their clamps splayed to change a resonance by that fractional degree that spares a canyon. They carry no tools because everything is too fast or too grinding for tools, but their skins are toughened with ceramic net and they can glue themselves in place for an hour against a push that would throw a car, if that were a thing here. It is a culture like firebreaks: all practice and nothing saved that is not embodied. When calm, they retell, because retelling is how you keep a pattern alive in drum and bone.

With them live small tuners that never leave organ mouths. Lichens without water, fuzz that is all oil and ash and protein, crowd in the quiet creases behind the beating curtains. They secrete fluororesins into microfissures and they change the roughness by hair-widths that shift a gas’s behavior. They are almost not animals and almost not plant. The striders smell their volatiles the way a mason smells lime, and know by scent alone whether a lip will pull clean when the gusts return.

There are bristle dwellers. Where the great static combs stand out, corona hums nightlong like a city grid. Long as cats’ tails and thin as grass, the bristles are furred with beings that live entirely on charge imbalance. They strand themselves hand-to-hand to span microgaps just before a discharge would leap anyway, taking a tax as the field collapses. Their bodies are insulators when quiet and conductors when squeezed; they flip property the way cephalopods once flipped pigment. A hungry night sends long slow crawls down an entire ridge as the chain reconfigures to sip at the next cresting plate. When a storm truly builds, they fold themselves flat and drive hooks into the bristle’s cuticle and let the big arcs go over them like weather under a bridge. Their dead become insulation; their remnants make a vellum along bristles that teaches the next brood where to hold.

Parasites are beautiful because they have to be. Slack is rare; anything ugly would be ground. There is a thinmouthed thing that rides inside the belt-veins by gluing its lips to the moving skin. It is translucent and you can see the pearl-packets tick through its gut. It does not take much—three in a hundred—and the belts never notice, but predators notice the smell of its oil in the venting sacs and station themselves along return paths and pick them, pale as crescent moons, with darts shaped by a life’s worth of microvvictories in phase.

Others strip glaze. There are ceramophages—rats by outline only, low and wry—that gnaw at wind-fused faces with teeth that are not teeth at all but fields of tightly packed whiskers of mineral stiffened with oil; they rake the glaze like a file and collect the resulting dust in cheek pouches that open inward to catalyze. When frightened, they jam themselves into eddy-pockets and go completely rigid; a gale can lift a flexible thing, but a formed gusset becomes part of the rib and the blast passes.

Microbes—if that is the word for assemblies this almost-mineral—pervade every oily channel. The solvent here is not water; every line of their anatomy is drawn to that fact. Enzymes in this country are hinges rather than hands: active sites that open and close with a bias, actuated by slight changes in charge and the push of passing vanes. Where a plant elsewhere would use hydrogen bonds to make a shape, these lifeforms use intercalated metals in rolling coordination complexes, like ball bearings that also happen to be chemistry. They wear coat proteins that flip when their electrical environment flips, and they feed like dry sponges feed: by taking in, changing, letting go, and never accruing more softness than can be defended.

Behind them all and under them all, the plant keeps making itself. Cones splay open with their green-gloss hard lips and wind out fibers, filaments stiffening taut to the pace of air. When a span snaps a province away, it isn’t loss so much as signal; the whole countryside hears that twang in its bones and extruders everywhere speed a fraction, not because there is a “thought” about replacement but because such was the lucky genotype: those that trued tension to failure frequency left more continent than those that didn’t. Foundries that were not built but grown spit beads with each pistonfall; belts like tendon take them to places where a new rib requires weight. Over centuries the plant has learned so many of its edges that it looks like geology, because that is what survival becomes when there is too much weather and not enough patience for artifice: smooth arch, true plate, stress path proven for a thousand storms.

And still, you can speak of people. If people are those with stories, there are stories.

Among striders there are scores memorized in heart and foot. They know the taste of a certain mineral storm that once broke the static plates in one ridge and blew a white tongue through three hollows two generations ago; they know it because the elders drum its line on calm days, because if the taste returns the dampers must take three long steps and one short, all in silence if possible so the predators, who key on mis-timed tremors, do not arrive as knives. They bury nothing. They pile nothing. If you would find their dead you find only bright lines on the inner sides of their bones where ceramic enmeshed a story and made its own gloss to honor it. Children are spun off at gusts; the young are called quick if they can find the lee lanes by smell and slow if they must be herded with pushes and beats.

Kites have culture too, though not in any vocabulary of community anyone not made of thread and sail would easily feel. They have designs. A lineage learns a set of angles that work across centuries on a given rib; a child-kite inherits more than tissue: it inherits seam; the way an ancestor placed a microvane thirty microns further out of plane than its twin is a genetic sentence. When a ridge falls quiet and tides a breeding century of calms, that lineage fails not with wailing but with a small change in the mottle of the colony’s edges. The next rib builds a slightly different scrap of wind and something else from distant kin finds it and inherits.

Some nights, when corona paints the bristles a careful blue and even the grazers unclench their clamps to test stillness, something else comes down from skyfall—living or at least instructive. Infalling dust is not just stone. It is record of other reaches where oil learned to think in different rules. Microcapsules open to electric shock and release catalysts this place did not invent. The plant is a conservator of recipes; a good enzyme does not care where it was born. Once in a long while a new way of binding a glaze, a new pigment that shunts a frequency the old mix wasted, sticks. Those rare nights the whole ridge brightens by increments for a decade and predators miss their strikes for a season until their pits retune to the slightly different hum.

There are hardly decomposers as such because nothing here rots. Things ablate; things vitrify; things sublimate to powder and become feedstock again. Waste is mostly noise and heat, and even heat is stolen. Things that die in calm are gathered by grazing mouths and pressed to oil, because oil is life’s common weal. Things that die in storm become arcs; their ash salts go into bristle vellum, their lines transfer into bone where stress writes.

If you must know whether something you would call a city lives here, the answer is no and yes. No walls not grown. No artifacts that outlast their function. But there are places of congregation—hollows where the main body shields enough quiet that strata of signals live together long enough to be more than emergency. There, striders lie flank to flank and let long waves of gentle force pass along them—a literature unrecorded except in copy, reenacted as song is reenacted when there is no sheet music. There, kites drape themselves tight and hang open to charge and do not feed, a gesture that means more in a solvent without smell than it does in a mammal world: it means we will not take while the plant resets a resonance we rely on. There, clip-beaked grazers perform a service: with beaks closed they push tone-knots into thin crust, tuning filaments by hair-widths while their young learn which parts of the face belong to the big body and which to the current precarious webbing. None of it reads as civic until you look at what it prevents: a snap here, a spill there, a waste of a good gale.

Who lives here can survive only if shaped to the narrow slack this place permits. Nothing that needs pliancy shows it to the wind. Everything that thinks does so with something that can bear abrasion. The closest thing to an eye is a net of piezo plates held in a ceramic skull; the closest thing to a voice is a pattern spread through a surface that carries. The closest thing to a hand is a clamp. The closest thing to a god is the plant, which is not a god: it is only what happens when a continent learns the lesson of its air by surviving all the errors that did not.

And still it is bright. And still the bristles glow at night. And the inhabitants—edged and clamped and cased against a cold that has no water in it—are busy because the world is running and they run with it. On rare calm days you see them as they are without gale: kites slack as snail-ears, predators standing oddly long-legged and ungainly, striders with their hearts no longer drumming, laying their faces along the ground to listen for the absent voice. You could think them statues for a moment if not for a twitch of a vanule, a taste of oil in a breath. Then the lip of a vent tightens somewhere, and another and another take up the cadence, and everyone aligns their living to the next pulse, because here to live is to couple yourself to a larger machine and take your share without breaking it.
