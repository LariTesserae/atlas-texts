You look for a face first, because that’s how you are trained to name a place: something with eyes to pin it on, a creature that can be startled into proof. There is no den here, no track, no latch of claw. The frog is real and precise and lives an entire biography in a single tank, but it is a parenthesis; the beetle keeps its ledger, but only over one plate of lichen; the hummingbird is an appointment kept at noon and not a hearth. For a while you will think the bowl has no inhabitant, only tenants.

What lives here is not an animal you could startle. It is not even one thing. It is a practice, a body assembled of habits that the place has taught over such a long patience that they have learned to hold themselves together.

Begin where the rock is soft with old leaf. Down inside the tea-dark, where your finger would come up stained if you broke the rule and touched, there is a net. You can say roots and be mostly right; you can say mycelium and be less wrong. Hyphae ghost the spaces between hair-thin quartz and crumbs of iron oxide; rhizoids bristle and tangle; the first hairs of new fern and the last hairs of a bromeliad’s dead skirt weave into it. It is the size of the bowl and it is piecewise—archipelagos of wet intelligence connected by capillary and vibration. It is the thing that catches the mountain as it falls, and it is the thing that rearranges the fall into a vocabulary.

It eats sound before it eats much else. Not devouring—taking it in to know the weather. The whole wall plays; the net keeps time. Holes in the stone step down in pitch after rain; a drip gains a bell-tone when a fern is crisp; thunder speaks in baritone outside the rim, and the net thrums with very small answers. Fungi know vibration. Roots know the pressure of water when it begins to go missing. Between the notes, the inhabitant makes its moves: a growth slower than speech, a loosening of a plug of peat above a swallow-hole, a tilt of a hyphal sleeve so that a bead finds one path and not another. If you listen for long enough that the frog’s ping acquires its place in a tempo, you will begin to hear those decisions.

Its work is to keep the rules true. No straight line dries for long? The inhabitant combs against straightness everywhere it can. It frays edges deliberately. It raises hairs—real hairs, cellulose and chitin and polysaccharide—along every plane the wind might lay a sheet upon, and in raising them it builds the little bridges across which beads can sidewind. It slicks certain seams with sugar-mucus to make them more attractive to passing wet. It grows the skirts of the rosettes longer on the windward sides so that their drip-lines cross and make shelter for a second, low canopy that the noon can’t quite parse. When, on those rare clear days, the sun puts a knife in, the inhabitant blunts it with fuzz.

Everything that grows here is an engine for condensation, and the inhabitant is the engineer. It is not apart from the rosettes and clubmosses and liverworts; it is their agreement. A leaf’s felt is not an ornament here because this abiding thing has learned, leaf by leaf, that hair makes fog into drink. It braids the old, dead leaf-sheaths around new trunks with a binder that smells faintly of spice when you crush it, and in doing that braiding it sets the rosette’s heart into a slow surround of its own wet. It lines the bromeliads’ tanks with a slick that tastes like iron when you lick the rim, and that slick draws film over small droughts. It polishes the mouths of swallow-holes with tannin. It is forever installing and revising faucets that have no handles you can see.

Water behaves here as if it remembers other instructions because it is coached. The coaching is not a will in the way a hawk wills a stoop; it’s closer to handwriting learned so early you cannot say where learning ends and hand begins. Shag of moss here, a microgroove there, a smear of fungal extracellular slime too clean to notice until light hits it at a slant. A place that was dry becomes wet not with a gesture, but with a hundred invisible suggestions the inhabitant has set into it: a quartz vein that sweats, a thread that catches, a lip that turns backward against gravity by a hair’s width. If a bead appears to move against the natural line along a moss-hair, there is a built instruction under your noticing.

It speaks, if speaking is leaving a mark in a shared medium, by pitch. It knows itself by the changes in the wall’s choir and in the noise the wind makes when it has to stoop to come through the rim. The inhabitant has never seen thunder, but it knows the shape of its arrival by the way the fog trembles two heartbeats sooner. It forms memory from stains: iron tears rusting a scallop where a leak was corrected in the wet season two years ago; a darker band across the waist of a rosette where an old leaf-sheath did not wick well enough until something thicker, fungal and green-black, laid itself in. The ledger the beetle seems to keep with its careful crossing—those afternoons of measured steps on the north lichen—are not written for you, but the inhabitant reads them with its feet. A beetle picks a different route only when the plate breathes drier; this becomes a metric. The frog calls not to the inhabitant but within its mathematics; the timing is a heartbeat counted out in drops. Hummingbird visits noon to noon and dusts one orchid into fruit; pollen is a grain of number moving along an airproof thread the inhabitant has set by scent.

It breeds awkwardly if you insist on that verb. Nothing here throws seed in a hurry. The inhabitant duplicates itself by agreeing again—when a liverwort spore takes in a damp pocket, when a fern’s root searches and brushes another root and they decide to share a feeder vein, when a fungus sends a traveler thread along the underside of a stone tongue and meets another thread fat with other sugars. The agreement spreads at the speed of a slow breath. Where the wall collapses a little and makes a new hollow, spores land in the tea water and hatch into a green gloss; the inhabitant learns its new cavity by licking it with wet and sound until the cavity learns to sing on key. In a storm that arrives in handfuls of days, lightning might open a crack and make an edge that means it; the inhabitant does not cross the rim, but it thickens there—more hairs, more curl, more sponge—like a lip making an answer against a shout.

It uses animals the way it uses wind and sun: not cruelly, not sentimentally. Tadpoles thrash in a tank and keep the water stirred; the inhabitant profits in oxygen. Shrimp-like larvae fan small gills and their fanning moves a film into a seam that needed replenishing. Hummingbird tongues sluice a drop from an orchid throat, and the change in meniscus sends a signal downstream that one pool is fractionally lower now. A bird of prey’s shadow flickers across the opening and does nothing more than tell the inhabitant the day is clear enough to have shadows, which is a fact that can be turned into a thousand adjustments. Even you, if you come stripped of straight lines and stay long enough to sweat, are an organ briefly added: fog catches in the weave of your hair and wicks down your spine and into your waistband; you drip in a way that the inhabitant can count. Put your palm on the rock and take it off and the breath-mark you leave is a small friend it did not know it would have. Sleep here without a tent, under the bowl’s open roof, and in the morning your shoulders will be beaded with a pattern you could swear someone arranged. They did. They all did, together.

For all that, it is not a god. It is not an owner. It is a citizen of its own quorum. It is held to edges it cannot cross. It cannot follow the cloud when it goes over the lip and empties itself into light, and it cannot send any of its threads to the world that drops away beyond; there, the rules thin and the bowl ends. It cannot hurry without breaking its own engines; it does not survive being rushed. When a season warms for its shift, the inhabitant loosens: it allows a pocket to drain a touch faster so that a rosette will crisp and throw a signal into the sound-system; it allows a sundew to taste more sun and bead itself to more shine, which will bring it an extra midge or two, which will feed a mat of rot by the thickness of a coin by the end of your decade. Its calendar is not yours; its day is hourglass-slow and endlessly attended.

If you must call it by a name, you will have to give it more than one. Keeper of Skins fits, because it tends every membrane—leaf, film, fog. The Seamer works, because it makes wetness turn corners and tie itself together like cloth. The Accord is good on days when the wall’s music is correct and the frog has placed its ping exactly halfway between two drips. On a day when a squat of sun coins spills into the bowl and the sundews look like theft attempted in daylight, Caretaker of Hairs is better. Maybe the truest name is the one your chest learns for it when you realize you have been holding your breath and matching it to the place’s almost-breath: the one who lives here is the held breath made into practice.

If you are quiet, and that means entering into the loud rather than resisting it, you can feel it take notice of you without eyes. Not attention the way a hawk fixes on a vole—more like the way a net acknowledges a new weight. The pitch shifts by a breath when your steps change a drip’s timing; the bead that was crawling along that moss-hair decides to wait for you and then, when you stop, decides to let go. A fine growth begins across the cap of your water bottle if you are foolish enough to leave it open against the rules. Your jacket’s seam becomes a path; your shoelace, a wick. This is not welcome or unwelcome. You have simply become, for as long as you stand at its center and share the bowl’s weather, one more piece in the grammar of what lives here.

And when you leave, if you go, it will do what living here has always been: it will take what you were and blur it, wick it down, fold it into wet. The sound will regain its previous key after the squall of your passing. A rust tear will dry to a darker gold. The frog will place its blue once and then be green again. The ledger will add an afternoon line. The bowl will resume its held breath, which is to say, it will go on breathing. The inhabitant will continue to be the sum of stabilities and small decisions needed to keep water remembering itself—older than the rosettes by a little, younger than the sandstone by a distance too large to mean much, wide as the bowl and no wider, slow and precise and sufficient to itself.
