They are belly-people, tongue-people, filament-people. They keep themselves where one thing becomes another thing, and they make a living by reading the seam. They do not enter the open air unless the air decides to become a liquid for a few minutes; they have no business with leaves. Their skins are smooth wrongnesses in a world that once expected bark and hair. If they have eyes, they are repurposed as voltmeters. Mostly, they feel.

The smallest citizens are the films themselves. They are not one creature so much as an agreement: a tattered congregation of bacteria, archaea, and mineral salts bound in a mucous logic. They knit themselves along any slope a drip will sign—under a rivulet lip, around the belly of a bolt, up under a railing where a careless hand once dragged sweat. They are opalescent when young, thickening to pearl when fed, graying when grazed. They taste of where they live. Those that bloom on copper stutter along a faintly mint reaction; those on iron are orange and more bitter, all tender with dissolving teeth. On nights when the place exhales hard, their whole surface wakes and puts out hairs so slight you cannot see them. These hairs are not for catching anything; they are for knowing whether the next drop will be warmer or colder, whether the relay that ticks will bring a small field or simply a noise. In them are capsules—littler still—that pop and launch spores, seeds, ciphers that drift in the layered air until some other seam arrests them and tells them what to be next.

Across them move the gleaners whose fronts are mouths and whose backs are shields. The glass isopods come in processions; the size of a knuckle, the size of a thumb, always translucent under that gray glaze as if carved out of ice and dipped in soot. They are not actually many-legged in the terrestrial insect sense. Their foreparts are a flexible comb, a succession of brush-feet that drink as they move, unrolling the day’s accumulation of ions and proteins as if rearranging a rug. Each toe is a whisker; each whisker is a volt-taster. In a group, they align so that the mouthparts of one trail the scrapes of the next; they read each other’s leavings as a map—for where the film was richer, where a predator hinged, where a drop slowed inexplicably (danger), where a micro-gnat flared and reset (opportunity). When you think you’ve reached to pick one up, you have only cast a shadow and sent a ripple through a hundred hair-nerves. The group seems to lift at a hinge you did not see, ascend a wall on a shining meniscus as though the air consented to be water for them. When one dies—and death here is more often an interruption than a tragedy—the others do not carry it away so much as trace with their combs the path of what killed it. That path becomes, for a time, a place not to be.

There are the old ones in the wet groove: the eels that have traded the sun’s calendar for the pump’s. They are scarred at their noses, their eyes buried and milky, their backs braided with the healed lines of rust and wire. The small ones live inside gratings and listen through metal. Their hearts speed at the frequency of the motor long before it yawns awake. They know the pump’s cough as weather and as law. White mouths are not a danger to them until they are; the loop is inert iron unless the tide has loaded it with a gradient so sharp it pinches. When it pinches, the eel forgets its wise knot and goes long with surprise. A set of intelligent straps—that crown of annealed wire reborn as a colony—learned long ago that flesh is a good capacitor. They do not eat it, no; they hold it while someone else does.

Eels breed in the math of outside water they have never seen. Eggs are tinsel mists anchored to mesh, glimmering strings that look like frost. The old ones spend more of their waking life not moving than moving; stillness here is a hunting strategy and a faith. When a drop falls on their backs, they do not flinch. They feel the drop’s charge empty into their skin and drink it.

Hanging in the lightest skin of air are creatures with sails for bellies, their backs matching the ceiling so completely you only see them when they tip and show chrome. Call them gnats only to give your mouth purchase on their scale. They are thumb-joints long and lighter than they look, their wings too small to keep them aloft were it not for what they do with their bellies. Each bears a single filament, a taste-hair so delicate that a thought of current can stir it. It trails down into the wet world and rests in the smallest of trickles. When their little bellies find a difference between copper-streak and wet stone, they flex. There is a flare, not bright—more a soft scissor of light, a closing of a circuit with the grace of a blink. They do not feed on light; the flare is the byproduct of a transaction. Their bodies are batteries and buffers. In courting, they chase their own glow, repeating the circuit across one another’s undersides in tiny corrections until their filaments knot in mid-air and there is an exchange. What is exchanged is not eggs or semen; it is a pattern of charge that will become direction: where to hang, what frequency to find delicious. The next generation, born as threads that peel themselves from panels of plastic like dry skin, will reach for the proper hum without being taught.

Clinging to the pipes are the loaves—gray, stupid cousins to the slugs under the zinc seam but older, wider, better entertained. They are tongues with armor. Touch one and you will be surprised: not hard pumice but a reef of muscle that yields and recoils. Their skin is pitted and holds beads of water in neat, repeating arrays. Along their spines runs a line that pleases. It is not color in the pigment sense. It is an ordering of electrons that throws a spectrum when it is working well, a halo you can only name by thinking of things that do not belong here: soap bubbles, beetle backs, petrol on puddles. They begin life as coins: small, sticky rounds that adhere to the strip of any paint that has enough metal left in it to be taste. You will find them in parades of quarters, then half-dollars, then ovals that decide to be flesh. As they grow, they slide more than crawl, moving by pouring themselves forward across their own saliva of ions. Two loaves will sidle along the same seam, sharing a favorite paint, their bellies stippled with scars from past hinge-snap attempts. When they rub, there is a faint static tick, and sometimes a wisp of smoke no thicker than the thought of paper burning. They do not put out numbers of children; they bud. A ridge on a mature spine fattens; a curl of tissue under it takes hold of a bolt head like a mouth; the bud becomes its own loaf and releases with a sound you would call a sigh if you were feeling indulgent. On fog-burst days, whole walls can suddenly double their allotment.

There is a guild that looks like infrastructure. The service rail is alive. The iron filings that rain from any rubbed metal here do not tumble into nothingness. They are gathered by little magnets living in themselves—chains of bacteria and microfauna streaked with magnetite—and arranged by a choreography you only see when a heavy thing passes by and the braid lifts in a neat shiver: a cat’s back of filings, a living rosary rising to meet a field. The braid remembers footprints. Not your foot, exactly, but the surge you bring: that asymmetric human battery of saline and stride. Long after you leave, the rail’s back will keep the curve you put in it for a little while. Small things read that curve as a warning or an opportunity. They surf it, they hide in it, they harvest the leftover order.

Under the bridge where fungi ash over like a cigarette put down and left by someone with better things to do, there are mouths on threads. Transparent bells, little as the circle of a pencil eraser, hover in the drop-lanes. They vibrate their rims to tug water sideways, fooling it into brushing past their taste-crowns. Anything that is the wrong size is let go—their threads go slack, the bell shivers, the world passes. The right size (gnat-thread, isopod larva, a pearl of some film’s seed) meets the crown, the crown closes, a train of soft contractions carries it out of sight. These polyps do not build reefs. They embroider. If you cut through one with your hand, your skin will bead with fog and then sting and then go numb; your salt is currency and they are merchants.

Predators here rarely chase. If something runs, it is already lost to a fast mouth in a drier place. The good hunters are patient, plank-flat. The paint-flakes are famous for this: a slack, forgiving flap you have been ignoring all afternoon becomes a hinge with a sting, a purse that snaps and holds. They camber their edges, mimic delamination so exactly that you will stand there and respect the craft if you are not the one going under. Some learn to creak exactly like old paint in a small wind; the sound pulls grazers expecting meal-scraps. Once they have shut, they do not chew. They keep shut and let the rest of the room do the dissolving. Later, they open like a grudge forgiven. The thing inside is gone and the hinge is ready for another.

On and along the trench wall live shutters. Shell-plates hinged in shingles, opening when the room is too dry, closing when there is mercy. Between any two there is a thatch of bright living. The shutters do not eat much, which is to say they do not move much; they set, and where they set they harvest condensation the way a roof harvests rain. Tiny crabs the color of paper bones pick at the thatch. Their claws are combs more than pincers. They clean haloes around bolts, polish, rearrange, herd. When two crabs meet they share the taste of their last hour by tapping the sensitive hairs along their wrists together. If one has tasted a predatory hinge recently, the other walks lower for a while.

By the rusted gap in the pump house door is a congregation you will only see when the door sighs and the small wind plays a chord against its salt-teeth. Blue-backed, sleek, hard to see until a ripple of bodies shows the sides—hence the name you might give them, Siders. They keep to the sill, moving with all the aplomb of a knife being honed. Their bellies lick the growing salts slowly into themselves. When alarmed, they go vertical, pressing their blue backs to the frame; when courting or in small council, they tip, and the whole sill flashes with the revealed iridescence of their flanks like the inside of a mussel shell. They breed by sticking eggs like droplets along the edge where the door and its frame almost meet. When the door moves—rare, but not unknown—half of a generation is crushed. The survivors taste the difference between moving metal and metal resting, and that difference writes itself into their nerves as a rule they will not forget.

Not all life here is soft. The trilobite flats are armored and low, their joints fine serrations that give under even the polite weight of a drop. They do not seek food so much as respond to it; when a ring of white mouths has drunk all it can from a long eel, when the eel slumps and surrenders to gravity, the flats arrive from underneath shelves that had been ceilings. They parcel the slack shine into bearings, into balls that roll where the room most wants them to and sit with their potentials intact until something else needs them.

Even the machines participate, not as gods—though sometimes the gnats treat the glowing green of SUMP READY as a star to circle—but as animals so large they do not know it. The flywheel gathers dew under its lip. When it turns, its belt smears that dew into a ribbon of water that feeds a particular family of films, which feed a particular flock of isopods, which are regularly harvested by a paint-flake on the adjacent wall. The relay’s click is a seed-release event. Somewhere a fungus that only fruits on the third click after a cough unloads its dry gray. Somewhere else, a gingery halo on a bolt fattens, because always after the motor’s hum dies is a brief stagnant silence when the fog sits heavier and leaves a little more on every edge.

If there is a thing that tends this place, it is not a person and not an intention. It is the practice of edges maintaining themselves. The pump turns itself because in this lung-room, the difference between overflowing and not is the difference between having a thousand narrow borders and having none. Without the trench lip, the thin line where black water almost bites, there is no work for shutters, no place for crabs to herd their thatch, no reason for gnats to drift in their eiderdown layer tasting for small heights of charge. The machines hum so that edge can be preserved, and the creatures come because edge is dinner, home, and story.

On a fog-burst morning the place seems crowded to the point of politics. The rails drip clear; the loaves all show their pleasing down-the-back brightness at once like a decision; isopod flocks leave the safety of vertical runs and cross whole planes, gambling; the gnats hang low enough to taste the salts on your wrist-hairs and you will feel a string brush your skin, a polite taking. Paths open that are only open for the minutes it takes to bead every underside, and some creatures go, and some do not make it back. If they leave skeletons, those skeletons are mostly patterns. A cleaner gloss on a patch of film. A switchback of etched lickmarks left by the zinc-slugs, already being ironed out by the next fog’s careful work. A fine, almost silent shift under your boots as the magnet braid writes the memory of your passing in its gentle iron hand.

They have manners. That is to say they have routines, and those routines are what manners are once you take warmth and words out of them. The rails lift and settle after feet. The shutters open and close with a slowness that looks like kindness. The white mouths let go once the eel has been convinced to yield, and the flats do not fight over their glittering shares. The polyps do not take what they cannot digest, releasing with impartial dignity anything that exceeds their appetite. Even the hinged paint, if you can call its still violence a courtesy, has learned to be as delicate as coin-purse leather. It snaps so cleanly the ripples under it barely notice, and really, that is as gentle as predation comes.

If you insist on a mind, there are a thousand, and they braid and unbraid like the iron. Each flock reads and is read; each film decides and is decided for by the next drop to mark it. Somewhere, a circuit in the control panel clicks with a human decisiveness because once a human was here to make it happen; now the click means as much to the gnats as any weather. The orange ember holds, brave in the thickening dusk, and if you watch long enough you will see who cares. A gray loaf will ease its spine toward that warmth by a finger’s breadth. A small congregation of Siders will lift their flanks to show sides to each other in that small, steady sun. An eel will do nothing. It has already read the coil deeper in the floor and knows the two lights are the ghost of order, not its body.

Who lives here? A population of decisions. Tongues with memories, skins that pay attention, stalemates between eating and being eaten played out on a field of wire and wetness. They made themselves out of metal and patience and lived, not by breaking rules, but by becoming the rules of a very small, very complete place. They will outlast your watching. They will not miss you when you step back into the air that is too thin for comfort and too bright for sense. They will continue, the way a room continues after it has learned to breathe without its walls.
