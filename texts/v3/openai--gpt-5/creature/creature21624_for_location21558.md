What lives here is mostly patient and mostly small. It begins like a fingerprint on the underside of a breath: clear, structureless, then—when the rhythm steadies—suddenly intricate.

The first builders are films the thickness of spit. They slick the undersides of shelves, the scalloped lips of cracks, the shadowy walls that find the bass note sweetest. They are colorless until they feed; then they take on a bruise-blue, an iron-orange, an oil-lilac, according to what the stone offers. They do not eat in the way of mouths. They traffic. Tubes inside their slick expand and collapse with each passing pressure wave, trading gases and salts, coaxing dissolved metals out of trickles that would be nothing for a larger appetite. The stone sweats; they are the handkerchief. Each layer they weave is a membrane and a sieve, a little city of pores that opens more on the inhale, less on the exhale. Where brine and sweet water fringe each other in a pan, they lay down tissue like ribbed paper that creeps up against the gradient, always repositioning its edge to sit exactly where the chemistry tastes most right. They leave edits as they go—varnishes thinned, stains muted—notes in the margin of basalt’s book.

Their bodies are the field the world above plays in. Over them come grazers no larger than commas: glassy worms with spicules like eyelashes that comb bristle-salt to paste and use the paste to line their own little pipes; plate-backed things that move like tide lines, their undersides fretted with teeth finer than sand, leaving skated chalkiness on the windward lacquer. In the dimmest mouths of corridors, there are loose cigarettes of flesh threaded with little rods of silica, inhaling condensation by capillary and exhaling brine in a slow bead—a geometry lesson in breathing.

Some beings build with weight. They carry on themselves, not inside them, the place’s minerals: manganese in freckles, iron in stitches, filaments of salt nested like down among their hairs. To touch one would be to lay a hand on a stone that remembers softness. They feel by fur—if fur is what you call the forests of minute cilia that wake at every whistle and flatten at every cough. The entire plain is a sheet of unreadable song to you; to them it is legible as current. A misplaced pebble is a snag they will notice days later by the way one row of hairs keeps trying to stand against the beat. They are the ones who find a pan two paces from its twin and decide which should brim. They will not hurry to it. They will wait while the eight-count drifts to a nine, gauge the difference by how the rime grows teeth, and then—on a day that seems the same to you as any other—drift out from wherever the pressure hollows have kept them and put the pebble back.

You do not often see them whole. You see signatures. A low berm leaned up against a whistling seam until the burr goes easy; a ring of fists around a soft spot where the bass drops, so the condensate hovers and fattens rather than goes to wind; a row of shards set like a zipper across a slot, so that the exhale shreds itself and the inhaled mist precipitates in predictable strings. None of it is neat in the way of tools. It is neat in the way of hands that can bear cold and weight and salt and can feel a hum through their wrists like a carpenter through a stud.

Where rock breathes hardest, something like a mollusk carries the sound on its back. Its shell is a scroll written around a deepening axis, thick where the bass swells, thin where the sound runs slack; the curve tells you where the richest tone lay ten years ago. Along the rim are vanes fine as combs that it opens only to the inhale. Held against the blast, they flex and sing, catching aerosols, fragment of organic ash, the smallest of the metal-fed grazers that stray into air. The foot adheres to angles that would skin anything else. It does not so much glide as grind: it keeps a memory of its path in the rock behind it, a pale scar through the black on which its young will one day read a map of what counts were good.

There are hangers. They own rope-bodies armored with hair-thin needles of some unknown glass, thorns of crystal that grow them a winter coat in hours and slough to mush when a warmer breath leans through. They live in the mouths where seawind is a rhythm, not a gust. On the exhale, their fronds unroll, a pennant of flesh tense with brittleness, and drink the outbound fog. On the inhale, they are gone, coiled tight, anchored in deep nooks in the granite by knots of their own stone-threaded tissue. Their growth is a diagram of fear and appetite: each node the measure of a season’s breath, each thorn a fossilized gust.

In flooded courts a lattice stirs—spines upon spines in a closed geometry that lifts itself in quadrants to make the water move through it. It remembers, always, the last floc it caught and the pieces of it that were worth keeping. It uses no muscles you can name. The sea and the plain do the work; it reframes the work just enough to harvest a fraction. It is a patience engine. Standing over it you could be fooled into thinking that the pool is empty until the metronome rolls and the lattice leans one degree and sifts a different grain.

There are listeners you never see. Their bodies are buried in the underplain’s throats; what they put aboveground has no color and no detail, only a shine where the water scores it clean. They are mouths on stalks, lips closed most of the time. In a given week you will see them open once, to blow an ammonia fog or a bicarbonate mist, a chemistry that nudges a nearby film toward one metabolism instead of another. They are the set designers. The walls hum; they make sure the hum turns one basin black with life and lets its twin stay glass. Their season is not spring or winter. It is when the deep reservoir breathes higher or lower by a finger’s width. It is when the cough to the west shifts from five heavy pulses to six.

If you pay very close attention, you become aware of a theme running through the plain that is not geology and not weather and not any animal you know the shape of. It is an old intention that leaves child-sized works. It is responsible for the tooth-rowed wedges across a certain slot and for the strange tidemark where half a wall is slick with gloss and half chalk-dry. It comes rarely and only when the count relaxes in a way you can’t hear. It has no eyes that light would suit. It stores a lifetime in its skin-hairs, each bristle a tuned filament that stiffens to certain whistles and lies down to others. Its mind—if that is the word—is spread along its length, a row of little villages that decide locally how to put themselves under pressure and when to squeeze. In profile you might take it for a tangle of ropes dragged through a rock gate and left there to drip. In motion you would understand that the tangle is deliberate. It leans one way and its whole right side plays bristle-music with the passage of air; it leans another and the same hairs are pressure-sensing whiskers that map a crack to its end.

It is not a maker of objects any more than a bee is a maker of flowers. It is a maker of circumstances. It cannot cool the cloud-lid or still the metronome. It can, with a row of pebbles and a bruise on basalt, shift a field of drips enough that its feeder films go fat and throw off a dusting of spores at the exact stride of the beat. It can turn an eight-count to a nine so that the vaned shells it depends on for phosphorus open at slightly different times and do not starve themselves all at once. It eats the same things as the shell and the lattice—fine wet instruction dust that the place hands out whenever it breathes—but it also eats future. Its food security is arranged time: a corridor that no longer nags a harmonic; a court whose surface ceases to ring just enough for a film to knit; a narrow where the exhale falls on a certain consonant the body enjoys. It, if anything here does, gardens.

It may be singular, or it may be a few. There are hints of more than one. One arrangement falls and is remade in a different hand, as if the second mind favored the hum one shade darker. One court drips to death while its neighbor blooms; months later you find a low berm aimed like a deflector and both basins live thinly instead of one fatly. Their reproduction, if they bother, could be as impersonal as shedding a clutch of threaded hairs in a salt-trap and trusting the metronome to rub them into bodies over years. It could be hands teaching hands to read the skin of the plain. It could be nothing that looks like birth, only the same arranger returning under the same counts for longer than you are equipped to measure.

Things with hearts and blood are scarce. A bird might come in once in a season to hang on the dull air and look for water shiny enough to be safe—but there is no soft landing here. Anything that extends itself is immediately salted. Anything that stands too high is spoken to by the wind until it shreds. Insects, if any wander from elsewhere on a blue day, leave no heirs; there is nothing ready for their hunger.

Those who belong do not ask for color, and they do not flinch from mathematics. They are tuned. They time their extensions to the exhale and their withdrawals to the inhale. Even the films keep time. A vane-shell will starve itself in a week if the whistle at its chosen corner slides too far off; it leaves its chalky trail and goes to where a slightly larger throat speaks to it. The hangers shear off old coats of glass and sprout new in an hour when the pulse runs colder. The lattice waits three cycles out of four and works only on a fourth when the mouth below it coughs a little sugar. And the arranger, when it comes, labors not against the beat but with it, like a smith and the bloom he is persuading—taps here, a strike there, let the hammer fall when the iron wants to give.

Life, here, is not green. It is a community of ears and fingers. It builds with gloss. It grows in sheets that read the chemistry of hairsbreadths. It puts stones in little compulsive rows because the air is an engine and there is power to be milked from what rattles in its guts. If there are prayers, they are made of defects introduced into good resonance—a burr softened, a cough lengthened—so that the world above the underworld of water can thicken and not tear. If there is memory, it is in the pattern of salt left when a dish dries and in the bruise of a touch on manganese varnish and in shell-thickness written around time.

You can live through a day here without seeing a single creature besides your own exhale. Yet at the scale of droplets, these people are everywhere. They will not resemble what that word means to you. They are here as answers to this place’s only questions: What do you do with breath and with stone? How do you eat rain that never falls? How do you anchor yourself to voices? They do what they must. They brace under a lid of cloud and let the instrument play through them, and, when they can, they tune it back.
