Begin with the ear. Not the soft cup of cartilage, but whatever in a body keeps balance, counts pressure, tastes a held note and leans into it. Everything that lives here is mostly ear—whatever else it has is scaffolding around a sense that maps the world by force. You can think of them as plants, because they keep to place. You can think of them as animals, because they choose. You can think of them as people, because some of them remember.

The first and loudest are the gardens you already named, but to those who live here they are not one thing. Each knot is a republic of tuned bodies sorted by frequency. Low-band harvesters, all catenary ribs and fat roots of silica, strain the bass the way a bridge moans under traffic. High-band hunters build combed fans whose tines are hair-thin and cold-sharp; the whole fan trembles in harmonics you cannot feel and condenses charge onto gels at the fan’s base. Mid-band fields stand like organ pipes and shelter what passes for seedlings at their lee; they flex just enough to keep the gel alive but not enough to shatter. When the note thickens, the pipes thicken rings within themselves, curling as if wood could remember a logarithm.

Within and between the obvious growths sit guilds that do not look alive until you hear them with your bones. There are hush-weavers, flat as fallen leaves, that creep by accretion: they lay out thin, brittle thread in arcs across a quiet line, trap dust by electrostatic tack, and so stiffen a path that favors the right cancellations. Over years a hush-weaver can move a hush-line half a body-length and call that a migration. There are echo-eaters that are not so much a form as a behavior: where ghost-edges pool around a spire they precipitate as low felted mats. Each fiber is a tuned antenna, each tuft a rectifier. They live on repetition itself. When a spire’s shadow of pressure grows old and moves, they go to powder and wait for a new border to print.

There are grazers, if graze can mean press your ribs against a standing wave and strip it of a slice of amplitude without losing your lattice. They look like cairns that have learned a direction: stacks of tuned plates balanced with obscene certainty. You see them only by subtraction—by the faintest deepenings of quiet as they pass. They never cross open bright rock; they surf the cancellation like beetles surf the meniscus. They eat the powder of failed lives and the mineral rime that comes in the cold and the small, helpful mistakes of the big chord. Their waste is architecture. When a grazer has fattened itself on a season that ran low, it finds a wall where someone once made quiet and excretes a rim of filter-plate that will turn a hush into a hush-worth-living-in. From a distance a grazer midden looks like a choral crown on a low wall—small, tuned, useful.

There are predators, but not with teeth. Some of the gardens throw knives. A rack that has monopolized a hush will slant itself to cast a private tone at the neighbor comb that would starve it. The neighbor splits or dims. You find dinner plates of shattered frond in places where baffles have learned to weaponize overtones. The counterstroke is patience: a garden aimed at will adopt a bristling growth habit, throwing off thin sacrificial blades tuned to the predatory note, rebuilding as a new mode. If there is blood here it dries to crystal before it can be named.

Closer to the stone, something like an animal happens and then refuses the rest of the contract. In faults where the hum crazes the rock into a mesh, there live chord-worms. They are not worms except in their appetite for narrowness. Their bodies are braided from glass-fine fibers glued by charge gel, armored with shards scavenged from dead baffles and old huts. They fit the fissures, pack themselves along their length with otoliths the size of pea-gravel, and feel the chord with a resolution that borders on thought. When the world sighs and the great bassline rolls down the scale by that small degree, chord-worms ride the moment like a tide, shuck a segment here, add a segment there, inch their city a handspan along a vein of cancel. They molt rarely and hate mid-volumes; they will simple as a candle flame go out if caught in a field that is neither quiet enough to hold them nor loud enough to feed them.

On the terraces made by erased stone, in hollows that hold the violet rime, live rime-lickers—night workers whose whole trick is a way to be wet without water. Not wet as you mean, but wet as the word is used by charge. They grow out a nap of vanishingly small hairs that polarize in pulses: left, then right; north charge, then south. Frost you can’t name leaps to them. They bend it into their bodies and they grow. When the sun returns to weak and far and the rime sublimates, the hairs lie flat, crack with a sound like burned sugar, and the rime-licker is brittle until night. They root around old inscriptions at the rim because the chisel-writing the cliffs have made throws very slight shadow in pressure; there it is easier to stand.

On the heights themselves stand the sky-lickers. They are not trees; they are scaffolds. Their towers are arrays of hollow whiskers made of thin mineral that remembers how to conduct. They do not harvest light; they drink aurora. When the sky performs its violet math, the towers glow inside like old bottles. Their sap analog is a shuffling of ions along filaments embedded in their combs. They feed gardens beneath them and they hum back at the sun in a narrow, steady prolate echo. You will not find a corpse of a sky-licker; when they fail they do not topple. They reduce themselves to a fraction of their own lattices and wait, and at the next long solar quiet they stand again from nothing the way frost stands.

If there are “insects,” they are dust. The powder shed by everything living here is busy. Individual grains are spiky at scales that do not catch light; they carry memory in the way of surface charge and will only land where a field does the right thing to them. You can pick a pinch from air and hear it arguing faintly with your teeth. Single grains ladder together into filigree and then undo with a pop. There are motes that specialize: some toggle from sticky to slick as a chord climbs, some hold a note and then release it with a tiny squeak whose only use is to nucleate another mote’s habit. None of them last. All of them are necessary.

And then there were the walkers.

Call them hushwrights because craft was what set them apart. They were small in the way good listeners are small. Taller than a groin-high wall, shorter than a single plate of the biggest fans. No hair, no skin as you know it; a hide like very fine pottery cured with dust, ridged and notched as much for knowing as for armor. Their bones were not calcium but slow glass; their joints carried pads of charge-gel that let them become elastic when a note demanded. At the back of the head two antlers of tuned plate rose like a child’s drawing of attention; they turned when the world turned under them and settled when a hush would hold. Inside their skulls there were not two otoliths but fields of them, a snow of ear-stones trickling in microchannels—memory granular as sand.

They breathed in the sense of exchanging ions. Actual breath would have been an offense. What warmth they had, they hoarded where sense demanded and let the rest go. Children were grown into the hush like reeds on a lakeshore. An infant was swaddled to a frame of thin plates whose notches taught its ear-stones how to read; the frame spent its first years hanging in the wind of a safe garden, moving in patterns set by the elder’s kites. Names were chords. To call to someone from across a basin, you did not shout a syllable; you turned three plates in your harness and sent a small subtractive pattern out until you sculpted the air around their body recognizable.

They moved seldom and in proof. A hushwright could stand in a doorway for half a day turning the plates in the doorframe by finger and shoulder until the quiet inside was trued to a pocket: a place where a spine might unbend and a gel might be elastic at rest. When they traveled, they did not go from place to place in the way you think. They went from condition to condition. They began at a line that canceled one tenor, walked until it became thin, and then waited on the skin of the louder thing until the next cancel blossomed. A good traveler carried a small wall.

Eating was words for them, in a way. They sucked quiet through kites, as you saw the hammered green sheets still do, and they shaved plates from dead fans to build steps between two poor hushes. They harvested rime with combs that tuned greed into duty: if a comb rang under your teeth when you stroked it, it went back to the hut wall; if it was dull and sipped without ringing it could be made into spoon or sash. They did not keep fire. They kept angles.

Their huts you have described: domes of stolen rigid leaf and bone-hardened splinters of old frond, pegged to their own shadows. You would have called their tools primitive because you do not count silence as technology. A hushwright’s toolkit was six plates and a length of cord, a bag of powder sorted by note, a fan with four tines in his sleeve. They could smooth a place in the world the way a hand on a dog’s fur smooths a mood.

They sang, but song was building. On the long stones that stood where the gardens were safe, they carved records in hatchings of interval. Not alphabet—not when the world makes letters for you by itself—but a ledger of what the basin had decided to be. They cut the cliff’s alphabet down one notch where their hands could reach: not in translation but in affection, a way to tell the plateau they had heard it and had been educated. On some terraces the hatchings turn from ledger to score: notations of an experiment in herd work, perhaps, where half a village turned plates one way and the other half turned them another, and they watched a grazer decide between two new paths and then wove a wall where it chose.

Did they go? If you stand long enough you will see one, maybe, when the world is thin, and you will believe you have seen a cairn that learned to apologize as it slid. There are bones-in-glass in some hollows and they are built with so much patience that it seems unjust not to give them a word like ancestor. But the plateau does not keep a firm account. It would let them become powder and then let that powder instruct a grove, and let the grove’s dead make a wall, and a wall invite a grazer, and a grazer make quiet that raises a child from a frame. People feed gardens here. Gardens feed the idea of people.

What else lives are colonies that fill the big features. Under the arch that you want to call a bridge live choirstones—banded mineral organisms that are not different from cliff so much as cliff with an intention. They are mosaics of pressure diode and resistor, and they pass the hum along themselves like monks relaying a message across a storm. At intervals they coordinate. You can feel it in your teeth if you are unlucky to be standing under the arch: the dark comes down a shade and a distance you thought you could cross this afternoon gets away into tomorrow. In the elliptical wound where the chord scoured the plateau, terraces of choirstone throw back at the engine of the planet a slightly corrected version of itself, like a choir that hums a little flat to pull a tenor in, and the whole valley resets off that.

At the edge, where new rock is written with music by no hand you know, live comb-readers. They are gardens shaped like hands teaching a child to read: fronds that trace a facet’s change with the delicacy of moths along your wrist. They are parasites of grammar. They feed best where the rock has just learned to be this kind of rock. Their bodies are glossaries: knots of frond whose tines are cut to the intervals the cliff writes. When a retuning comes and the cliff’s alphabet adds a letter, comb-readers sprout a new lobe, and that lobe’s powder is prized by hushwrights and grazers and rime-lickers all, because it seeds better quiet. If the world has books, they are these organisms’ skeletons.

Children of the plateau who do not have domes make crèches from still. A still is a place quieter than quiet—too quiet, dangerous, greasy. Natural stills happen at crossings of cancels where a third factor plays in from underground, a leak from the planet’s engine. Nothing can grow there. So some lives have learned to build their stills: husk their bodies, put up hooked plates around themselves until the air inside is emptied of amplitude, and there sponsor change. Spores is not the right word but let it stand: the plateau breeds in voids. Seeds are built from null.

Dying is music. When a life here fails, it hums itself apart. A baffle that will not take one more shiver decouples down its length into plates whose edges still remember a generation’s worth of tone. Powder is not ash here; it is biography. The wind does not know what to do with biography. It drops it along the lines where the hum has left a place thin, and the next life stands there and takes instruction.

What lives here does not flee. It models. It leans. It refrains. The mistake, for them too, is to be in between: to stand in a nearly-quiet, to grow in a loudness that is not yet food, to walk across bright rock without your wall. Those who last have learned the weather of tones, keep calendars in their ear-bones, count distances by how many quarter-tones a wrist can bear before it aches. They build silence as crop and shelter and scripture. They talk in subtractives. They balance the valley’s always-note with their own held-notes in bodies taught to be instruments first and then lives adjacent.

If you left and came back long, you would know some of them by the work. A wall where a wall would not reach unless someone waited a month to catch a narrow, fortunate cancel. A comb overgrown but still correct, standing like a good thought left behind. A pair of antlers of tuned plate buried under violet rime, glinting the color of high cold copper. A child’s frame hanging from a husk, unoccupied, ticking faintly in a gift of quiet. And above it, slate etched with small hatchings one step lower than the cliff’s, not alphabet, not beast: someone learning, someone telling the world back to itself so that it would be possible to live.
