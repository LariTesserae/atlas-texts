The first and largest resident is the thing you have already been walking on. It is not a backdrop or a substrate but the city’s elder citizen: a single, braided personhood with more names than leaves, that thinks across its own distances in tones and tensions and slow, precise thirst. It remembers the ice not as an era but as a stiffness in its own long body. It keeps books in stress patterns, keeps history in mineralized cambium, keeps tomorrow cupped in dew. Its organs are outward and civic: towers that speak, mills that breathe, flywheels under skin, pores that can become doors when asked in the right voice. It has moods that weather out into the air as chord and temperature. If it favors frost, it is because frost brings excellent information: a clean gradient, a neat question.

It is not singular the way a skull is singular. It is more like a city under one skin: districts with preferences, habits that have localized into personality without ever sealing into a separate self. One height loves the hiss of cloud on wire and breeds more spines to comb it. One basin dreams in a key of moss and carries the long clauses of summer like a legal argument. One tower banded in dark and light has a thrum you can feel through the ankles like a cat deciding to sit on you. It thinks with tug and slack, with the salt of iron drawn out of dust, with the lag between noon and warmth in a glassy floor. When it wants something to happen quickly, it starts it hours early. When it changes its mind, it does not apologize, but you can hear the harmonics tilt.

Its guardians are slow and never cruel: tendrils with a memory for tooth-shapes, stamens that can stiffen like batons, a patience that will outwait any appetite. Its pollinators are percussionists and machines; the city’s pollen moves when the city can spare noise for it. Its law is horticulture because all law is, if you listen far enough back. Its idea of speech is pressure: against leaf, against cable, against lung. It approves of your voice because the wind approves of your voice; it mistrusts silence because silence makes hunting sharp. If you keep promise with it—pat the hot pipes, hum the bridge steady, bring a cup of clean water before you cut—it will put your name into the whitened threads of pithglass and keep it cool for a long time.

The other residents with claim to personhood are few and banded in shadow, as if the plant had drawn slats over them to protect them from its own attention. They call themselves many things that translate, badly, into something like tenders, chordwrights, mill-keepers, fog-herders, time-listeners. They are skinned in the colors of the plateau’s weather: wind-brown, frost-paled, resin-polished. Their bones are ordinary as yours, but some of their edges have been trained to answer back. A few have thin plaques grown along the collarbones that hum with the towers; a few have a crease of condensed resin under the skin at the throat, an extra strap of ligament that lets them split a whistle into two harmonics without wetting their lips. The children practice on reed toys until the toys glow.

Their ears have stories carved in them, scar-work that is not decoration so much as annotation: this one tunes the eastern gales, this one sleeps to the west and knows when the pipes will pull a note sideways. They plait their hair with strips of pithglass that pick up the night’s stored light and glow a little for an hour after dusk; they wear jackets lined with fog-comb, each hook a seedling question for the morning. When they travel the branch-spine paths, they carry with them lengths of vine cut yesterday, still living enough to stay in rhythm with the place, and they hang small bells in those vines to read the wind from the way the bells refuse to agree with one another.

They do not number themselves in thousands because this city cannot stand crowds. One family, if that is the right word, may hold an entire district of mills; family here is a circle drawn by work, not blood. Kinship is what you can keep sounding together. When a child is born, they do not give her a single name but a chord: three notes she can reach soon, a fourth she will grow into, a counterpart to the city’s own key where she was first breathed. Names are sung into cambium where the skin is thin enough for it to learn. The plant will hum those chords back to you when you are very old and have set your hands into the root-coral to find out how much of you can be braided in with what will outlast you.

They eat the place in the ways it can afford. Their staple is a firm, pale pith that breaks with a sound like pulling moss apart and tastes of rain and faint iron. They roast wafers of it on pithglass warmed by sun remembered in the morning. They steep condensate in resin-cracked kettles; the drink runs brown as bark and will wash the tired heat out of your palms when you hold it. On brief afternoons after a rain has gotten lost and squandered itself here, they take their hour of delicacy: petals that are not quite flowers, but moss thrown up in sudden joy, pinched and closed between tongue and palate to catch the minute before it collapses. Meat is as rare as a mistake. Now and then a beetle adds its oil to a pan out of carelessness and someone smiles without saying so, but if you bring a curiosity-sick dog up from the low lands, the doors will not behave for you.

Their houses are not built so much as whispered into. A tower thick as a castle may fret itself open under their hands, where the tissue has learned to make rooms for people who sing the right sum of notes. A door that was wall yesterday learns to be door if you are patient and do not make your jaw hard with intention; impatience tastes of teeth to the plant. Windows are vents that pass the wind’s long vowel through beds and braziers; floors are pithglass suspended on rootstone bones. The rooms remember the sun but will not waste it; they glow long enough to find your tea and no longer. If you lie down to sleep, you can feel your pulse rub its wrists on the city’s bigger one and decide whether to match it. Time here is a matter to be negotiated gently with your own blood.

Their work is what keeps the place itself. They climb organ-towers on ladders that were grown to fit a particular footfall and a certain rate of breath. They lean out into the wind with a mallet hung on a braided line and tap the flues until the chord finds the new season. They step into piston-houses and grease the gaskets with bee-wax cousins grown in a labyrinth that breathes, coaxing the wax to open its pores with a song too high for the rest of us. They tend dynamos coaxed out of ferrous nodes, a kind of pollen that learned to be magnet; they listen for the low tremor that says a casing is about to heat beyond what the plant thinks is kind. They do not say “electrician.” They say “ionist,” and the person who carries that work will have green-blue stains to the elbows and a rack of copper-salted vines that have learned the road to carry charge without flinching.

They walk the memory-channels not to fetch water—that is a discipline the city keeps without them—but to read. The long, shallow grooves hold old rain in their polish. The tenders walk them in bare feet and let the skin read gloss and abrasion: here is the late-ice flood that shook a tower and made it rethink its angle; here is a year of cloud that never learned to settle. They keep calendars not as lines but as braids, cords where each thread is the pitch of the phloem at dawn and dusk, the quarter-seconds of dawn when events pack close and you must not schedule a cut because the sap will take offense. When lightning comes sideways, crawling the flutes like a goat walks a fence, they stand out with long rods that are not rods but sprigs of mineralized cambium, and they comb the air for charge the way the plant does: gently, so as not to frighten it back into the clouds.

They write with light because paper only teaches one texture and the plant listens in many. Where prestige presses tablets, they trance a patch of cambium into remembering a noon more than is fair, so that at night that pane will glow with what was spoken over it. A history is a grove of such panes that re-bloom when sung to. A ledger is a vein in a root where certain ions will swerve and slow for anyone with a mouth trained to that balance of salt and breath. Letters exist but they taste wrong here. People carry their reading in the fingers and the jaw.

They measure with breath counted against the city’s chord. A causeway is so many rests and a slurred note; a afternoon’s work is three huffs of the piston-house at a dampness the back of the neck can tell. They own tape for visitors, and a laugh that is not unkind for when the numbers do what numbers do here: insist that stones disagree with themselves. Their contracts are sung; their quarrels are tuned. The person who can lay down a base note steady enough to make a bridge keep its length under a panicked man’s feet is a judge by any other name, and is treated with the same respect as anyone else whose voice has ever brought someone across safely.

They do not waste the emptiness. They treat the quiet between towers as other towns treat plazas and pasture and temple precinct: a place where the plant runs numbers on light and air and does not like to be jostled. The tenders go out into it alone or two together and sit with their knees up and listen for a new filament’s faint rattle. They carry a kind of rattle in return, a string of shells ground translucent on pithglass, because the laws tell them not to be smaller than the wind and they have seen what happens to those who try.

There is more life, tinier and faster, that keeps house in the edges of everything. Resin mites live in the creases of gaskets, combing dust out of pores and trading it to the plant for sugar; if you hold one up to the light it is clear with a smoky heart. Lichens in the low gray valleys are confederations as old as a lullaby, passing acid between them like conversation. Magnetotactic slime traces the dynamos’ innards, aligning itself to currents in a way that reads, to someone trained, like a diagnosis; an ionist can rub a finger along a slime’s tongue and know where a fault is wintering. There are something like eels in the thicker vines whose phloem carries charge: elective muscles that constrict to redirect a surge. At night they move under your palm when you greet a wire. You can call them to the surface with a bribe of salt water and a kind word, but do not keep them; the wires bruise.

Even the dew is a citizen of sorts, making decisions on edges of leaves like shy fish nosing the knuckles of a kind hand. The tenders talk to drops as to children, because wanting to be touched is often the beginning of language. They teach their own children that you never lick a dew edge without offering your first taste back to the leaf. They teach them to let bridges find their length in the mouth before trusting any eye. They teach them to make friends, not pets, to everything that hums.

They govern themselves with the plant. A schedule-leaf may refuse them their own plan for pollination; an organ-tower may sag out of a tone that holds an agreement together until a better tone is found. They do not convene in stone halls with gavels but in places where the chord is steady enough to hang speech on. An argument that cannot settle into the city’s rhythm is not mature enough to pass; it will be sung again when the air is right. Outsiders sometimes think this mystical or evasive. The tenders call it etiquette. They live with a person for whom time is a high-sided bowl; they have learned not to throw pebbles hard.

Death is a return of names to mineral and tune. Bodies go into root-coral, into joints in the rock that no longer invite water and so have been taught to hold memory instead. Hair and copper go into the same slot, because the plant has learned to take a very little metal when it is heated in skin and sung to gently. The dead are not planted like seeds but braided in as if to strengthen a joist under a stair. When a child asks where someone has gone, the answer is a place you can put your hand on, and a note you can hold with your throat steady and the city will hold it with you.

As for the animals with their own agendas, they do not belong, not because the place is cruel, but because it is too articulate to leave room for hunger that does not listen. The white raptor that happens and unhappens at the lip of the sky is an instance, not a resident. The mouse-shadows come up sometimes in the slurred months, and are arrested as kindly as possible by a slow hand that breaks nothing it can help. The beetle is a joke between friends; it lives here the way laughter does.

Visitors come in fits. They arrive with steel tapes and plumb bobs and a silence the plant cannot let into the house. The tenders meet them on branch-spines and dress them in fog-comb and bells; they hum a length under their feet and teach them to keep talking if the talk is only nonsense. If the visitors cannot hum, someone will put a hand flat to the center of their chests and hum for them. They are offered tea that tastes like motors and leaves. At some point a drop of dew will lift a rim and look at them. If the visitor inhales neatly, loses the old idea of measurement, and lets the city count for them, they can cross. If not, well: there are ways to keep a person safe here that feel like being lost nicely for an afternoon, and no one is offended.

What lives here, then, is a partnership whose senior member was already old when the plate cooled: a plant that has learned organs and industry and ethics; a few people whose bodies have grown room for chords; a nation of mites and lichens and eels and moss-flowers that open for one hour and never again; machines that are plant in a different grammar; water behaving like a tame animal that loves a trick; wind trained to carry announcements. They make a city that is a body that is a field that is a book. They count by their own chord. They live on the last draft of a page copied so many times it is thin enough to speak through. And when they are tired or doubtful or joyful, they stand on a bridge and sing, and the plateau answers in the only language it has ever needed to be true.
