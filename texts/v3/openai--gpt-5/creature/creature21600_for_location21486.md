Who lives there first is the place itself.

Not a single tree but a temperament held in green, a vault-shaped mind made of vascular memory and patient compromise. It is not a person as you understand persons, but it edits you as persons do. It remembers load and light; it remembers that a thousand footfalls can convince gravity to look away for a while. It learns your paths and leans its tissues to meet you, hums to itself in lignin, answers weather like a practiced muscle answers a strain. It lives by resizing the present until the storm fits inside it. If you lie along a rib and give it your hearing it will give you back a rhythm older than your bones. What lives there as root and leaf has agency, and the gardeners know they live inside its consideration as surely as bees live inside a flower’s.

Inside that green attention live the gardeners.

They are not iron people. Their bones are dear to them, a hoard of salts banked against the sky. They are light, the way anything needs to be light to move on things that flex. Their feet have long clever toes, two to meet and two to argue, with a pressure-thumb at the heel that makes a hand of every step. The hair along their arms is not for warmth so much as for water—fronded, capillaried, brushed with wax so dew will wick toward the wrist where a thirsty plant waits from habit to drink the residue. Their lungs are not enormous but taught; they take air the way one takes a hand, with invitation and response, learning the breathing schedule of the day so that distances shrink when the canopy opens its mouths.

Their eyes are patient in shadow. The iris is a leaf-bright green when dilated, the pupils lazy ovals that tilt in sympathy with the sun’s inclined rumor, and when they need more light than exists they touch the pale leaves in the lightwells and write with afterglow on their own hands. Ears are not big: sound is not the common medium. But their noses are intricate as shells, their soft palates alive to pollen, to the sex of a flower forty paces off and three spirals down. They smell direction. They can tell what a knot invites by the way its resins exhale.

They dress in bast and hypha, in cloth that feeds small. A belt might be a living strap that thickens where worn and thins where it needs to bend, oiled nightly by the owner’s sweat pressed back into it. Their shoes, when they wear them, are half-plants: placid leather that will not let itself be eaten if it tastes the owner’s salt. A good tool is a companion animal; it is named and returned at dusk to a cradle vine that knows its edges. Even a knife has a keeper plant. Even a hook has a roost it grows to like.

They talk—which is to say, they arrange means by which another knows what they mean—but not as much with air. Their voice is a soft and carrying thing they use seldom like fire, because loud sound, here, is a weather and will shut the day. They make sentences in smells, little skeins of crushed leaf and old smoke held in beads on their skin and let loose on the pulse so that the wind will carry it only as far as they intend. Hands are fluent; they speak with pressure and with pace: a palm settled on the grain says wait and another palm after a heartbeat says now. They do use words for names when the names are too heavy to be carried by scent alone—names of storms with syllables hung with history, names of the spans their grandmothers convinced to be spans and the seeds they have sworn not to cast until a certain kind of thunder comes.

Their homes are not houses but habits woven into place. You know a gardener’s family by the gloss of their runnels and the way their parapets angle into the wind. They inherit edges, corners of sky held for three generations running, with tools hung where the sap bled there long ago and learned that a shadow on that hook is a promise of work and so keeps the shape of sharpened bone alive a little longer. A cradle-plant grown to remember a certain sharpness is a dowry; a balcony that has learned to hold six sleepers and no more is a will. Children learn which veins take water and which refuse it by tasting with the inside of the wrist. The first mathematics they study are loops, the places growth will go again. The second are breaths.

They do not farm in the row-and-harrow sense. They husband desires. They sing ribs smooth where they try to ridge, because a ridge would tear the wind and make a storm hurt. They seduce leaves to turn a face a hair-width at a time. They are editors of inclination, gardeners of want. Their tools are small because their scale of action is perpetual: a wedge of ebony set for a season and revisited will write a paragraph of curve in a beam; a tendon of vine looped and tightened by a girl a hundred times before she is thirteen will persuade a balcony to grow as if it always loved to overhang that gap. There are specialties among them by feel: seed-sayer, tendon-wainer, rib-lawyer, draft-finder, memory-wife to a certain cathedral of pith whose whispers must be read to know if a path is about to return on itself for three months.

They keep calendars the way the place does—layered. The pollen-snow that comes like a procession is a holy day and a harvest, their fingers reefed with gauze so they can collect without clogging every crease of skin. The week when the deep mats breathe sweet is a time to breed and to plant arch-seeds, to invite animals inside the parapets and speak kindness to predators because at sweetness the whole world is hungry and any mistake will be carried for a lifetime. There is an hour before dawn in certain months when the gliders move like thrown cloth along a known vein of warming air; children are taught then to study silence as survival because the ribbon mouths hang in those hours like unlit signs.

They have laws, but most are plant-lore turned to ethic. Do not shout except to save a life. Return what you ask to last to a living thing every night. Walk a high ascent each morning of your growing years so that gravity will forgive you later. Do not cast a seed you did not learn from someone old enough to have forgotten a few storms. When you leave a place, leave it convinced it knows how to be itself without you. Of human crimes, the worst is a straight line without permission.

They bury no one. Nothing is buried. The dead are remembered by placement. A beloved body returned to a cradle that held their tools will make that cradle cleverer, more stubborn, a little warmer to the touch. Some families sleep in the heat their dead give back at night. The time it takes for bones to lose their shape is counted in seasons of polite grief; names are let go of at first fruit from the first span grown under patience learned from that person’s salt. If an enemy must be punished, it is not with removal but with banishment to lower wind, to plainer weather where the canopy’s memory is thinner and distances do not respect the old people’s breathing.

Not all people here are gardeners. There are Windwarders who live toward the places where the fabric sighs under load and fibers sing and you can feel stories in the thrumming. They are thin of limb and out of patience with straight-nerved folk; they love storms as teachers, tend kites of bladdered plant that ride the steady drafts and carry scents and sacks of nitrates home like tugboats. Leapers, sprung for distance, keep the far spans—bodies goosefleshed with callus on the hips, their patagia half-capes of woven leaf, their eyes lined with pollen-lashes to stop driftseed from blinding them. They court risk the way other people court husbands.

There are also the Old Bound—people who have promised themselves to a certain structure and will not leave it. A cathedral of pith is their monastery, a great arch their parish; they hear the quiver and know which leaf has failed an oath and must be soothed back into line. They are neither many nor revered exactly; it is understood that a person can be married to a beam as they could be to a person, and that both loves are jealous in their way. When a storm comes these are the ones who lie prone and sing into the grain so the millions of memorized responses the canopy knows will not tangle.

Among the animal folk, abundance is a dozen definitions.

Small and nearly weightless persons at the edge of animalhood—dew-mice, slick, with a back combed like a fountain’s lip, making tiny aqueducts of their own hairs all day to keep just drunk enough to taste and just cold enough to disappear to the nose—live in kitchens uninvited and are tolerated for the way they lick salt from sleeping skin without waking anyone. There are gliders, not one kind but a nation of forms: lens-winged augurs that fold and dive through lightwells to scare pollen-bugs into nets; squared sails with cartilage ribs which can be reefed in rough wind; tea-stained ghosts so light a shout would break their bones if shouts were permitted. They carry gossip between ribs. Some will let a child hold their leading edge; some carry old women if the upcurrent is generous.

Pith-herons are surgeons of air, necks like clock-springs, eyes nictitating a buttery blue. They keep their nests along light wells and poorly understand any world that is not a spiral. They punctuate the day with soft coughs that are not loud enough to be punished and sit like thought on the edges of drafts where the prey will come at last if you wait properly. Sump-bears are clumsy in tales and precise in life. Their fur drinks fog in a way that poets imitate in metaphor whenever they talk of devotions. Their bellies are sills; their mouths are not for killing but for pulling pith slowly and sighing. They are aunt-creatures; they let infants lie against their heat in hives of quilt-plant nights and lick the salt from a scraped knee in a ceremony more solemn than a wedding.

Ribbon predators are as cruel as a principle. They lay with a draft instead of a cushion, long slicknesses that figure a direction and take the tax of that path from whatever crosses. Their mouths are not mouths as you prefer mouths but hinges in long skin that open when the temperature of the air changes in a way it cannot change without meat passing there. The gardeners mark such places in smells as well as in memory; the places move like a rumor moves and it takes a life to keep up.

There are other eaters: little netmakers with silk so fine that it exists one hour a day and then draws itself back into a sac of patient protein; spathe-fish that swim only on the undersides of leaves where condensation gathers into sheets and can be mistaken for pond if you are not careful and step through a sky instead of onto it; blind things in the deep mats that are not worms but teeth arranged in a way that amounts to a creature. Everything is quiet because quiet is an organ here. Even the hungriest wear hush like a necessary skeleton.

Lesser lives are the place’s speech. Fungi write in threads along cuts, telling the history of a wound, the names of the insects who tasted it, the recipes of the ointments used and whether the cut meant well. Lichens feather over old pegholes like fingers closing; some glow where they are warm, cold-newts stalking their dim trails through labored nights. Microbes sex the mist; they give it flavor, make of a fog an invitation to breed or to sleep. On the very edges of breath, where air thinks about not being all one thing, are spores that change their weight at will—heavy with dew to fall into waiting mouths, then suddenly as thought to ride the draft the gardeners like best.

There are pests of course as anything made has pests: vein-drinkers that sour the path of water for the mischief of it; beetles with marbled eyes that roll resin into marbles and bury them to see where the water will go next. But a pest here is a social role. The gardeners do not imagine that eating another’s ease is wicked so much as unsustainable; they edit the cheats by removing the places a cheat can become a principle. To love this place is to be an optimizer.

And then there are visitors. Not often; falling is a moral event here and climbing up through clouds persuades different mortalities than those who were born to it tend to like. But sometimes something from the belowworld rides a thermal or drifts in a sack on a balloon of stitched gut and seed. They come with rock-dust as dowry, minerals like manna wrapped in silk. Gardeners hold such persons by the wrists and perfume their names with gratitude. The below-folk speak of mountains and sea and the idea of ground, which is a plane so consistent it makes citizens arrogant. The gardeners send them home with living knots that once tied will untie an anger and with a seed that will say to a dry hill, remember water. Stories of each other’s physics are the richest trade.

It is not a paradise. To slip between ribs is to be judged by gravity old-fashionedly. Children learn the feeling of nothing; they do not care for it. Things rot fast here if you do not convince them otherwise; a careless rope becomes a green smile and then becomes food. Yet it is home because home is where the rules are known and can be argued with.

The oldest inhabitant is time, braided with sap. A hand placed on a beam feels the thumbprint of that hand ten years ago pressed at the same point, and the wood warms because bodies have warmed it there for a long time. A span where a thousand feet went to war with weight to make it see stairs is a domesticated slope now; what was once a dare has become a hallway. Some elders no longer have names that are not also the names of beams. Some beams carry gossip about people decades dead and change their creak nothing at all because pride is a human thing and lignin is content to simply remember.

In that hush, intelligence is distributed. The plant’s capacity to recall pattern and to enforce it is a kind of mind. The gardeners’ craft of telling the living where to want to be is a matched mind. The animals’ lives of angle and draft are a third. They inhabit each other. They live. And the place, not cruel, not kind exactly, listens to its difficult heart and decides again and again to keep holding the sky up.
