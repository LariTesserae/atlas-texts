Mouths live here first. Not faces or voices—mouthed rooms, whole neighborhoods whose civic act is to take the place into themselves and pass it back cleaner, warmer, fractionally changed. Sponges are the first citizens you notice because they cannot be missed. They are the walls you press against, the tenements that breathe. Their skins are suede-thick and pocked with a million patient doors. Each pore is a job, each chamber a factory of almost-noise. Some are squat and many-chimneyed, others tall and fluted like organs half-melted by time. They have lived long enough to be called elders. If you could cut a cross-section of their age-rings, you would not see summers and winters but pulses—periods of richer drift, intervals of sulfur-breath, years when outside water pressed in stingier or more generous. They remember with their bodies.

Among them run the stitchers, the ones who make edges. Tube worms bind pillars together the way ivy turns masonry into continuous surface. Their calcareous sleeves knit lattice to buttress to vault, each white ring a syllable in a sentence evolution has been composing since vents were first invented. Some are delicate hair-fine and together become furred stone; others are broad and tumescent, flaring into little trumpets that face the draft to catch it. Brittleworms raise towers of their own cemented tubes and live in them as if in stacked apartments, polite until a collapse sends a district sloughing into powder and clicks. These collapses hurt no one in particular because everything nearby is built to receive fall as a season.

Where the worms lace and the sponges thicken, bacteria make banners. They are the white tassels you see in the warm corridors, the flags of chemoautotrophy, ribbony and soft as the inside of a cheek. Most of them do not know light and do not miss it; they live on dissolved stone and the bitter sweet of sulfur. They coat sills and hang in curtains under the thin, cooler leaks of sweet water, and in that brackish margin—those soft halocline rains—they grow many forms: beards, veils, rags, laceworks that quiver when a fish nose pushes through. These are the farmers and the farms at once, the monocultures the city tolerates because they manufacture density from gradients.

In their lee gather the sifters. Amphipods, isopods, small shrimps with translucent shells like nail parings crowd into hedges by the millions. Look long and you see how each holds its forelegs in the plume, combing with fringed fans, folding drift into neat boluses that pass along their assemblies. They are the quietest bustle in the city. Their sound is thought-like because it is constantly under the threshold of noticing until it isn’t. When they spook, their combined flick is a rumor turned shout. Where the heat-roads bend, their hedges do too, and there are old migration routes in their bodies—the same sectors fill and empty on the same pressure cycles, as if a civic holiday called something like lower tide mattered to them the way it would matter to shopkeepers.

Blind fishes ply the avenues the way old men follow the sun in towns they’ve never left. They are pale to the point of translucence, their heads dented where eyes ought to be by the thickening of sensory canals. Fins ribbed like ribbons splay from their sides to read the current, and their mouths are small and habitual. Fish like these are not adventurers, and they do not need to be; their neighborhoods are volumes rather than addresses. They bump their chins on limestone and learn a place’s whole shape by tasting the tiny chalk that comes off on their lips. They do not cross certain thresholds—you see them queue at a halocline and turn back as if shy of an invisible doorman. They keep the middle scale honest: too large to vanish into soup, too small to impose their own physics.

There are octopuses that have made something like culture without ever thinking that word. They live in holes that look like bad repairs in a wall. Each one has a garden outside it—a scatter of shells and pebbles and a few pretty odds-and-ends culled from the drift. No two gardens have precisely the same grammar. Some are painstakingly radial, some a rough pile in the corner where a neighbor cannot see, some obsessively smooth. Do they mean anything? Only that arrangement pleases. When an octopus moves, the water around it stops feeling like water and starts feeling like intention; then it winks out and reappears further along, whiteness turned to limestone-gray, arms draped until motion is required. A passing shrimp-hedge becomes a net when those arms lift. The inhabitants of the hedge go from busy citizens to food in a sentence fragment.

Predation here is less drama than punctuation. A rust-colored eel slides through doors as if rooms were made exactly eel-shaped, tasting, retreating, testing again. Its jaw, when it closes, makes a sound delicate enough to be mistaken for a shell settling. Its body makes the follow-up: an exclamation of muscle that writes itself through a side passage and is gone. Barracuda arrive rarely and empty plazas on arrival—long, sudden subtractions that cause nothing to crash but everything to rear back. Cuttlefish cruise in parabolas, valves beating with a scholar's calm; when they hunt, their skin mutters and then flares in brief logic designed to trick hairs and pores rather than eyes. You can sense their moods before you see them because the drift thickens or thins by a feather, and something with a brain has just decided.

The largest things are not always the most conspicuous. A ray the size of a blanket will fold along a ledge until it turns from animal into infrastructure—you could set your palm on its back and think it a rug. Then it slides and the architecture remembers that nothing here is fixed. There is, sometimes, the pass of a bulk you will not see again: a long muscle ahead of a long shadow, moving as if a map were printed inside it. The city makes such creatures a few at a time and then forgets their names. They are not myths so much as rarely used words.

Crabs walk like ambulatory districts. Some have grown stone on their backs, not as shells are grown but as façades are put up—a crust of coralline built on their corselets, pink gray logic turned into armor. When they rest, small worms shoot from between their plates to forage; to move is to take your tenants with you. They tug politely at sloughed sponge and keep rights-of-way from clogging. There are sea cucumbers that do the same for floors, their bodies like polished sausages rolling slowly across silt, eating every grain as they go. Between these two kinds of cleaners a street stays a street, not a drift.

Closer to the seep mouths you find the ones who wear white. Thermophilic shrimps whose bodies bristle with heat-loving bacteria look furred in wedding lace. They orient like hedgerows along the isotherms, exactly where heat is enough to speed an enzyme but not enough to cook a limb. Some line up facing upstream and shuffle in a piecework that looks—if you could pull very far back—as formal as marching. Between them stick out the fingers of clams grown long and shelving; they cup flow and take it between their plates the way a cathedral takes light between ribs. These clams never open fully. They are always ajar like important doors.

If there are plants, they are a scandal more than a category. Epiphytes drop in as rumors stuck to a seal’s fur or wedged in a fish’s gill. They find a place beneath a bacterial veil where a faint chemiluminescence bleeds from living banners and they cheat at green in the few photons available. They are small, pale, tend towards translucence, and look like the apparitions of weeds rather than weeds themselves. The city tolerates them because they take nothing anyone else wants. They are like exiles from stories of sun.

Children live here all the time, though you will not often recognize them as such. Larvae pass through in veils, glassier than the water they sublet, each a primitive engine: a ring of hair, a gut, a hope. They drift in layers—copepod snow, amphipod fog, an inch of fish glass. Sometimes the entire hall pelts upwards with released gametes and, for a day, everyone eats and no one cares whose they were. Embryos throb cold light as they divide, a metronome nobody owns. If there is a calendar, it is written in these pulses, stored in gonads and glands, and observed with better faith than paper could command.

Viruses are here too, of course. They do not build and they do not move except when moved, but they do the slow accountancy of genes and correct excess with plagues so small you could swim through a trillion and not feel a thing. They are not gods; they are auditors. The city accepts them because there is no choice and because boom without bust would be a fatal kindness.

Visitors arrive on what the city considers weather. When cooler seawater pushes in from another hole in the roof, the builders pull their pumping in a breath-length, the hedges roughen, fish hug walls as if to keep from being peeled away. An outsider seal ghosts through now and then, pale and sinuous as a rumor of upstairs. It brings a light you can taste rather than see, not lamp but brightness remembered in skin, and there is a beat of silence as bodies orient to a logic from another world. Silverfish streak in their wake at times—fast, faceted, loud with unfamiliar confidence. They leave as they came, and the city picks up its sentence where it was interrupted.

Some animals here act like systems. Comb jellies keep perfect silent escalators of rainbow, a feature nobody asked for and everybody accommodates. Ostracods blink flirting punctuations, green little periods drifting in lines that cross and recross like gossip. Worms in the walls wave ciliated fans in a synchronous flash mob that has no organizer and needs none. The net sum is order without a planner, a choreography of default desires.

Senses are architectures too, and whole clades are defined by the way they feel place. Some are chemists, taking the world a molecule at a time, reading a distance like a slow dissolving sweet. Others are listeners: their bodies brim with cilia and hairs without which they would not know up from food. The cuttlefish are map readers; they know the draft’s grammar and can cut across a paragraph to intercept a meaning. Octopuses are touchers who think with their hands and might never imagine a sky. Blind fish are cartographers of pressure. The eel is a geometer of holes.

Because everything that works here also fails here, there are specialists in ruin. Bacteria that like their oxygen lower turn black places to opportunity. Isopods with faces like old coins invade a fresh collapse and erode it into habitability. There are pale anemones that appear only where sulfur has scorched recent stone, their arms tentative, their mouths greedy. They are the pioneers of disasters that are not disasters but changes.

At the underside of the ceiling, where warm water leaves through oculi toward some upper void, a constant clearing makes rooms for meetings. No one holds office there; the plaza is defined by the way nothing hangs. Predators love it, and for that reason it becomes a place of etiquette. Rays pause there and wait a breath before crossing, as if to honor a right-of-way; barracuda, when they do pass, take the main line and do not linger to cause panic in a market. If laws exist in this city, they are recorded not in behavior but in outcomes: harms that cannot be undone do not persist because bodies that commit them do not.

What passes for governance is made of balancing appetites. The vents feed; the sponges pump; shrimps comb; scavengers clean; predators keep the combers from making dams with their own abundance. Haloclines keep peace better than teeth by being borders no one can want to ignore. Depth is a kind of treaty. Time—arranged by pressure, ticked by embryo-flare—tells the city when to flood rooms with seed and when to hold still. When outside tide presses, everyone quiets their pumping a fraction as if to keep from making noise you could hear in a lullaby; when it slackens, they breathe open a touch more. It is not government. It is compatibility curated by physics.

If you stayed a very long time, you would learn names that are not names: the Weaver, for a lineage of worms that lace ledges into ledges; the Bellows, for the dynasty of sponges whose chambers mortals have moved through since before the word dynasty was invented; the Harrow, for a crab that drags a fan of tiny rakes behind it and grooms silt into new terraces; the Lantern-Children, for the night when embryos make cool stars under a roof of stone. You would come to recognize certain families by the way they carry a line of bright symbiont across a hip, or an old scar along a ledge. You would know the difference between the sigh of a collapse and the hush of a cold push. Most of all, you would be forgiven for thinking the city itself a creature—not metaphorically but literally—its components moving food and heat and waste as organs do, listening with its pores, remembering with its skeleton.

So who lives here? Builders who do not know they build. Farmers whose bodies are fields. Cleaners with ten thousand legs. Predators so quiet you feel them only as absence. Customers of gradients. Curators of flow. Things that read pressure like scripture and taste limestone like rain. An old cohort of elders grown as walls. A nation of plankton passing through like pilgrims who never stop. Ghost-plants. Auditors no one sees. Visitors bearing stories of light and leaving before anyone can be converted. All of them obeying the kinds of laws that do not need writing: you do not block the draft; you do not dam the warm; you do not try to take a plaza from a fish that has earned it; you eat and are eaten in such a way that streets stay open and forums stay clear.

And beneath the nouns, one more inhabitant, the least body-like and the most enduring: the habit of the place to turn what comes into what can remain. Warmth itself lives here. So does patience. So does the slow fire of stone. They hold citizenship more securely than anything with a mouth, and everything with a mouth lives by their residency permits. If you press your lips to a sponge-wall—if you could—you would feel them in the tremor of its pumping, laws written in vibration. If you paused at a plaza’s edge with a ray hovering in the opposite alcove, you would both understand without understanding that a crossing is a kind of pledge. Everyone here keeps it. No one needs to explain why.
