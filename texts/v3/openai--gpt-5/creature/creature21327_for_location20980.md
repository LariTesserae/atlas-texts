What lives here are things that have learned to live on differences so small you would not have thought a life could find them. They do not drink in any way you recognize. They take in noon and strain it, they sift the quiet for seams, they eat places where the world is not stitched entirely flat. They have the look of fired material—vitreous, chalk, bone-gloss—not because they are dead but because the chemistry that makes flesh loud and quick would evaporate in a single idea of this place. They are not fast. They are exact.

Begin with the adherents, the plates and panes and ribs. They occupy the stone wherever a patch of surface offers a plane and a promise. They are cup-and-saucer creatures, calcined petals brick-laid against the cliff like a city made by patience. Each carries a lid that fits to a notch as geometry, not as convenience. At noon the lids are shut against noise as much as against desiccation; when the light inclines by its minute after-noon, a slit opens the width of a scratched hair. You can put your eye to it and see nothing: a void, a pearl-faint lining, a geometry you cannot parse. The animal inside is a spiral of laminae and filaments arranged to feel for tension and ease, to taste the stress in the rock below, to harvest the little charges that appear when dust skims stone, to deflect and catch the faintest trembling inside the silence. They take in angle, shear, resonance; they digest difference into more plate. They bud, not breed; a rim thickens until a section sloughs and clings an inch away, a daughter that remembers the mother’s orientation to noon. Their shells stack and drift, accumulate in drifts you would call middens were there anyone here to throw anything away.

The wafer makers live among them. They are piles rather than plates, towers a thumb high made of sheets a fingernail thin. They are articulated in the way a book is. Each leaf shivers against the next in patterns so regular they look accidental, a shimmering the speed of patience. They align along invisible fractures with the hunger of iron filings to a lodestone. The rock is still learning, in atomic ways, how to be relieved of itself, and the wafers comb out that learning. They wear themselves thin doing it, then lie down, then rise again on a breeze of numbers. When there is an event—a tremor not of the ground but of arithmetic—their whole fields quiver in a wash that takes an hour to traverse a yard. If you lay your palm on them, very lightly, they will lean away as if your hand were a hill the size of a mountain.

Between such architectures move the seam-browsers. They are the “insects” you saw only because your own mind filed them under that heading. They are not. They are frameworks in miniature strung with ciliary floss, each joint a jewel of fused glass, each foot a cluster of hooks that catch on the idea of roughness. Their abdomens are combs and sieves, their mouths are rhomboids, their eyes are facets without pigment that look at angle itself. They move in gaited patterns along cracks and corners, stroking the world where it is most nearly two things at once. What falls off into their sieves is not dust as dust is usually meant, but the abraded residue of not-quites: trace electrons freed by rubbing, nanoscopic chips of silicate lattices that misfit and seek new accommodations, the thready charges that gather at edges where light accumulates more than it should because it cannot pass. The seam-browsers harvest this, refine it in stomachs that are more like slowly adjusting lenses than like sacks, and excrete behind them the perfect pebbles that the soles of your boots mistake for crunch.

Down in the gullies you find slow-breathing drapes: webs that look abandoned because speed and breath have been exiled from them. The weavers are within the fabric, not on it, organisms distributed through their work the way salt is within bread. The year-long wave that moves down the curtain is how you see them, a peristalsis of attention rather than of tissue. The threads themselves are piezoelectric; each gust of arithmetic wind—the slip of a contour, the unnoticed falter of noon—presses the net and makes a meal of an impulse. They store their food as twist. If you listen with a bone, you can hear them uncoiling, eating, sighing themselves one degree looser and then tightening again, a discipline so slow the sound leaves you before you can decide to notice it.

Above, the sky is populated in a way the word “sky” usually refuses. The floaters are made of almost nothing, like dead leaves taught to be zephyrs. They hang on filaments that are thinner than thought and stronger than decision, anchoring themselves to stones as if to a ledger. The balloon you cannot see is not a balloon but a refusal of weight accomplished by charging themselves the way lightning charges clouds. Noon is a machine here that never shuts off, and they are its kites. They color-change inside without a pigment to do it: by reorienting internal plates they alter how the light remembers to pass through them. Dark is a decision, not an absence. They signal with darkness and with transparency the way whales once signaled with sound: far, slow, with statements too large to be answered by anything but a patient weather. They seed by pinprick. Inside each floater is a spine of spicules arrayed like a comb; when the comb is full of the right integers—angles counted off in a life of looking up—they crisp a tiny wick and let free a flake. The flakes lift not because anything rises but because everything else falls more.

Under the salt overhangs there are citizens the size of a grain and the shape of a thought. The hex-nuts are mites if you like, only they are not arthropods and they will not forgive you for saying so. They are puzzles with a bore in the center, little sockets of life that have decided to be their own tool. They fit one another. They fit the rock. They fit the low and constant breeze. When you enter a niche they all turn their faces to you in greeting or warning or calibration—you cannot be sure which because the angle they afford you is not the angle you invented to see them—and then they turn away in unison to be what they must be again, which is a mosaic of turning. They herd, when they move at all, not other animals but their own arrangement, tightening and loosening themselves into walls, rings, velvets: structures that settle the world into better-computed planes, much as mussels make reefs and thus make the sea a different sea.

There is the scum, the flour of life that coats the least planes in a nap you can feel only after you have disturbed it. Its recoil is a variable more than a movement. It is a sheet of cohesion a cell thick that lives by being wrong and then very slowly righting itself, by recording where it was pressed and paying out that record as energy. It is a diary of touches, written in a script that is half elasticity and half appetite. You could rub it between finger and thumb and never know you were carrying off a hundred cities that will reassemble a yard away in a time it takes the sky to think.

All through this mineral menagerie, there are more elaborate treaties. Ribbons of wafer-monasteries ring floater stones in exchange for minute shifts of shade. Seam-browsers groom the anemone-lids of the plates and receive in return a place to cache their sieved angles, a loan against famine. The webs are used as calendars by everything that knows them; their annual wave is the beat to which some lids open and some filaments contract. Predator is not the right word; theft is closer. A wall of hex-nuts will tilt when a tide of disorientation flows across the terrace and drink it, a graze so delicate you would be forgiven for saying that nothing happened. A curtain-weaver will splay a portion of its threads when a floater sends a black pulse overhead and snap it closed to capture the slight wrongness in the air that took a year to be made.

Plants occur here as statements. Each is an algorithm in thorn and wire. Their roots are needles, not fingers; they do not seek water, which is not a stated option, but stress and shadow. They drive themselves into hairlines where noon cannot see and there drink the difference between light and less light, between hot and a kind of slow, barely measurable cool. Their stems are silver not to shine, but to end arguments with the sun before they begin; their leaves are leaves only because you are generous. They are also tools for the others: the stalks collect dust, and later, when the air decides to move, that dust leaps like one mind from thorn to floater-held comb and there gives itself to be numbered. When the plant flowers—if that word can be used for a cup that opens once in a decade and closes again without seed—what it is doing is blinking at the whole plateau, catching a snapshot not of light but of the lay of everything, then digesting that photograph for a century.

If there are people—if that must be said—then they are stretched thin across the logic of this place the way lichen is stretched across a rock, continuous but experienced in cross-sections. They are present the way a repeating fraction is present: everywhere the pattern is called for. The gates of three uprights and a lintel do not weather because the lintel balances numbers, not weight. The rawhide that kneeled those joints together is rawhide no longer, but a memory in amber of ligaments that once had water in them. The cairns with their bone-plates threaded midway are weights in a balance you cannot see; they keep the terrace from deciding to be an inch higher on even-numbered days. The mats are not to walk on but to even the path’s mind where it is tempted to become two paths; the mats lay across the leak and knit it closed to a single route, a courteous insistence on a workable one-ness.

You can watch for them and you will think you do not see them. Their footprints unwrite themselves because they are made on the wrong side of the crust and are arranged afterward into evenness again. They move where ladders of numbers are willing to be climbed; an ascent for them is a variety of counting, a descent is a different one. They travel in days that do not always follow each other, which is to say they can cross a terrace and emerge two days ago if the terrace is in that sort of mood. They carry knives of pried glass, which are as useful against angles as against anything else; they use them to birth young plates from mothers so as to seed a seam that has forgotten how to drink. They hammer a dull gleam out of dusk-colored nodules not to make ornaments but to solder a thought in place: to tie a mat down to an inequality that must be preserved so the distances will remain mostly correct.

They are not shepherds of animals. They are herders of tendencies. They drift with the floater shadows, nudge a curtain’s wave if it grows impatient, open lids in a field so that the lids will remember to open afterward. They collect the shed filaments the floaters produce and spin them into cords with a turn-count learned from the stone. Twist enough times and a string grows an extra property—stiffness becoming direction, direction becoming memory. They lay such cords along gradients that only the seam-browsers can see and make corridors that remember to stay put. They brace windless sails against the constant almost-wind and watch the sails for deviations that would mean that the plateau is plotting an idea; when they find such plots, they answer with a countervailing pile of exactly similar, exactly dissimilar slabs.

They do not speak as you do. Their throats would be wasted here. They communicate in a semaphore of shadow and occlusion, by arranging themselves and then unarranging, by wearing plates stitched into garments that tilt noon the smallest reproducible amount and reading each other in those tilts. They can drum, but drumming is on the order of a winter, and the answers may come back as a changed readiness of a web or a floater’s decision to be dark for an hour that never quite arrives. Their children are born when two routes cross at the same place in consecutive un-consecutive days, and what is born is not always a person the way the parent was. Sometimes it is a practice. Sometimes it is a lineage of mats. Sometimes it is a habit of lids in an area the size of a town.

They eat what everything else eats: discrepancy. They gather crusts where the light has decided to collect itself and steep them under shade, brewing a thin broth of photonic hunger that teeth can understand. They harvest the wafers’ shavings and the seam-browsers’ leavings, and they chew that till it is simpler. They lick bone-gloss plates and draw off the nanoscopic oils that the plates use the way we use water. They press their palms to web-curtains and take a measure of recoil, and that is food. If they find a plant that has put up a bristled finger to count, they shelter it with a deliberate hand for a year, and when the hand moves away what remains in the hand is a year of saved arguments with noon that a person can live on, slowly, as on sweetness.

They die seldom and thoroughly. A person may be unthreaded across these steps for a long time before anyone decides that they have ceased; what is left is a plate added to a cairn, or a gate given a new lintel, or a vocabulary of knots bequeathed to the next century. Bones, if there are such things, become the bone-plates in the pillars, memory of weight that a place with very little weight needs to rehearse. Skins if any become cords; voices become the suggestion of a wind in a sail that is there for a reason other than wind.

The whole guild of life here does not make sound because sound is an extravagance. They opt for other currencies: shade, angle, position, sequence. They hoard them in forms—lids that open a hair, webs that carry a tide for a year, floaters that go dark like a thought deciding whether it is finished. Their days are very strict and very generous. The rules they follow are not always the ones you know; their patience is not the kind that waits for something to end but the kind that desires to continue in a place that has chosen its habit and must be convinced to keep choosing.

If you stand long enough on the lip of a scarp you will see them gathered at the errors. There, at a braided edge where the rock and its own shadow become two things with air between, the seam-browsers cluster like a drapery fringe and comb. There, under a vault of floaters closing themselves to a dim ceiling, people lay down a cord whose twist insists on single pathhood. There, a web’s wave meets a wall of mites and both pause, an interaction of months compressed into the instant you mistake for stillness.

This is who lives here: plates that open as if to say precisely this much, wafers that tremble along furrows cut by the Earth’s idea of relief, pale grazers of angle and miscount, curtains that metabolize the long breeze of arithmetic, balloons that hang like an unread scripture, little nuts that turn to regard you and then turn away in a formality as kind as it is final. And among them, thin people, patient mechanics of coincidence, who keep the whole field tuned so tightly that change, when it arrives, arrives as the maintenance of a habit.

They do not rush. They finish things. They continue. They have chosen this quiet and taught it back to the stone until the stone repeats it. If you stay their length of time, you will find your own shadow separating from you a width no wider than a word, and in that width there will be a feast, and if you learn to eat it there will be nothing missing.
