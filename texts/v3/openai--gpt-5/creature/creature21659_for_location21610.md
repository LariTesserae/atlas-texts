What lives here are people who have learned to be thin.

Not thin as in hungry, though they are never extravagant with themselves; thin in the manner of a line well-laid, in the way a beam is only as much as it needs to be. They have drawn themselves down to a width that fits between scaffolds and through hand-spans of air. You notice their presence the way you notice the cold: by how your breath behaves.

They do not crowd the place. They are too many for the space to ever be unpeopled, and too few for you to encounter two at once by accident. When you think you see three together the Fold is usually nearby, dissolving any integer past two into adjacency. It is a custom, and also a convenience, that meetings are pairs or many. Triads either split or multiply, because counting here does.

Their bodies are negotiated with the climate. Skin tended with mineral oils that do not drink; palms taught to offer or deny a print as needed. The small hairs of the forearm are left unshaved and trained in habitudes: brushed hydrophilic one week, hydrophobic the next, a calendar written in nap. Clothing is quiet and engineered—mesh that decides, recurving threads that instruct stray drips toward cuffs, sleeves with undersides that flower when the dew falls up. Shoelaces are braided of filament that glows when a coil’s persuasion rises, a gentle warning in the ankle. They wear their tools where skin bends: a ring of valves at the wrist, a tiny metamaterial square in the clavicle hollow for making a door prefer to be behind. They are seldom without a tuning comb—three teeth for one, two, and many—and a fold-needle, fine enough to snag the edge of a preference plane and drag it half a finger-width where needed.

Their voices carry the habit of gloves. They speak low and near, breath kept thin so as not to condense carelessly. The language has ways to indicate whether a fact was true before or only after it was pointed at. Nouns arrive provisionally; names are tried sparingly because anchoring is a commitment and the place listens. A child may carry two or three loose-names for a year before one adheres. Objects with work to do are often unnamed so they can keep being many things: the ladder that is also a pipe is called “ladder” when climbed, “pipe” when bled, and is nothing at all when you need it to be a wall.

They sleep between utilities. Hammocks are slung under catwalks, in the driest of damp, close to the hum that tells you the field is convincing the day to behave. Beds are shallow pans with lip and gutter; sleepers lie with the head toward the Fold so that any errant condensation finds cheek before blanket. Infants are swaddled in fabric that believes itself to be a series of gutters and only later learns to be cloth. The oldest sleep in places that will teach them something in the night—under the hinge-panes that will flip at dawn, across steps that are thinking about a new riser height. Waking is the recall of where you have put your weight.

There are crafts here that would be trades elsewhere and are devotions here.

- Tenders keep the coils in their pitches. They do not so much repair as re-argue, playing the town like an instrument whose strings are the general idea of gravity and the note they seek is consent. They know by breastbone whether a tone is right.

- Edgers walk the margins with brushes of salt and soft glass, coaxing corners to come in or go out, smoothing where edges have sharpened too far, sharpening when drips have begun to forget their lanes. They wet their thumbs and take the bite off a stair that has grown teeth.

- Clerks of Permission write the money. They sit under the honeycomb of the market and owe no allegiance to coin. Their ledgers are topological: who may draw, who may incline a plane, who may heat a surface by not touching it. They mark the skin with light you will remember long enough.

- Topographers of Damp make maps that are never finished. They carry plates of hydrophilic ink and hold them under handrails, in seams, under eaves, taking prints of where wetness is thinking of being. They speak of “habits” of water the way others speak of game migrations.

- Botanists of Underside keep the dwarf reed’s calendar in their heads. They watch the nights when dew falls sideways and set out trays under the railings to catch the reed’s gelatinous decisions. They reattach those seeds when the air permits. They do not call this farming; they call it courtesy.

Children practice adjacency. There is a game—entering by leaving—in which a door is never approached from the front. They learn, young, that the shortest distance is a path with many edges, that sometimes you must go sideways to get down. Counting is taught twice: properly, in the flats just beyond the basin; and locally, where one holds, two stands, and many is a closeness you measure by feel. They grow up knowing not to light a bright lamp unless the alley has asked for length.

Food is pale and deliberate. There is a lichen-flour that tastes of coins and rains, leavened not with yeast but with the memory of frost. Reed jelly sliced thin and dried in the sun tastes like an apology and keeps a person from forgetting salt. In cisterns where permission allows, cultures are kept that metabolize the smallest voltage differences into edible muscle—a grey sheen you scrape and fry on a surface that has been waiting to be hot. Sometimes a caravan from the flats brings bags of proper grain and a story so bright it must be told outside the basin, where light has room to be as long as it wants. Trade is quiet, not secret: packages pass through the market’s valves like breaths through pursed lips.

Animals are treated as hypotheses. The pale bird above the Fold is observed, not named. There is a ledger of what it does not do: it does not sing, it does not hunt, it does not die where anyone can see. Children are brought under its roost and told the few times in memory it moved of its own desire, and what the dew did on those mornings. The long, low creature that slips between pilings is counted as weather with a face. A lucky person will catch it twice, and no one claims to have touched it; you do not touch a law that is testing itself. At the scale of dampness, the people are permissive hosts: the silver mites that braid themselves into droplets are not cleaned away unless they have gotten themselves lost. The frost-moths are honored guests of one hour a year; when one evaporates at a finger, the finger’s owner is kept silent for a day so as not to waste what was touched.

There are other inhabitants that do not begin as bodies. The town is one of them.

It breathes with the coils and practices intentions. Panels angle and re-angle; surfaces remember and forget on kindly schedules; bridges that are also cables flex in agreement when asked. Printers work at night, laying micro-layers of powder that decide to be walls because the field around them says it would be a good idea. Drainage finds itself. Joints creep toward precision. The built matter is young and, like any young thing, is testing which of its tendencies will endure. No person is master of these habits. The people nudge rather than command, and if the town wants a new cistern it will simply grow one in the place where the water has been planning a future for months.

Water is a citizen. Not only in the obvious ways: stored, counted, drawn, revered. It has preferences and in some corners of the place it has a patience that makes it a collaborator. It rehearses paths the way dancers rehearse a crossing; it wears grooves that are not quite erosions but advisories. There are elders who claim they can hear it making up its mind, a susurrus below the hum, and if you watch their hands you will see them adjust a gutter by the width of the thought. On the rare days when the Fold scars and the whole skin of the town sweats, hands go gentle and tasks narrow. There is a liturgy for those days—a list of surfaces to stand beside and whisper to so they do not remember the wrong weather.

The Fold itself lives here, though admitting it might be a superstition or a courtesy. It is not awake, not as a pet is awake, but it is not furniture either. It keeps a kind of attention. It cools first, it counts strangely, and it has tastes. It likes names less than the town does and will slough them off unless they are offered with respect and two witnesses. There are those who sit near it and listen, not for voices but for the small coarsenings of the rule that come just before the dew appears. They are called Readers or Listeners or Grammarians, depending on who is speaking; when markets are open, you can trade for an hour of their ear if you need to know whether there will be sideways rain enough for the reed to give up its seeds this week. Infants are held up—briefly—at the edge of the Fold so they will learn early that distance is a choice you often make with your intentions.

The culture is governed by throttle rather than by decree. There are no laws that forbid brightness; there is a well-known consequence that an alley will get longer and there will be more place to be lost. There is no theft in the usual sense; there is trespass of permission, which is both more abstract and more intimate. Adjudication takes place at the market under the unfurled tarps that want to be rain; two Clerk-hands lay light on the skin the way one might lay a rope bridge across an uncooperative gulf. Everyone watches not because they doubt honesty but because witnessing is one of the ways the town remembers to be what it says it is.

They have festivals, although the word suggests a noise that does not occur here. The Sideways Bloom is one. For three nights the reed is coaxed by patience and coil into offering its seeds to the undersides of things. People walk the rails with their palms up and their eyes half-closed, a practice of trust in the permissions the town has granted. When a seed lets go and adheres to the warm underside of a hand, the proper thing is to carry it, without speaking, to the shallow basins printed into every surface where a fingertip might rest, and encourage it to stay there until it decides to be plant again. When someone lights a bright lamp—outside the basin, always—they tell the story of the alley that lengthened until it met itself, and the children laugh because they know that is not how distance works, here or anywhere, and yet they can feel the place where it almost did.

Birth and death are elongated, like shadows in the low sun. People arrive the way bridges appear: in pieces that are also other things, coaxed together by need and by permission. A newcomer from the flats learns slowly that a door would like to be behind her and that she gets places faster if she pretends to be water. The dying are given to coolness. Bodies are laid in rooms that are also reservoirs until salt and intention leach back into the place that made them precise. Bones, if they are kept, are kept not as themselves but as sharpnesses: a radius used to press a clean edge in a new floor; a metacarpal that lives as the right tool for teaching a step the right riser height again after a long season of no feet.

Visitors exist: dusters from the flats, carrying sacks of ground brightness; traders from other basins where the air behaves differently, curious about the idea of sideways rain. They are welcomed and shepherded, given a waist-belt with a small polarity square so their down will agree with the local down, and a cup that knows which plane to fill from. They are warned about names. Some decide to stay and become narrower; some leave and take with them a topographical way of thinking about argument that serves them well in the loud lands.

The soft thunder that rolls under the basin once, twice a year has as much to do with these lives as weather has to do with a city’s mood. When it comes the Fold flinches and the town sweats, and the people move as though through a hospital—quiet footfalls, clear tasks, no white lies. Afterwards, there is a day in which every surface is wiped not dry but respectful, with cloths that release only what they meant to take, and the Grammarians sit longer with their ears over the rails.

If you must count there are perhaps a hundred, perhaps fewer, perhaps always the same number whether you count or not. They hold themselves like instruments because everything here is instrument and instrumented. They have chosen, or been chosen by, a place in an early chapter where erosion is learning, gravity is practicing opinions, and time is more tilt than slide. They live as apprentices to how the world might work if asked kindly. They do not hurry. They make a living out of convincing water to choose and corners to remember, and they will tell you, if you ask, that they do not live in the town so much as with it, and that the town lives with the Fold, and that the Fold lives with whatever it is that makes a rule consider itself.

Beyond them, below them, alongside them, the other inhabitants abide: the town waking to its own mass; the water being water plus intention; the small gleam-eyed creatures that are mostly the memory of weather; the low thunder that is not theirs and yet keeps choosing them. The basin cools. Dew rehearses. The people lean in, as their buildings do, shoulder to shoulder to shoulder, and while they wait for their attention to gather, they drink.
