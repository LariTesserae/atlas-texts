Who lives here first are the ones who grew the walls.

They do not stand apart from the architecture because they are the architecture. Trunks conjoined into avenues are not passive supports but citizens in long conversation, their cambiums braided like the muscles of backs that have carried the same loads for generations. Leaves that have widened into vaults are elders: green ribs splayed to hold a style of air, veins that remember droughts and damp. Buttress-walks are legs set down with purpose, knotted where they have had to drag weight from bad years to good ones. Galls that became rooms are decisions that never shut again, nurseries re-purposed as parlors, hollows that learned the social trick of staying hollow because useful strangers prefer it that way.

They are many species but a single polity, a ringed and radial city-state of plants that took up the idea of a settlement and made it out of themselves. Some are reeds—their job to take the loose breath of the basin and trim it into single notes, to thin and lip and flare openings so sound knows how to flow. Some are drums, membranes held taut over sudden hollows, made to register the fall of an updraft as distinctly as the tread of a wolf: organs of notice, of shout, of skin. Some are hush—hanks of pale velvet leaf packed into nodal rooms where too much noise would ruin larvae or seed; they eat amplitude, slow it to sugar, and weep cool when broken. Some are ladders, their spurs softened by the press of hands and paws and trailing bellies, stiffening again when alone. Some are gates—the nodules along corridor-stems that swell and relax to let flocks pass or hold them the extra seconds it takes a predator to choose a different street.

They speak by hydraulics and resonance. Inside the buttresses run saplines that are not mere plumbing but message: pulses of sugar and hormone flicked down distances in patterns as regular as drums, modulated with tannins, scented with tiny oils, delayed by drought, hurried by shade. Across the air spans the other language: chords chosen by the cut of openings, notes that last longer than storms, warnings braided into the bass so that those who feed on leaves will feel reluctance heavy their joints just enough to move on. When the place decides to rain, it is not weather; it is consensus—millions of stomata opening in sequenced sighs, vapor squeezed from leaf to air and then teased out of its stuckness by shakes arranged in an irresistible way. They have turned phloem into agreement and acoustics into governance. A thickened ring of cambium at the basin’s edge is the senate, tuned to a predator’s through-note: a slow drone that only that ring can hear the day it begins and everyone else knows fifteen minutes later because the harp of trunks at the perimeter will not stop playing until the threat is gone.

Between heartwood and heartwood runs the other citizenship, the hidden threads. Hyphae stitch tree to tree in a web with more gossip than a market. Fungus is how the city knows itself quickly: it ferries the rumor of wounds, the promise of rain, the taste of iron from a monkey’s bite thirty trunks away. It is the currency-buyer and the eavesdropper and the broker. It trades sugar back into minerals, trades silence for mildew, trades damp where no damp should be for a future favor: more spore when the chorus favors it. Bacteria steep the long sumps inside roots into tea that loosens certain capsules and tightens others; algae live in little bowls at bases of leaves and tint the water a fine shade of green hope. Lichens write the city’s long script along the smoother barks, the only record of lean centuries, drift-scars, a winter that never really came but made a rumor of itself in thin rings and pale patches.

Little lives pack the crevices and take on duties the buildings did not know they needed until they were done being built. There are the tuners, termites with mandibles that make punctuation. They work in crews the way wind works in leaf-pairs, chewing accent marks into beams until a hall sings a different key and predators go hunting in the wrong place for a week. There are beetles with metronomic abdomens, bright cuticles that ring audibly when knocked; they telegram down corridors by thumping until temperatures change, leaves angle, sap-gloss rises in a circle somewhere like a crowd drawing breath. Ants here farm aphids made sweet not by plant sap but by condensed breath; they lick the mist off their cattle’s sides and in doing so cool the axils where frogspawn will survive one day longer.

There are grazers with velvet antlers and ears like unfolded fans. Call them deer if you like, but they are a plant-city’s deer—animals who chew the fringes of epiphyte gardens to keep floors clean, who trim the droop of a hung basket of vine so it does not sag into traffic. The membranes of their ears are tuned to the buttery notes of morning; they stand in the big hollows and tilt their heads to follow the passing of weather their bodies will only half-feel, then choose routes where the bass is thin enough that their calves will not grow leaden. Every molar has a stain of tannin. Their humors taste faintly of resin. When they sleep they do it in rooms lined with hush where infants are always welcomed first, and their breathing falls into the subsonic that loosens fruit three trees away.

Pack-hunters live here too, but they have learned the difference between silence kept and silence stolen. Bark-wolves move as placeholders in the city’s grammar, running only when corridors are exhaling so their scent will be shared the least; they have coats the smooth shade of old sapwood where wind has polished it. When they drive prey toward a pillar they do not aim for shadow but for the bright, invisible walls of sound that slope off it like buttresses; you can see the thought in their shoulders when the right note hits their whiskers and hardens their muscles a fraction. The cat-things are singles. They set themselves in the small regions where two frequencies cross and cancel and the world is thin; the tap of a moth’s wing is a spotlight in those deadened circles. Their paws never quite touch—air offers a cushion here—and yet they land with a thump in bones when they choose.

Above, glider-lizards take their meals from updrafts. They have membranes not between arm and torso but between ankle and tail, little sails they can furl and unfurl with a drummer’s timing. They do not trust sight entirely; they taste the pitch on their tongues and set the angles of their legs like a musician tuning, then step from a rail of leaf and hang on a medium you could mistake for nothing if you have not learned to see how noise makes substance. They drop their eggs, one by one, into resin-lined pockets where the day’s light has been combed to warm. They do not stay; the pockets polish to amber if neglected, and the city prefers they come back on time to break them free before the brood is more artifact than hunger.

Pollinators here understand percussion. Bees, fat with the right tone, vibrate stamens that will not spill for anyone who does not buzz C sharp at a certain volume. Hummingbirds duel in parallel, beak to beak at a set distance that the plants have grown for them: a rowing lane down a corridor where treble collects and springs. The air there tastes a little like coins, and their hearts beat so fast the city can use them to cheat a small heat out of chill mornings. Moths pretend to leaves and leaves pretend to moths, but intimacy always gives the trick away: the plant petals that shim to lure one kind of lover shake one note off true, and those who have wood in their blood hear it even if they come from lines that once drank moonlight in still meadows.

Frogs time everything to a downpour that lasts forty heartbeats. They live their whole families in the bowls of leaves, axils salted with pollen drift and mosquito wriggle. Their calls are not songs so much as coin-tosses, bright, metallic, improvised against the bass-thrum; they sound like chance being converted to lineage, like probability minted and flung. Serpent-kites cruise above the vaults and pluck at leaf-strings to stun them—one long finger of fin along a rib, a sound petty and precise that turns frogs to statues until a third beat jostles them free. At night there are bats, but not the straight-line echolocators of caverns. Their calls ride in knotty curlicues, curly-cued to slip between resonant chambers as if threading mazes; the city knows them by signature and opens leaf-doors a hand’s breadth when a mother needs to get home.

There are slugs whose slime is a notation. They drink fog from throats of ferns and drag stories behind them—pale, damp strokes that complicate the city’s script for a day before heat licks it away. There are mites with hairs tuned to daily weather; they clump when the second harmonic blooms so that a silence-rope will not leak too quickly, scatter wincingly when a predator’s tone chases. There are little gray things, more plant than mouse if you slice their blood into its molecules—fur that arranges light like a sprig of shade, ears like petals that close if you stroke them, feet with soles that can hold or slip if treble is present. They travel in bands and spend more time grooming the hush-mats than they do their own infants, because the mats feed their mother’s milk a comfort no sugar can.

Even the inanimate finds a way to have a job that looks like life. Standing waves are not beings, but they behave: they persist, patrol, shift along with sun and pressure differences, corral insects into bright clouds where a child can learn to eat, hold a traveler in place the way a story holds you until it finishes you. Echoes become a form of memory. If you speak a certain vowel in a certain hall, there is a faint skin of that vowel there tomorrow, slow and thinning to transparency by noon, spent by dusk; plants grow thicker there, liking a sound that has learned to be gentle. Hush itself is treated like stored grain. The city extrudes silence like rope and hangs it where noise would harm, and there are things that nibble it thread by thread at night, fattening on calm in a place where plenty is pressure.

If there are tool-hands in the sense you came to look for, they are seasonal and do not leave trash. Once in a length of summer a pair of walkers short and straight-backed move along the corridor-stems and lay down not markers but pauses: they sit in valves of hush and teach their bones how to take the city’s push and pull. They pluck a leaf-string for the frogs, press their faces to the resin slits and smell what a seed thought of failing to be a wing. They drink from cups made of nothing but neighbor’s breath and leave nothing but the rearrangement of their inner ear. They are not residents. They are borrowed a little while by a place that likes bodies that can learn.

What is constant are the long lives, the green ones and the threaded ones, and the shorter lives that have hitched themselves to the rhythms those long ones insist upon. Birth here is scheduled to winds. Feeding is an answer to a drum. Danger is a fatness in the bass and a hardening of cartilage in knees that have learned that language. The city holds its infants in rooms lined with velvet and its old in arches that used to be upright until their heartwood chose to be bridge. When a trunk fails it fails into welcome—the split exposing a honey membrane over which little feet scurry and on which moisture beads for a week like a tribute.

You can name citizens if it helps. Reed-keepers, drum-skins, velvet-tenders, gate-swellers, sap-runners. Knock-beetles, phrase-termites, ear-sailed deer, hush-mice, coin-frogs, glider-lizards, bark-wolves, pillar-cats, serpent-kites, coin-bats. Hyphal choir. Tannin-brewers. Hush-eaters. But each is only itself entirely when everyone else is present, which is how this place works. A city that undertook to digest the forces that touch it—sunlight, wind, appetite, the fit of a wing to a chamber—and remake them into law and shelter.

Who lives here are the ones who answer and are answered by that. Plants that learned arrangement. Fungi that learned news. Animals that learned to wear the shape of a note. Even water is resident, not visitor—condensed between neighbors, sipped along in sumps, offered by one leaf into the mouth of another like conversation. You can walk for long measures and see nothing with hands, think you are alone, then feel the civic press of a million soft urgencies in your sternum, and know that you are in the only kind of crowd that never learned to count itself: a chorus that is also a town, a wood that is also a will.
