They are visible before they are noticed. Low, layered figures, moving with the same patient confidence as fog; silhouettes with rounded shoulders and wide belts, hair bound up in green that is not only hair. From far off, they could be rocks with intentions. Up close, they are a compendium of small exactnesses: breath taken in quiet sips; hands that are all knuckle and tendon, made for tying; eyes that keep one part of their attention on the ground’s intention and one part on what the air is planning.

They are not loud about being alive. The plateau has taught them the cost of motion and the price of heat, and they keep both accounts famously. Children learn to count not by numbers but by the order in which things release water: frost from a cord, dew from a thread, a pool from its edge. Names are tied at first—literally, in a waist-cord with five loops—and only spoken when the air is easy and someone has a spare syllable.

They call themselves many things, most of them verbs. Tenders. Setters. Ringers. Those Who Persuade Stone. Those Who Hear the Fog Decide. Outlanders might be tempted to say “people,” but here even that word sits down and makes room for other possibilities. They are human in the sense that they have faces and walk on two feet and know what a bruise is, but their biology has been kneaded by altitude and winter and an intimacy with the green that lies low.

Their bodies are small in the way of precise tools. Ribs wide, hearts slow, the blood dark with iron wrested from black bowls of water that taste like a mouthful of keys. The thin air has taught them to make speech a careful thing. Much of their language is clothless: fingers signing with a vocabulary of knots; shoulders and chin and eyelid giving position to agreement and doubt; a pull on a shared line across a room that says I heard what you did there and yes. They do speak—plain, short words that don’t steam too hard in the cold, and songs made almost of one long held note—but they spend them as if they might have to buy a morning’s warmth with the change.

Their skins are a pale stone-color, not white but something you could chip tools from; wind-burnished, freckled with the iron spray of the wells. Hair does grow, but it is kept braided into living wraps that wick small water from the air and return it slowly to the scalp. Eyebrows are purpose-built—you can watch someone orient by the tilt of frost that gathers there, deciding which way to walk by which arch grows heavy first.

They dress in gardens. The wraps on arms and legs are felted layers of moss and lichen and woven fiber grown to order on cord-frames, always alive. The clothes eat light and exhale comfort, drink rime and pay it back as humidity in the shelter of a sleeve. A novice’s cloak is a small green square with ambition; an elder’s cloak is a landscape, thick with mats coaxed to different textures for different weathers. You can read a person’s experience in the seams of their moss, how they have repotted it and spliced it, where it has learned their habits and begun to anticipate their movements.

Their houses are partly them. The green roofs are tended as hair is, trimmed and combed and taught to lie down when the wind does not need to be tempted toward a particular net. Doors are thick lips of stitched moss, turned with a push of a shoulder that knows where the seam forgives. Underfoot, floors are a double thing: flat stone wearing a mat. The mat whispers when you step on it; it tells the room who has come in and whether you have brought back the air you left with.

They eat the place carefully. Bread is not flour here but a cake made by patient fungus, washed of its bitterness with snow and taught to be mild by repeated company with stone. There are soups that are almost teas, made by pouring water through a stack of herb and lichen and fiber and catching what you need to taste in a bowl at the bottom. You might be handed something that looks like a square of turf and be told to eat it from the bright side where the tips are golden—sweet as quiet can be. There is a syrup drawn from the gummy resin that makes dew-catcher threads; it smells faintly of amber trapped in frost, and a single drop can convince a hard day that it was worth it.

They keep no animals because the plateau simply does not, but that does not make their towns empty. The low halls are crowded with relationships. Apprentices learn by living with hands that have tied the world for longer. It is possible, here, to belong more to a knot than to a parent: lineage is recorded in the way a bundle is bound, in the spacing of lashings on a tool-net, in whether your thumbs turn in at the last or out. A person may inherit a loop on a master’s belt; from then on the loop remembers how you work and holds your weight when you lean back over the edge of a cistern to adjust the lip of a wall.

If there is a central office to these lives, it is the fog-well. The black bowls in their cities receive water as if they had been given a promise. Every street, every slope, is an argument gently laid to convince the morning to end in the center. Someone watches the surface all day, not to see themselves but to see everything but themselves: the shape of a gust in the way a skin trembles, the thin tilting of the world toward or away from winter visible in the way the water refuses a skim of ice. Their word for governance is the same as their word for equilibrium. Decisions are made with placement. A law is a stone moved a thumb-width and everyone watching nod because yes, that is where it lives best.

Guilds are not so much professional as grammatical. Fogwrights teach the sky how to recognize itself: they choose where nets belong, tune the cords, harvest and temper the amber threads. Rime Gardeners keep the living walls in their mood, feeding them with the trickle from under eaves and turning their faces toward late light. Ringkeepers maintain the drilled circles at the edges of neighborhoods; they are as close to police as anyone needs, but their work is to keep cords true, not people. Stone Persuaders listen to rock with their hands until it agrees to sit. Air Readers become that person everyone looks at before lifting a weight, because they can tell when the plateau has softened its gravity by a hair.

The quiet is not solemnity. It is a tuned space. During the late afternoons when fog-lakes decide to walk, whole courtyards lean their backs into benches and hum. You could think it is music, and it is, but it is also calibration. The condensers buzz in their cages of ice and those inside match the note; an hour later, the microclimate under the bower holds three percent more water for the mats to drink. If a child laughs at the exact right pitch by accident, an elder will ruffle their hair and say you have good lungs, let the net teach you how to use them.

They have games. One involves walking in a dry morning directly from a house to the far ring without stepping off a contour, blindfolded, guided only by the weight of air on the skin above the upper lip. In another, lines of thread are staked a hand-width above the ground and each player is allowed to strike them with only exhale, tuning a chord across the courtyard that makes any pool nearby show its smallest ripples. There is betting. There is bragging, but it is quiet and consists of placing a polished black rock on the lintel to say I did this on a day most people wouldn’t have tried.

Moving from city to city is less heroic than it sounds and more like gardening across a distance. Paths are tended by not erasing them. Rope-lines traced along ridge spines can be plucked like instruments to see how far they’ve drifted. Travelers carry a traveling place with them: a bolt of green rolled at the waist, two collapsible fog-combs, a small black tray to make an evening of sun last long enough to warm hands while they lash a sleeping dome down. They plan departure not by the hour but by the mood of dew; a journey that is a day in the dry and two hours in the wet is a poem the young are given to memorize so they will not be confused when the world keeps its word twice.

They know the bass hum that crosses the plateau and leaves the air shimmering as a kindness the old world forgets to hide from them some nights. They do not agree on what makes it. Some say it is the long pipes of bedrock singing as stress runs its hand along them. Some say it is the cities conversing at a scale no arm can measure, bullroaring to keep company. The prevailing ethic, applied to rock and neighbor and weather, is persuasion by compliment rather than force. Even their counting respects the plateau’s limited patience with being fixed. They keep two books: one of numbers you can write and another of the same numbers understood as a kind of tide.

Birth is gentle and complicated. New life here arrives with a kit. There are no cradles as elsewhere; instead, a frame of cords is hung at the edge of a condenser and a hammock of live moss is knitted into it from all sides. A baby grows inside the smell of green and water, learning early that comfort can be a climate you share with a plant. In their first winter, every child is given a net no bigger than a scarf and taught to harvest their own drink from air. You will not see anyone scolding. The plateau takes care of instruction. If you misplace a stone rim and your porch gathers ice for a week, you will come to the same conclusion a harsh word would.

The dead are not buried deep. Cold teaches exposure, not concealment. A body is swaddled in the best work of the household’s hands and laid on a flat stone that every morning looks east and every evening looks again. The green is allowed to have them, slowly. Tools are redistributed by the knot—they pass not to kin but to those whose fingers already turn the right way.

Metal is a rumor, a secret some belts know. When a sliver appears, it has fallen from the sky. Nails the length of a finger drive into places where wood and stone disagree about who should hold which load; they are not pulled again. There are blades with a gleam that does not come from bone or stone, and these are not owned by anyone in the way ownership is understood elsewhere. They are held in trust by the one who will only use them when wood will not answer to a pebble’s tap and there is no other correct thing to do.

Religion, if you feel you need a word that big, happens in the inventory. Before the first frost of a large winter there is a Ritual of Placement in every city. You will see every doorstep emptied, every stone lifted and set down again a hair to the left or right, every cord checked and retied, every net rehung by someone whose fingers remember why this spot worked last year and which were the mornings it did not. Everyone sings the compliment of their own house to the house across the way, and for a night the entire place hums like one big condenser and does not mind. They have saints of arrangement: ancestors who are remembered because if you run your hand along a certain wall you will feel the exact place they moved a rock to make an entire lane less lonely.

There are, besides the breathers, other lives the plateau keeps. The condensers are constructs but also colonies; their lattices of cane and mineral and vine host a society of things that are not plants in the ordinary way. In certain great winters when their cages crystalize, you can hear the thrum of their working from a mile off—a sound that lifts itself along the emptiness and puts a line of silver hairs along your wrist. The fog-lakes are shy animals that deny they are animals. They have routes and habits. People know which basins they favor and speak about them as neighbors: that one is a slow-comer; this one sulks unless you have brushed the heather beside it in the week before.

The moss itself creeps—everywhere, but also in minds. You will hear someone say, without coyness, that their thinking has moved a little toward the door since last thaw. They mean it. Arrangements have a way of adjusting not only where things are but what they are supposed to be. Lovers here do not romance each other with promises of the everlasting; they offer instead to learn the same knots and like the same weather.

To outsiders, the silence can feel like judgment. It is not. It is a form of labor. Quiet is the way everything listens for what it must not miss: the tick of a single bead falling into a cistern; the torn-paper sigh of a seam in rock opening by the thickness of a nail; the footstep of a fog-lake when the wind thinks it is being very clever sneaking past a net. The earliest lesson given to a child—after the trick of making their own drink—is not to hush, but to hear.

In that hearing, the plateau participates. The world here is not an adversary to be surmounted or a resource to be mined. It is a co-worker whose vocabularies are water and weight and patience, a colleague of enormous seniority and recent eccentricities. What lives here has learned to build with it as one builds with a friend: placing laughter at the right end of the day; putting a hand on a shoulder and moving a fraction together until the thing between you holds in the new way and does not need to be forced again.

If you were to stay long enough, you would become a little like them by accident. Your stride would choose contours without being asked. Your breath would ration itself in syllables that count as much as they say. You would come to trust, more than you thought you could, that water will show up if properly invited and that distance means as much as weather allows it to. You might wake one morning with your bed facing yesterday and only smile, because of course it does, and you will set your palm against the floor and wait for the very slight agreement that comes back when the house realizes you are awake and would like to try today again with the door a little further to the left.

They are patient, exact, persuasive, and unspectacular on purpose. They are the plateau made manual: hands, lungs, and a long practice with gentleness paired with rigor. They carry their instructions in their belts and in the plants that keep them warm. They live not quietly so much as with quiet, a medium where the smallest gesture can be felt across a city and where the oldest rules of the world—slightly misremembered, slightly forgiven—are obeyed not for fear, but because they make a good place out of a hard one.
