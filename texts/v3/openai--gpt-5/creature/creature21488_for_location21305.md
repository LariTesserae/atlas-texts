They are citizens of edges.

If there is a temperament shared by all who make this small chamber a city, it is a love of difference, of the line where one thing becomes another. Light into not-light, dense brine into soft, ice into water, smooth into rough. They read those transitions the way a diurnal creature reads a skyline. Senses here are tongues and fronds and thin skins tuned to the pressure of a neighbor’s wake. Eyes are decorative, if present at all. Hands do not exist. Everything that is done is done by extrusion, by ciliation, by the invitation or refusal of minerals.

Closest to the skylight live the Threadgarden, the photosynthesizers with patience. They are not one species, but a textural community: hair-fine filaments that splice themselves to the ice so gently the roof does not notice, thicker cords that braid around those hairs and keep them from shearing off in the little storms. They taste the migratory dusk from above and hold it in slow sugar. They have no hurry. Their bubbles are their voices, soft and infrequent, each one a decision to share a fraction of what they have gathered. The beads of gas slide along the scalloped ceiling until they are big enough to soundlessly erase themselves. The Threadgarden’s sugars leak, by design, into the nearest veil of denser brine, and that is enough to feed the slim grazers who touch and go along the ledge and to keep the bacterial rinds nearby brighter than they would otherwise be. It is a precarious wealth and everybody knows it. When the cold comes harder and the faint glow of the rind pulls away, they braid tighter and dim, and wait.

On the walls and in the hollows the Rind itself holds seat. It is not a single pelt but an evolving overlay of bacterial and archaeal consortia that find energy not in light but in slope: the tiny downward roll from mineral to less-mineral, iron to rust, ammonium to something less eager. It is color before it is organism—cold blue at a distance, green where the Threadgarden spills sugars, rust-flecked where a seam leaks iron from high ice. It tastes of sour and metallic and ancient seawater. When it grows thick, it sweats a transparent syrup that makes dust stick; when it is gnawed to nap, it pricks crystals out of solution that will be the first sentence of a wall. It lights the city without intention. Within it, and sometimes stitching its patches together as a seamstress would stitch cloth, live bacteria whose exhaled carbonate is so slightly warm against the almost-freeze that the Rind above them billows infinitesimally, and a ledge that would have been deadly becomes survivable. They are less animals than weather, and yet their decisions—to acidify a corner to let a predator through, to alkalinize a seam until it becomes a buttress—are among the most consequential.

At the floor and along the established routes of slow current lie the Lattices: spongeworks in planes and saddles, sheets of perforated animal that pretend to be furniture and are, in fact, both industry and law. Their bodies are conversations among cells about aperture size and pump rhythm. When a sheet opens wide, the current thickens through the holes and young pass through. When it tightens to a thousand pinpricks, a porter is repelled, redirected, made to squeeze along a different path that has been agreed upon. The Lattices feed always, but their feeding is incidental to their public service. They sort. They steady the loose fines so that other builders can lay mineral. They hold small revolutions by changing their porosity collectively, turning a shortcut into a dead end, or a back alley into a boulevard.

Every buttress, every roofline in the carbonate quarter has been written by Tubewrights. They are white, jointed, almost-worms, almost-crustaceans, adapted to the pressure of their tubes the way we are adapted to air. Their skins carry an oil that both insulates and tastes: the tags they lay at every corner encode “continue here” in a catalog of bitter and sweet that their kin can parse but others cannot. They carry saturated brine like a weapon, piping it against the roof to induce fresh ice when it is needed as a brace, or against raw ice to steep a spot with minerals so that carbonate will better take. A rib grown overnight is not miraculous; it is a dozen Tubewrights working in series, each extruding a crystal’s worth and knowing, by taste alone, where to stop. Their minds are local, short. Their memory is durable and external. The tube remembers. A Tubewright may die and its segment will go on as a smooth chalk corridor embroidered with short ridges: marginalia in mineral of what it knew. They bud their successors from the inner face of their own home. When the room becomes too crowded, a Tubewright will dissolve its rear wall and hand a passage to the Lattice for use as a new side street, and everyone involved reads that softening of carbonate as permission.

The couriers with the red kernels of eyes—they have more claim to the word “fleet” than ships ever did. Shrimplings, translucent except for a pair of crimson points, Shuttle down the brine rivers where salt runs a little harder and temperature a whisper warmer. Their hind legs are caddies full of sand. They pinch and lift and are gone. They know the owners of hungry rings—the annelid polyps that turn their crowns of cilia into moving gutters—and deliver grain into those mouths in exchange for a lick of fat that will see them through a cold snap. When two shrimplings contest a seam rich in iron or a gasket of sugar-laced brine, their antennae duel in a vocabulary of tap and sweep and hold. One yields as much to etiquette as to threat, for the city punishes those who tear a halocline with their panic. They spend their off-hours—if such a thing exists—riding the slender return flows up the left wall, cleaning their legs of grit against the Rind and letting their gills pull taste from the water: where the nursery-sign is coming strong; where the cold has bitten; where a brinicle has been rumored. They sleep by going still inside a knot of Lattice, like a thought passing into fabric.

Pearled Porters, isopods armored in bead and plate, move as if gravity increases for them and them alone. There is no room here for impatience. They do not carry building stone; they carry space. Their passage makes grooves others follow. The faint click of their plates on what is not-rock writes a metronome for their steps. They are old in a way the others are not, their molts decanting history in a helical heap on the midden. Gene-trade is more promiscuous elsewhere; the Porters demur. They keep symbionts sacred in their under-plates that let them digest the tough leavings of the city—the skins sloughed by the Lattice, the mislaid tubes of dead Tubewrights—and in return, their bellies glow: a pale, sour light, symbiont-borne. That glow is not meant as beacon, but it works as one. It calls traffic to the safe side of a loggia. It draws grazers to a row that can bear them. When a Porter wants privacy, it muddies its gut with fines, and the light dims. Streetlights, undone.

Suspended in the slacker pools where tedium would be, the Sweepers make their whole bodies into brooms. They are membranes elaborated in lace, cilia-tufted, almost-jellies but with an organization incompatible with falling. Each beat of their cilia has a timbre, and the Sweepers recognize one another by the way they rumple the water with their labor. When night changes the blue of the Rind—it is always night, but darker happens—swarms of dust grow bold, and the Sweepers rock and tilt at angles that gather that dust into their mouths. They have colonized most of the quiet corners without argument. Their work is its own defense: where they sweep, the Threadgarden and the Rind are not smothered. When a brinicle begins to drip a poison-cold finger from the roof, the Sweepers feel the arrival as a wrongness in viscosity and edge away, leaving a bare square that the finger will obediently claim and freeze.

The predator that uses doors that are not doors wears no name in the city’s own tongue; the taste that signifies it says only, here-is-withdrawal. We might call it a Ribbon if it would notice the flattery. It is eel-slim and mirror-skinned, heavy enough to ride the knife between two veils without falling through, light enough to curve away when the curtain trembles. It hunts imprecision. A larva forgets to feel for shear with its cirri and flutters across a halocline; the Ribbon twitches and the larva is an absence. It might go a day without so much as a sip. It can go a week. The patience in it is not learned; it is the physics of being a taut line. It brightens when full; a slick heat rolls off it into the nearest Rind and a green patch answers with extra fervor. Predation is not separate here from agriculture.

Down among the drifts of fines live the Archivists: worms as thin as handwriting, frilled with tiny, regular legs, and with heads less like heads than like dispensers. They do not go up much. They write along the grain of chitin and calcite, eating old houses into sentences of pitted growth and leaving behind a glue sweeter than Threadgarden sugar. Into that glue the city lofts a new generation of tubes. The Archivists’ glue is what lets a tower arise from ruin, and also what binds bones into a mound that is not trash. Their bodies record in layers of metal what the brine was last tide and last year and last crack. A Porter will sometimes pause and lick the upper millimeter of an Archivist's deposit, and then change the way it clicks, reassured or warned by what the taste says.

Everywhere there are feathery combs and the fat lung-sacks behind them: not lungs, here, but slow, deliberate peristaltic pumps that keep anemone-kin fed and tubing. They flutter in the richer seams with proprietary aplomb, installed like shopkeepers in doorways that are in truth just inclines in chemistry. Their feathery crowns are readers and authors both: they taste the moving meal and they slice it into finer streamers with a rhythm that says, this way is nursery; this way is toward predators; here, go slowly. Behind them, their bodies heave the processed water out to the edges, reinforcing the haloclines with their output. Many of them host bacteria along their inner faces in careful rows, and those bacteria make the carbonate plates that, stacked like shells, become roofs.

The nurseries themselves are small acts of conspiracy. A dozen mothers of a dozen kinds may share an alcove behind the mucus plug. Eggs that look nothing alike float together, each anchored by a gloss thread the color of tea. They are tended by taste. A Tubewright will put its palps into the plug and draw out alien eggs for caress as if they were its own; a Shrimpling will lay sand in the plug if it senses the plug weakening. Babies here bruise themselves into knowledge. You can tell where a clutch has hatched by the scuffed gloss of a halocline in that room; a faintly milky wavering where clear had been. The city celebrates a hatch with no noise. The Rind brightens and sugars spit; the Sweepers shift their beat to a tempo that pens the newborn along the correct seam; the Ribbon flickers as if deciding, and then subsides, as if reminded of some old agreement it has with the pumps.

Some live as purely in the brine as we do in air. There are filmy planarians that keep their whole intelligence in their skin, reading the acidity of the chamber like text, flattening when a gradient steepens into a wall so that they are sails rather than ships. There are forager bells—medusae no thicker than a fingernail—that keep miners in their tentacle-knots: little chemoautotrophs that pay their rent by purifying the brine inside the bell, keeping the bell’s nerves fluent. There are ciliate combs that have colonized the haloclines themselves, living as lines and using anyone who rides that line (Ribbon, Shrimpling, even a tiny worm) as abrading stones to thin their neighbors and thicken their own rooms. Not everything that inhabits the city has a house. Some are houses.

Viruses fall through here like letters. They do not linger. They catch in the nets of the Lattice and are parceled—this one into the Rind where its tricks will be made into lasting metabolism, that one into the nursery where it fails to write and is simply sugar. Now and then a leaflet of genes takes root, and the city changes what it can digest, or how easily it climbs ice, or which edges it can read. News, then.

Government—you would miss it if you looked for a face. It is cilia-beat along a main flow, suggesting slowing now. It is a thin acid line projected by the Rind where the cold bites harder, saying, do not push. It is the combined light of Porter bellies gathered in a wreath that signals a detour while a brinicle grows and settles. It is a spiral of carbonate, a city-making gesture that took the lifetimes of things with lifetimes measured in days, that now straightens a river that turned too quickly for the newborn; that sends the hungry where the Sweepers can manage their leavings. It is courtesy learned, not commanded: touch first, then taste.

Food is not abundance here; it is precision. Grazers who come to the market where the inflow fans are neither stupid nor reckless. They choose with palps what patch of Rind to scissor and which to leave, because to remove a kempt slab is to remove a wall, and a wall collapsed is a street changed, and the city will punish an unannounced change with wake-hardness where the grazer thought to find softness. Predators do not gorge. They subtract. A baby is a simple proof that a seam exists; remove too many proofs and the seam itself fades under disuse, and the predator’s future is poorer.

There are saints here, if you like, though they do not possess virtue so much as habit. There is the Curdler, a mat of bacteria that likes its own exhalations so much it grows only where dozens of others blow on it; where it grows, a sticky quiet accrues that becomes shelter and then a quarter. There are the Veil-Singers, ciliate communities that tune the haloclines to particular densities by flapping in modes, keeping the sheets from fracturing when the roof groans. There are the Line-Riders, larval of someone else entirely, who skate the tension planes as a childhood and take with them the memory of where the planes were when they grow heavy and begin to build.

Any map would be old the hour after you inked it. But you would find again those who make this city possible if you returned. The Threadgarden, with its bubble-verse of soft exhalations. The Rind, humming in minerals and intent. The Lattices, whose bodies are bylaws. Tubewrights etching and reading the taste of their own work. Shrimplings measuring with their antennae the worth of a seam. Porters glowing when the roads want light and dimming when they do not. Sweepers beating dust into food and food into order. The Ribbon crossing rooms without touching a wall. Archivists linking a thrown-away shell to a future home with a line of glue so clean it tastes like logic. The feather-crowns and pumping sacks gesturing the circulation of a people without fingers.

It is not strange to them that a room can be a falling sheet, that a door can be a density you open with salt, that a streetlight is the abdomen of an old animal who is digesting and does not mind being looked at. It is only right that news is an acid blush through the flow, that a plan is written as a mineral tag at a corner, that emergency is the smell of fresh water where it does not belong. They would not thrive where we stand. Out at the surface, overwhelmed by emptiness and light, they would be edits in a book too blank to hold them. Here, in a place that fits them like a lung, they are what cities mean when they begin: not a set of buildings, but the practice of making neighbors out of gradients.
