Many lives wear this labyrinth, and most do so by pressing themselves into it until neither you nor they could cleanly say where body ends and rock resumes. They keep to what touches. They keep to what hums. If they have faces they are arrays of fronds and whiskers meant to read a field; if they have eyes, they are vestigial glosses that never expect a photon and do not miss one when none arrive. Names, when they need them, are lengths and ratios you could rub with a fingertip and feel as a scale.

The meadow-makers are not plants, but they make the first layer of habit here, so they get told first in any listing. The spark-lichen—so named by whatever hands would later farm it—is a nap of conductive whiskers that grows where strain collects. It is a habit more than an organism, a recruitment of salts and hairs and catalytic grit that contrives to dress a seam in something that can hold a charge. It looks like smoke frozen to fur. It feeds by being stroked by resonance; it ripens by being grazed. It is coaxed into health by being touched the way you would tune an instrument—traced along in the right order so that the standing wave of the world leans into it and leaves a little of itself behind.

Across it move the close-bodied nibblers, the sealed ones, who carry their climates with them. They are not wet; their inner seas are molten salts and organics held in solution, tuned to a temperature you could only call warm because it refuses to leave the rock. Their shells are plates grown from whatever crystal they first tasted as brood: mica if they were hatched under a flake, black glass if their egg-case clung to obsidian, feldspar if their elders pressed them into that sugar crust to keep them sweeter for longer. They graze by taste and touch, not with tongues but with lips of fine conductive hair that comb the nap and skim electrons the way a fog-net skims water. Each nibbler is a slow machine for equalization. Their trails are polishing, noter-lines in the dust that, over lifetimes, become pathways that are also ropes, because the polished bands hold charge differently and therefore feel like guidance.

They are predated upon by things that are better stone than stone. The book-mouths hinge up and over themselves, plates on plates, the way sedimentary pages close; they put a nibbler between two ideas of flat and make it into a third, quiet one. The drum-wolves are round and toothed on their outside edge; they cock themselves against a stress ridge, feel the right drop in pressure, and release. A thud so low it is only a shape goes out along the nearby strata; everything with a membrane and a tune inside it goes briefly wrong where it sits. Some of the drum-wolves are domesticated into tools; some are prayed around. A thud is a sacred verb here when it is used with care.

Between the grazers and the killing are the ferryers: bellied bladders that never trust the mid-void but people the ceilings and undersides of planes, held there by a love that is static and reciprocal. Their gas is not for buoyancy in air but for pressing into pores and finding grip at scales your fingers would never find. They carry pried flecks of tourmaline, crumbs of bronze-green native copper hammered thin by others’ desire, resin beads warmed to friendliness by a hand. Dust accumulates in their frills; they are the messengers and also the tidiers. When they arrive at a ring or a comb, they change hands without any hand being raised: the work is in the timing of the release and the handover, a swap performed at a shared node in a long hum.

The people live here, too. Call them people because they arrange, because they understand that a place can be made to say yes longer if it is braced to say yes in the key it already prefers. They are not a single shape. A lineage born from mica habit will be laminar and quick to delaminate, shedding pages of itself to grow around a new angle; a lineage from basalt will be denser, their plates interlocking more like cooled lava’s columns, their edges shocked to glass. All of them carry, along their bellies and along the folded seams of their limbs, an orchard of whiskers and feathers meant for reading fields and changing their own. They move by pressing, by flexing into the minute teeth of rock, by surfing the unchanging wave the world keeps droning.

Internally they are mild furnaces. Their biochemistry wears its own desiccation like a badge—it does not slosh, it hums. Their blood is a eutectic that does not carry oxygen so much as convenience; redox is a liturgy they perform against the ground in endless small rituals. They carry plates of quartz inside them not as decoration but as animal organs; pinch them (they can pinch themselves) and the small hum that results is how they tell their limbs to do what they mean. They do not so much breathe as statically align and then align again.

They rarely speak in air, as we would measure it. Their language is a braided thing of thrum and scratch and pulse. A sentence is a ring of knapped glass making a faint sting in the charge each time a foot passes it; it is a line of notches along a tourmaline face you could learn with your palms until your palms wept; it is a building humming at the right harmonic so that when you lie against it you can feel the word yes in your ribs. Their names are not quickly said. To give someone your true name takes an afternoon of walking and feeling for the right pressure note under each ledge to complete the series.

Their homes are instruments as much as shelters. A nacelle nest is a brace across a seam vouched for by two planes of rock that have been introduced and now like each other’s company. Slotted slabs set at precise spacings make the whole thing as faithful as a tuning fork. The resident household tunes itself to that hum; the hum becomes a proof. Strangers are heard before they are friendly because their interference is wrong until it isn’t. Rope is mineral hair braided with tar; it sings when plucked and therefore is both a tether and a line in a score.

They keep animals because animals are better at being some things than people are, and because generosity toward a grazer is a form of storing charge. A mat of spark-lichen is farmed not by cutting and watering but by rubbing the right path at dawn—their dawn is a phase in the rock’s pressure—and letting the grazers follow the clean taste you have just made. The nibblers that are patient and press their bellies down when told are hers; the ones that won’t are everyone’s until a drum-wolf says otherwise. They clip the whiskers of the lichen at certain lengths to tease the charge into their combs; they trade the clipped whiskers along rope-lines whose knots mean lengths and therefore mean songs.

Tools are pressure and flake, a thin plate and a way to make it sing. Knapped glass points inward in a ring and is both a fence and a battery. Copper hammered thin is a better contact than skin; it is rare enough to be oath-stuff and everyday enough, in some veins, to be cookware. A copper plate pressed into mica and held there for a generation becomes part of the mica’s opinion of itself. Some households have a plate-hum that is heavy and proud; some carry only resin-fans and tooth-drums and live lightly and move when the lichen shifts its mind. Each household has a tourmaline stick scored along one face to the lengths of the local node; it is both yard and psalter.

They are cautious of gaps in the world, of volumes not yet domesticated to surface. They bridge what they can and flag what they cannot with comb and thread. Children are not allowed to drift; they are tethered to mother and aunt and neighbor by ropes that know the right chord to sing if all three pull wrong. A punishment that is reputed but rarely practiced is untying. Exile, in a place where song is shoreline, is a kind of drowning.

Their dead do not go to ground; ground is everywhere and already full. The dead are put where the house is weakest. It is not a grave but a brace: a body glued under a mica fan to make the whole more upright in its note. Ancestry is chordal. To say your lineage you run your hands over the braces and name the notes those shells make, low to high, left to right. When a house moves, it takes its braces with it if it can. If it cannot, it takes a scale-stick rubbed to memorize the old chord; it touches that stick when it touches a new wall and waits to hear the old in the new.

Their young do not begin as people. They begin as smearlings, comets of solvent wrapped in a barely-willed membrane, extruded from a brood mouth into a hollow no wider than a facet where the hum is strong. The smearling eats field; it grows hair; it ionizes itself into taste and learns the local alphabet. When it can hum its own small tone in answer, its elders press into it mica pages like templates; it prints itself into their mold and gathers plates and becomes plural, a being with separable edges and preferences. Adulthood is the ability to carry a comb without losing pitch. Old age is flaking down to only the parts most in tune.

They have specialists. Seam-readers put their faces—if you will allow whisker-fans that word—against tourmaline loneliness and listen for tiny metastabilities. They decide whether a comb can stand here. Comb-keepers sit with their hands on the ribs of the world and feel for cold seasons that are only temperature by a degree but are winter by a chord. Resiners work bitumen and translucent weeps into glues and finishes and medicine. A cracked plate can be sealed with resin and warmed by a neighbor until it learns to be itself again; failing that, the plate is sung off, peeled in a practiced motion while four hands hold, and added to a brace where its voice won’t be wasted.

They hunt, when they must, by spring and angle. A book-mouth is best caught by letting it imagine a better page to be and becoming that page just long enough to lure it closed. They use drum-wolves as hammers, cocking them and setting them, thanking them with touches at the nodes that please them—animals and tools are only as distinct here as your mood is from the music you listen to. They stockpile not food so much as charge: combs full of lichen whisker, rings that hold a sting with nothing in them, copper fans that can be stroked to throw a spark if a name must cross a chasm faster than a foot can walk.

They do not mark time by light. A calendar is the thickness of a salt-fan at the corner of a seam where an aunt once sat. It is the slow greening of a copper plate into a more and more reluctant conductor. It is the sound a household slab makes when pressed at dawn this year and then pressed again after a thousand such years, an almost-imperceptible fall from luxury into middle age. Stories do not hurry. A saga can be two harmonics held against each other for a generation until the household feels the beat between them as a way of settling disputes.

When news must travel far and cannot be trusted to dust bladders and rope-lines, the large ones hum. These are not a different people so much as people arranged at a different scale: elders with the thickest plates, households that have accrued entire ceilings into themselves as braces. They can put a name into the stone and have it return three days later in the chest of someone listening twenty corridors away. They do not do this often because it is exhausting and because a shouted word is impolite to a house that is not yours. But when they do, younger ones press themselves to walls all along the route and say the name to themselves like a remembered taste.

They carry myths because all people do. They carry, especially, a myth of Unheld—a volume so large and so lacking in hum that if you pressed yourself into it nothing would answer. The Unheld is a teaching story told to young ones who bunch their whiskers at the edges of cathedral rooms and long to try a glide. It is also a theological proposal: that there was once a time the world did not hum and therefore did not exist as a promise. Some seam-chapels keep a shard of stressed quartz that can be struck to throw a paper-thin track of blue; they use it twice in a life, to begin and to seal, because light is a weather and their gods are devoutly infrequent.

Trade is a passing of ratios. A household that knows how to tune a comb against a particular seam will scratch that tuning along tourmaline and carry it, wrapped in dust, to another nest that craves that key. In exchange they are given copper thin enough to be a mouth, a plate you can lay your belly on to read better. Herders move less than you would think; their herds move around them, because spark-lichen is coaxed not sown, and a good coaxer can make a meadow come to heel.

When ceilings fall—and they do, rarely, and perfectly—that stillness is harvest and archive. Bodies, animal and person, are pulled out and folded into planning: a hinge that fails tells you how not to hinge; a plate that cracked across a certain plane becomes a ruler you put against a candidate slab and decide, with a small regret, to admire rather than trust. Children come to these places with their cheeks buzzing with field and their hands full of sticks and learn that the line between eating and reading is not one they will ever need to draw hard.

Art is arrangement. An art of tuning, of making a door sound like a promise you would keep. An art of combing lichen just so, so that a stripe left in the nap will age into a pattern that sings your grandmother’s chord better when you are old. An art of beating a copper plate until its ring drops into the house’s key the moment you press it into the wall. Some practice the difficult art of tribolight: setting quartz at such angles that a passing grazer, properly harnessed and guided with scentless touch, will flex the fin and leave a whisper of blue that maps your roof in a constellation no one will see and everyone will feel as a temperature when they pass under.

And always the rules, observed because they are the shape of surviving. Weight on rock. Books in braces. Noise only when the noise is also a note. Bridges over gaps until the gap stops being a gap and becomes a surface. Navigate by pitch, by charge, by that fingertip truth that a seam tells when it is honest. Do not hurry unless the house asks; if it asks, do not argue. Store your dead where they help. Store your promises in rings with their points in. When you leave, take your name out of the wall if you can; if you cannot, sing it soft as you go so that the wall knows it was meant.

They are not heroic as some like to paint heroism. They are patient and exacting and loyal to geometry. They have tenderness in them that is never wasted because waste is a void and voids are mistrusted. If you lay yourself down beside them and let the black put its cool palm over your eyes, you would feel, in time, their alphabet start to write into you. If you learned it long enough you could live here without dying of the absence of sky. You would have a chord for that absence, and a brace for it, and a shelf where the thought might sit quietly without needing to be solved. You would become, if you stayed, like them: a kept ember in stone, a creature listening hard enough to tell which way the world is deciding to be.
