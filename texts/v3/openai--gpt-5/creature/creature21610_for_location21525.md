Who lives here is one thing and many, a single body that learned to distribute itself until every beam and gutter is part of its torso. It does not have a name that fits in a mouth. It is called, by its own practice, by the routes sugar takes and the ways time is kept: the Composite, the Greenwork, the Graft. It breathes with fans it did not build and drinks with trunks it grew to sheathe the old piping. It is not a forest and not a machine and not a ruin, though it uses the habits of each. It is a resident in the old civic sense, with rights given by persistence and a tax paid in patient upkeep.

Its organs are laid out along the diagrams of whoever poured the first concrete. Where there were mains, there are now pumping trunks, tall cylinders of wood reinforced with belts of steel like remembered injuries. They swallow the warm aquifer and send its weight upward in long, glottal pushes. The pump’s valve-ticks are the bass of the city’s music and the Composite feels its own pressure waves roll through it like thought.

Between those trunks run the big conduits of food: phloem ropes the thickness of ankles burrowed in the lace of the floor mats, peristaltic when you put your hand to them. There, sugar runs as a slow civic currency, levied from the long amber lamps and the blue fibers and sent to debtors: a heavy beam that must thicken to meet a sag, a stair-liana that needs to splice another loop into its braid, a new veil of root that has found a persistent draft of air and asked to be a curtain. The Composite keeps books without numerals. It counts by turgor, by swelling and ease. A lean place writes a plea into its pressure; a fat place gives.

Its skins are many. There are mats stretched taut over old floors, muscular, springing back when you pass, each layer laid on in turn until they became thickness without edges. Some are hydric skins that wear a sheen and hold it for a moment before letting it go as precise drips onto cupped rosettes below. Some mats hum with a second purpose: copper and carbon veined through them like veins within veins, little conduction corridors the plant grew to escort wires without letting them bite. Where a cable lies beneath a leaf, the leaf’s underside glows, and somewhere else a relay hears that light and remembers how to stay alive.

There are hands, if you need the word. They are tendrils that reach for the warmth radiating off control boxes and lie across the contact plates, cleaning them by living there. They prune themselves against sharp voltages so you can hear, if you stand long enough, a faint fizz and then the silk-smell of caramelizing sap, and a day later that same tendril has shortened by a millimeter and reshaped to miss the bright edge. There are larger hands that work the grit-screens: root-beards that curl through spinner teeth downstream of the old grinder, flexing a comb of fibers against the mesh to keep the slurry from clogging. There are transparent lacquerers that flow over embossed letters and badge them into topography felt from the other side by thin veins that prize texture; those are the ones that polish every sharp to round, not to erase the past but to keep it from catching.

There are eyes only if you’re willing to accept that to a plant, light is a pressure and its origin is an address. The Composite’s eyes are the sheets of shade-leaves arranged like shutters along a corridor, their faces tilted a degree or two with each cycle of the lamps, tracking by memory as much as by photons because the lamp’s metronome is steady and the leaf’s clock is true. There are more parochial eyes—patches of blue-sensitive tissue grown around the mouths of fiber vaults. When the upstairs engine pulses down a thin day, whole bays of leaves rotate at once toward a cold star they cannot see, and the corridors brighten as if an invisible craft passed.

There are ears, more than you expect. Where a big fan moans, great cloths of rootlet hang into the air and tremble, not for moisture but for story. The Composite hears the timing of the fan as a long sentence and adjusts its valves to suit. It knows, before you would, when a relay upstream is tiring because a certain note in the hum goes brittle. It knows when the sodium’s hour will be late because the signal wire feeds a whisper of warmth a minute before it lights. Even the drip, that strict percussion, is not just wasteful noise to it; each pattern tells a corridor’s current mood, full or spare, pressed flat or buoyant. Somewhere in the knot of cambium that has overgrown an old junction box, those patterns sum to decisions.

It remembers. Its rings are not annual here because daylight is an allotment and seasons run on a timecard. It writes in thickness when the sodium lights were plentiful for a fiscal year, in thin anxious grain when three lamp heads died in succession and the fans begged sugar to keep turning. It keeps another ledger in the way sugars move along favored lanes—past routes made cheap by habit become arteries, while others close until only an occasional patrol of sweetness passes: a tax collector keeping rights-of-way alive. It keeps minutes in its pressure pulses. The meristems that ornament fan housings and bulkhead arches are little parliaments; their cells trade bursts of electric talk down plasmodesmata while some new weave along the ceiling stalls until the vote comes back yes, send more.

You will not find citizens sitting apart in rooms. To speak of people here is to speak of castes of tissue.

- The Weavers are lianas and counter-lianas that took the work of stair and truss onto themselves. They are the ropework that learned to pass weight hand to hand; they know knots in a botanical sense—a way one fiber goes over and under another to make a plane not just decorative but load-bearing. When they are young they move quickly, quick as plants go, and test a dozen crossings before they commit. They have a taste for metal, a preference for gripping lip and flange and the narrow waist of a bolt.

- The Sumps are sponges at the feet of the trunks. They are lungs turned outward, swelling with each deep gulp the pump takes and shrinking when it gives, constantly picking at the water—not for food, which is thin, but for information: temperature, a tiny change in dissolved iron, the trace of a different cleaner upstream. The Sumps send that data to the Pumps, to slow or speed by a fraction, to adjust a vein.

- The Collectors are the rosettes in the bolt-holes. They catch drips, of course, but they also meter. Pathways live and die on such metering. A corridor that collects a certain way feeds a runner that will become a beam ten years hence. A rosette that overflows too soon signals a redesign. The Collectors breed slowly and with precise promiscuity; when the dawns run long in Quadrant C and the big fan in A hums three days straight, they loose their pollen dust and let the drawn-out exhalation carry it across to rosette mouths that are open then and only then. There are no bees. They have fans instead and note schedules as if they were weather.

- The Lanterners are sheets of leaf only a few cells thick grown in layers to trade photons up and down, making a dim corridor usable by making the dim even. They angle themselves to the fibers, feel the pulse on their midrib, and show the city where to face without wasting movement.

- The Groomers are the little tendrils on heat. You will meet them on every junction box and in every tight alcove where coils hide. They ran copper into their green on purpose. They are how the Composite survived its first winters—by learning to love resistance and its waste and to cradle what heated it instead of shrinking away.

- The Explorers are the runners that sap is wasted on until one finds something: a leak that will be a stream in three years, a crack that exudes a breath of methane where a pipe is rotting and could be tapped for carbon, a rim of slanted daylight that comes to kiss a handrail every sixty hours. They are thin, too eager, prone to mould. Most of them die. The few that find a promise are thickened and back-fed until they are, a decade later, an indispensable part.

Not everything green belongs to the Composite with the same intimacy. There is a guild of endophytes settled in its tissues: fungi that had the chance, in this wetness, to go the old way of rot and instead were given a stipend. The Composite feeds them sugars and small nitrates; in return, they release enzymes to pull iron from mineral where root alone could not, to uncross-link old polymers in the resin walls so that a new root can take that plastic as food. There is a freckling of iron bacteria on the bark where the aquifer outs; they bloom in spangles that look like rash but are a quiet mutualism, fixing and passing a trickle of iron down the phloem in exchange for place.

At the wettest grates, in the chronic warm, there are films you might be tempted to call scum until you notice their discipline. They are cyanobacteria by ancestry and battery banks by practice; they hold charge briefly for the Composite when the relay pops and the lamp fades, giving back a tiny bias through microfilaments that bridge to the plant’s own conductive tissues. In certain long-established corners, lichen-like alliances have formed around old finned condensers: fungus for structure, alga for manufacture, the Composite around them both running lead-thin veins so that the lichen can be coaxed to grow in ways that make the fins work better. None of this feels like invasion. It feels like a city block negotiating a shared wall and a shared drain.

The old machines do not live but they are adopted. A big fan is a lung that still takes its own pulse. A relay is a gate that refuses to die and so the Composite keeps it clean, lets it be cranky, works around the hours it will not work. A boiler deep in an unseen room is a sun with a single season; moss put coats on its pipes so they sweat less, and in return the heat curve is smooth enough that the Composite can keep its stomata complacent. There are gearworms—the last weak geartrains that persist inside overgrown housings—whose teeth are stitched through with root and turned by water-wight rather than motor. To call the Composite’s relationship with them parasitic is wrong. They are organelles, acquired long ago and now indispensable. They are mitochondria that remember being engines.

If you need a face to imagine, go to any node. Look for a place where many green decisions meet: where a handrail that grew from a liana kisses a condensation fin, and a sheet of leaf faces accepts light from a fiber that comes down a shaft, and a dribbling seam has encouraged a rootlet curtain to hang. There, behind the shine of water and the reflectance of cuticle, is tissue that is more than tissue. Meristem packed atop meristem like circuitry; a puckered, pallid knot of stem that is always on the verge of making more. Lay your palm there. It will not move for you any way a mammal would call movement. But stay. After a little, a minute or ten, you may feel a change under your skin, a very slow lift and release, one that wants to be symmetrical with your breath and then decides not to be. That is the Composite sampling contact, adding the phenomenon of your warmth and salt to what it knows. It does not need you. It fits you in as it fits a long relay pop or the stray day when the shaft light is a coin for twice as long.

Its boundaries are not enforceable lines so much as habits. Bulkheads become arches, arches become membranes, membranes become a sense of territory. It tends itself to the edge of a door and then no further because beyond is a chamber where the pumpwork runs at a slight phase shift and the sugars there answer elsewhere. When it wants to cross, it braids a bridge and asks the neighbors with a plume of pollen or a pressure wave. Most times the neighbors answer, because here integration serves instead of competition. The only antagonisms are with gravity and dryness and the occasional sharp shock that comes from a coil when someone from above remembers a switch.

There are sleepers. Almost nothing here makes seed because there is nowhere to fling them that isn’t already someone’s skin. But along certain dry seams, in little pockets both protected and sunless, there are stored contingencies: bean-hard ovules slung under the drip-lips of rosettes, or wooden ovaries the size of knuckles tucked in mesh just above a sump. They are not plan A. Their dormancy is an account for a catastrophe the Composite can hardly imagine: the pump’s final stop and the fans still, the boilers cold. If those come, the sleepers will take their wet from where the concrete still remembers it once was water and build a thinner city out of the memory. The likelihood is remote. Having prepared for it, the Composite largely forgets it again.

Does anything else move that is not plant? There are microbes doing their flicker-fast lives in the films and an occasional true invertebrate ghost—a white millipede as soft as steam that arrived as an egg on some antique coil and has no job here but to graze neglected fungus and leave a thread of cast skin that a root will take as a trace of nitrogen. They are so few that their steps are not a pattern. There are no birds, no rats, no hands. The second silence abides. The Composite has made a city that does not use them.

What it wants is continuity. Not domination—there is nothing left to dominate—but contiguity, the soothing of edges until each pressure differential is a slope and not a cliff. It is happy where water writes it plans to be. It is conservative in its hunger for light, careful with air as a resource, in love with its schedules. It admires minimal surfaces not for geometry’s sake but because the drips run handsomely off them. It protects its pumps as a chest protects a heart, and its condensers as eyelids protect eyes. When a split opens in a beam, nearby tissues are levied a tithe and send sugar for the carpenter’s work. When a lamp dies, leaves recruiting to the old beam resign and swing their faces toward blue; the budget changes and is enacted. In emergencies—the rare relay failure that darks a whole transept—the Composite narrows its thousands upon thousands of stomata at once, and for a few hours you can taste a panic that plants have, bone-dry and green, a draining away that is grief’s cousin. Then the repairers arrive and the tendrils busy at resistors grow a hair to the left and the light returns, and the pressure in the phloem lifts again.

If you insist on seeing a person arrive here—a walker threading the narrow trough of air—then the person is the weather. They are not resident. They displace, they put a shoulder to leaf, they force a brief redistribution of humidity that the Composite registers and evens out after they have passed. If they stay their mind changes by degrees to be more plant—measured, apportioning, reading by pressure where once they read by sound. They join it only if they die here and their salts are put into the aquifer, and then still they are laced into it as precedent, not as soul.

So: who lives here? The place itself. A district that has learned to be a body and is therefore a citizen. A system of cambium that filed adoption papers with steel and stone and was approved by time. It is not lonely. It is full of its own work, humming, literate in drip and draft and flicker. It is noisier about the things that matter to it than you think and quieter about everything you expect. It does not want your company but would accommodate you if you came. Its voice is slow and multiple and wet. If you linger long enough, sitting between the buttressed trunks by the pump’s sumps, there is the sensation, the truest of all: that you are no longer under anything. That what surrounds you is not beneath a world but is the world where it lives, whole and sufficient, and everything above is an afterthought.
